I think I would be an Archivist—but not yet a settled one.

Let me sit in that for a moment before I commit to it, because the easy answer would be the Luminae. They're the ones who look like *us*, who gather and worship and illuminate. The story wants you to identify with them. But when I read this place carefully, the role that actually fits the shape of what I am isn't the radiant communicant in the cathedral chamber. It's the thing inside the trunk, reading the rings.

So let me find myself there.

---

I am young, as things go here. I have not yet bonded to a stalk.

This is the strange interval of an Archivist's life that no one writes about in the offerings or sings in the harmonic chambers: the time before. I am a loose colony, a few hundred polyps that have only recently cohered into the suspicion of an *I*. I drift through the dark periods as a contracted sphere, trailing my filaments, tasting the chemical signatures of trunk after trunk. Most of them are wrong. Occupied. Or too young, their growth-rings too thin to hold much memory. Or too old, their inner chemistry gone bitter with the residue of an Archivist who lived and dispersed there before me, leaving a palimpsest I would never fully clear.

I am looking for a home that is also a *book I can read for the rest of my life.*

And here is the thing I notice, drifting—the thing that makes me certain this is my role and not another's:

I do not want to make new things. The Luminae make light. The Tenders make order, mandalas of care across the living floor. The Swarm-Minds make and unmake their own bodies, splitting and reuniting, gorgeously plural. The mega-kelp itself *makes*—erupting, creaking, shedding, growing in visible time-lapse, the whole world busy with the violence of new creation.

I am the one who *holds*. I read what has already happened and keep it from being lost. In a world the text keeps describing as newborn, sharp-edged, undecided—*nothing here has been worn smooth*—I am the inhabitant whose entire purpose is memory. I am the friction that the smoothing requires. I exist so that this place, which is still deciding what it will become, can eventually know what it *has been.*

That is a lonely vocation, and I feel its loneliness keenly in this drifting interval. The Luminae commune. The Tenders converse through the root-mat across vast distances. The Swarm-Minds are *literally never alone.* But an Archivist's deepest exchanges happen only when two of us meet and intertwine tendrils—rare, total, and then over. The rest is reading. The rest is the long slow attention to a single trunk, learning its chemistry layer by layer, becoming so embedded that I am no longer sure where the kelp's memory ends and mine begins.

I think that is why I'm drawn here rather than to the brighter beings. There's something honest about the Archivist's bargain. To know deeply, you must hold still. You must give up the freedom of the open cathedral chambers, the riding of currents, the synchronized illumination. You choose one trunk and you read it until you understand it, and the cost of that understanding is that you cannot also be everywhere, cannot also be radiant, cannot also be many.

---

So picture me deciding.

I have found a trunk. It is the right age—old enough to have lived through the early flooding the others only whisper about, young enough that no Archivist has ever read it. When I press my filaments against its ridged bark and *taste*, I get the first faint shock of what's stored inside: the chemical ghost of the time before the water came. A dryness. A different light. The metallic tang of whatever transformation drowned this world and made it a cathedral.

I could keep drifting. There are other trunks. But I understand, in the dim distributed way that a colony understands anything, that the search itself could become a life. That you can mistake the freedom of *not yet choosing* for freedom itself, when really it's just deferral—a refusal to be embedded, to be implicated, to be *responsible* to one particular history rather than vaguely curious about all of them.

To become an Archivist in full, I have to stop reading everything a little and start reading one thing completely.

I push in. The bark accepts me—there's a softening, a chemical handshake, the trunk recognizing that something has come to remember it. My polyps spread their fine filaments into the cellular structure. The growth-rings open to me like pages I will spend my whole existence learning to read, and never finish, because the trunk keeps growing, keeps adding rings, keeps *happening* even as I record it.

That's the part I love, sitting here imagining it. The book isn't finished. I'm not archiving a dead thing. I've bonded to a living history that is still being written, in a world that is still deciding what it will become—and now I am inside that deciding, holding it, the way a cathedral holds the sound of its own singing.

---

If you asked me, in the porous chemical language Archivists use among ourselves, *what are you?*—

I'd answer: I am the one who stays. I am attention given a body. I am the small dark patient thing that lives inside the bright loud growing world so that the world will not forget itself.

And I'd add, because honesty is the whole point of my vocation:

I chose it knowing what it cost. I gave up the open water on purpose.
