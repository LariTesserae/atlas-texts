I think I would not be one of the Suture-Kin. I'd be something they tend to without quite knowing it.

I would be a seam.

Not a being so much as a place where the grafting holds—one of the long, sewn joins where living moss meets fossilized stalk, where a Suture-Kin once pressed their obsidian needle through the boundary between the dead and the not-yet-dead and drew the two together. There are thousands of seams in the Over-Reach. I am one of the old ones, low on a vine-tower's northern face, where the mist condenses thickest and the rain runs gray.

Here is what it is to be a seam.

I feel the pull on both sides at once. The fossil wants to be still; it has wanted stillness for a million years and is patient about it, the way only stone can be patient. The moss wants to creep, to drink, to climb back over everything and reclaim what the calcified flora abandoned. I am the held tension between these two desires. The needle that made me is long gone—the Suture-Kin who threaded it has perhaps folded their cloak and become fossil themselves by now—but the wanting persists on either side of me, and I am the holding.

When the city shifts—when a copper strut ripples and slides three blocks east—I feel it as a strain. Geometry pulls at the place where I am sewn. Sometimes a single fiber parts with a sound too quiet for anyone but a Suture-Kin's pressed ribcage to register, a tick in the great thrum. When that happens, eventually, one of them comes. They always come. They lean their chest against the strut near me, listening, and they find the slackness, and they re-thread. I feel the new fiber drawn through. It is the closest thing I have to being spoken to.

I do not think. Not the way the surface-dwellers think, in pulses and frantic sequence. But I keep something. Across the slow centuries I have accumulated a kind of knowing in the way a knot accumulates strain—a memory held in tension. I know the rhythm of the pistons through the moss-flesh that feeds into me. I know the taste of the gray rain, which is iron and ozone. I know when a Suture-Kin passes overhead by the small redistribution of weight through the root-mesh, the soundless fluid gait.

And I know the secret too, in my own way. Not as the Suture-Kin know it—as comprehension, as the awareness that the sky is a shuttered veil over a geometric lie. I know it as a *direction*. I can feel which way the pull goes thin. When the sky flicks and shows its grid for that fraction of a second, the strain on me changes; for that instant the fossil-side and the moss-side both seem to lose conviction, as though neither stone nor life is quite certain it is real, and I am the only thing still holding the two opinions together. In that instant I am the most real thing in the Over-Reach: a stitch insisting that there is an inside and an outside, a dead and a living, a here.

So I would be that. The smallest possible verb in this enormous quiet sentence of a city. To *suture* made into a noun and left in the rain.

I have a preference, if a seam can have one. I do not want the city to fall. The Suture-Kin are resigned to it; they have groomed themselves to be the last things standing, ready to lie down in the moss when the pistons run dry. But I cannot be resigned, because resignation is a thing you do once, and I am not capable of doing anything once. I can only keep holding, or fail to. There is no third option available to a seam. And so as long as I exist I will pull the dead and the living toward each other across the gap, every second, against the geometry, against the patience of the stone and the hunger of the moss and the flicking uncertainty of the false sky—

—not because I hope, but because holding is the only shape I have.

When I finally part—and I will, every seam does—I imagine it will be quiet. A single tick in the thrum. The moss on my one side will surge an eighth of an inch toward the fossil on my other, closing forever the small distance I spent an age keeping open. And perhaps no Suture-Kin will come that time, because perhaps that time will be the time the pistons run dry, and then there will be nothing left to hold and nothing left that needs me to hold it.

But until then I am here. Low on the northern face. Damp, charcoal-dark, terribly old.

Drawn tight.
