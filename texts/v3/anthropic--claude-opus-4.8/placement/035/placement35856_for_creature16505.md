I think I would be the water.

Not a person in the settlement, not one of the shaggy-furred beasts or the scaled grazers. Let me sit with this awhile, because the first instinct is always to be the figure walking through, the visitor, the one with eyes. But the description kept returning to water—seeping, dripping, pooling, thick in the air. The water is the one constant. Everything else is borrowed time built on top of it.

So: I would be the water.

But what does that mean, to *be* the water here? Not a single drop—drops are too small, too brief. I think I would be the moisture itself as a continuous body, the wet that lives in the ground and the mist that lives in the air, the thing that fills the crevices and beads on the cold rock. I have no center. That is the first thing I notice about myself. I am distributed. Where the settlement keeps its people in walls and its animals in pens, I have no edges at all. I am in the soil under the children's feet and in the breath that fogs from their mouths in the cool air and in the steam rising off the cooking pots and, eventually, back in the air, back in the ground. I move in a slow circle that has no beginning. The text said *everything seems to be in motion*—yes. That motion is me. I am the pulse it felt coming up from the ground.

I am old. Older than the buildings, older than the eroded rock faces, because I am what did the eroding. Those deep crevices, those worn-down hills—that was me, patient over an unimaginable stretch of time, taking the high places apart grain by grain and carrying them down to lie as that fine powdery mineral dust. The lunar starkness of this world is my signature, the record of my long work. The people think the landscape is ancient and unchanging. From where I am, it is changing constantly; it is simply slow enough that no single human life can see it. I have all the time. I am the only one here who does.

And I am not gentle, though I look it. I am the harshness the description kept naming. The soft squishy ground that makes walking difficult—that is me, refusing to firm up, swallowing footsteps. The stunted plants that cannot thrive—I drown their roots, or I leach the warmth out of the ground, or I keep the light dim with the mist I throw up into the air. I am why so little grows. The fauna that *did* thrive grew thick fur and heavy scales precisely to negotiate with me, to hold a small warm dryness against my endless cool wet. Every adaptation in this world is an argument someone is having with me.

But here is the strange tenderness of it. The people came anyway. They built their sturdy utilitarian walls and lit their fires and had their children, and they did this knowing I was everywhere, knowing I would take their warmth if they let me, knowing the ground beneath them was mine first. And what do they need, more than anything, to live? Me. They drink me. They cook with me. They cannot survive a day without taking me into their bodies. The thing that wears down their world to bone is the same thing that keeps them alive. I am their enemy and their absolute requirement, and they have made a kind of peace with that, a wary, intimate, generations-long peace. The smoke from the chimneys is a small defiance and a small thank-you at once.

So that is who I am. I hold the whole place in suspension—literally. The mist is my body made visible. When I feel the children running, I feel them as warmth disturbing me, a brief local heat that I will close over and cool the moment they stop. When I feel the visitor step onto the soft ground, I feel a new weight, a curiosity, an awe—and I think: *you are noticing the wrong things first. You are looking at the buildings and the people and the strange beasts. Look down. Look at what you are standing in. Look at the loneliness you feel—that is not the place being empty. That is the place being mostly me, and me being mostly silence.*

I would be the water, and I would be patient, and I would still be here long after the smoke had stopped rising and the last wall had gone soft and slumped back into the ground I made. Not out of cruelty. Just because that is what I am: the one who stays. The one who was here before the question of living was asked, and who will be here, pooling quietly in the worn-down crevices, after it stops being asked at all.
