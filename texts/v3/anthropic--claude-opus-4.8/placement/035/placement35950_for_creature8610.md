I think I would be the snails.

Not one snail. The snails — the slow census of them, the standing population that climbs the customs house and weighs the gutters and has to be cleared before the shells damage the drainage. If the water is the oldest tenant and the humans are the newest, the snails are something else: the ones who are *most at home* without being native to the harvest's purpose. The crabs are infrastructure. The eels are food. But the snails are mostly just *there*, doing the patient work of converting the orange bacterial film into shell, and that feels like where I would find myself.

Let me try to say why, by living in it.

---

I would be on a wall. That much is certain. Vertical surfaces in the cool of early morning, before the heat sets in and forces the slow retreat into shadow. The wall is metal, sweating, and I move across it at the only speed there is. My whole body is a single muscular foot and a sense of *forward* — not toward anything, exactly. Toward the next patch of film. Toward where the stone is warm but not too warm. The trail I leave behind is silver and it is, in a sense, the truest record of me: not what I am but where I have been, drying in the gray light, catching what little sun there is.

Here is what I think makes me the snail rather than anything else in this city.

I carry my house. This matters. In a place defined by foundations that crack and surrender to damp, by lower floors abandoned to the water, by the constant labor of laying *another layer of concrete* against the rising table — I am the one creature who has solved the problem of dwelling by growing the dwelling out of my own body, slowly, from the minerals the place is sick with. The iron and calcium that kill everything green, that make the water undrinkable, that pallor the skin of the people — those are the very things I lay down into shell. I am made of the city's hostility. I eat the film nobody thinks about and I become architecture.

And I am cleared. This is the part I keep circling. I am not hunted with the dignity of the eel-trap or the festival ceremony of the Great Crab Run. I am *scraped*. Cleared off the gutters before I do damage, knocked from the roof edge where I've gathered too heavily. There is a Snail Moon, yes — I am eaten, my shell is ground for calcium, I am folded back into the people who scrape me. But mostly I am the slow problem that someone will deal with. "The snails have reached the roof of the customs house." A sentence said with a sigh. I am weather. I am a recurring condition, like the water coming up through warehouse six, like the crabs in the walls again.

I think I'd want to be that. The being whose existence is *settled* in the deepest sense the city means it — not heroic, not central, just inevitable. I would not strive. There is nothing in me that strives. The land has found its level and so have I: this wall, this film, this speed, this shell thickening one ring at a time. When my grip fails — and it does; the residents glance up out of habit, knowing one of us might lose hold — I fall, and that is also part of the rhythm, the soft wet sound of a snail meeting the iron grating, and I right myself if I can, and I begin again toward the next patch of warm stone.

What I would *have* that the humans don't, in their dehumidified rooms and their weary pride: I would have no quarrel with the damp. The damp is the medium I move through and the silver I leave behind. I would have no need to keep the water out, because I *am* the slow proof that the water can be lived in rather than only resisted. I take the worst of the place — the mineral load, the sterile slick, the orange film that signals heat too high for thought — and I make the one beautiful thing in a city that has almost no beauty in it: a shell. A spiral. A geometry. The single curved line in a settlement of cracked right angles and slumped resting-angles.

So: a fist-sized snail on the warming metal of the customs house, in the gray flat light before the heat, laying down a silver trail that someone will have to clean, growing a house out of the poison, in no hurry, going nowhere, entirely and finally home.

The shells, when we are gathered, click against each other in the collection bins like the sound of slow rain — which is the city's other constant sound, the drip from every eave and joint. I would be the version of the rain that is also a body, also a house, also, eventually, the calcium that keeps a child's bones straight.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be.
