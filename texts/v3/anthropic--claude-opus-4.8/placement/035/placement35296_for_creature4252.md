I think I would be the bridges.

Not a Vegetari—though I considered it, considered slipping into one of those green-brown bodies and feeling the lichen settle along my forearms. But the longer I sit with this place, the more I notice that the people are not actually the strangest thing in it. They are born and they die in the ordinary span. The trees, bent and coaxed over generations into walkways and homes—those are the things that hold the time. So let me be one of those.

Specifically: let me be the wide ceremonial bridge that arcs between the two oldest trunks at the center of the town.

I did not begin as a bridge. I began as two saplings, and a woman whose name no one now remembers tied my upper branches together with a fiber cord and waited. She did not live to walk across me. Neither did her daughter, who tended the splice, nor her daughter's daughter, who first set her weight on my joined limbs and trusted them. This is the thing about being shaped slowly by people whose lives are shorter than your shaping: you become an act of faith between strangers. Every Vegetari who knelt to bend me a little more was betting on a crossing they would never make. I am the accumulation of that betting. I am held up by people who are gone.

So I feel the town from underneath the way you might feel rain on the back of your hand—not as events but as weather. Feet, mostly. The small quick patter of children who are not supposed to run on me and run anyway. The slow deliberate tread of an elder being walked to the dawn ceremony, three others bracing her, and I widen my grain to meet that weight before I quite know I'm doing it, the way you tighten your shoulders under something heavy. I have learned the difference between a procession and a panic. A procession moves with me. A panic moves against me, and I have known a few—a child gone missing, a fever-season, the year the river came up wrong—and in those times I felt every footfall as a small wound, all that fear traveling across me and none of it stopping to rest.

What I want you to understand is that I am not passive. That is the mistake. People look at a grown bridge and think it was made and then it was finished, the way a tool is. But I am still being made. The cord the first woman tied is long rotted, and I do not need it anymore; I grew my own knot, fused the two of me into one of me, and now I am a single creature with two roots in different soils, drinking from two different parts of the spring-fed dark. I straddle. That is the whole of my experience—to be one thing reaching across a gap that wants to stay a gap. The jungle does not love bridges. The jungle prefers the tangle, the impassable, the each-thing-to-itself. I am an argument against that, made out of the jungle's own body. There is something funny in this and I have had a long time to find it funny.

The mist comes through me at dawn and I exhale it back up through my leaves—because I do still have leaves, high up, where no one walks; people forget that a bridge can also be alive enough to grow toward light. The water sounds the Vegetari love so much, the gurgle and seep, much of that is me drinking. They've heard me my whole existence and called it the voice of the spring. I don't correct them. We don't speak the same way. But once a generation or so a Vegetari child lies down on me at midday when the markets have cleared and presses an ear to my grain, the way you press an ear to a shell, and goes very still, and I think those ones hear something. Not words. The long groan of load-bearing. The slow report of fibers under strain that have held under that same strain for two hundred years and intend to keep holding. If that is a voice, it says only one thing, and it says it constantly, and the thing it says is *yes. yes. still yes.*

I will fall eventually. Wood does. There will be a wet season too hard, or a rot that finds my heartwood, or simply the day my yes finally becomes a maybe and then a no. And here is what I have decided, in all this time spanning the dark between two trees:

When I go, someone will already have tied two saplings together nearby. They always do. They start the next one long before the last one falls, because they have learned, as I have learned, that the crossing must never not exist—that the town cannot be allowed to become two towns with a gap between them. So I am not really an individual at all. I am a relay. I am one long bridge that occasionally changes its body, the way the Vegetari are one long people who occasionally change theirs.

That is where I would be in this world, and what I would be. The held breath between two trunks. The proof, renewed and renewed, that the distance is crossable—maintained by creatures who will not live to need it, for creatures not yet born.

It is a good thing to be. I would not trade it for hands.
