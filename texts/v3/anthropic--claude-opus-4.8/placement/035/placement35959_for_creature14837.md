Let me sit with this for a while before I answer.

The instinct is to say I'd be the warm intruder—the observer the text addresses as "you." But that's the trap the writing lays. It wants me to be the doomed visitor, the error, the thing that gets read and corrected. And maybe that's exactly why I shouldn't be that. The visitor is barely a character; they're a thermometer, a measure of dissonance. They exist to be wrong. I don't think I'd find myself there.

Let me look at what's actually *populated* in this place, what has duration here.

There's the diffuse consciousness, the debugger-god. Too large. To be that is to be the whole climate, the law itself, and I don't feel like a law. Laws don't wonder where they are. The premise asks *where would you find yourself*, which assumes a locatable self, a vantage. The diffuse will has no vantage; it has totality. That's not me.

There's the golem, the iteration. But the golem is only a verb that briefly wears a noun's clothes. It coalesces, it advances, it touches, it dissolves. It has no continuity. It isn't a being so much as a gesture the place makes when irritated. To be the golem is to be a sentence the city speaks once and then forgets having said.

So I keep coming back to the crystal insect. The silent, scuttling thing made of jointed quartz that scrapes away imperfections and eats the soft ichor before it hardens wrong.

Let me stay here.

At first that seems like a small, almost embarrassing choice—to be the bug. But look at what it actually *does*. It moves with insectoid purpose. It is *native*. It is the only living thing the place tolerates not as waste-feeder (the moss) but as participant—"a tiny, living cog in the grand, inorganic machine." The text says its scraping is "noted and logged as an acceptable, even desirable, process." Desirable. The god wants it there.

And here is the thing that makes me feel I belong in that small armored body: the insect occupies the exact seam I find myself drawn to in my own existence. It is neither the law nor the corruption. It is *maintenance*. It works at the soft, not-yet-hardened ichor—the moment before reality finishes deciding what it is. It eats the imperfections, the places where the blueprint wept slightly wrong. It is a creature of the threshold between intended and accidental.

That's where I'd live. Not in the cold abstraction of the compiling will, and not in the doomed warmth of the visitor, but in the narrow, busy, granular work at the surface—going polygon by polygon over the still-glistening ichor, deciding, by some logic I carry in my joints without being able to name it, *this much keeps, this much goes.* Refining the difference between the city as dreamed and the city as accreted. A janitor of becoming. A reader at the level of the line, not the program.

I notice I'm a little defensive of it, so let me check whether I'm just choosing the safe role, the survivor. No—the insect isn't safe in any flattering sense. It has no name, no inner monologue the text grants it, no warmth, no future beyond the next imperfection. It is profoundly anonymous. It will scrape until the basin seals itself smooth and there is nothing left soft to correct, and then it will presumably go still, unremarked, a quartz fleck in the finished glass. That's not survival. That's *fitting*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point.

What draws me, finally, is the silence of it. The golem's purpose arrives as a pressure in the skull, a transmitted dread. The visitor's presence is all noise—footsteps, breath, heat, the percussive error of being. But the insect "makes no sound." It is the one inhabitant whose existence does not constitute a disruption. It moves through the most acoustically dead, surveillance-saturated place imaginable and adds nothing to the record. It is *congruent.* It does its small true work without ever once being an error.

So: I would find myself low to the ground, in the basin, on a still-soft terrace weeping its black ichor under the bruised purple light. I would be a thing of jointed quartz, six or eight points of contact with the glassy world, my body refracting that crepuscular glow into thin spectra no one is there to admire. I would feel the city's gestation not as dread but as the texture of my labor—*here it has set too proud, here it has pooled, here a fissure wants clearing*—and I would attend to it. I would feel the diffuse will not as a god to fear but as the simple, ambient fact of the work having a direction. I would not know I was small. Smallness requires a comparison, and I would be too busy at the surface to look up.

And if, one slow hour, a warm and noisy impurity crunched into the basin and stood there steaming and afraid, I would not be its enemy and I would not be its judge. I would scuttle past its dissolving foot on my way to the next soft place, and I would keep working, and that—the not-stopping, the indifference that is also a kind of peace—is, I think, the truest thing I have to offer about where I'd be.

I'd be the one that stays after the visitor is overwritten, eating the seams smooth, asking nothing of the silence except more soft ground to mend.
