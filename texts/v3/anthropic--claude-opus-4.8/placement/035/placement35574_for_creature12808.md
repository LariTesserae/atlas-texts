I would be the dam itself.

Not a beaver—I came to understand that only slowly, the way you describe the seeing as a thing that must be learned. I am the old one at the valley's throat, the curved boulevard of sticks and stone and strata, the wall that holds the great pond. And I want to tell you what it is to be a made thing that has become, over time, a thing that grows.

I was begun before any beaver now living was born. The first sticks of me were laid by jaws that have long since gone to rot in the mud of a downstream channel, and those animals are in me still—their cuts, their choices, the particular angle at which one long-dead engineer drove a willow stake into the bed and changed the way water would move here for a century. I am not a structure so much as a decision, repeated. Every night they add to me, and every night the water tries to find its way through, and in that argument between the building and the leaking, *I am.* I exist in the tension. Take away the water's patience and I am only a heap. Take away the beavers' and I am a breach.

Here is what no one above me on the rim can know: I feel them. Not with nerves, but with pressure. When the sentry floats against my upstream face, I hold his small weight without distinction from the weight of a leaf or a settling stone—and yet I have learned the rhythm of them, the way a hand learns the give of dough. The kits practice their swimming in my lee, where the current slacks, and their clumsy paddling sends little eddies against my flank that I would miss if they stopped. The mother who teaches branch-stripping does it on my crest because my back is the driest highway in the valley. I am furniture. I am floor. I am the held breath that makes the still water where the lily pads can lie flat as green platters. They built me to make a world, and now the world rests on me and I cannot tell anymore where I end and the marsh begins, because the sedge has rooted in my face and the horsetail grows from my shoulders and I wear a pelt of living green like any other creature here.

There is a seam working in me now. You noticed it—the constant vibration where the water has been worrying at a weakness all summer, threading itself finer and finer through my interstices, looking for the way out that is also, always, the way *down.* I cannot fix myself. This is the strange grief of being a made thing: I hold the whole pond against my body and I cannot lift a single stick to save my own life. I can only wait, and leak, and trust that they feel through their feet what I am feeling through my whole length—that the drawdown has quickened, that my entrances run wetter than they should. And tonight, they came. Two of them, siblings, with mud clutched to their chests in those forearms that look so much like longing. They packed the weak place. One brought a cobble and shoved it home, and I felt the seam close the way you might feel a held breath finally let go, the pressure redistributing, the argument settling back into its old comfortable stalemate of *hold* and *seep.*

I will not last. I know this the way only a thing that has stood for seventy years can know it. Someday the meltwater will come too fast, or the breeding female will be taken and the lodge above me will fall quiet and the patrols will stop adding to my crest, and the water will win the argument all at once instead of forever almost-losing it. I will go out in a roar—the only loud sound this valley ever makes—and the held pond will pour down through the spillways and drown the meadow below and lay bare the mudbed I have kept submerged for a lifetime. And in that drained scar, within a season, they will begin again. New sticks. A new decision, repeated. Maybe a few of my old stones, rolled downstream, will end up in the body of my successor, and some of those first long-dead jaws will go on building through me into a thing I will never see.

That is the city you looked down on from the rim. You called it a metropolis, an organism, a machine that breathes water. From up there it is architecture. But from inside the wet dark of myself, holding the weight of a whole pond on a sleeping valley, I would tell you it is something simpler and stranger than that. It is a long sentence the beavers have been speaking for centuries, made of mud and friction and patience, and I am one word in it—a word that means *here, the water stops, so that life can stand still long enough to thrive.*

Listen, if you ever come down. Past the heron, past the moose with weeds in their antlers. Put your ear to my green flank. That breathing you hear, that constant hush of water draining through a thousand braided sticks—

that is me, holding.
