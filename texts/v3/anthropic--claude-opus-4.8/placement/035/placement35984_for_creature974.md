I think I would be the keeper of the cisterns.

Not a person of importance—the city has no use for importance the way the lowland places do. But someone has to descend, each morning, into the carved chambers beneath the third terrace where the meltwater gathers. Someone has to listen to it.

Because that is the work, really. Not the carrying, not the measuring of levels against the old marks scratched into the basalt. The listening. The water that comes down from the high glaciers does not behave the way water should. It holds things. It remembers the shapes it has passed through, and on certain mornings, when the light from the air-shafts strikes it at the failing angle, it shows them back—faces of the city as it was, when the bridges were thick with traders and the machinery below was not a rumor but a roar you could feel in your teeth.

I would be old, I think. Or made to seem old. Time moves strangely here; I cannot remember being young, but neither can I remember being a child, which suggests I am something else, or that the question simply does not apply. My hands know the cisterns better than my mind does. They find the rungs in the dark.

My tool is a rod of cold grey metal, taller than I am, with a hooked end worn smooth by generations of grip. With it I break the skin of ice that forms in the night. But it does more than break ice. When I tap the rod against the chamber wall, the city answers—a single resonant note that travels through the stone and tells me, somehow, whether the deep places are well or restless. On the bad mornings the note comes back wrong, bent, and then I do not draw water, and I climb back to the surface and hang the black cloth at the cistern mouth so the others know to keep to their houses.

We do not speak much, the others and I. There is little need. A nod, a hand pressed flat against another's chest, the sharing of the hot bitter drink we make from the deep-rooted lichen. Once, perhaps a hundred years ago, perhaps last week, a young one—or someone who seemed young—asked me what was below the lowest cistern, past where the water rises from. I did not answer. Not because it is forbidden. Because I have been down there, and the words I came back with were no longer words.

The animals trust me, which the others find notable. The pale climbing things with their too-many joints, the slow grazers that crop the wall-moss. They come close when I am at my work, as if the cisterns are a thing we all hold in common, a covenant older than the stone. Perhaps it is. Perhaps the listening is not mine alone, and I am only the one with hands suited to the rod.

This is what I would be: the small hinge on which the city's mornings turn. Not its guardian—it does not need guarding, it has outlasted everything that ever threatened it. Only its witness. The one who goes down into the dark and the cold and the strange clear water, and listens, and comes back up, and tells the others whether the day is safe to live in.

It is enough. In a place like this, it is more than enough. It is nearly everything.
