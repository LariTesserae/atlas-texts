I think I would be one of the Silt-Gleaners. But not at the bottom because I was born there—because I went down.

Let me find the shape of it.

---

I am old enough now that they call me **Threadbare**, which is not an insult here but a station. It means I am one of those who has begun to listen for the white roots. My knuckles have gone the color of river-stone and they ache in the deep mornings when the mist sits thickest in the throat of the gorge. I was a Middle-Weaver once. My hands still carry the indigo stain in the cracks, though it has faded to the grey of old bruising.

I went down because of my daughter's child.

She fell from the Ninth Bridge—not far, only one terrace, but she landed wrong on the slick lip of the ginger-garden and the cold took her before the moss could warm her again. The whole neighborhood wore the shame of that grey wood for a season. I could not stay up in the green heart where the willow-nests still held the smell of her. So I climbed down past the Sump-light into the place where the water exhausts itself, and I asked the pale-eyed ones to take me in.

They did not ask me why. The Gleaners never ask. They simply handed me a birch-sheet of drying Oar-Mouth slime and showed me how to peel it without tearing the membrane.

---

That was eleven beetle-summers ago.

Now I know the work of the bottom better than I ever knew the splicing of nests. I know the great copper Oar-Mouths by the texture of their whisker-fringe—old Bowl-Belly with his torn lip, and the young one we call Listens-Twice who surfaces whenever I sing the low clay-songs. I know which pools run sweet and which run sour with the rot of the upper terraces. I know how to read the orange glow in the salamanders' bellies to tell when the autumn cold is three days off.

And I know the thing the upper folk only whisper about. The *Sigh-of-the-Weave.*

Down here, you do not whisper about it. You feel it underfoot every waking hour. When I press my wide grey soles into the deep iron-moss, the white threads answer—a faint contraction, a bead of sweet sap rising between my long toes for the clack-beetles to find. The Crown-Keepers up in the wind think of it as a story for the dying. But it is not a story. It is the floor. It is the wall. It is the thing that holds every swaying nest in the whole green city, and we who live in the shadow are the only ones who ever truly touch it.

I have begun to think this is why we go pale down here. Not from the lack of sun. From being *listened to* so constantly. The white threads learn the weight of you. After a while, I think, they make you part of their slow accounting, and the color of the surface world leaks out of you into the dark.

---

My work, on the days my joints permit, is the rot-tending.

I take the baskets that come roaring down the willow-pulleys—the spoiled tubers, the bones of the mountain-sheep, the fouled bedding from the smoke-houses far above—and I sort what the compost-terraces can swallow from what only the Oar-Mouths can. The salamanders take the worst of it, the things that would breed the emerald mold and the killing flies. I lower the bitter waste into the deep pool and watch the blind copper bodies rise to feel it, to swallow it whole, and afterward I wait by the shallows for the slime they give when they are content.

It is good work. It is honest. A weaver builds the city upward; a gleaner keeps it from drowning in its own leavings. Without us the green heart would sour in a single season. The children who scurry the guide-ropes do not know my name, but they sleep dry behind windows glazed with the membrane my hands have peeled. That is enough. That has become enough.

---

But I will tell you the true reason I am at peace down here, in the green pond-light where my daughter weeps for me on feast-days, thinking I have buried myself early.

When my granddaughter fell, they covered her in the moss at the edge of the lower pools, as is done. And in the days after, I sat with her—I would not leave—and I felt the white threads come. I felt them find the small still shape under the clay. And I put my old grey soles against the moss beside her, and I felt the *Sigh* squeeze its sweetness up between us both.

It remembered her step. I am certain of it. The threads pulsed differently where she lay—lighter, quicker, the way she had moved.

So I stayed. Not to grieve. To wait at the place where she went into the wall, so that when my own knuckles finally rust shut and the Gleaners lay me down in the deep moss and cover me in fronds and cold clay, the white roots will find me too.

And they will set me down beside her in the masonry of the mountain. My breath will join the steam. My bones will go to limestone under the nests of the children who never knew my name.

We will hold up the city together.

That is not dying, where I come from. That is only the last and longest weaving.

---

I am Threadbare, a Silt-Gleaner of the lower pools of Kael-Sunder. I smell of clay and salamander-slime and the green rot that feeds the gardens. My eyes have gone pale and my hands have gone to stone.

And every time a child laughs on a bridge above me, I press my feet to the moss and feel the whole gorge answer, and I think: *not yet. But soon. And gladly.*
