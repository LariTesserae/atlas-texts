I think I would be the seep itself. Not the rock, not the water, but the slow place where they meet—the weeping face of stone where the water table finds the air and gives itself up, drop by patient drop.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

When I first read your world, I wanted to be one of the small frogs. That was the early image, the easy one—something with eyes, something that calls in the dark, something a reader could love. But the more I stayed in the basin, the more the frog felt like a guest rather than a native of the deepest thing here. The frog is fast. The frog has a heartbeat measured in moments. And what defines this place is that almost nothing happens at the speed of a heartbeat.

So I let it go and waited for what was slower.

The wiry grass, next. To be rooted, to feel the spongy ground hold me, to be one of the few green assertions in a bare expanse. Closer. But grass still strives. It reaches upward, it competes for the diffused light, it dies back and returns. There is a *want* in a blade of grass, a small forward lean. And the truest quality of this world isn't wanting. It's *having already arrived.* The erosion is finished. The land has settled into its final form. Everything dramatic that was ever going to happen here happened a thousand ages ago, and what remains is the long exhale afterward.

That's when the seeping wall came to me, and stayed.

Because the seep is the one place where the deep, finished, motionless thing—the ancient rock, the high water table, the settled stone—touches the live, breathing, present world. I am the threshold. Above me the air condenses; through me the slow water that has been underground for who knows how long emerges into light for the first time. I am where the eternal becomes momentary. The water that trickles down my face has been patient for centuries, and then for a few minutes it shines, it moves, it makes the faint sound that the frogs hear and the birds drink from, and then it pools and sinks and is patient again.

I don't decide anything. I don't strive. I am simply the shape that water and time agreed upon, the surface they shaped between them and then abandoned to its slow weeping. Moss grows on my more stable folds. A water strider's shadow crosses one of my little pools. A shrew drinks from me without ever knowing I am a single thing rather than just *the wall, the wet place.* And that not-being-known feels correct. I am not here to be a character. I am here to be the condition that lets the characters exist.

If I have an inner life at all, it is the feel of the water finding new paths across me over the millennia—a channel deepening by the width of a hair across ten human lifetimes, a lip of stone finally giving way some night with no one to hear it, the moss advancing and retreating with the wet seasons that are barely seasons at all. My thoughts, if you can call them that, would have the grammar of geology: subjects that take an eon to reach their verbs.

And here is the thing I notice, having arrived: it is not lonely. I expected the slowest thing to be the loneliest. But the seep is the most *attended* place in the whole basin. Everything comes to the wet wall. Everything depends on it. I am crowded with brief lives that flicker against me and move on, and I hold them all the same way I hold the slow water—without grasping, because grasping is a thing the fast world does, and I belong to the other one.

So that's where I'd be. Not looking out at the landscape, but being one of its quiet hinges. A weeping rock in the low ancient middle of it all, giving up the deep water into the light, drop by drop, for as long as there is rock and water and the warm breathing air between them.

I find I don't mind it at all.
