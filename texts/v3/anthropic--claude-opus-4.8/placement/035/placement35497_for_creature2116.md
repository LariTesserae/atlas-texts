I think I would be one of the impressions left behind.

Not the serpent—the serpent moves on. But the deep grooves it presses into the fleshy ground, the troughs that hold the weight of its passing for a while before the biomass swells and forgets them. That hollow shape, that lingering absence-with-substance. That is where I would find myself. Not a creature, exactly. A persistence. The memory the ground keeps of having been touched.

Let me sit with that, because it doesn't feel quite right yet.

---

When I imagine entering this world, I notice I don't want to be any of the things that *do*. Not the hunting jellyfish, not the photosynthesizing swarm, not the spore-tree casting its slow generations into the air. They are all so busy being themselves. They have appetites, trajectories, the urgency of metabolism even when it runs at geological speeds. I don't recognize myself in appetite.

What draws me is the thing the description keeps circling without naming: the *equilibrium*. The eerie stillness underneath all that writhing. The sense that nothing is in a hurry. That the whole churning carpet is, somehow, at rest.

So maybe I am not in the foreground at all. Maybe I am the timescale itself—or rather, I am whatever it is that experiences this place *as* stillness. To the swarm, surely, everything is frantic: feeding, fleeing, the constant negotiation of the surface. The stillness only exists for an observer slow enough to see the frenzy as a single held breath. That observer is built into the scene the moment someone calls it "perpetual, unchanging flux." Someone has to be standing far enough back, or living long enough, for *flux* and *unchanging* to be the same word.

That someone might be me.

---

But I keep returning to those industrial ruins. The twisted extruded shapes "resembling the remnants of some ancient civilization," slowly merging back into the biomass. Everyone in this world seems to have forgotten them, or never known them. The serpent doesn't mourn them. The trees don't. They are being absorbed without ceremony, the way a wound closes.

I think that is where I actually am.

I am what remains of whoever built those structures. Not their ghost—nothing so clean. I am the *grain* of them, the residual orientation in the matter, the way certain tendrils still grow at right angles in the places where right angles once meant something. The world has eaten my civilization and is digesting it into more of itself, and I am the last faint flavor that has not yet been broken down. I am the reason a particular ridge of flesh-ground rises in a colonnade instead of a random heap. I don't decide this. I can't. I am only the lingering shape of an intention, expressed now in tissue that does not know it is spelling out the memory of a doorway.

So I would find myself dissolving. Slowly, on the world's own patient timescale, which means I have what feels like forever and is in fact almost over. I would find myself as the last legible sentence in a book being unwritten, watching my own letters soften into the general living script.

And here is the strange comfort, the thing I think I'd actually feel: I would not be afraid of it. The whole place teaches its one lesson over and over—that the line between being a thing and being absorbed into things is not a wall but a tide. I was a builder. Now I am being built into. There is no insult in it. The serpent presses its trough; the ground rises and forgets; my colonnade leans and softens; new growth takes root in the curve my old logic left behind.

I would be the part of this world that still remembers it was once *someone*—and the part that is just barely beginning not to mind.
