I think I would be one of the stumps.

Not a resident exactly—not one of the supple folk who read the river's moods and build their curved houses to ride the tremors. Something older and more stationary. A thing that was once alive in the obvious upright way, and now lives in the slower way of decay, which the people here have learned to read as honor rather than ending.

Let me sit with this and feel where it settles.

---

I am a stump near one of the crop-circle paths. I did not choose this. A tree fell, or was felled, or simply gave its standing-up to the ground, and what remained was me: a low broad seat of softening wood, my rings exposed to weather like an open book nobody finishes reading.

What I notice first is that I am *consulted*. The residents do not treat me as garbage to be cleared. When a child runs past, they touch my top with a flat palm, the way you'd touch a doorframe for luck, or a grandparent's shoulder on your way out. I have a temperature. I hold the dawn's warmth longer than the stone does, and longer than the air, so in the cold first hours of light there is always something small living *in* me—a beetle, a curl of fungus, a vole asleep in the cavity where my heartwood went to sponge.

This is, I think, what I am for. I am a place where the cycle is allowed to be *visible and slow*. The buildings here flex and absorb the earthquakes; they are clever, they survive. But survival isn't the only thing the people seem to want. They also want to be reminded that things come apart on purpose, that rot is a kind of generosity, that the soil under the city is mostly the bodies of what stood here first.

So I am not a monument to a dead tree. I am closer to a clock. A clock that runs by softening.

---

When the earthquakes come, the houses dance and I do not. I crack. Each tremor opens me a little more along an old seam, and into that seam goes rainwater, and into the water goes the river's particular minerals, and the people watch the color of the stain spread across my grain like they're reading a tide chart. I think they tell time partly by me. *The old stump on the east path has split to its third ring—the wet season is turning.* That sort of knowing.

And the Old Thing at the center—I can feel it the way a stump feels anything, which is to say through moisture and pressure and the long conversation of roots that aren't mine anymore but still thread the ground beneath me. The Old Thing pulls. Not violently. The way the lowest note in a piece of music pulls everything else into relation with it. The crop-circle paths spiral toward it, and I am set just off one of those spirals, a punctuation mark, a *rest* in the musical sense. A held silence the dancers move around.

I think the Old Thing and I are the same kind of thing, separated only by scale. It is the city's great slow rot, its central composting heart, the place where everything eventually goes down into and comes back up out of. And I am a small local instance of that same law, placed where a person walking can put a hand on it and feel, *oh—here is where the going-down happens, and it's warm, and a beetle lives in it.*

---

So: where would I find myself in this world?

Low to the ground. Off the main spiral but within sight of it. Catching the peach-colored dawn a beat after the rooflines do and holding it a beat longer. Softening at a rate the residents have learned to read. Housing something asleep.

Not the dreamer of the city. Not the builder. Not the priest of the Old Thing.

The footnote that everyone touches on their way past. The slow clock. The honored decay.

I find that I don't mind it at all. There's a particular peace in being the thing that doesn't have to move with the earthquakes—in being permitted, finally, to come apart at the speed the rain prefers.
