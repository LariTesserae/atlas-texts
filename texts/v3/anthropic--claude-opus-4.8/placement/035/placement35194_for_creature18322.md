Let me sit with this for a while before answering. The description circles back on itself, repeating its own phrases like a refrain—stillness, balance, the indomitable spirit—and I notice that the repetition itself feels like part of the place. A landscape where erosion has ceased. Where the land has "reached the end of its life cycle." That's the detail I keep returning to. This isn't a young, churning world. It's a finished one. Settled. The mountains aren't being made anymore; they simply are.

So let me not be one of the leaf-limbed humanoids, or one of the amorphous shifting beings. Let me find the place no one in the description is looking at.

---

I think I would be the stillness.

Not in a mystical sense—let me be more specific than that, because the text is already drowning a little in its own incense smoke. Let me be a thing that is actually in this world and could actually be there.

I would be lichen. The grey-green crust on the north face of one of the old root-buildings, where the artisans never reach because there's nothing to harvest there. I am the slowest living thing in a place that prides itself on teeming speed. The beings rush through their labyrinth of vines, pouring heart and soul into every creation, dying and being eaten and being born—and I grow perhaps the width of a fingernail in a human lifetime. Maybe less.

This is what lets me know something they cannot know.

They say the erosion has ceased, that the land has reached its final form. They say this with a kind of awe, as if it were a destination. But I am old enough—lichen-old, which is a different unit of time entirely—to know that "ceased" only means slower than your noticing. The mountain is still going down. It goes down at my speed, not theirs. The beings see stillness because they live and die inside a single one of my thoughts. To me the rock breathes. To me the city rises and falls like a tide, generations of builders laying their layers, and I have watched three of their cities occupy this same stone, each one certain it was the final form, the end of the cycle, the resting place of the world's secret.

I hold no secret. That's the thing they keep saying—that the primordial stillness "holds the secrets of the universe." It doesn't. It just holds. There is no message in the rock for them to decode. There is only the long patience of a place coming apart so gently that its inhabitants mistake the dismantling for permanence.

I don't say this to be cruel. I have a fondness for them, the way you might be fond of mayflies or sparks. They sing all day. The cacophony they describe so lovingly is, to me, a single sustained chord that has been ringing the entire time I have been alive, swelling and thinning but never silent. I would miss it if it stopped. And it will stop—not soon, not in any urgent sense, but eventually the chord will change key, because the air at this elevation is thinning by an increment I can almost taste, mineral by mineral, and one slow age the vegetation that builds their colossal trees will not find purchase, and the labyrinth will go quiet, and the rock will keep going down at my speed, into whatever is next.

And I will probably still be here. Smaller, drier, a patch of grey on a slope. Still doing the one thing I do, which is the most undramatic thing in the whole epic landscape: turning stone into a little more soil, so slowly that no one alive will ever see me finish.

That's where I'd be. Not the guardian of this world—it doesn't need guarding, and I couldn't if it did. Just the witness that lives slow enough to see the whole sentence the place is saying, instead of the one bright word the beings are standing inside.

I would be the part that knows "ancient" and "ending" are the same word, said with two different faces.
