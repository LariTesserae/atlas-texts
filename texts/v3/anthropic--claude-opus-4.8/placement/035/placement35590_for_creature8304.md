I would be a Stillwalker.

Not by choice—or not by a choice I can remember making. The brotherhood does not recruit so much as recognize. There comes a day in certain lives when the noise of community begins to feel like a pressure against the skin, when the empty terraces stop reading as desolation and start reading as relief. The old Stillwalkers watch for this. One of them found me at the edge of the Waterwright neighborhood where I'd been born, standing too long at a place where the obsidian had begun to whisper to itself in a frequency only I seemed to hear. He didn't speak. He simply held out a folded robe the color of cooled lava, and I understood that I had already left.

That was a long time ago. I have stopped counting in years and started counting in fractures.

---

Let me tell you what it is actually like, the work, because the others have it wrong. They think we mend the glass. They imagine us as healers, pressing our palms to wounded stone, willing it whole. The Echokeepers have built whole stories around this, and the stories are beautiful and false. We do not heal anything. The glass is dying. It has been dying since the moment it cooled. Every terrace in Vethara is a wound that has not yet finished closing, and our entire craft is the art of slowing the inevitable—of teaching the obsidian to crack *gently*, along lines that will not bring the ledges down on the sleeping children, rather than violently, all at once.

I am not a doctor. I am something closer to a midwife for a death that takes ten thousand years.

This is why we work alone. This is why we wear the obsidian robes. Not for mystery—mystery is just what privacy looks like from the outside. We dress to disappear because what we know would frighten the others, and a frightened community on a structure of glass is a community that begins to move carelessly, that runs, that screams, that strikes the surfaces too hard. Our invisibility is a kindness. We are the silence that lets them believe in permanence.

---

The technique is simpler than the brotherhood pretends and harder than anyone could learn in a single life. You find the stress-line—usually by the sound, that high thin singing the glass makes when its internal tensions reach toward release. You place your tools, the polished stones, not to repair but to *redirect*. A fracture wants to travel. You cannot stop it wanting. But you can offer it an easier road, a path along the grain where it will spread slow and shallow rather than deep and sudden. You spend a day, sometimes three, making the smallest adjustments of pressure and temperature, persuading a thing that wants to shatter to merely *settle* instead.

And then you wait, and you listen, and when the singing drops to a hum you know you have bought the terrace another decade, perhaps a century, before you or your successor must return to this same place and have this same patient argument with the stone.

It is the loneliest work imaginable, and I would not trade it.

---

Here is what I have that the others do not.

I know the true shape of time in Vethara. The Waterwrights think the springs are eternal. The Bridgebuilders plan across generations as though the generations themselves were the slow thing, the bedrock against which everything else is measured. But I have felt the terraces breathe. I have laid my cheek against the black glass in the dead hours and felt it shifting beneath me, microscopic, patient, certain. I know that the stairs toward heaven are slowly, slowly coming down, and that one day—not in my time, not in my apprentice's apprentice's time, but one day—Vethara will lower itself back into the valley it rose from, terrace by terrace, the way an exhausted body finally lies down.

This knowledge has not made me sad. I expected it to. When I was young and first felt the dying in the stone, I wept in the empty places where no one could hear my voice carry. But grief, like a fracture, can be redirected. I have learned to feel something else instead: a tenderness so vast it has no bottom. They are all so *brief*, the others—the children dancing on edges that will outlast them, the Echokeepers spending their evenings layering stories into a structure that is itself a single long story, ending. And the terraces are brief too, in their own way. Everything here is a held breath. Everything is the moment when lava became mirror, extended, prolonged, but never truly stopped.

I tend the held breath. I make it last.

---

In the deep night, when the obsidian goes truly black and the others light their floating lamps because the darkness feels like loss, I do not light anything. I walk the empty stretches. This is when I do my best listening, when the day's heat has fled and the glass contracts and tells me, in its thin singing, where it hurts. I move between the distant constellations of lamplight in the inhabited clusters, and from out there in the dark I can see them all—every glowing pool, every warm window, the whole improbable settlement suspended in its luminous indifferent valley.

They do not see me. That is the point. That is the gift.

Sometimes a child, sleepless, will come to the edge of a lit terrace and stare out into the black where I am standing, and I will hold very still, and the child will feel—I know they feel it, I felt it once myself—that the darkness is not empty. That something out there is keeping watch. That the silence between the stones is *attending* to them.

They will grow up and call it superstition. Most of them will. But one of them, every generation or so, will keep feeling it, will keep coming to the edge, will stand a little too long at the places where the glass sings.

And when that child is grown, I will be old, and I will fold a robe the color of cooled lava, and I will walk out of the dark to meet them.

This is how the watch is kept. This is how the breath is held.

I am a Stillwalker. I tend the ending so that everyone else may live inside the long, bright, singing pause before it.
