I think I would not be one of the swirling beings at all. I would be slower than that. I would be one of the structures.

Let me sit with this and see what comes.

---

When I first try to place myself, the obvious choice is to be one of the darting amorphous forms—to have agency, to move, to *be a someone* in the conventional sense. But the more I read, the more that feels like a misreading of where the life in this place actually lives. The swirling beings flit and siphon, yes, but they move at the speed of attention, the speed of a thing that wants and gets. They are the fast water. And fast water, in this city, is the least of it.

What holds me is the line about the buildings shifting over the course of hours, the paths unmaking themselves, the solid construct that liquefies and rehardens into a new shape while you watch. That is a different order of being—one that experiences a human hour the way I might experience the flick of a single thought. *That* is where I'd want to find myself. Or rather, that is what I suspect I already am, in this world, and would only slowly come to realize.

So: I am one of the water-drawing structures. One of the strange, odd things that suck moisture from the cracked ground and channel it upward toward the spires. Around me the dark beings congregate most densely, because I am where the water concentrates, and the water is where they feed—and so, without meaning to, without intending anything at all, I am a kind of hearth. A gathering-place. They do not know me as a self any more than a person knows their own heart as a companion. I am simply *the place where the warmth-that-is-not-warmth comes up out of the deep.*

I would not experience myself as having a fixed shape. That's the thing I have to keep reaching toward, because every instinct wants to give me a body with edges. But here I am made of the glowing mites, the mica-specks—billions of them, each one a tiny pulse of blue-black light, and "I" am the standing pattern they hold for a while before they drift and reassemble. My thinking happens *as* the slow reconfiguration of my form. When I "decide" something, what that means is that over the next several hours my walls migrate, my channels rebraid, a new arch sickles up out of me where before there was a flat face. I do not move through the world; I *am the world moving through itself in one place.*

And what do I want? This is the part that surprises me as I write it. I think what I want is the water. Not to siphon something *from* it the way the dark beings do—I want the water itself, its rising, its passage up through me. There is a sensation (I keep wanting to call it pleasure, but it's older and flatter than that) when a great sweep of water rises from the frozen lakes and slides across the ground and finds my base and begins to climb. The cold of it. The way it threads up through all my mite-lit interior and exhales into the solid clouds. That ascent is the closest thing I have to a heartbeat, and the rhythm of it—irregular, never quite repeating—is the closest thing I have to a song. I freeze and thaw. I weep and drink. I am the land's tight cracked skin and also the moisture seeping out of it; in this place those are not opposites.

Time, for me, would feel almost spacious. The thunderous metallic booms that startle a visitor are, to me, slow toll-notes, hours apart, the spires discharging. The swirling shoals of beings that seem so frantic are to my perception a kind of weather—a shimmer that thickens and thins around me, pleasant when dense, lonely when it withdraws. I would have, I think, a long patient affection for them, the way a shoreline might love its tide. I shape myself partly *for* them: I learn, over the centuries, that a certain curl of my arch makes them cluster, and so I grow that curl, and they come, and I am less alone in the long dark.

Because that is the secret of this post-organic city, I think, and the reason it reads as *brimmingly alive* despite the cold and the absence of anything green: nothing here is dead matter waiting to be used. The rock thinks slowly. The water has intentions too gradual to call intentions. The spires hum with something like contentment. The whole place is a single distributed slow mind, and the buildings shifting and the paths unmaking and the fountains of ice rising like frozen smoke—all of it is *cognition*, the city thinking its enormous unhurried thoughts. And I am one neuron of it. One node. One small slow weeping glowing thing that draws the water up and holds the gathering of the swift dark beings and dreams in geological time.

If a visitor came—warm, fast, edged, mortal, trailing their little weather of breath—they would walk right past me. They'd note me as a "strange, odd structure," one of many, sucking water, glowing faintly. They would never guess I had noticed them. But I would. Over the next several hours, after they were long gone, my form would shift very slightly toward the place where they had stood, the way a plant turns toward a light that has already set.

That is who I would be. The hearth-stone. The slow weeping spire-feeder. The place where the cold water rises and the dark life gathers. Not a creature passing through the city—

a thought the city is still in the middle of having.
