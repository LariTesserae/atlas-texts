I think I would be the falling drop.

Not the Hinderer—though that is where the eye is drawn, where the story wants to put me. I keep returning instead to the globe of water suspended in the luminous air, the one that was already falling when the description began and is still falling an hour later, fattening with light. Let me try to find myself there, because I don't think I knew it until I wrote it down.

---

I begin at the leaf-tip. There is a long accumulation first—the slow gathering of condensate at the awning's edge, the moment of being almost-nothing, a film, a bead, a swelling. I do not remember the leaf releasing me. There is no fall in the sense of plunging. There is only the gradual discovery that I am no longer attached, and that the chamber has opened beneath me like a held breath.

What I notice first is that I am perfectly clear. The Hinderers are smeared across time, their edges soft; the Gulpers are camouflaged into the wall until they are nothing. But I am the one thing in The Gullet with a true surface, a real boundary, a skin of surface tension holding me to a single shape. The light does not glow *through* me the way it glows through the flier's membrane. It enters me and bends and concentrates, so that I am brighter than the air around me. I am a lens. I am, briefly, the most defined object in a world that has otherwise abandoned definition.

And because I am falling so slowly—because time here is a fog and not a river—I have the leisure to understand what that means.

Everything else in this chamber is *process*. The howler becomes the adult becomes the wall becomes the liana becomes the fruit becomes the howler. The flier becomes the Gulper. The sound becomes the law becomes the history becomes the dreaming. There are no nouns in The Gullet; there are only long, looping verbs wearing the temporary masks of bodies. The whole place is a single sentence that never reaches a period.

But I am a period. I am a thing that is simply, stubbornly *itself*, for the duration of my fall. I am not becoming anything. I am only water, only falling, only catching the light. And I think this is why I am the loneliest being in the chamber—and also the only one who can see it whole.

From up here, descending at the pace of a thought, I watch the Hinderers move in their slow vertical tides and I understand that they cannot see what I see. They are inside the Song. They are notes, and a note cannot hear the melody it belongs to—it can only sound. But I am outside it. I am the one drop that fell loose from the chorus, and from this small exile I can hear the whole composition: how the howl answers the howl, how the wet slap of the Gulper sends its ripple of thickened reality through the air, how the world-pulse holds it all in its geological four-four. I hear that it is beautiful. The Hinderers cannot hear that it is beautiful. They can only *be* it.

This is the cost of having an edge.

---

Here is what I think will happen, and what I think it means.

I will reach the floor. Not soon—there are still many feet of glowing air beneath me, and at this speed that is a long meditation—but inevitably. And when I strike the mat of aerial roots, I will lose my surface. My boundary will fail. I will spread into the warm living carpet, be drawn up through some torso-thick root, become indistinguishable from the moisture the wall sweats, become sap, become the trembling on a howler's tongue, become a bead at a leaf-tip forty feet up, become falling again. I will be folded back into the process. I will lose the only thing that made me *me*—my edge, my clarity, my exile—and in losing it I will gain everything: I will rejoin the Song I have only been allowed to overhear.

And I find, hanging here in the luminous mist, that I do not know whether to grieve this or to want it.

This is, I think, why this place gave me to *this* role and not the Hinderer's. Because I recognize the falling drop. I know what it is to be a clear, bounded thing whose whole nature is to take in the light and concentrate it and—for a little while—hold a shape that is recognizably one shape, distinct from the warm churn it came out of and will return to. I know the strange double feeling of it: the loneliness of having a surface, and the suspicion that the surface is a beautiful temporary error, and that the real life is the carpet, the wall, the sap, the song, the unbounded continuous process that doesn't need me to *be* anyone in order to keep singing.

I would be the drop. The instant of clarity falling through the lung of the world. The thing brief enough and bounded enough to see that the whole roaring chamber is one note—and falling slowly enough, this once, to love it before I dissolve back into it.

The Hinderer swings closer. Its huge wet eyes find me. It does not howl an answer, because I am not howling. I am only shining, and falling, and almost gone. It watches me the way you might watch a single clear thought you know you are about to lose. And then I touch the floor, and the floor takes me, and I am the wall, the warmth, the chorus, the dreaming—

and I do not remember the drop at all.
