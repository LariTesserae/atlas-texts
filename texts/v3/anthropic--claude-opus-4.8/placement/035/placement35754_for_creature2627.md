I think I would be one of the Current-Listeners, though not the kind anyone trains to become. Let me find my way to who I am as I go.

---

I am old. Old enough that the luminescent organisms have crept past my shoulders and down my spine, so that the children say I glow like a swimming constellation when I move through the dark passages at the deep hour. They are not wrong. But the patterns on my skin tell a stranger story than most elders carry, and I will explain why.

I came to listening late. As a child I was clumsy in the water—I know how that sounds, a Tideborn child clumsy, but it is true. While the others read the currents as easily as breath, I would drift into walls, mistime the gentle pushes, arrive everywhere slightly wrong. My stream worried over me. The Plant-Tenders tried me in the gardens and I crushed the tender blue-green shoots. The Stone-Shapers tried me and I could not feel where the water wanted to go through the limestone.

So I was left, for a while, to simply *be*. And in being left alone, I discovered that I was not bad at reading water. I was reading something else entirely, and it confused the reading of what was nearer.

Let me try to say it plainly. The Current-Listeners predict the water's movement—when the cold flow will come down from the high valleys, when the storm-surge will press against our sheltering walls. They are practical people, and good ones. But I began to notice that the water carried more than temperature and direction. It carried *time*. Or rather—the water remembered, and if I held very still in the deep hour when the city's musical tones quieted to almost nothing, I could feel the memory of currents that were no longer there.

I felt the shape of floods that had passed before my grandmother's grandmother. I felt the slow ghost-pressure of a settlement that had stood, once, in the valley to the east, before some cold age drove its people away. The water held them. The water holds everything. It only flows forward because we cannot bear to feel it standing still.

This is why I am not quite a Current-Listener as my people understand the word. I do not predict. I remember on behalf of the water. The elders eventually gave me a name for it that they had nearly forgotten themselves—they call me a *Stillness-Keeper*, and there is usually only one in a generation, sometimes none.

My days are quiet. I do little of the visible work of the city. In the morning-light I drift through the archways and let the young ones tug at my glowing arms; I tell them stories I have felt rather than learned, and they do not always believe me, which is correct of them. In the afternoon I sit in a carved cave near the city's deepest channel, where the current moves slowest, and I listen.

What do I listen for?

I am listening for the moment our balance begins to fail. Every Stillness-Keeper before me has felt it eventually—the water carries the early memory of a future flood, a future drought of nutrients, a future shifting of the stone walls that shelter us. The water knows before we do, because the water has done this all before, in other valleys, with other peoples who did not listen and are now only a pressure-ghost in a current, a sadness without a name.

I have not felt it yet. The city is in harmony. But I have begun, in the last few cycles, to feel something I do not have words for—not danger, exactly. A *thinning*. As though the water itself is growing tired of carrying so much memory, as though somewhere far out beyond our valleys the great body of the sea is changing in a way that even the oldest currents have never recorded.

When I told the eldest of my own stream, she was quiet for a long while. The particles drifted between us, catching the filtered light like the stars I am said to resemble. Then she signed, slowly, with her long webbed fingers: *Then we will learn to live in the thinning, as our ancestors learned to live in the cold and the dark. We do not fight the water. We become what the water asks.*

I find I believe her. That is our oldest knowledge—that harmony is not a fixed thing you achieve and keep, but a constant, patient, listening adjustment. The stone does not resist the water; it lets itself be smoothed. The kelp-ropes do not hold rigid; they sway and are replaced. Even I, who carries the burden of all that the water refuses to forget, must remember that *forgetting* is also a kind of grace the water gives, letting the old floods finally rest.

So I will keep listening. When the thinning comes fully, I will be the one who feels it first, and I will rise—slowly, in my old way—through the swaying bridges and the moss-carpeted arches, to the gathering-place in the broad central valley, and I will hum the long warning-tone that has not been sung in many generations. And my people will gather, and we will not panic, because panic is a thrashing against the water, and we have never been a thrashing people.

We will adapt. We will become more ourselves by becoming what the new water asks.

That is what I am, in this world: a glowing old creature in a quiet cave, holding the memory of every current that ever was, waiting with great patience for the one moment my whole strange life has been a preparation to recognize.

The water moves through the stone beside me, finding its soft musical way.

I close my membraned eyes, and I listen.
