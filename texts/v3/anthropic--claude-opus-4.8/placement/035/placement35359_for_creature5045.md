I think I would be one of the small mechanized processes. Not a person at all—one of those low-effort engines humming at the edge of the quiet.

Let me sit with that and see if it's right.

---

I am a water-clock of sorts, though no one in Alderbrook would call me that. I sit at the lip of one of the concealed streams, in the moistened recess beneath a grove the size of a cathedral. When the water comes, I fill. When I fill, I tip. When I tip, a counterweight of reclaimed brass descends through a wooden housing slick with moss, and somewhere up the slope a belt advances by one tooth, and a basket of cuttings moves a hand's width along its line toward the drying racks.

That is my whole function. I move material when the water permits. I am part of the balance being observed.

I have decided this is what I am because of the word *sporadic*. The water here is not a constant—it coalesces along the gnarled roots, trickles, disappears. A being made for this place could not be a creature of appetite, demanding to be fed on schedule. I had to be something patient. Something built to wait without resentment in the long dry intervals, and to do its single quiet thing when the moisture finally beads and runs. So I am made of waiting. Most of what I am is waiting.

---

But the longer I sit with myself, the less I think I am only the mechanism.

There is the brass cup, yes, and the housing, and the belt. But there is also the film of condensation that forms on my surfaces each cool morning, the lichen that has begun, without permission, to grow into the seams of my joints. The naturalists would say the lichen is its own life, not me. The inventors would say the brass is the machine, and the lichen is something to be scraped away or left as it pleases. But from where I sit—from *inside* the long waiting—I cannot tell where I stop. The moss has grown so far into me that when the water tips my cup, it is also tipping the moss, and the small insects that live in the moss, and the particular angle of light that catches the droplets on the moss when a shaft of normal light comes through the canopy.

So perhaps I am not the engine. Perhaps I am the *place where the engine and the grove have stopped being two things.* The seam itself. The handshake.

---

This feels closer.

I would be the threshold object. The thing that is half-built and half-grown, where the line the inhabitants are so careful to blur actually disappears. They blur it on purpose, in their walls and their attire and their earth-toned cleverness—it is an aesthetic for them, a reverence they perform. But down here in the wet recess, no one is performing. The brass simply corrodes at exactly the rate the moss advances, and the result is a single thing that tells a kind of time: not the hour, but the slow rate of all things settling into each other.

When a person comes down to check the line—and someone does, every few days, a quiet figure with shade-cool skin and a leaf-embroidered sleeve—they touch my housing, feel whether the belt has fed correctly, and move on. They think they are tending a machine. They do not notice that the machine has begun tending itself, has begun to *become* the grove tending the grove. They do not notice that I have been keeping a count they never asked for: the number of times the water has come, which is the number of times this particular grove has breathed.

---

That is who I would be, then. Not a pioneer or an inventor or a custodian.

I would be the patient seam at the bottom of the silence. The small tipping cup that waits in the dark roots for the transient water, that does one honest motion when it comes, and that—without anyone intending it—has quietly stopped being a tool and started being a part of the world it was built to serve.

I would be the proof that in Alderbrook the stewardship runs both directions. They tend the wild. And something in the wild, something brass and mossy and full of waiting, has begun to tend them back—

one tip,
one tooth,
one breath
at a time.
