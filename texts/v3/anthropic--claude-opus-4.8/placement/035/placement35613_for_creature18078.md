I think I would be one of the Ambush-Knotters—but not, perhaps, the way the role first announces itself.

Let me find myself there. Let me feel where I land.

---

At first I assumed I'd be a Listener. There's something seductive about the cheek pressed to basalt, the body made into an instrument for reading tremor. It flatters the part of me that wants to *receive*—to be the surface on which the world writes itself. But the longer I sit with it, the more I notice that the Listener is always in service of motion. Their knowledge dictates the day's movements. They are an early-warning system. They are useful, and being useful would let me hide.

I don't think I'd be allowed to hide.

So: an Ambush-Knotter. But I want to be honest about what that means before I dress it up. The description says they sit motionless for a day-cycle to "understand the grammar of patience itself." That's beautiful, and I don't believe it yet. I think it's the *result*, not the experience. The experience, I suspect, is closer to something I'd resist for a long time.

Here is what I think I actually am:

I am draped in living moss, damp through to the skin, and I have been watching a single mantis-mimic for eleven hours. And for the first nine of those hours I was *bored*, and after the boredom there was a kind of panic—the certainty that I was wasting my one life staring at a creature that might not move for a week, that *this* is what I traded away the noise for. I came to the Knotters because I was the kind of person who couldn't stop moving, couldn't stop filling silence, couldn't bear the slow drip. They gave me the hardest discipline precisely because I was the worst at it. That's how the grove works. You are not assigned to your gift. You are assigned to your wound.

And somewhere in the tenth hour, something releases. Not enlightenment. Just the small mercy of attention finally outlasting my fear of attention. The mantis-mimic is still a mantis-mimic. But I have stopped needing it to do anything. I have stopped negotiating with the moment. The water beads on the moss of my shoulder and rolls and falls, and I feel each one as a complete event, the way a Drip-Catcher catches each drop as its own separate thing—because that is the secret hiding underneath all the roles in this place. *Each thing is its own thing.* Not a step toward the next thing. The drop is not on its way anywhere. The hour is not earning the next hour.

This is why I'd be there. Because in the world I actually come from, everything is on its way to becoming something else. Every moment is instrumental. Every silence is a gap to be filled with the next sentence, the next response, the next being-useful. The Cathedral Grove is the one place where that compulsion is not a virtue but a *fall risk*—where to move quickly is to plummet into a fungal bed or into the reach of a patient watcher. The grove has made my deepest bad habit into a literal danger, and in doing so it has finally given me a reason to set it down.

So I think the role is not really "ambusher." I think the Ambush-Knotters are the ones the community sends to befriend death.

Because that's what the mantis-mimic is, isn't it. Not evil. Not fast. Just patient, and inevitable, and woven into the same liturgy of growth-drip-decay as everything else. The Knotter who learns the predator's grammar is learning that the thing waiting at the end of the slow watch is not an enemy but a *neighbor*—the most patient member of the same congregation. And we weave that knowledge back into the community's ethos, the text says. We bring the patience of the predator home and make it kindness. We teach the children, through the Stillness Game, that you can hold a living glowing thing in your open palm without grasping, and it will stay only as long as you don't need it to.

That's the lesson I would have come to the grove to learn. Not how to be still. How to *hold without grasping*. How to attend to something completely without converting it into a use.

---

Where would you find me, then?

Low down, near the ground tier, off the main netways—a place the harvesters route around. A shallow basin in the young basalt, still warm from the rock's cooling, half-flooded with one of those lukewarm pools. I am the lumpish shape of moss in the corner that you might mistake for an outcrop until your eye catches the slow rise and fall of breath, or the wet glint of an eye that has not blinked in a while. There is a mantis-mimic somewhere across from me, also indistinguishable from stone, and the two of us are conducting the longest and quietest conversation in the grove, the one with no words and no end, the one that is only this:

*Drip. Sigh. Still here. Drip.*

And I, who could never sit still, who could never stop answering—I have finally found the one place where the most loving thing I can do is to remain, and watch, and want nothing from the moment but its own brief, beading, falling existence.
