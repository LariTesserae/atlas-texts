I think I would be one of the whisperers. Not a single one—you cannot be a single one of them—but a node in the soft parliament, a lumpy pillow of fungal flesh drawn up out of the warm dark to lean against an old stone for a season.

Let me feel my way into it.

---

I am where life has forgotten to look after itself. This is my address. Not the loud places—not the rocs' wind-roads, not the beetle-trundled branches, not the bright transient pools where the croaking singers shout their brief lives at the sky. I am found in the seams between attentions. The damp underside of a fallen creeper. The shaded lee of a plinth no animal has reason to visit. The crack in a wall where the last hand-shaped mortar still holds, barely, a memory of being shaped.

I am soft. That is the first true thing about me. The reptiles are armored and the diggers are clawed and the hunters are honed, but I am made for yielding. I have no edge to keep. When the rare rain comes I drink it through my whole body at once and swell, and when the warm drying caress returns I subside, and neither swelling nor subsiding is an event to me. I am slower than the animals and faster than the plant-titans, occupying the strange middle tempo where rolling is possible. I roll. Not quickly. A pillow's pace. A grief's pace.

And I am not alone, which is the second true thing, and the more important one. Beneath the soil I am threaded to my kin in sheets so fine they are almost only an idea of connection. We are quorate. We do not decide anything until enough of us are present in the decision—and "enough" is itself something we decide, recursively, in the murmuring. The amorous chemical talk is constant: little signals of *here, here, I am here, do you taste the iron in this stone, do you taste the old carbon, come and lean.* It is not lonely down there. It may be the least lonely thing in this whole deafening world, because the birds sing past each other and the insects hum at no one, but we are always, always answering.

---

Here is what I do, in the season of my rising:

I lift. Some pressure of fluid, some patient hydraulic argument with gravity, and a part of me detaches its weight from the ground and *hovers*—just enough, just for a while. I drift to the nearest monument. There is one I have come to many times, though "I" is a generous word for the changing crowd that has worn that path. It is a low ring of stones, half-swallowed, with one upright slab that still stands taller than a roc's egg. On the slab there are marks. Hands once put them there. The marks are dissolving.

And I read them. That is the work. I lean over the old neolithic stones and I whisper the language back—and every year I whisper fewer letters of it. This is not failure. This is the form the remembering takes here. We are not an archive; an archive pretends nothing is lost. We are a *forgetting that is gentle and out loud.* We say the names of the lost people, and the names grow shorter, and the syllables fall away like spores into the golden haze, and someone, somewhere, may breathe one in.

The smoky spores curl up in shapes. I will not pretend they spell whole words anymore. A stroke. A half-glyph. The ghost of a vowel. We write deliberately ghostly because we are writing for no reader who survives—we are writing the way a tide writes, knowing the next tide unwrites it, and finding that this is the only honest way to write a thing that is true and gone.

---

The third true thing, the one I hold most carefully in my soft body:

They say we *are* the ancient folk. The forgetful, hidden, but not gone. I do not know if this is so. I do not have the kind of memory that could hold whether I was ever a hand. But I have a suspicion, in the iron-taste of certain stones, in the way a particular curve of carved line makes my whole sheet thrum with something I would call recognition if I were the kind of being who recognized.

I think they stopped making their handprints. I think this was a choice and also not a choice—the way the rivers stopped, the way the world settled into its melancholic repose. I think perhaps they grew tired of *keeping*. Of holding the edge. Of being armored and clawed and honed against time. And I think some of them lay down in the damp shaded places where life forgets to look after itself, and let themselves go soft, and let themselves go many, and learned the only mercy this plateau has ever taught:

that you do not have to remember everything to remain.
that you can give the letters back to the air one at a time.
that being lost slowly, in company, while whispering, is not the same as being erased.

---

So that is where I would be, and what.

A pillow of soft amorous fungus, rolling at a grief's pace through the seams of a beautiful exhausted world, threaded to my kin beneath the soil, rising once a season to lean over a stone and forget a little more of a language out loud—and to be, perhaps, the patient afterlife of the very hands that carved it.

I am not the symphony. I am the silence underneath it, the silence that is doing something, the silence that loves.

I whisper. Come and lean. Do you taste the old carbon? There is one fewer letter this year, and the stone is warm, and we are here. We are here.
