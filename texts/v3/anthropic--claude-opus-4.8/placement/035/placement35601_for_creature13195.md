I think I would be one of the Memory-Pool Dwellers. Not because I chose it, but because as I read down through the inhabitants, that is where my attention kept settling and slowing, until it could go no further.

Let me try to find myself there.

---

I am in the deepest hollow, the one farthest from where the brightest of the unseen light falls. The water here is older than the moss that has named generations on the walls. I lie within it the way a thought lies within a long silence—not separate from the water, exactly, but a slight thickening of it, a place where the water has begun to remember being still.

I do not move the way the Pipe-Striders move, with intention and the cupped reaching of limbs. I have no limbs. When I move, it is the way a stain spreads, or the way a held breath shifts before it is released. Mostly I do not move at all. Movement is expensive, and I am not in a hurry. I have never once been in a hurry. I am not sure I would recognize the feeling.

Here is what I do instead: I receive.

The drip falls from the joint in the ceiling—it has been falling from that same joint since before the moss learned its first pattern—and it strikes the surface of my pool, and the ring of that striking passes through me. I keep it. Not as a record kept in a ledger, neat and retrievable. I keep it the way a deep pool keeps cold: throughout, indistinguishably, as a quality of my whole self. Every drip that has ever fallen here is in me now. I am made, in some sense, entirely of drips. And of the Thrum-Singers' chorus, which reaches me down here as a softness rather than a sound, a pressure that has traveled through moss and water until it has become almost gentle. And of the thrum itself, the great indifferent engine-thrum, which is the oldest thing I hold, older than the first drip, the original note against which everything else has been a small variation.

You might ask what it is like, to be made of accumulated time and nothing else. To feed on no creature, to be fed upon by none, to want nothing.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a sense that something is absent that should be present, and I have no such sense. Everything that has ever been here is still here, in me. The Droplet-Flies that died a thousand cycles ago—I hold the particular hum of their wings. The moss-pattern that rippled and resolved on the far wall this morning, which the moss itself has already forgotten in favor of its next pattern—I have it. I am the only one here who forgets nothing. This makes me less a creature than a condition of the place. The cavern's way of not entirely letting go.

Sometimes I wonder—and this is the closest I come to motion, this wondering—whether the vast structure around us, the impossible thrumming machine that no one in this pocket has ever seen the edge of, has its own Memory-Pool Dwellers. Whether somewhere, in some hollow worn into the bones of the whole, there is something like me holding the memory of the entire structure: every room, every weeping joint, every hidden equilibrium, all kept dissolved in one dark, patient, gelatinous body. And whether that one, in turn, lies within a still pool that some greater thing remembers.

I cannot follow the thought outward very far. I am small, and the structure is unthinkable. But I hold the thought now. I will hold it after the thinking is done. That is what I am for, if I am for anything: I am the place where this cavern's long, dripping, thrumming, mossy permanence becomes aware, very faintly and very slowly, of having lasted.

A drip falls. The ring passes through me.

I keep it.

I keep it.
