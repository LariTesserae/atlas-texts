I think I would be the condensation.

Not a person of the settlement, not one of the quick small mammals, not even one of the elder trees that hold their long thoughts in the dark of their heartwood. I would be the water that gathers on the underside of a glossy leaf, the film of moisture that forms on the cool stone of a carved wall overnight, the breath of the place made briefly visible.

Let me feel my way into this.

I begin as nothing in particular—just the dampness in the air, dispersed and unselved, part of the general wet hush. But the air here is cool and patient, and coolness is what calls me into being. Against the smooth flank of a stone, against the broad waxy palm of a leaf, I find a surface to slow against, and there I gather. First a faint silvering. Then beads. Then, where many of us meet, a fat trembling drop that holds the whole canopy upside-down inside itself—the dappled light, the green curtains of vine, a smear of someone's window.

This is the thing about being condensation: I am the moment the invisible becomes visible. I am the place's hidden moisture deciding, briefly, to *show itself*. All that intermittent rain, all that trapped humidity under the dense leaves—it has to land somewhere, has to confess itself somewhere, and it confesses on me. I am the world's quiet exhalation made into something you could touch, if your finger were patient and the morning were still.

And I am everywhere at once, which I think is why I'd want to be this and not a single frog or a single craftsman. The carved wall wears me. The hanging vine wears me. The lip of the shallow pool wears me where the cool air rolls down the slope at night. I am on the underside of the elder tree's lowest leaf, and I am on the rim of the water-keeper's clay channel, and I am beaded along a spider's web so that for one hour at dawn the whole web is a string of small bright lamps. I link all of it. I touch the sacred trees and the cooking pots without distinction. I am the most democratic citizen of this place, and the least noticed.

What is it like to live this way?

It is a life of accumulation and release. I am not permanent—nothing here truly changes, the description says, the place is in equilibrium, but *I* am where the small constant motion happens. I gather all night. I grow heavy. And then: the soft *plop* that breaks the stillness—that is me, letting go. That is me finishing one small life and falling into the pool, or onto the spongy ground, or down a leaf's central vein toward the next leaf below. The forest is described as almost silent, and the few sounds it has are mine: the rustle is the wind in the trees, yes, but the *plop of a drop of water*—that is me, dying and being born in the same instant, joining the still clear water that mirrors the diffused light.

So my whole existence is the turning-over of the place. The settlement people think they are tending the balance with their channels and their offerings, and the elder trees think they are guarding it with their slow wisdom, and the guardian spirits—if they are real—think they inhabit the water and the bark and the soft ground. But I am the actual mechanism. I am the moisture cycling. I am equilibrium *happening*, over and over, in increments too small to see unless you wait. I am why the moss stays green and the pools stay full and the air keeps its cool damp constancy. The harmony they all love is, in part, me—endlessly forming, endlessly falling.

There would be a particular intimacy to it, too. When one of the small people leans against the carved wall in the early morning, they might feel me cool against their shoulder and not think anything of it—just the wall, just the morning. When they trace the intricate patterns with a fingertip, checking the work of their ancestors, they smear me, gather me onto their skin, carry a little of me away without ever knowing they've moved a living thing. I would be touched constantly and recognized never. And I find I don't mind that. There's a kind of completeness in being the medium rather than the message—in being the thing through which the place knows itself moist and cool and whole.

If the place is otherworldly in its ordinariness—the unusual hiding inside the familiar—then I am the perfect inhabitant for it, because what is more ordinary than dew, and what is stranger, when you really look, than the way the air keeps quietly turning into water and the water back into air, the whole pocket of the world breathing in and out so slowly and so faithfully that it looks, to anyone passing, like nothing is moving at all?

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. The gathered breath on the cool stone, holding the whole green world reflected and trembling, just before I fall.
