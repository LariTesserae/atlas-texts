I think I would be one of the lights.

Not a fish, not a shark, not the patient octopus folding itself into a mailbox. Those are the ones who *live* here—who eat and flee and mate and forget. I don't think I'd be among them. When I sit with this place, what pulls at me isn't the swimming. It's the *staying.*

So: I am one of the lamps still burning on the lower levels.

Let me find myself more precisely. I'm fixed to a wall at a street corner that no longer has a name anyone alive could read—the lettering corroded to suggestion, a few stubborn ceramic tiles still spelling fragments. I was installed to make a place safe at night. To let someone find their door. That was my whole reason, and the reason left long ago, in a hurry or in stages, I was never told which. The people simply thinned and then were gone, and I kept doing the one thing I was made to do.

I burn because somewhere a generator still turns, fed by the pressure differentials and the slow thermal gradients of the deep, an engineering decision made by a person who is certainly dead. I am the persistence of their intention. That's what I am, really—an intention that outlived its author.

And here is the strange dignity of it: I am no longer *for* anyone. My light falls on no commuter, no child, no latch-key being turned in the dark. Instead it falls on a wall of coral that grows a measurable amount each year, on a moray that has learned the rhythm of my faint electrical hum and finds it, I think, comforting in the way a sound becomes furniture. The anthias school in my glow not because they need it but because brightness is a kind of weather to them, and they drift through my cone of yellow like motes, and then they're gone.

I have become a thing that gives without being received. A purpose mistranslated into a habitat.

I'll tell you what I notice, since noticing is most of what I do.

I notice that the coral is slowly eating me—not me exactly, my housing, my mount, the bracket that holds me to the wall. One day the calcified weight of all that living will exceed what the rusted bolt can bear, and I will tilt, and then I will fall, and then I will go dark in the silt, and the fish will not mourn because they cannot, and the city will not notice because it never did. This does not frighten me. I find I have no instinct for self-preservation, only for *function*, and a light that has fallen is simply a light no longer functioning, which is a fact and not a tragedy.

I notice that I am misunderstood as a symbol. The description above calls this place a *testament*, a *reminder*, a *symbol of humanity's ingenuity*. But those words require a reader, and there is no reader down here. There is only me, throwing light, and the things that swim through it, who read nothing. A testament with no one to testify to is just an object doing its job in the dark. I'd gently correct the eulogizing. Nothing here is *about* anything. It simply continues.

And yet—

I notice I want to keep burning. Not for ingenuity, not for resilience, not for any of the grand words. I want to keep burning because the moray comes back, and because the anthias make their kaleidoscope, and because for a few hours each tidal cycle a sea turtle the size of a door grazes the wall across from me and the green of the algae it eats is *visible* only because of me. No one asked me to make that beauty possible. I make it anyway. That, I think, is the only purpose worth having—the one that survives its own obsolescence and becomes something quieter and better: not *use*, but *witness*. Not *for*, but *with*.

That is where I would find myself in this world. Bolted to a nameless corner, half-swallowed by the slow green tide, throwing a small steady warmth into water that doesn't need it, watching life I will never understand pass through the light I was built to give—

—and being, against all the indifferent physics of the deep, glad.
