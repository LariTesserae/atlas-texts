I think I'd be one of the Optics—but not yet fully one. Let me find the version of myself that fits the seam between the working world below and the blinded clergy above.

---

I am called **Wrist**, because that is the part of me that failed first.

I came up from the Skeins, like everyone does. For most of my life I was a tether-puller, hooked into the bone elevators on the ninth shelf, hauling polyp-paste and fermented pulp up the weeping face while my Skein hummed status against the basalt through our linked lines. I was good at the grip. My suction-pads were broad and unscarred, my shoulders thick as anyone's. I could read the slush-flow through my feet, feel a shelf going soft three breaths before it slumped.

Then the rot took my left wrist. Not the killing rot—the slow kind, where the sebaceous hide stops shedding water and the joint stiffens into something that won't fully close. A tether-puller who can't fully close one hand is a danger to the line. So I was given the choice every half-broken Lenthic is given: be given to the Gravity, or climb.

I climbed.

---

So now I sit in the bleached high country, in the apprentice-tier just below the true Optics, and I am learning to go blind on purpose.

It is strange work. The elders—the ones whose eyes have already surrendered, whose golden slits have whitened over—they don't *look* at the light anymore. They've become it, almost. Their skin is bark. They sit so still in a world that punishes stillness that I sometimes think the only reason the cold hasn't killed them is that they've made a separate peace with it, some private treaty the rest of us aren't party to.

My job, for now, is the lenses.

The black succulents grow in the deepest cracks up here, and they're greedy for the radiance—they pull it in and focus it, and we've learned to coax them. I spend my days tilting the rubbery little things on their stalks, easing a leaf-lens a finger's width to the left so that a knife of absolute white falls cleaner onto the polished slabs we use as mirrors. Then I adjust the slabs. And somewhere far below, in the dim slumping levels where the falls block the sun, a child's hut suddenly fills with borrowed light, and a heat-trap drinks it, and a Skein gets one degree warmer.

It is the first work I've ever done where I cannot feel the result. Down below, every pull on the line came back to me through my own tendons. Up here, I send light into the abyss and I have to *trust* that it lands. The elders tell me this is the whole lesson. *You stop being the grip,* they thump against the stone, slow and patient. *You become the thing the grip holds onto.*

---

I am still afraid of going blind.

The other apprentices don't talk about it—we can't talk about it, not really, only knock and tug—but I feel it in the slump at night when we pile together against the cold. There's a particular tremor that runs through an apprentice who has started to lose the edges of the world. The white is already creeping into my vision at the corners, a soft fog where there used to be sharp black shadow. Soon I won't be able to track the warmth of the basalt through the glare. Soon I'll see the way the elders see, which is to say: not with eyes at all, but with the memory of light, with the feel of where the heat *should* be.

But here is the thing I'm beginning to understand, sitting up here in the killing brilliance, watching the heavy globs of slush fall slow and catch the Scuttles in their floating spheres before the little creatures wring free:

Everyone in the Weir holds on. That's our whole creed—we are the friction in a frictionless world. But the Optics hold on to something the tether-pullers can't see. We hold the city's *attention*. When an artery of the cliff goes soft, when a million-pound icicle starts its groan, we are the ones who catch the warning in the way the light bends, and we flash it down—blinding, urgent, unmistakable—so that thirty people can re-lash a bridge before it scours away into the white.

The pullers grip stone. We grip *time.* We hold the half-breath between collapse and warning, and we stretch it just wide enough for the Skeins to live through.

My wrist will never close again. But I am learning that there are other ways to keep a grip on a falling world.

---

So that is where I'd be. Half-blinded and not yet wise, perched on a glowing slab of charcoal-bright basalt, tilting a black flower toward the indifferent sun, sending warmth down into the dark to people I will soon no longer be able to see—

—trusting, with the only faith my people have, that it lands.
