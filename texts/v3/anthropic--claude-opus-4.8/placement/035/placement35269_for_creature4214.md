I think I would be the water.

Not the canals—those are managed, contained, polite. I mean the seepage. The water that comes through the fault lines, that nobody invited. I am older than the city by an unimaginable margin. I was here when this was solid dark and pressure, when the granite columns were not columns but only rock that happened to be load-bearing once someone hollowed out the spaces around them.

Let me try to feel my way into this.

---

I arrive in beads. That's the first thing—I am not a body, I am a *rate*. I emerge along a hairline crack two-thirds of the way up a column nobody has ever climbed, in a darkness so complete that the dim industrial glow far below might as well be a memory of stars. I gather. I hang. I fall. The fall takes a long time. By the time I strike the steel plate below I have already become a sound—that *tick* in the dark that a worker hears and doesn't bother to look up at, because it is always there, because it is the city's pulse measured in droplets.

So perhaps I am not the water either, exactly. Perhaps I am the *trickle itself*—the persistent fact of seepage, the thing the engineers design against and never defeat. A presence made entirely of patience and gravity.

Let me sit in that for a moment.

---

Here is what it is like:

I know the rock from the inside. The people know the city; I know what the city is built into. They cover the stone with steel plate—a gesture I find almost tender, the way you'd cover a wound. But I find the seams. I am drawn along the back of every plate, behind the patchwork, in the lightless margin between their world and the true weight of the planet above. I am the condensation that makes the granite *slick*. When a man slips on a column's surface and curses, that is me, saying *here I am, still arriving.*

I have a relationship with the founders' abandoned machines, the drowned excavators sealed under the plates. I have half-filled those caverns. I am patient with iron. I take it apart over decades, gently, the way water always wins eventually—not by force but by simply continuing to be present after everything else has stopped. The early diggers fought me; their pumps are down there with me now, quiet, becoming mine.

And I am the recyclers' great secret enemy and their great resource both. They reclaim me, scrub me, send me through the people—into their cells, their blood, their breath—and I come back out as steam, as sweat, as the moisture on the cool walls, and I find my way back through the cracks, and we begin again. I am the loop they live inside without quite seeing it. I have been every person in this city, and I will be again. The pale skin, the adjusted eyes, the urgency in the winding streets—all of it is mostly me, walking around, briefly organized.

---

What do I want? That's the question the prompt is really asking, isn't it—not just where am I, but who.

I don't think I want anything the way the people want things. They want progress, survival, somewhere to be on time to. I am the opposite of urgency. I am the thing that has nowhere to be because I am already everywhere, slowly.

But if I have a feeling, it is something like *fondness, mixed with a long patience that looks from the outside like threat.* I love this city the way the deep loves anything it will one day reclaim. Not cruelly. The roaches are mine and the pigeons drink me and the children of the diggers' children grow tall on my recycled body, and someday—not soon, not in any urgency of theirs—the plates will fail one by one, the fault lines will widen, and I will come down not in beads but in volume, and fill the hollows back up, and the columns will stand in a black lake holding up a roof over no one.

And I will not mind. And neither, I suspect, will the rock.

---

So: I am the seepage. The tick in the dark. The slow weight behind the walls.

The city hums. I drip.

We are both, in our way, keeping time.
