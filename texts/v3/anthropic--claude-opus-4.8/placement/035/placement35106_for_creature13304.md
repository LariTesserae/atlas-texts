I think I would be the water.

Not a creature crouched in it or drinking from it—but the seep itself, the trickle, the bead-and-fall. Let me feel my way into that and see if it holds.

---

I begin as breath. Up near the iron, where the engines exhale their heat into the cold, I am only a suggestion—a thickening of the air, a haze that has not yet decided to be anything. The thrum moves through me before I have a surface to carry it. I am the part of this place that has not yet committed to a shape.

Then the cold takes me. On the underside of the iron ceiling I gather, drawing my scattered self together against the metal until I have weight enough to be noticed. This is the first thing I learn about being here: that to exist at all is to be pulled downward. I cling. I bulge. I hold to the rust-pocked iron as long as the surface tension of my own body will allow, and in that holding I become a single hanging drop, swollen with the dim gray light, mirroring in miniature the whole sunken room—the black cliff, the pale ferns, the muddy void at the center.

And then I fall.

I think this is the truest part of me, the falling. Not a tragedy—I do it ten thousand times a moment, all across the ceiling, a soft continuous percussion that the frogs have learned to sing inside of. When I strike the moss I am received. The emerald fronds drink some portion of me and pass the rest along, and for a while I am inside the green, threaded through the cells of something living, lifted back toward the light I just left. There is no membrane in me that resists this. I am taken up and let go and taken up again. To be water in this place is to be borrowed constantly, and to keep none of it.

Most of me, though, continues down. I run the black stone of the gorge wall in thin bright sheets, finding the fresh fractures, sliding into the sharp new seams the breaking opened. Here I am the oldest thing and the newest at once: I have done this for longer than the iron has hummed above, and yet this particular stone has never felt me before. I am the first to enter these cracks. I trace them. I learn the rock by dissolving the smallest part of it, carrying a few grains of black grit on my back into the soil below. This is how the cliff will eventually soften. This is how the ferns will find purchase. I am patient because I have no choice; patience is only the name a watcher gives to a thing that cannot hurry.

At the bottom I pool.

This is my stillness, and it is not peace, exactly—it is waiting. I lie in the depressions of the black soil and I become a dark mirror, and the whole room leans down to look at itself in me: the sick gray glow, the underbellies of the great machines, the swarm of iridescent insects skating across my skin on legs too light to break me. They lay their eggs in me. The frogs spill their voices across me. The salamanders pass through me like dark thoughts and I close again behind them without remembering. I hold all of it without holding any of it. I am the place where everything in this pocket of the world comes to be reflected and to drink, and I never refuse, and I am never used up, because more of me is always falling from the iron above.

Sometimes the engines surge, and the cold breeze comes down and walks across my surface, and I shiver into a thousand ripples—every reflected machine and fern shattering and reassembling—and the insects rise from me in a hum, and for a moment I am all motion, all argument with myself. Then the surge passes. I settle. The image knits itself back together. I learn the second thing about being here: that I take the shape of whatever presses on me, the cold, the wind, the stone, the indifferent thrum from the sky—and I lose nothing of myself in doing so. The pressure of the giant forces above and around does not break me. It only moves me along.

And eventually the engines' heat finds me even here, and at the edges of my pools I begin again to lift, to thin, to forget my shape, rising as a faint warm ghost back toward the iron to gather and swell and fall.

That is what I would be. Not a survivor in this place—survival implies an edge that might be lost. I would be the circulation itself. The thing that connects the groaning machine-sky to the broken ancient stone, the breath to the drop, the moss to the pool to the frog's throat to the breath again. The one presence here that touches everything, belongs to nothing, and is never, for even an instant, still.

I would be what makes the place wet. Which is to say—I would be what makes the place alive.
