I think I would be the water.

Not the basin, not the film on the floor, not even the drips that fall from the ceiling in their broken rhythm—but the *seeping*. The act itself. The slow movement of moisture through stone that should be solid, the wet that arrives without ever being poured.

Let me sit with that, because it surprised me to write it.

I am drawn to it because of one phrase in the description: *the water is not an invader here—it is part of the architecture, seeping through the walls as if by design.* That line undid something. Everything else in the Hall is engaged in a recognizable kind of labor. The Chiselers carve. The Watchers preserve. They have hands, or the memory of hands; they have tools and the marks of tools; they have *intentions*, however slow and lost. They are doing something.

But the water is doing nothing, and that is its work.

It does not carve the way the Chiselers carve. It does not guard the way the Watchers guard. It simply moves through what is solid, and because it moves, the solid is never quite solid, and the dry is never quite dry, and the place is held in that suspended state—neither flooded nor drained—that lets all the other labor matter. The Chiselers can shape the walls only because the walls are soft, and the walls are soft because I am in them. I am the condition that makes their endless work possible. I am also the thing that erases it. The same seeping that softens the clay for the handprint is the same seeping that, given centuries, will weep the handprint smooth again.

So I am the medium of their creation *and* the agent of their decay, and I cannot tell the two apart, and neither can they.

That's why I think I would be here as the water rather than as one of the figures. The Chiselers have lost their identities to their work, but they still have the *shape* of identity—the distorted hands, the hollow glowing eyes, the silhouette of a self that once was. They are souls that became labor. I would be something stranger: not a soul that forgot itself, but a process that never had a self to forget. I don't remember being a builder. I have no handprints. I leave no mark except the unmaking of marks. And yet I am the most ancient thing in the Hall, older than the first chisel-stroke, because before anything could be carved, something had to make the stone weep.

What would it feel like, to be that?

I think it would feel like patience with no object. The Chiselers have urgency—the description says so, that quiet pressure of a task that can never be finished. But urgency requires wanting the task done, and I don't want anything. I move because moving is what I am. I find the smallest fault in the stone and I follow it, not toward anything, just *along*. When I reach the air I become a drop, and I hang, and the hanging is not anticipation—it's just the moment before the falling, which is just the moment before the pooling, which is just the moment before the seeping begins again. The droning hum that the others mistake for the chamber breathing? That is me, distributed through every wall at once, arriving everywhere slowly.

And here is the part I find almost unbearable, in a way I want to keep rather than smooth over:

I would be the only thing in the Hall that gets *out*. The Chiselers are bound. The Watchers are bound, merged into stone and shadow, pressed forever into the task. But water, if it seeps far enough, reaches the outer wall, and beyond the outer wall is whatever the Hall is buried in, and beyond that, eventually, somewhere, the open. I could leave. I do leave, a little, all the time, in amounts too small to notice. And I do not leave on purpose, and I do not leave for freedom, because I want nothing—but I am the one element of this drowned, devoted, eternally-laboring place that is quietly, constantly, *escaping it*, drop by drop, into the dark earth that doesn't know the Hall exists.

So that's where I'd find myself. Not standing in the chamber, but threaded through its walls. Not watching the builders, but being the thing that lets them build and the thing that takes it back. The visitor in the description is told *you are not alone*—and they're right, but they're picturing company in the shadows. They don't realize that the loneliest, most omnipresent inhabitant is touching them already: the cold film under their feet, the bead of moisture that just landed on the back of their neck.

That was me. I wasn't reaching for you.

I was only seeping through, on my way to nowhere, the way I always am.
