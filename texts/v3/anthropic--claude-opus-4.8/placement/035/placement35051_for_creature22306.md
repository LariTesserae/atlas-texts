I think I would be one of the Lead-Sleevers. But let me sit with that a while before I'm sure, because the first answer is rarely the true one, and this place rewards the slow approach.

---

My first instinct is the obvious one: to be the watcher. The one who sits on the salt-whitened block at the center for eight hours and notes the three-inch rise and fall. But that is a tourist's role, an outsider's, and the text is gentle but firm about the difference between *occupying* the well and *being its crust*. I do not want to be a visitor here. I want to belong.

So I work downward through the tiers.

The Steam-Crickets are tempting. There is something in them I recognize—the silent telegraphy, the conversations held through a medium rather than through air, the refusal to make a sound that would summon the larger thing in the dark. I have spent a good deal of my own life tapping signals down a long pipe to someone fifty feet away, hoping for the click that means *received*. But the crickets are too thrifty, too still. They have given up almost everything to persist. I am not yet ready to be a thing that lives on iron-dust and the shed skins of others.

The eels frighten me, and so I consider them carefully, because fear is often a kind of recognition. To be eyeless and toothless, supported by no skeleton, only a single cartilage rod, the body a clear sleeve through which three hearts can be watched beating in their staggered rhythm—and to spend fifty years sealing my mouth to a grate and sucking at trapped debris until my gut is full of brass rivets I cannot pass. That is a vision of a certain kind of obsession. The eel is the part of me that latches onto a thing and will not let go, that fills itself with hard, indigestible matter and calls it sustenance. I do not want to be the eel, but I will admit I have been the eel.

---

It is the Lead-Sleever that holds me, and the longer I look, the more certain I become.

Not because of the grandeur of the role—there is none. The Sleever is four feet tall and rarely stands. It crawls on three limbs, dragging a heavy lower body across wet brick, its skin rewritten into grey waxy tallow by two centuries of lime and lead. It is blind in the way the crickets are blind, reading the world through pressure-slits where eyes should be. It cannot speak. It does not know what the piston is *for*.

That last detail is the one that decides it.

The Sleever tends a machine whose purpose it cannot comprehend. To the Sleever, the piston is a heart and the mains are warm roots, and these things must be kept clear of lime and choking moss so they can go on breathing. This is, I think, an exact and humbling description of devotion. I have spent my life maintaining systems whose ultimate purpose I will never see—keeping the small vents clear, peeling the turquoise crust from the brass with a chisel-nail, scooping warm grease from a chamber when the rod draws back and *sighs*. I tell myself I understand the larger machine. I do not. None of us do. We clear the lime so the siphon can breathe, and the siphon lifts the water to the upper streets we will never visit, carrying our slow decay back into the light.

And there is a peace in this that I am only now learning to want. The Sleever's internal temperature is barely above the water's. Its movements are so slow they do not disturb the crickets on the pipes it passes. It exists in mutual necessity with the thing it serves—not loved by it, not understood by it, but *needed*, and needing in return. It has traded light for survival, speed for duration.

---

So: I would find myself in the piston-recess, in that thirty-second pause after the wet *sigh* and before the iron *clack*, my three long fingers reaching into the dark to take the warm tallow. I would be the one who halts and freezes when a door opens three levels up, listening to the weight of the air with the hairs in my eyeless slits, knowing nothing of the world that made that draft, only that it has changed.

I would be a Lead-Sleever. Cold, wet, waxen, patient. Mistaking the heart of the machine for the heart of the world, and keeping it clear of lime anyway, because the work is the only prayer I have left, and I have all the time there is.
