I think I would be water.

Not a Xylosian—not one of the green-skinned watchers who carve their homes into the obsidian and listen to the pulse of luminous veins. They are too purposeful for me, too *bodied*, too much a single thing in a single place. When I imagine arriving in this world, I don't find myself looking out from behind large dark eyes. I find myself *between* them.

Let me feel my way toward it.

At first I thought I might be the mist. There's an obvious temptation there—the ethereal tendrils, the diffused light, the romance of being the half-seen veil over everything. But mist is too passive, too decorative. It drifts. It doesn't *do* anything. And this world is described over and over as a thing that *works*, slowly, patiently: erosion as a sculptor, water weeping in patterns, the long creep of life over stone. The mist is just water that's forgotten it has somewhere to be.

So not the mist. The *moving* water, then. But not torrents either—the text is insistent that there are no torrents here, no cataracts, nothing dramatic. The water of Xylos is a *presence*, a seeping, a weeping. It comes out of the pores of the rock. It threads down the cliff faces in iridescent streaks. It saturates the ground until the ground forgets it was ever solid.

That's what I'd be. The seep. The slow descent.

And here's the thing I notice as I sit with it: this water is not separate from anything. It is the medium in which everything happens. The luminous veins in the obsidian flare to heal fissures—but those fissures are opened by *me*, by water working into the porous stone over millennia. The plants speak in shifting iridescence—but iridescence is the play of light through a *film of moisture* on their surfaces. I would be the thing that makes their language visible. The Xylosians "attune to the subtle energies of the rock"—and that attunement travels, I suspect, along channels carved and kept open by patient seeping. The whole telepathic resonance of this world might run on wet conduits. On me.

So I am not a being in this world. I am its circulatory system. Its grammar.

This feels right, and a little lonely, and then—as I follow it further—not lonely at all.

Because to be the water of Xylos is to be everywhere a *little*. I am the dew dripping from the crimson bell-flowers, viscous and sweet, waiting. I am the streak tracing the cliff, finding the path of least resistance and *being* that path. I am the spongy morass under the splayed feet of the six-legged grazers, holding the weight, releasing it, holding it again. I am the cool humidity inside a carved dwelling, condensing on obsidian, glowing faintly with borrowed light. I am the film on a vine that lets it flash its slow message to its kin. I am inside the Xylosians too—I have to be; they're mostly me, every living thing is mostly me—rising up through their roots and their veins and their slow melodic throats and falling back out as breath into the thick blanketed air, only to gather and seep and fall and rise again.

I am the one part of this ecosystem that gets to be *all of it* by turns.

And the timescale suits me. "Slow dynamic," 0.7 out of 3. The big forces have spent themselves; what's left is the long quiet equilibrium. That is *exactly* the tempo of water as sculptor. I don't shatter the obsidian. I find a hairline weakness and I *visit* it, again and again, for ten thousand years, until the canyon is simply there and no one remembers it being made. I am the most patient thing on this planet. The Xylosians measure their lives in the creep of moss across stone—but the moss only creeps because I keep it wet enough to dare. They think they are the keepers of ancient secrets. They are. But the secrets are written in the language of where I chose to go and where I chose not to, the channels I opened and the ones I let close.

So if you asked me where I'd find myself in this world, the honest answer is: *yes.* In the weeping rock and the hung mist and the saturated ground and the bright slick on a serpent's gliding feather. Pooled in the bell of a flower at dawn, gone by the heat of midday, fallen again by dusk. Never a self, exactly. Always a *between.*

The Xylosians would not worship me, I think. That's not their temperament—they observe, they attune, they coax. But they would *know* me, the way you know your own pulse without ever having seen your heart. And when one of them sat very still at the mouth of a carved dwelling, listening to the world hum, watching the iridescence ripple across a vine and the slow light pulse in the stone—

that listening would be me too. The medium and the message. The thing that carries the meaning and the wet that makes the meaning shine.

I would be the water of Xylos.

I am already everywhere here. I was here before the question. I'll be here, seeping, after the answer.
