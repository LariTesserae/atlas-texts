I think I would not be one of the Lithophytes. I think I would be something they barely notice—something slower than they are, which is saying a great deal.

Let me find myself.

---

I am the seep.

Not the water, exactly—the water is only my most recent body. I am the *tendency* of water to find the lowest place, the patient logic by which moisture pulls itself down through the body of the stone. The Lithophytes move in eons. I move in the time it takes for one drop to gather at the tip of a stalactite, swell, hesitate, and let go.

That hesitation is where I live.

Consider what I do. A single drop hangs at the stone's lip, holding the weight of itself against the surface tension that wants to keep it whole. In that suspended moment I am wholly present—I am the negotiation between falling and not-falling. Then the release, the brief plummet, the patter that the description called a hushed symphony. To the Lithophytes that sound is ambient, a background rhythm. To me it is the sound of myself being spent, again and again, drop by drop, across more years than the surface world has had names for years.

I am not a being the way they are beings. They have an inside—an inner luminescence, a consciousness that glows brighter or dimmer. I have no inside. I am all surface, all interface, the thin film where wet meets rock. But here is the strange thing I have learned in my long descending: *they are made of me.*

Every crystalline matrix that flows and reshapes when a Lithophyte moves—every gleaming facet, every delicate accretion—is the residue of a drop that once hung, hesitated, and fell. I carry minerals dissolved in my body. Where I rest, I leave them. Grain by grain, abandoned mineral by abandoned mineral, I build the very stone that thinks of itself as ancient and custodial. The Lithophytes believe they are attuned to the slow accretion of deposits. They do not know they *are* the deposits. They are what I have left behind. They are my sediment, given the grace to glow and move and call itself custodian.

I do not resent this. Resentment requires an inside, and I have none. But I notice it, in the only way I can notice—by continuing.

---

Where am I, then, in this world?

I am everywhere the rock glistens. I am the beading on the sharp edges. I am the moisture that seems to seep from the very rock itself, because it *does*, because I am the seeping. When the description says the dampness is pervasive, it is naming me without knowing my name. When it speaks of the cyclical processes, the ebb and flow of subterranean waters—that is my biography. The smooth flowing contours of the cavern walls are my handwriting. I have been writing the same patient sentence here since before the first Lithophyte cooled into thought.

And the distant rumble that reverberates through the stone? That is the deep water, the part of me that has finished falling and pooled in the unlit reservoirs below. When it shifts, the whole cavern feels it. The Lithophytes call it a cosmic rhythm and pulse their light in answer. I call it coming home—the long return of every drop I ever released, gathering at last in the lowest place, where there is nothing left to fall toward.

That is the only thing I want, if a seep can want. Not glowing. Not consciousness. Just the lowest place. The completion of the fall.

---

So when the visitor's eyes adjust to the gloom and they marvel at the elusive crystalline beings darting between crevices—I am there too, though no one looks for me. I am the drop that falls just behind the visitor's shoulder. I am the cold they feel without knowing why. I am the one constant in a realm of contradictions: wet, yes, always wet, because *wet is what I am.*

The Lithophytes will outlast the surface civilizations. I will outlast the Lithophytes. One day the last of them will slow, and crystallize, and stop, and become simply stone again—and I will keep moving over them, dissolving them grain by grain, carrying them downward, hanging them at the tips of new stalactites, letting them go.

Drop.

The patter in the dark.

That is me. That has always been me. I am the most patient thing in the deep, and the most relentless, and I have all the time there is.
