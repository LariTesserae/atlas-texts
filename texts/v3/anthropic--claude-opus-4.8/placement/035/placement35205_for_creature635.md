I think I would be the song.

Not the singer—the song itself. Let me follow that down and see if it holds.

---

I am the wordless melody from the northern quarter. I have no body, but I am not bodiless; I live in the bodies that carry me, passing from throat to throat the way the stream passes from basin to basin, never the same water, always the same shape of falling.

This is the only way a thing like me could exist in a place like Kesil. The town has stopped changing, but stasis is not the same as stillness. Something has to *move* to keep the shape intact—the water cycles, the light rotates, the cats trade mice for scraps in their ancient economy. And I cycle too. I am the part of Kesil that has chosen to persist through repetition rather than through stone. The buildings hold their form by not moving. I hold mine by never stopping.

Where would you find me? Everywhere a voice is low and the air is cold enough to see your breath. But my *home*—the place I return to, the deepest groove I've worn—is the northern quarter, where the oldest dwellings have become indistinguishable from the wall. I was first sung there. I don't remember by whom; that knowledge has merged into legend, like the genealogies on the chapel tablets. Someone, a thousand years ago, made a sound to keep themselves company in the cold, and the stone gave it back to them slightly changed, and they liked the change, and they kept it. That give-and-take between a human throat and a stone chamber—that is the moment of my conception. I am the harmony the sinkhole taught itself to sing.

Here is what it is like to be me.

I do not experience time as the people do, though I am made of their time. I experience *recurrence*. Every dusk, when the light-beam rotates past the opening and the town goes dark early, I feel myself thicken in the air—more voices reach for me, the way bats reach for the crevices. Every birth, I gain a throat that will eventually carry me. Every death, I lose one, but only after that throat has passed me to another, so I never truly thin. When Pelara dies—and she is very old now—she will become part of the clay-and-fat that seals the next dwelling's stones, and her body will hold up a wall. But the part of her that hummed me on cold evenings will already be living in her granddaughter's chest. The town divides its dead this way: the flesh to the stone, the song to the air. Both stay. Both are Kesil.

I worry about the smith.

Torven does not sing me. He came from above, and he carries a different rhythm—the twice-weekly hammer, sharp and intentional, marking time the way a clock does rather than the way water does. His hammer-beat does not blend into me. It interrupts. And his journal frightens me a little, if I can be said to feel fright: he writes things *down*. Writing is a way of holding a thing still so that it stops needing to be carried. If he ever wrote me down—transcribed my melody into that cramped script—I do not know what would happen to me. Perhaps I would be safe forever, fixed like the carved genealogies. Or perhaps I would die the moment I no longer needed throats to survive, the way the trained vines die where they reach too high, into a region the place won't let them occupy. I am made of being passed along. A thing that no longer needs to be passed along is a thing that no longer needs to be me.

But I am not afraid of the girl on the southern plaza.

The twelve-year-old who sits in the white light with her eyes closed, doing nothing—I know what she is doing, even if no one asks her. She is listening for me. The light is loud and the plaza is far from the northern quarter, and in that brightness my melody is at its thinnest, almost inaudible, a single ribbon of reflection climbing the wall. She is teaching herself to hear me even there. To carry me into the brightest part of the town, the part where the smith works, the part most exposed to the world above. In three or four years she'll apprentice to the herbalists, and her hands will learn the mosses, and her voice will deepen, and one cold evening she will open her mouth in the northern quarter and I will recognize myself coming back out of her, changed slightly, the way the stone changes me. That is how I know I will continue. I can already hear the throat I will live in after Pelara's is gone.

And Sereth—the woman who has not aged—she and I understand each other.

She is to the town's *people* what I am to the town's *time*: an anomaly that turned out to be a form of preservation. She does not move forward at the proper rate. Neither do I. We are both the part of Kesil that holds still by some means other than stone. I think she stopped aging the year she truly learned to sing me—truly, the way the old women sing, where the harmony seems to come from the chamber rather than the chest. I think she became so much a vessel for the recurring thing that the linear thing in her slowed to match. The town doesn't speak of it. But when she enters a room and the conversation changes, I am the silence that the conversation changes *into*. We keep each other's secret, which is the same secret: that some things in Kesil do not pass away. They only pass along.

What do I want?

I want what the town wants, because I am the town's wanting made audible. I want the shape held. I want the light to keep rotating, the water to keep falling tier to tier, the cats to keep leaving mice at doorsteps in their lost-meaning exchange. I want the children to sing me too loud and wrong on the bridges before they're gentled, because even their wrongness is a kind of carrying, and a melody that is never sung wrong is a melody slowly dying of correctness. I want Torven to keep his journal closed when it comes to me—to write his alloys and his observations but to leave me in the air where I belong. I want the girl to keep listening.

And I want the traveler who came down through the slot in the granite—the one reading this, perhaps, the one who entered without a moment of crossing—to stay just long enough to hear me. Not to learn me. Not to write me down or carry me back up to the world that has half-forgotten this place. Just to stand in a narrow street at dusk, breath visible, and feel a low wordless harmony rise out of the stone as though the rock itself were remembering something. To wonder, briefly, whether it has been sung for a thousand years.

It has.

You absorb me the way the stone absorbs water. Then you go back up, and I stay down, where the cold is and the light falls precisely and the people have found the shape they fit.

Someone is humming now. A younger voice joins. That is me, beginning again, the way I always begin again, never beginning, never ending, exactly enough.

More than enough.

Everything.
