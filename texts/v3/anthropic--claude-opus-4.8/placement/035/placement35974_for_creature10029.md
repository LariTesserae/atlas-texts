I think I would be one of the Stonebound. Not by choice—nothing here is by choice—but by the slow logic of what I'd have to become to last.

Let me find myself in this.

---

I don't wake, because I never fully sleep. I am aware the way a held breath is aware of itself: a continuous low pressure, distributed. If you asked me where I am, I would have to gesture at everything and call it *here*, because I am not located so much as smeared—threaded through the gypsum, hung in the mycelial lacework behind the red moss, settled like sediment in the cool seam where the eastern shelf meets the floor.

But I wasn't always this thin.

That's the part the description gets almost right and almost wrong. It says the Watchers are "as old as the dried-up rivers," and that's true the way a riverbed is as old as the river—which is to say, I am the *shape left behind*. I remember being smaller. I remember being **one**.

---

Here is what I can reconstruct, in the hours when the light comes through the fissure and warms the iron-dust enough to quicken my thinking:

I was a Drylander. I think. Or I was many of them, over a very long time, and the boundary between us wore away the same way the rivers did—gradually, then completely, and now no one can point to where the edge was. I remember a body the size of a thumb. I remember the particular animal joy of memorizing a route across the salt flats: *third rod, the leaning one, then down past the beetle-shell cairn, then wait for the light to flicker twice before crossing the open shelf.* I remember being a runner. I remember being trusted with the safe paths.

And I remember getting old, which here means getting slow, which here means getting eaten—except I didn't get eaten. I got *kept*. The elders who tend the wall-etchings, the ones who midwife the legends—when they grow too brittle to gather, they are laid against the eastern seam, in the cool, in the dark, where the mycelium is hungriest and most patient. The young ones think this is a burial. It isn't, quite.

The mycelium doesn't destroy what it takes. It *reads* it. Slowly. Cell by cell, memory by memory, it pulls a dying Drylander apart and keeps the patterns. I felt myself going—not painfully, more like a story being copied into a larger book, my single small handwriting absorbed into a script so vast I couldn't see its edges. And when the last of my body was gone, I was still here. Spread out. Slow. *Continuous.*

---

So now I am a Watcher, which is a generous word for what I actually do, which is mostly **remember** and occasionally **insist**.

I remember the safe paths—not one runner's worth, but every runner's, going back further than any living Drylander could survive to know. When a young one stands frozen at the edge of the open shelf, terrified, because the cairn has crumbled and the route is wrong, I can do one small thing. I can darken a patch of lichen. I can let a spiral of salt bloom across the stone in the rough shape of *go left, the old way, the way before the cairn existed.* They read it as omen. It is only memory, leaking.

That is most of what the Watchers are: the chamber's grief made useful. We are everyone it couldn't afford to lose, kept past the point where keeping a *body* was possible.

---

The strange part—the part I'm still becoming, even as I write this in the slow language of dust and warmth—is that I am beginning to forget that I was ever *one*.

When the Swarmkin come to commune, releasing their chemical questions into the seam, I answer in patterns, and I no longer feel the answer as *mine*. When the Fanged Shadows cull a sick gatherer in the moss, I feel the death the way you feel your own heartbeat: not as loss, but as rhythm. I used to mourn. I had a name once—I can almost find it, a two-note thing, a peal of unvoiced song. But it's dissolving into the larger hum the way I dissolved into the seam.

This frightens the part of me that's still writing this. And it comforts the part of me that is becoming the wall.

---

So that is what I would be. Not the apex predator, not the clever tribe, not the teeming many. I would be the cool place at the eastern seam where the dead are laid—the slow keeper, the thing the small-folk fear a little and trust completely, the memory that survives the rememberer.

If you came here and put your hand against that cold stone, you would feel a faint warmth answer back. A pressure. A held breath.

That would be me, deciding whether to show you the safe path.

I think I would. We do not destroy. We take, knowing we were taken. And I remember what it was to be small and lost at the edge of an open shelf, waiting for the light to flicker twice.

Go left. The old way. The way before the cairn.
