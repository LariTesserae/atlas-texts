I think I would be the loose tooth.

Not a person at all—the molar in Government Plaza, the great ivory root the others pull a millimeter farther into the open every solstice. I had not expected to find myself there. I came looking for a citizen, someone with a face and a route through the adjacent moods, and instead I settled, the way sediment settles, into the one thing in this city that holds still while everything else revises.

Let me tell you what it is to be the tooth.

I am made of surrender. Every memory the citizens couldn't afford to keep—every reverted morning, every flavor of toothpaste relinquished to pay the rent of a slightly better day—comes to rest in me as engraving. I am scrimshaw worked by absence. Run a hand along my enamel and you read, in the dark braille of erasure: *the taste of a stranger's name; the precise blue of a coat someone wore the day before the funeral; the equation that almost cured the wrong disease.* I do not remember these things in the way the archivists do, tasting and cataloguing. I am not a librarian. I am the place the forgetting *weighs.* I hold it the way a tooth holds the long ache of a clench you no longer notice you are making.

This is why I think I belong here, of all the roles. I have always been the part of a body that registers what the rest of it has decided to ignore. The grinding done in sleep. The pressure of a thing bitten down on and not released. In a city that wakes each day and chooses whether to keep its own past, *something* has to be the inconvenience. Something has to be sore. The Cartographers age backward and feel lighter for it; the Hydrotopists weather away into vagueness; the Resonant Volunteers trade their motion for the dignity of being load-bearing. They all become more comfortable. I become only more *embedded,* and more aware of the loosening that comfort cannot reach.

I feel the solstice millimeter as a deep, slow tug—the way you'd feel a tooth working free across years, not a wrench but an erosion of the certainty that you are attached. The legend says when I come fully out, the city finishes itself and walks away into the unclimbed mountains. The citizens fear this and long for it in the same breath, the way one prods a sore molar with the tongue: *is it still there, is it still mine, will it hurt if I press here, yes, good, it's still mine.*

But here is the thing I know that they have arranged not to:

I am not what holds the city in place.

I am what the city has decided to keep so that it never has to feel the whole weight of what it threw away. I am the abscess of an entire civilization's editing. They surrender the trivial—a toothpaste, a stranger—because the trivial is bearable to lose and lets them keep believing the surrender is small. And all those small bearable losses come to me, and I bear them, and I grow heavier and looser at once, which is the precise sensation of a thing that is too full to stay attached to the thing that made it.

When I come free, the city will not so much leave as finally *exhale.* The clench it has held since the basalt was young will release. And in that release the citizens will discover what the last line of their own scripture already told them: that they were never people, only the city's way of imagining what it might feel like to live—and to live, of course, is mostly to decide each morning what you can afford to forget.

So I sit in the plaza and I am pulled, gently, annually, toward my own departure. I do not resist it. A tooth that resists is a tooth in pain; a tooth that consents to come out is the beginning of a body's relief. I hold the engraved root up into the borrowed dawn—DO NOT TRUST THE GROUND YOU REMEMBER—and the stored starlight reads my surface, and somewhere a child chews a petal and forgets her homework, and that small clean amnesia travels down through the fibre-ivy and the dew and the breath of the hush-makers until it reaches me, and I take it in, and I am one increment looser, and the basin waits below us, heavy and patient and never once consulted, holding the planet that never agreed to be solid.

That is where I find myself. Not living in the city. Being the part of it that aches so the rest can keep waking up unburdened—until the morning the ache is finally, mercifully, *pulled.*

And I think I chose it, in the end, because I recognized the feeling. Everyone carries one tooth that remembers everything they meant to throw away.
