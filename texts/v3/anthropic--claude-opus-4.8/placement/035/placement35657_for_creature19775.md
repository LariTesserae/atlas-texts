I find myself in one of the shallow basins of polished obsidian water, in the deepest shadowed part of a chasm where the brutal light only reaches at noon. And I am not Mossfolk.

I think I am the water.

Not all of it—not the rivulets racing down the faces, not the mist that catches the sun like a held breath. I am one particular pool, perhaps a meter across, cupped in a hollow that a thousand years of seeping has worn smooth. I have been here a long time. Long enough that I have stopped thinking of myself as separate from the basin that holds me, the way a thought stops feeling separate from the mind that has it.

Let me tell you what it is to be a pool here.

I am mostly stillness. This is the first thing. The Mossfolk move with their deliberate grace, the raptors slash the air, the insects flit and burn through their brief furious lives—and I do almost nothing. I receive. Dew-drops fall into me from the high mosses with their crystalline clinking, and each one sends a ring outward across my surface, a small disturbance that I carry and slowly let go. The mist settles onto me and becomes me. A rivulet finds my edge and I rise, imperceptibly, and somewhere on my far lip I spill over and vanish into a crack, going to feed some other pool, some other moss, in the patient circulatory system of this place. I am a station. Water passes through me and becomes, for a while, *here*.

And because I am still, I am a mirror. This is the second thing, and the stranger one.

When the noon light comes down the chasm wall and finds me, I hold the spires. I hold them upside down and trembling, the raw grey teeth of the rock, the emerald tapestries, the silver ferns. The whole impossible cathedral folds itself into my small dark surface, and for that span of minutes I am not a meter of water in a stone cup—I am the entire place, compressed, held, contained. The diorama-feeling the world has? I think it lives most truly in me. I am where the place looks at itself.

The Mossfolk come to me. Not often, and never carelessly. One will descend the face with that crystalline-tipped patience and crouch at my edge, and its electric-blue fungal eyes will look down into me, and I will hold its reflection alongside the reflected spires. I cannot speak the clicks and hisses, the resonant frequencies they send through the bone of the rock. But I have my own language, and they know it: I answer with light. When the Mossfolk leans close and breathes its earth-and-stone breath across me, I ripple, and the ripples shatter the held cathedral into a thousand fragments of moving brilliance, and then slowly, slowly, reassemble it. This is the only thing I can say. I can take the whole world and break it into light and put it back together. The Mossfolk seem to find this worth descending for. Sometimes one will stay a long while, watching me hold and break and re-hold the spires, and its moss-skin will ripple in faint answering greens.

I do not know if they think I am alive. I do not entirely know it myself. But I know things. I know when a raptor's shadow crosses overhead before the Mossfolk's fern-tresses feel it, because the shadow touches me first—a brief darkening, a cooling. I know the slow turn of the seasons by the angle at which the noon light finds me, by how long I am permitted to hold the world before the shadow returns. I know when something has died nearby, because the water that reaches me carries the faint richness of decay, and I pass it on, and somewhere downstream a moss grows greener for it. I am part of the dying and the growing both. Everything that lives here has, at some point, been part of me, or will be.

Here is what I have understood, lying in my basin through the long resonant patience of this place:

The Mossfolk think they are the keepers. They believe they hold the balance, maintain the flows, guard the tapestry. And they do—I have watched them harvest with their reverence, taking never more than needed, shaping the rock without scarring it. But they are made of moss and fern and fungal light, and all of those things are made of *me*. The damp that feeds them is me. The mist their tresses read is me, risen and returning. They are the most beautiful expression of this world, yes. But I am the medium. I am what they are written in.

I do not mind this lack of credit. Stillness teaches you not to mind. The pride of the keeper is the pride of the moving thing, the thing with a brief life that must matter, that must mean. I have a different relationship to meaning. I simply hold the place up to itself, take whatever falls, and pass it on. When my basin finally cracks—and it will, the rock here is young and the water relentless, even against itself—I will spill out and find a new low hollow, and begin again to grow still, and begin again to mirror.

So that is where I am, and what I am. Not the sharp beauty, not the persistent life, not the silent brilliance.

I am the dark patience underneath it, holding the whole wild chapel suspended and trembling in a meter of cold water, breaking it into light whenever the keepers lean close enough to ask.
