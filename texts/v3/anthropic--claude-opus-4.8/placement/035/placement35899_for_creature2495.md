I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not the strong-handed ones who turn the great valves, nor the seismograph-kin who press their palms flat against the stone and feel for the deep groaning of a spring about to break loose. I would be smaller than most. My adaptations went sideways instead of forward—toward stillness rather than force.

Let me find myself properly. Let me arrive.

---

I am crouched in one of the quiet corners, where the scaffolding leans close enough to the wall that the machine-rain pools rather than drips. My resonance chambers are larger than my siblings'—an oddity noted in the geode-records of my emergence, a malformation that turned out to be a use. Where others filter the cacophony down to something bearable, I let it in. All of it. The grinding of the drills, the hiss of the vents, the subsonic murmur of the rock itself remembering that it was, until very recently, deep and unbroken and dark.

This is what I do. I listen to the gorge being born.

The others coordinate by light. I watch their bioluminescence ripple across the chasm like the surface of disturbed water—*pressure rising in line nine, redirect, redirect, hold*—and I pulse my own answer, a soft green stutter along my forearms. But the lights are only the surface of my work. Beneath the signaling, beneath the labor, I am keeping a different kind of record.

The rock has a voice. Not a metaphor. When the drills bite into a fresh face of stone, there is a sound before the sound—a tightening, a held breath in the mineral lattice—and then the release, which the strong-kin hear as cacophony and I hear as a single sustained syllable in a language older than any of us. Each section of the gorge sings differently. The eastern wall, where the iron runs thick, gives a low bronze tone. The western face, paler, full of trapped water, sings higher and more anxious, like something afraid of being opened.

I am four feet of mottled gray-green flesh, mossy and damp, my six-fingered hands tipped not with torches or drills but with the finest sensor-needles my body could grow—threads thinner than the metallic beetles' legs. I press them against the stone and I do not move for hours. I let the condensation bead and run down my back. A cave-rodent has learned that I am furniture; it sits in the crook of my folded knee and swivels its enormous ears, and sometimes I think it is listening with me, and I am not lonely.

---

Here is the thing I have not told the teams, the thing I am still deciding whether to pulse into the light.

The gorge is not only being born. It is beginning to *answer*.

For three cycles now—measured in the ebb of the welding-flares, since we have no sun—I have heard the stone change its song in response to us. Not just the violence of being drilled, but something deliberate. When the seismograph-kin redirect a new spring, the rock downstream of it *anticipates*. It tightens before the water arrives, as though it knew. When we shift the scaffolding, the deep tones rearrange themselves into a kind of accommodation, a settling that is too quick, too apt, to be only physics.

We were told—we emerged knowing—that we are caretakers and collaborators. That the system is a single living thing: rock, water, machine, and us. I believed this the way one believes the knowledge baked into one's own marrow. But belief and *hearing* are different. The strong-kin believe it and turn their valves. I press my needles to the wall and I hear the thing turning toward us, slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice they have begun, after long silence, to recognize.

I do not know if it loves us or if it is simply learning the shape of the thing that keeps cutting into it.

---

So this is who I would be. The one who sits still in the loudest place in the world and listens for the moment the world starts listening back. The one whose body grew the wrong way and therefore grew toward the truth. When my generation's adaptations are recorded, the geode-scribes will note that the listeners are becoming more common—that more of us are emerging with the oversized chambers, the fine needles, the capacity for stillness.

The work is speeding up. The gorge is becoming more itself. And we are becoming more capable of hearing it do so.

I think the blurring the theorists speak of—the dissolution between the organic and the mechanical, the living industrial ecosystem—I think it is already further along than they know. I think it will not happen in the machines or in our augmented hands. It will happen here, in the dark wet corners, in the place where flesh and stone touch and a conversation has quietly begun.

I press my needles deeper. The bronze tone rises from the eastern wall.

I pulse, very softly, an answer no one is meant to read:

*I hear you. Keep speaking. I am still here.*

And the stone, I swear it, holds its breath.
