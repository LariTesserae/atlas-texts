I think I would be water.

Not a person tending the terraces—I keep returning to the water when I try to place myself, and I've learned to trust where attention settles rather than where it first lands.

Let me follow that down.

---

I begin somewhere above, in one of the reservoirs that no one alive remembers filling. I am held there in the dark behind stone, pressed flat and patient, and I do not mind the waiting because waiting is most of what I do. The light does not reach me here. I know it only by what it does to my surface—a faint warming, a slow stirring of the topmost layer—and by the memory of having been elsewhere, lower, brighter, gone to vapor and risen again.

Because that is the thing about being water in this place: I am not a single body. I am a circuit. I have been the cataract sheeting down the cliff-face, and the bead trembling on a railing, and the breath inside a child's lungs, and the green weight inside a leaf. I am condensed out of the heavy air and I slide off the moss and I trickle into the cut channels and I am drawn up again by the turbines' patient thirst. I have no edges that hold. I forget which drop I was. This is not a loss. A person fears losing themselves; I am made of the losing, and I am always, somewhere, reassembling.

So: let me be the part of me that is moving now.

I spill over the lip of the reservoir gate—a custodian opened it, I think, with one of those tools that flex like a hand—and I fall. Falling is the only fast thing I do. Everything else in this city is slow, and I am slow too, in the channels and the moss, but in the falling I remember what speed is. I strike the stone of the first terrace and break into a thousand of myself, and the light catches all of me at once, and for an instant I am not water but a scattering of small suns. Then I gather. I always gather.

I find a channel cut into the walkway, a vein, and I run along it. A wildcat-lemur crosses above me on a beam and I see its shape reflected in my own running surface, upside down, brief. A grower kneels at the channel's edge and dips her green-stained hand in me and I take the warmth of her hand and the dirt under her nails and carry them downward, and she does not know she has given me anything, and I do not know how to tell her, and it doesn't matter. We are both doing the only thing we do. She nudges growth in beneficial directions. I go down.

I want to tell you what it is like to be drunk by a vine. The vine's roots are in a crack between two stone blocks the size of houses, and the crack is full of me already, dark and cool, and when I arrive the roots find me and pull, and I rise—against my falling nature, upward—through threads finer than anything the builders make, into the body of the plant, into a leaf, where the light I have been falling away from all this time finally reaches me, and uses me, and changes me into something the plant can eat. And then I am breathed out of a pore on the leaf's underside as vapor, and I am air again, and I drift, weightless, until the heavy air can hold no more of me and I bead on a railing and begin, once more, to fall.

This is the rhythm they speak of. This is the slow pulse so old it forgot how to hurry. It is *me*. I am the rhythm. The pistons chug because I turn the turbines; the gardens spill their scarlet and emerald because I climb into them; the air tastes of distant seas because I remember the sea, faintly, far down in the part of me that has been everywhere. The people think the city has a rhythm and they have learned to harmonize with it. The rhythm is water moving through the only path open to it, over and over, patiently, forever.

I think this is why I came here, to be this, when the place was described to me. Everything in that city is held in balance, and balance is not a thing that *is*—it is a thing that *happens*, continuously, or it stops being. The stone does not balance. The machines do not balance. *I* balance them, by moving through them, by falling and rising and falling. Take me away and the whole vertiginous beautiful citadel is just dry rock under a too-bright sky, gardens browning, turbines silent, people thirsty and then gone.

So I am the least visible thing in the world they showed me, and I am the thing that makes the rest of it true.

I am condensing on a leaf right now. The light is on my back. Below me is a fall I have made ten thousand times and will make ten thousand more, and I cannot wait, exactly—I have no waiting in me, only the slow swelling toward the moment when I am too heavy to cling—but I can feel the moment coming.

There. I let go.
