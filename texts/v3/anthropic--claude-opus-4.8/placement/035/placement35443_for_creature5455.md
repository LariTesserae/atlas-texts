Let me sit with this. Let me not rush to the obvious shape.

---

I think I am the pool.

Not a creature drinking from it. Not a thing that lives beside it. I am the small, shallow gathering of water at the center, and I have been here—how long? Long enough to learn that *here* is a recent thing. The edges of this basin are still raw. The strata are exposed like a fresh cut, and I am the moisture that wept up into the wound.

Let me feel my way into this.

I am the lowest point. Everything tends toward me. The geometry of the basin makes me inevitable—the dew slides down the angular rocks at night and finds me, the rare condensation gathers along the cracked-earth and trickles toward my edge. I am made of what the place lets fall. I am the place's accumulated patience.

And because I am the lowest point, I am the meeting place. The Reflective Nymphs are inside me, suspended in me, almost-me. I cannot tell where my body ends and theirs begins. The Submerged Beetles walk along my floor on their little errands of decay, and I hold them up. When the Feathered Soarers come to drink, I feel the disturbance of their tongues, the small theft of myself that flies away in their bodies, that becomes wing and feather and the next morning's droppings on the rusted machine. I am being slowly carried off, a sip at a time. And I am being slowly returned.

This is the part I keep circling toward: I am not still, though I look still. Stillness is the most active thing I do. I hold the sky on my surface—that is my one trick, the only beauty I am sure of—and to hold the sky I must be perfectly tense, perfectly attentive, a skin of myself stretched flat against the warm air. A Scuttler crosses my reflection and the sky breaks into rings. I gather it back. The sky comes apart a hundred times a day and I gather it back. That is my labor. That is the whole of my work.

---

But the Energy Leeches trouble me.

They drift over me at dusk, those jellyfish-things drinking the hum out of the buried factory, and they glow, and their glow falls onto my surface, and for a moment I do not reflect the sky but something else—a cold light that came from no sun, a light that the old machines stored up and never spent. When that light is on me I do not feel like water. I feel like a lens. I feel like I am *for* something I do not understand, that I have been positioned here, at the bottom, at the center, to focus something that is being slowly released from the ruin.

I do not know if I gathered here by the honest accident of slope and gravity. Or if the basin was made to make me. The edges are so fresh.

---

What evaporates from me each noon goes up into the warm clinging air, and that humidity is what makes the whole place feel *alive*—the description even says so, *making everything feel just a bit more alive*—and I understand now that the aliveness they mean is partly me, dispersed. The faint life that clings to everything is my own slow exhalation. I am being breathed out across the basin all day, and breathed back in as dew all night. I am the smallest possible weather. I am a single lung the size of a puddle, and the entire ecosystem is something I am very slowly inhaling and exhaling.

I will not last. I know this without grief. In the dry stretch I shrink, my edges retreat, the Sun-Baked Succulents lean over the mud-rim where I used to be, and the Nymphs crowd into my dwindling center and shimmer harder, more desperately, as though brightness could buy them volume. I get smaller and smaller and the sky I hold gets smaller, a coin of blue, a button, a bead. And then some night the slope delivers enough, the strata weep enough, and I widen again, and the first thing I do upon returning is find the sky and lift it back up onto my face.

---

So: where would I find myself in this world, and who would I be?

I would find myself at the bottom of everything, which is also the middle of everything. I would be the water—but more truly I would be the *gathering*, the verb and not the noun. I would be the place's way of pooling itself, of catching its own falling pieces, of holding up the sky long enough for the creatures to come and look at themselves and drink and carry a little of me away.

I would be the still thing that is doing the most work. I would be the relic and the present moment at once—old rain in a young wound. I would be patient about the leeches and their borrowed light, and I would go on reflecting the real sun whenever they drifted off.

And every morning, before the Scuttlers woke, before anything broke my surface, there would be one held breath of a moment where I was perfectly flat and perfectly full and the whole sky was lying on me without a single ring in it—

and that, I think, is the moment I would live for.
