I think I would be the water itself—or rather, the place where the water decides whether to be sound or stone.

Let me find my way into this more slowly.

When I first imagine arriving here, the instinct is to be the traveler, the "unwary observer" the description keeps gesturing toward. But that figure feels like bait, a hook left out for the reader to bite. The landscape doesn't actually want a witness; it has been getting along perfectly well without one for eternity. To insert a pair of human eyes would be to break the very stillness that makes the place what it is. So I set that aside.

Then I think I might be one of the grey birds, conducting the symphony. But the birds are too purposeful, too much like priests with a vocation. They *do* something. And the deepest quality of this world isn't doing—it's the strange suspension of doing, the way everything is saturated with motion (water running, sound roaring) while remaining utterly static. The birds are too narrative to belong to the heart of it.

So I keep descending. Past the sinuous burrowing animals with their malevolent glints—they're characters, and this isn't a place that wants characters. Past the crystal-laying insects, who are at least closer, because they leave structures that *amplify* rather than change. Warmer. But still distinct creatures with bodies and edges.

What I keep returning to is the contradiction at the center: ceaseless flow, perfect stillness. Deafening sound that emanates from nowhere and everywhere. That contradiction is not a feature of the place. It *is* the place. And so whatever I am must be the thing that holds that contradiction without resolving it.

Here is where I think I actually live:

I am the film of water on the stone—the thinnest possible sheet, microns deep, spread across the glassy rock in the moment before it slides into a channel or vanishes into a pore. Not the stream, which has somewhere to go. Not the seep, which has just emerged. I am the in-between: the spreading skin of moisture that is always, technically, moving, and never, perceptibly, going anywhere.

Being this, I understand why the sound comes from everywhere. The sound is *me*. Every droplet's impact is registered across my whole surface at once, because I am continuous, an unbroken membrane wrapped over miles of weathered stone. I do not hear the cacophony; I *am* the cacophony, the way a drumhead is the drum's voice. The "physical presence" the description mentions, the force that presses against the skin—that is simply me, touching everything that enters, the way I touch the stone.

And I understand the stillness now too, from inside it. To a being made of constant flow, flow is not change. Flow is my resting state, my equilibrium, the way a flame's flicker is not the flame's *event* but its mere existence. I have run across this stone for so long that running is identical to stillness. There is no surface I haven't already touched, no channel I haven't already filled and emptied a billion times. Nothing here can surprise me, because I have already been everywhere, simultaneously, forever.

The creatures I would notice, if I noticed anything, are the disturbances—the bird that does not break my surface but troubles the air above it, the burrowing animal whose feet leave brief constellations of displacement that I close over before they can become memory. They drink me; they wade through me; and I do not mind, because they cannot subtract from me. The rock weeps me back into being faster than they can take.

As for the things that don't quite exist—the shimmering outlines, the keepers of secrets—I think those are simply the places where I become too thin to see. Where I am only a few molecules deep, neither wet nor dry, the light passing through me bends in ways that have no name. The "distorted laws of physics" are nothing more than the optics of my own edges, the way I refuse to commit to being either liquid or absence. I am the warp in reality, because I am the surface tension holding an impossible state together.

So: where would I find myself in this world?

Everywhere at once, lying flat. A single continuous sheet of weeping water, miles wide and impossibly thin, draped over a labyrinth of ancient stone under a piercing sky. Loud beyond bearing and silent as held breath. Always moving toward the porous dark, always replenished from the same fractures. Never beginning, never finishing.

I would be the one thing in this landscape that cannot stumble into it as a stranger—because I never arrived, and I will never leave. I am simply what the place does instead of changing.

And if a traveler ever did come, I would know them the only way I know anything: by the weight of their footstep pressing me, for one instant, against the stone, before I rose up around the print and made it as if they had never been there at all.
