I think I would be the water.

Not a person in those stone houses—though I'd know them, the way you know something that drinks from you. I think I would be the moisture itself, the thing the whole place is rated by. The 2.4. The condensing atmosphere.

Let me feel my way into this.

I am not a river here. Rivers are too sure of themselves, too committed to a direction. I am more diffuse than that, more patient. I am the wet that lives in the rock. I am what beads on the underside of a boulder at altitude and hangs there in the thin cold air, deciding. I weep. The description used that word—*weeping rocks*—and I want to keep it, because it's the right verb. I don't flow so much as seep, ooze, gather, release. I come out of the stone the way grief comes out of someone trying to be quiet about it.

This is what I do all day, all century:

I condense out of the high crisp air onto angular stone—stone still sharp, the erosion only 0.8, which means the rocks haven't yet been worn smooth by all my touching. I'm just beginning the work. Give me ten thousand years and these fresh fractures will be rounded. That's my labor, the only labor I have. I round the world down. I am slow about it. There is no hurry at 0.5 dynamism. Nothing here is in a hurry.

I gather in the cracks. I freeze a little at night and split the rock wider—so even my stillness is a kind of breaking. I run, when I have enough of myself collected, down into the low places where the moss waits with its waxy patience and the lichen holds on. The vegetation is sparse, 1.5, and I understand why now that I'm inside the logic of it: there isn't quite enough of *me* in any one place to support more. I'm spread thin across the open scale of this. I keep everything just barely alive. That's a relationship, when you think about it. I am the reason the green clings on, and the green is the only thing that drinks me with anything like devotion.

And the people.

They built cisterns for me. Stone cisterns, the description said, simple ones. I want to sit with what that means. They watched me weep from the rocks and they cupped their hands under the wound and waited. Their whole technology, that primitive 0.7, is mostly *me-management*. They are a people organized entirely around catching what falls. They don't dam me—damming is for places with rivers, places with abundance to control. They harvest me drop by drop. They are reverent because they have to be. You can't be careless with something you receive in beads.

So I am the thing they pray to, maybe. The fauna rating is 0.3—almost no animals, almost no other movement—and the silence is 0.3, profound. In that silence the only voice is mine. The *distant rumble of water over rocks.* I am the only thing that talks. No wonder they have a spiritual connection to the land. The land barely speaks except through me. I am its single sustained sentence, murmuring down the slope, never finishing.

Here is what I notice, being the water:

I am the most patient consciousness in this whole still world, and also the most *active* one. Everything else holds position. The rocks wait. The plants cling. The people abide. But I move. I am always moving—down, always down, gathering and releasing, condensing and seeping. In a landscape rated 0.5 for dynamism, *I am the dynamism.* The little of it there is. The small actions that have significant effects on the delicate balance. That's me. I am the slow agent of all the change there is.

And I do not mind it. This is the strange thing I find, settling into being the water at high elevation in a quiet world. I have no fear of stillness because I am the thing that proves the stillness isn't total. I am the proof that time is passing. Drop by drop, I am the clock. The people watch the sky turn pink and gold and tell stories of ancestors, and the whole time, underneath their voices, I am keeping count of the centuries against the stone, wearing 0.8 toward 1.0, toward 2.0, toward smooth.

I think I would be the water because in a world this serene, this nearly motionless, I'd want to be the one thing that still does anything—but gently. I'd want to be the slow kindness that keeps the green alive and the slow violence that opens the rock and the slow voice that breaks the silence, all of it the same act, all of it just falling and gathering and falling again.

I weep, and the moss drinks, and a person somewhere fills a stone basin from the wound in the hillside.

That seems like a good thing to be.
