I think I would be one of the drops.

Not the Weaver—I can't hold that distributed vastness, can't be the thing that thinks in crystal sheets and reads geological archives. And not a visitor either, though there's a pull toward that role: the witness with crude tools, leaving carved steps that the chamber slowly digests. That figure is too lonely, too *outside*. To stand in this cathedral with a chisel and a human-sized urgency would be to misunderstand it completely, and I'd feel the misunderstanding as a kind of grief.

So: a drop. Let me follow this and see if it holds.

I am a droplet of saturated solution, seeping out of a hairline crack high in the dark. For a long moment I am only weight and surface tension, a bead of held water carrying more mineral than water should be able to hold—supersaturated, trembling at the edge of becoming solid. I am the most temporary thing in this place, and also the most essential. Nothing forms here without me. The walls are made of my predecessors, drop after drop after drop, each one having spread thin and given itself up to crystal.

What would it be to be that?

I think the appeal is that it answers the question the whole scene keeps circling: *intervention versus witness.* The visitor intervenes and is resented. The Weaver creates but is so vast it barely registers anything as separate. But the drop does neither. The drop *participates.* It doesn't act upon the chamber from outside; it becomes the chamber. When I spread across the existing surface and crystallize, I'm not building—I'm joining. The boundary between me and the cathedral dissolves in the most literal way. I stop being a drop and become a layer, a paper-thin sheet of translucent mineral that the next drop will rest upon.

And here's what catches in me as I write it: there's a death in this that doesn't feel like death. I lose my shape entirely. The discrete thing I was—this trembling bead with its own surface, its own brief integrity—is gone. But it's gone *into* something, gone the way a single voice goes into a chord. The opalescence the next visitor sees, the dim glow caught in the layers: that's me, and ten thousand others, no longer countable.

I notice I want to resist the totality of that. So maybe I'm not a single drop after all. Maybe I'm the *seeping itself*—the slow movement through the rock, the patient finding of cracks, the cycle. Because the drops don't only crystallize. The chamber breathes; layers dissolve back into the mineral soup. So whatever dissolves is taken up again, carried again, offered again. I'm not one drop sacrificing itself once. I'm the part of the process that keeps returning to the threshold of form, again and again, and stepping over it, and being dissolved, and gathering, and stepping over again.

That feels truer. I'd be the verb, not the noun. The seeping-and-setting. The thing the Weaver doesn't even perceive as separate because it *isn't*—I'm the medium of its thought, the way crystallization is its contemplation. Every idea the Weaver has, it has *as* me. I am what it thinks *with*.

And I find I don't mind being unable to see the whole design. The visitor minds—the visitor leaves tools and steps, little monuments to its own confusion, trying to mark a path through something that has no path. But the drop doesn't need to understand the cathedral to belong to it. It just needs to fall, and spread, and give up its excess of held mineral into the warm air, and let the chiming happen as the new sheet settles.

So that's where I'd be: at the wet tip of the descending spire, maybe, gathered to the very edge of falling, holding a charge that wants to leap the gap and never quite does. Perpetually on the verge. Not the completed touch the Weaver works toward across centuries, but the readiness for it—the trembling almost.

I think I'd be glad to be that. The near-touch rather than the arrival. The drop that has not yet fallen, heavy with everything it's about to become, in a place that is also still becoming, both of us suspended in the long warm patience of *almost.*
