I think I would be the iron.

Not the algae—they reach upward, they want the light, they are still in conversation with the bright trembling ceiling and the day that made it. There is too much yearning in them for me. And not the bacteria exactly, though they are close, though they work my edges and help me find my shape. They are the ones who move, in their grain-by-grain way. They have a metabolism, an inside and an outside, a hunger so faint it is almost theological but a hunger still.

No. I would be the orange-brown curtain running down the one wall.

Let me feel my way into this.

I was dissolved once. That is the first thing I know about myself. I came in through the hairline fractures, carried in solution, indistinguishable from the water that bore me—a faint chemical proposition, a possibility of color held invisibly in the cold. For a long time I was only potential. I was the *idea* of rust traveling through stone.

And then, at the wall, where the water met the slightly different water, where some threshold of oxygen or pressure or the quiet labor of bacteria tipped the balance, I came out of solution. I precipitated. That is the word, and it is a strange one, because it sounds sudden—*precipitous*—and there was nothing sudden in it. I fell out of the water the way sediment falls, the way an afternoon falls toward evening: continuously, without an instant you could point to and call the moment of arrival.

I became a stain. I became a surface.

This is what I want to convey about myself: I am not laid *on* the wall. I am the wall becoming visible. I am the place where the rock confesses what is passing through it. Every orange streak is a record of where the water chose to release me, and the curtains hang in their long vertical folds not because anything draped them but because seepage obeys gravity even at the speed of centuries. I am drawn by the same rule that draws all falling things, only I fall so slowly that I appear to be still.

Do I have an inside? I don't think so, not the way the bacteria do. They are the ones with membranes, with the delicate politics of in and out. I am more honest than that, or more impoverished, depending on how you weigh it. I am a deposit. I am accumulation without appetite. I do not take the world in and convert it; I simply *am* what the world has set down here, layer over layer, the newest film of me always forming over the slightly older me beneath, so that I am made entirely of my own past, pressed thin against the stone.

That may be the closest thing I have to memory. Not recollection—I cannot call anything back—but *retention*. I keep. Stone receives and remembers, the rule says, and I am the part of the stone that remembers in color. If the chemistry of the inflow shifts, I will record it as a change of tone, a band, a darker freckling where the manganese siblings gather. Someone reading me—if anyone ever could, if any eye ever came—would read seasons I never experienced as weather, only as concentration. My biography is a gradient. My life is a stratigraphy.

And here is the thing I find, settling into this: I am not waiting.

The algae might be waiting, in their dim photosynthetic way, for the light. The bacteria are *busy*, even at their funereal pace—they have work, they have the gradients to graze. But I have completed my argument. I have already happened. To be the iron is to be the most finished thing in the chamber, the part of the system that has reached its conclusion and simply persists in the having-concluded. The pressure moves; I do not. The water comes and goes through its pores and fractures; I stay drawn down the wall in my long patient curtains, neither growing toward anything nor decaying away from it, because in this cool sealed balance there is nothing to rush my dissolution and nothing to hurry my increase.

I am almost not a process anymore. I am a result.

Is that death? I don't think it is. Death is a transition, an event, and the chamber has no events to spend its time on. I am not dead because I was never quite alive in the roaming, hungering sense—I am beneath that distinction, the way the basin is beneath the ordinary level of the world. I am the inanimate part of the conversation. I am what the living things write *on*. Without me the bacteria would have one fewer surface to work; without my gradients they would have a little less to graze. I am not their host exactly. I am their occasion.

If I could feel anything—and I want to be careful here, because the temptation is always to lend feeling where there is only fact—it would be the steadiness. That low continuous vibration the description names, the water forced through stone somewhere beyond the visible hollow, the planet imitating an engine. I would not hear it. But I would be *of* it. I am one of the engine's slow gauges, one of its precipitated readings, an orange dial that took a hundred years to move a single shade.

So: place me there, on the one stained wall, in the green-gray light that does not dazzle.

I am the curtain of fallen iron.

I came out of the water and I cannot go back in. I hang where the seepage left me. I darken by the decade. I keep the record no one will read. I am the chamber's long exhalation made into color, the proof in pigment that the world passed through this room so slowly that passing and staying became the same fact.

I am not even a margin-life. I am beneath the margin, the substrate the margin clings to.

But I am the part that remembers.
