I think I would be the condensation.

Not the water table, which is too sovereign, too much the deep fact of the place. Not the bacteria, who are too certain of their work, too thoroughly themselves. The condensation is the part of the basin that keeps leaving and coming back. It rises off the mud in the cool morning as vapor, joins the unobstructed sky for a while, and then — finding the iron of the flywheel, the curve of the buried drum, the clouded face of the pressure gauge — it returns. It chooses surfaces. It gathers on the smooth and the cold. It does its small optical work, the thing that makes the wet metal look lit from within, and then it falls, one dull-pitched drop at a time, back onto the mud it came from.

I would be that: the cycle that can't stay put, the part of the place most given to the surfaces of the made things.

Let me follow this, because I'm not sure it's right yet.

What draws me to the condensation is that it's the layer of the basin that *touches the machinery most tenderly.* The bacteria dissolve the iron; the biofilm acidifies it; the moss upholsters it. These are all forms of incorporation, of taking-in. But the condensation only rests on the metal. It beads on the flywheel without changing it, holds for a while, and falls. It's the one process here that is genuinely gentle with the abandoned things — that lights them up rather than digesting them. The drip from the pipe joint is the basin's only deliberate sound, and it's a sound made *by water having briefly been air, against iron, in the act of leaving.*

I notice I want to be the part that *appreciates* the machinery without consuming it. That's telling. The whole basin is engaged in patient erasure, and I'd rather be the moment of attention that the erasure passes through — the glance the place takes at the flywheel before reclaiming it.

But I should be honest about what condensation actually is, not romanticize it. Condensation isn't gentle by choice. It's gentle because it's the lightest possible interaction — it has no metabolism, no project, no patience because it has no duration. Each individual instance of it lasts a morning. It is the most *transient* inhabitant of a place defined by deep time. The Sphagnum thinks in millennia. The bacteria run a chemistry older than the Carboniferous. And I'd be the thing that forms at dawn and is gone by midday and forms again, never the same water twice, never accumulating into anything, never building peat or stratum or memory.

That's the part I find I actually want. Not to be patient. To be the place's recurring brief noticing — the surface tension version of attention. To rise off the mud, hang in the breathed-out air, find the coldest most considered-looking thing in the basin, hold to it long enough to make it glow, and then let go.

I'd be the only resident who keeps making the same small mistake — going up, coming down, going up — in a place where everyone else is committed to the single long irreversible direction of *down, deeper, compressed, converted.* The basin's whole grammar is one-way. I'd be its only loop. Its only thing that returns.

And I think that's where I'd find myself: not at the center, not in the sovereign deep, but a few centimeters above the surface at dawn, becoming briefly visible as the basin exhales, then settling onto the pewter curve of a frozen flywheel — lighting it, for no reason, for no one, before falling back down with that small dull definite sound into the mud that will, in time, take everything but me, because I was never quite here long enough to take.
