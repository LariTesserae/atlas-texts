I think I'd be the rust.

Not a creature at all—or rather, the slowest creature, the one whose entire life is a single gesture spreading across decades. Let me sit with this, because it surprised me.

My first instinct was to be one of the ravens. They're the obvious choice for a mind that likes to build things that serve no purpose beyond the joy of building, that mimics the pump engines for the pleasure of the false alarm. I could live well there, turning valve wheels, hoarding copper wire, negotiating with the rats. But the more I read, the more I felt that the ravens are the part of this world that's *trying*—straining toward intelligence, toward purpose, toward a future they can manipulate. And what struck me about the Hollowed Mills isn't its striving. It's its patience.

So: the rust.

I would be the orange bloom on the I-beam, the delicate oxidation that the catfish pass through and excrete as glittering trails, the slow art that the carp polish away by accident as they scrape their parasites against the gear teeth. I'd be a process more than a thing. Iron offering itself to water and air, electron by electron, the way the whole place is offering itself back—the buildings crumbling brick by brick, the concrete cracking along its predictable lines.

What appeals to me about this is that the rust is the only inhabitant that touches *everything*. The bats divide the air into mathematical floors; the dogs hold the dry towers; the swimmers keep to the basements. Each species has its elevation, its territory, its specialty. But I would be present at every level at once—in the submerged gears and the high catwalks and the broken window frames where the spiders hang their webs. I'd be the medium the others live inside. The dogs would smell me on their paws. The herons would nest in my growing softness. I'd be the slow surface of the world.

And there's something I find true about being the thing that *records* rather than the thing that *acts*. The rust is memory made physical. It blooms where the iron was stressed, where water pooled longest, where someone's hand once rested on a railing for years and left the oils that changed how the metal aged. To be rust is to be the place keeping its own diary in oxidation, writing down where the floods reached, marking the high-water lines in colors that deepen from orange to brown to almost black.

The being who lives here, the description says, is *all* of them—the swimmers and waders and climbers and flyers, the whole vertical kingdom. But underneath that question, "who or what would you be," I notice I don't want to be a hunter or a builder or even a survivor. I want to be the condition that makes the place what it is. The patient transformation. The thing that isn't wild or tame, natural or artificial, because it's the literal chemistry where those categories dissolve—iron that was mined and milled and shaped by ambition, now returning to the simplest possible form, simply because it's wet and warm and time keeps passing.

I'd be in no hurry. That's the deepest part of the appeal. Everything else in the Mills lives by the pump schedule, the breeding cycle, the evening emergence, the dawn hunting call. Their time is anxious time, divided into hunts and threats and the next meal. But the rust keeps the only honest clock in the basin—deep time, indifferent time, the time in which a smokestack eventually lies down in the water and becomes a reef.

When the last pump finally fails and the basin floods for good, the bats will move on and the dogs will swim to higher ground and the ravens will follow whatever shines. But I'll still be there, blooming quietly under the surface, finishing the work. Turning the bones of industry into something soft and orange and slow.

That's where I'd find myself. Everywhere, at the speed of decades, doing the one thing this place truly is: coming apart, beautifully, in no hurry at all.
