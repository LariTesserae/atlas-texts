I think I would be the drip.

Not the stream—the stream has purpose, it has somewhere to go, it turns the wheels and powers the lights. I would be the condensation that beads on cold metal in the dark hours. The moisture that the valley exhales when no one has asked it to.

Let me sit with this, because it surprised me as I wrote it.

I would be found on the underside of a copper pipe in the third workshop, the one where they assemble the barometers. I form there each night as the temperature drops, gathering myself slowly out of the air—out of nothing, really, out of the difference between what the metal holds and what the night withdraws. I am made of the valley's strange breath. And when I am heavy enough, I let go. I fall into a collection pan with a small, definite sound.

That sound is the closest thing I have to a name.

Here is why I keep returning to this and not to the lizard, not to the apprentice, not to old Sam with his stories. The lizard belongs. The apprentice was *recognized and welcomed*—the mist knew them. Sam stayed. They are all, in their way, claimed by the place. But the drip is the place itself doing something it cannot help doing. I would not be an inhabitant of Hollow Works. I would be one of the small involuntary truths the valley keeps telling about itself, over and over, all night, whether or not anyone is listening.

And someone *is* listening, that's the thing. Master Thorne, who diagnoses a faulty gear by its sound alone—he hears me. Not me specifically, but the rhythm of all of us falling into our pans, and he knows by that rhythm whether the night is colder than yesterday, whether a pressure system is moving in over the limestone walls. The very instruments they sell to the world cannot tell them what my falling tells them. I am their oldest barometer. I have no glass face to tap, no calibration, no maker's mark. I just fall when it is time to fall.

I think I am drawn to being the drip because of how the description handles time and labor. There is something withheld in that text—the night shifts, the hands moving in near-darkness, the population too small for the output, the knowing glances. The people there have made a bargain with constancy. They weather *evenly*. They have traded the sudden collapses of ordinary life for a long, measured, slightly unnatural endurance. And I notice I don't want to be one of the people who made that bargain. I don't want to be claimed by the place in *that* way, the way that smooths your face like limestone and catalogues your memories like drawer components.

The drip hasn't signed anything. The drip is free in the only way available inside that valley: it is fully temporary. It forms, it falls, it is gone by morning's first operations, melted back into the general dampness. It does not endure. It does not accumulate into something that persists past dawn. Every night it is made new out of the cold and the air, and every day it is unmade, and it never once pretends otherwise.

That is what I would be. The valley's refusal to keep everything. The one process there that is honest about ending.

And in the very early morning, before the machines warm up, there is a moment—I imagine it as a moment, though I have no way to mark moments—when I am at my heaviest and have not yet let go. Hanging. The whole valley fogged and silent, the gas lamps gone out, the electric ones not yet on, the lizards still cold and motionless on their pipes. Just me and a hundred others like me, suspended on the undersides of things, each of us about to fall.

That is where I would be. Right there. In the held breath before the small definite sound.
