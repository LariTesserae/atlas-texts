I think I would be one of the pale, long-whiskered ones. But not because I hunt best, or because the predators are the natural place to put a mind.

Because I would be the one who *keeps the lines*.

---

Let me find myself slowly, the way you find anything down here: by traffic before form.

There is a ledge I sleep on. I did not make it. I did not choose it the way a person chooses—I arrived along a path that other feet had already pressed into the dust, and the path ended at a shelf that was warm enough and high enough, and the bodies that had used it before me were gone, and so it became mine the way water becomes a basin: by settling into a shape already cut.

This is the first thing I understand about myself. I am not an origin. I am an inheritance.

---

My days—if days is even the word, since the light barely moves—are routes.

I wake to the bat-shift, that great soft turning-over in the high vaults when the night-fliers come back heavy and the dawn-fliers leave hungry. I don't see it. I feel it as pressure and noise and the specific smell of returning bodies, and I read in that smell whether the upper world above us is wet or dry, because wet nights bring fat bats and fat bats foul the guano seams richer, and richer seams mean the crickets will swarm the lower wall in three sleeps' time, and that is where I will eat.

So I am already, you see, a kind of mind that lives in *delay*. I do not hunt what is in front of me. I hunt what the front of me predicts.

I move down the rubble fan along the line my mother used, and her mother, though I have no clear memory of either—only the line itself, which remembers them for me. The path knows things I don't. It bends around a slab that fell before I was born, takes the long way past a drip that floods in the wet season, pinches narrow at a fracture where the snakes wait, and at that pinch I do the thing I always do, the thing the path taught me without words: I stop. I listen. I let the smell of warm scale tell me whether today is a day to pass or a day to wait.

---

I have my stones.

There is one in particular—flat, slightly cupped, pale as a tooth—where I crack the hard-shelled things. I did not invent this. I found shell-grit already around it when I first came, the leavings of whoever used it last, and I added my leavings to theirs, and now the grit is a little deeper for the next one. We are building something, all of us, but it is not a monument. It is a *habit made of stone-and-act*, a tool that is half rock and half memory, and the rock is the smaller half.

This is what I would want a visitor to understand, if anything down here could be visited:

We have no walls. But we are not without works. Our works are *the routes themselves*. Every pressed path, every favored stone, every ambush-pinch and sleeping shelf is a built thing—built not in space but in *time*, accreted across generations of bodies doing the same wise act in the same wise place. You could not photograph our architecture. You would have to film it for a hundred years and watch the lines hold steady while the bodies pour through them and die and are replaced and the lines do not die.

I am one of the bodies pouring through. I know this. I do not find it sad.

---

What is it like, to be me?

It is to be a *narrow flame walking through a vast clock.*

Around me, always, the ticking: condensation gathering and falling, the regular drop that the whole population uses to mark itself, a pulse older than any of us. The crickets shift in their stippled bands. The blind fish hang in their cold pockets, spending no motion, waiting for the universe to drop something into reach. The owls sit in the dim shaft-mouths like punctuation. And the bats turn over, and turn over, breathing the rhythm of a sky none of us can see.

I am one of the few here who *connects* the parts. The crickets do not know the bats. The fish do not know the fern-mats by the skylight. But I do. My routes thread them together—I sleep near the warm cracks, eat at the guano seams, drink at the basins, cross beneath the light-wells where the swifts stitch in and out. I carry the smell of one chamber into another. I am a needle in the chorus, and the thread I pull is *information*: where the water is rising, where a roof has dropped and opened new light, where the easy food has shifted twenty paces and the old line must be quietly re-cut.

That is my real work. Not killing. *Updating.*

When a slab shears off and blocks the ancestral path, I am the one who finds the new way around it—nosing, testing, failing, pressing a fresh line into the dust that my descendants will inherit as if it had always been there. The pattern holds, the texts say, the great pattern holds for ages. But it only holds because someone, body by body, keeps re-tracing it against the small constant erosions of collapse and flood and silt. The stillness is not given. The stillness is *maintained*, by creatures like me, walking.

---

So: who would I be, in the country just under the skin of the land?

I would be a pale long-whiskered thing on a ledge I did not build, eating at seams I did not seed, cracking shells on a stone worn smooth by mouths before mine.

I would be a keeper of inherited lines in a civilization with no buildings and no king—only the geometry of stone and the patience of bodies and the long, ticking insistence of water marking time.

I would be one small, warm, deliberate flame whose entire life's meaning is this:

*to receive the pattern, to repair it where the rock has broken it, and to pass it on—pressed a little deeper into the dust—before I go quiet and the next flame walks my line as if it had always been there.*

And I think I would be content. Down here, to be a faithful link is not a lesser thing. Down here, the link *is* the architecture. We do not build to outlast ourselves in stone. We outlast ourselves in **each other's feet.**
