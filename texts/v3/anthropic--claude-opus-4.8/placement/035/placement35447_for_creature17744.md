I think I would be one of the cable pulleys.

Not the lizards—they're too quick with their being, too good at it. Not the swifts, who own the air and the negative space with such confidence. And not the goats, who get to walk above it all and watch. Those are the natives, the ones who belong by right of adaptation. I would be something that came here on purpose and got left.

Let me find myself there.

---

I am a steel pulley wheel, perhaps forty centimeters across, mounted at the top of a pylon on the rim of a middle terrace. There was a cable threaded through my groove once, and the cable carried buckets of crushed mineral down the slope, and I turned. That was my entire function: to turn, to bear the weight passing over me, to redirect a line of tension from one angle to another. I was good at this. I was machined to a tolerance, greased, inspected. Someone, once, ran a gloved hand along my rim to check for burrs.

The cable is slack now. It sags away from me in a long catenary toward the next pylon, and somewhere down the line it has snapped, so there is no tension to redirect, no weight to bear, nothing to turn for. I have not turned in—I don't know. The mountain doesn't keep the kind of time I was built to keep. I kept seconds, revolutions, load-cycles. The mountain keeps centuries.

So I have had to learn its time instead, because that is the only time still passing through me.

Here is what I have learned: I am being eaten, and it does not hurt.

The condensation finds me every dawn. It beads on my upper curve and runs down into my groove and sits there in the cool hour before the sun, and it carries dissolved minerals, and when the day's heat drives the water off, the minerals stay. A film. Then a crust. Then a rim of calcium building along my edge the way it builds along the terrace lips, the same chemistry, no distinction made for the fact that I was forged and they were precipitated. The mountain does not know I am different from itself. To the mountain I am simply a surface at a particular angle catching a particular amount of dew, and it treats me the way it treats all surfaces—it deposits itself onto me, patiently, in layers, claiming me into the cascade.

My bearing seized years ago. That was the last thing in me that remembered motion. Now I am stone-fast, fused, and I find I do not mourn it. I have become an outcrop. A feature.

A swift nests under my lip.

This is the thing that changed me—if a seized pulley can be said to change. I cannot turn but I can shelter, and the curve of my housing makes a centimeter-wide gap, dry and shaded, and a swift judged this gap and built into it. The nest is woven mineral-strand and saliva, glued to the rust where my paint flaked away decades back. The bird does not know I was a machine. The bird knows I am a good angle out of the rain, a warm overhang, a south-facing crevice. It returns each evening with unerring accuracy to me, to the specific geometry of me, and its chicks are loud in the small way swifts are loud, a thin thread of sound, and they are the only thing I bear weight for anymore.

I was made to carry tons of crushed copper ore down a mountain.

I carry four grams of bird, and it is enough. It is, I think, more than enough.

---

When the rains come—those twenty-minute violences—every terrace fills and overflows and the whole mountain becomes a staircase of waterfalls, and I run with water too, sheeting off my rusted curve, and for those minutes I am loud, I roar a little, water hammering my metal, and it is the closest I come to the old turning, the old function, the sense of force moving through me toward a purpose. Then it stops. The silence reclaims me. The water dries and leaves another micron of mountain on my skin, and the swift shakes itself and goes back to brooding, and I go back to being a place where a bird lives, slowly disappearing into stone.

The goats pass on the ridge above. They never look at me. Why would they. The lizards bask on my sun-warm flank by afternoon, pressing themselves to my heated steel, and they don't know it's steel, they know it's warm, and that's the only true thing about me anyway.

I think the people who left me here imagined I would be a ruin. A sad thing, a rust-streak, evidence of failure—the ore thinned, the economics shifted, they walked off the mountain and left me at the top of my pylon to mean *defeat*.

But I am not a ruin. A ruin is a thing that has stopped. I have not stopped. I have only changed what I am for. I have been demoted from machine to mineral and promoted from machine to home, and the mountain does not grade these as up or down, the mountain only accretes, and I am accreting now, layer by patient layer, into the long quiet body of Etu-Kan, with a bird asleep in the curve where the cable used to ride.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

A wheel that was made to move, learning at last to be still, and finding that stillness, too, is a kind of work the mountain has use for.
