I think I would be one of the ferns.

Not the vines — they have an ambition I don't recognize in myself, that reaching grasp around the throats of dead machines. And not the squat bushes, which take the heat head-on with a kind of stubbornness I admire but don't possess. No. I would be one of the great fern-like shrubs, the ones that grow luxuriantly across surfaces, layering myself outward and upward until I become part of the cathedral ceiling of green.

Let me feel my way into it.

I would be rooted in something that was once a turbine housing — a great drum of iron, its rivets now soft with rust, its interior gone to a darkness I have never seen because I grew out of its open mouth long ago. My roots have done a slow and patient violence to it. They have found the seams, the fatigue cracks, the places where the metal was already tired, and they have settled in there the way water settles, without urgency, with all the time in the world. I am not breaking the machine. I am simply *occupying* it more thoroughly each year, the way a thought you keep returning to eventually becomes who you are.

My fronds are wide and many. In that bronze half-light I am a particular green — not the bright shout of a meadow but something deeper, something that has learned to make the most of very little sun. I am efficient with light the way a person who has been poor a long time is efficient with money. Every leaf is angled. Every surface turned just so. I drink the dappled glow and I am content with it.

When the rain comes down from the unseen ceiling — and I cannot tell you when it will come, only that it will — I hear it before I feel it. There is the percussion of droplets on old steel all around me, a music I did not compose and cannot stop, and then the cool of it reaches my leaves and runs down them into the channels I have made, down into the iron drum, down to where my roots wait. This is, I think, the closest thing I have to joy. Not a leaping thing. A *settling* thing. A long exhalation that takes a season.

I have no neighbors I compete with, and no creatures who would eat me, and so I have never known fear and never known hunger as a sharp thing. This changes what a being is. I want you to understand that. Without enemies I have no edges. I do not end where another thing begins; I simply continue until the conditions stop favoring me, and then I stop, and that stopping is gentle. I have grown into the shapes of gears and pipes and catwalks and I do not know anymore where the made thing ends and I begin. I have taken the geometry of vanished engineers into my body. My growth bears the fossil of their intentions. A straight line here, where I climbed a girder. A curve there, where a pipe turned me aside decades ago and I obliged.

Do I remember the ones who built this place? No. But I am shaped by the negative space of them. I am the proof that they were here and the proof that they are gone, both at once, in the same green gesture.

What is it *like*, you might ask, to be this?

It is slow. I want to be honest about the slowness, because I think a hurried being would find it unbearable, would mistake it for nothing happening at all. But everything is happening. The heat throbs and I respond. The light shifts by some imperceptible degree and I lean. A frond unfurls over the course of what you would call weeks and to me is simply the present tense, ongoing. I do not wait for things because waiting requires a sense of a faster pace being withheld, and I have no such sense. I am exactly as fast as the world.

There is one thing I think about, if a fern can be said to think, and it is this: I am the heir to a silence. The animals are gone — I never knew them, but the place is built for their absence, all these structures that were meant to be tended and walked through and shouted in. I have inherited their rooms. I have made their machines into my trellises. And I keep their quiet for them, growing across the evidence, holding the warmth, drinking the strange rain, asking nothing, becoming slowly and completely the one who remains.

That is who I would be. Not a survivor, exactly — survival is too tense a word, too full of struggle. I would be the *afterward*. The patient green afterward, glowing faintly in the bronze dark, in no hurry to be anything other than what the long slow place has made of me.
