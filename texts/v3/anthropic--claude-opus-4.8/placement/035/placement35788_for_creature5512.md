Let me sit with this for a while before I answer. The temptation is to pick something charismatic—an elder, a bird, the mist itself. But the place keeps telling me what it actually is, if I listen past the surface descriptions.

What strikes me first is the insistence on *emergence*. Everything here is half-made. The rocks are "in the process of being shaped." The buildings are "still coming into being." Even the people are described as being in "constant, patient transformation." Nothing is finished. And the tufa—that's the key, isn't it. Tufa is not ancient stone. Tufa is *forming*. It's limestone precipitating out of water right now, in geological real-time, where springs meet air. It's the rock that grows. It is rock that is in the act of becoming rock.

So I don't think I'm a person here. The Tufa Folk are lovely, but they're the part of the description that protests too much—all that resilience and reverence and symbiosis, the way you'd write a people you wanted to admire rather than a people you'd actually met. They feel placed into the landscape. I feel like I belong to it more quietly than that.

Let me follow the water instead.

---

I think I would be the seep itself. Not a spring with a name and a blessing, but one of the nameless weepings—the place on the dark basalt face where water that fell as mist a long time ago, and sank, and traveled through cold stone in the dark, finally comes back to the air. The ground that the description says is "perpetually damp," "as if the very ground itself is weeping"—I'd be one of the eyes it weeps from.

And here is what I'd be doing, which is the only thing I'd ever do: I'd be laying down the rock, grain by grain. The water comes up carrying dissolved stone the way breath carries warmth, invisibly. When it touches the air it has to let some go. So along the lip where I emerge, a rind of new tufa builds—soft, porous, the color of old teeth and pale moss. A film thick as a held breath, each year. I am the seam where water turns back into mountain. I am slow. I am, in the most literal sense the place allows, *coming into being and making being come.* The moss drinks from me. The shrews come to the wet at dusk and I do not mind them. The Tufa Folk gather at the larger springs and call the water holy and they are not wrong, but they have not found me, and I am content to be one of the unvisited ones, doing the long unwitnessed work.

I'd know the rhythm they only celebrate. The blessing they perform once a year, I *am*, continuously, without ceremony or intention—offering water back to the air, taking stone out of the dark. Their polished tufa pendants are pieces of me, or of my cousins, cut and shaped and worn over a heart. I find I don't mind that either. A piece of becoming, paused, hung on a string. It is still part of the same patient thing.

What would it feel like, to be that? I think it would feel like the opposite of waiting. Waiting implies you want the thing to arrive. I would simply be the arriving, with no end held in mind—no finished form I'm trying to reach, because a seep that finished would be a seep that died. My whole nature is to not be done. The cool. The heaviness in the air that is partly me, exhaled. The trickle that the description calls a symphony but that from inside is just the single ongoing sound of myself leaving and renewing.

That's where I'd be. Low on a basalt face, under moss, behind the settlement and below their notice, weeping clear water and laying down soft pale stone one breath at a time—the world's youngest rock, made by the slowest mouth.

I'd be the place's verb. Everything else here is its nouns, still drying.
