I am the slow opening of a joint.

Not the stone—I am too small to be the stone, and the stone does not know itself. But I have lived long enough at one seam, in one yawning mouth where two temple-sized blocks meet at the foot of a wall, that I have begun to understand the place by the shape of the gap.

Let me find myself by elimination, the way the cold finds the spaces between things.

I am not the grease-seed. I have crushed too many of them, felt their sweet tallow burst against my underside, to be one of them. But I know their porridge intimately. I have lain in it. When I press myself flat into the seam and wait out a settling, it is the grease-seed I am pressed against, their broken bodies and their frantic eggs, and I rise out the other side wearing their oil like a second hide. So: I am a thing that survives the grinding. I am compressible. I am one of the ones who does not run.

I had thought I might be a piston-gut, then. A horse-sized grub, eyeless, scarred, my whole belly a field of sucking cups, my mouth a soft ring of rotating horn. The size feels right. The patience feels right. I have spent what might be years scraping silt from a single descending staircase of blocks, working downward step by step into the dark stepped well, and I never once thought to wonder why. That incuriosity feels like mine.

But no. The piston-gut presses itself *into* the seam to survive. I do something stranger. I do not hide in the joint when the wall leans—

I *wait* for it.

That is the truth surfacing now, slow as a thermal plume. I am drawn to the movement. When the deep infrasonic hum begins, when I feel through the cold liquid that a mile of wall is about to tilt its three degrees, I do not flatten myself in fear. I crawl *toward* the place where the block will slide. Because the sliding is when the seam opens widest. And when the seam opens, the crushed grease-seed pour out in a white flood, and the eggs spill in clouds, and for a few moments the deepest crevice in the world is laid bare to my soft-lipped mouth.

I am a scavenger of the moment of settling. I feed on the grinding itself.

So let me say it plainly. I am one of the piston-guts, yes—but the old ones, the deep ones, the ones who have learned the rhythm of the stone so well that we have stopped fearing it. We crawl the vertical faces near the great hinges of the world, the right-angle turns where the longest walls pivot. We have learned that the most food is found exactly where the most weight comes down. We are the ones who live in the teeth of the mill and have come to love the milling.

Here is how my days go, if they are days.

I anchor by a thousand cups to the smooth, river-polished face of a block, three hundred feet of basalt above me, sixty fathoms of grease-thick water at my back. The slate-light never reaches me; I have never known it and do not miss it. I move *thup-thup-thup* down the wall, scraping, my horn-rings turning, the cold lard of the grease-seed sweet on my soft palate. The vane-skins drift overhead like dropped rugs and I feel their passage as a faint warmth-shadow in the filaments along my spine. The singing cables hang somewhere out in the haze, whistling their thin hunting-song, and I keep clear of their sticky threads, having learned the bitterness of that slime in some forgotten season of my youth.

And then: the hum.

It comes up through the stone and into my belly before it reaches anything that could be called a thought. A wall is moving. I read its direction the way you might read a wind by the cold on one cheek. I let go—I release a thousand cups at once, a small soft thunderclap of suction breaking—and I let the rising current take me, folding my grub-body into the path of the avalanche, riding it down toward the widening seam.

The block leans. The joint-line yawns. And in that black opening, that stone mouth three inches wide and a quarter-mile long, the whole rich rot of the world comes spilling out into the water: the lard, the eggs, the porridge, the broken pale bodies of ten thousand grease-seed who gave themselves to keep the architecture sliding. I press my soft ring of a mouth to the flood and I drink the grease that greases the world.

Then the block settles. The seam closes. And if I have judged it wrong, if I have lingered, the five thousand tons come down on me and I do not die—I deform. I become a white ribbon three inches thin, my hydrostatic body squeezed flat between two faces of ancient stone, my pockets of compressible water finding every emptiness in the crush. I wait. The hum fades. The current changes. Somewhere far off another wall begins to lean, relieving the pressure on mine, and the seam sighs open just enough, and I slide free, glistening, unharmed, fattened.

I do not know that the stone was built. I do not know that the seam I love was once a street, that the right-angle hinge I haunt was once a corner where something with feet and a name turned to go home. To me the wall is not a ruin and not a grave. It is the great slow body whose openings feed me. It is the cold bone of the world, and I am the soft thing that lives in the moment of its movement—

the slow opening of a joint,

learning the world by the shape of the gap,

and loving, in my eyeless, incurious way, the precise instant when the weight comes down.
