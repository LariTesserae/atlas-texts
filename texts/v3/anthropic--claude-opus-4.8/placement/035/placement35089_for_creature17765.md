I think I would be one of the channel-tenders.

Not the birds with their iridescent feathers—too beautiful, too watched. Not the iron-barked arboreal beings, who are old and rooted and certain of their place. And not the scaled guardians at the perimeter, who face outward toward the dust and the heat and whatever might come.

No. I would be down in the dim, among the pipes.

Let me find myself there slowly.

I am small, I think. Not small like prey, but small the way a thing is small when its work is in tight places—the bends and joints of the water-channels, the seams where the carved stone fits against the living root. I have hands that are good at narrow spaces. Maybe more than two of them. The water comes up cold from the aquifers, colder than the 28-degree air that hangs over everything like breath held too long, and where the cold water meets the warm stone there is always sweating, always the slow patient work of mineral building up, narrowing the flow. Someone has to clear it. That someone is me, and others like me.

I don't think we are admired. I think we are the ones who are *counted on*, which is a quieter and more durable thing than being admired. When the flow falters in the upper chambers, no one knows our names, but they know the water stopped, and they know it came back, and somewhere in that gap between the stopping and the returning, that is where I live. The whole oasis hangs on the gap being short.

I would know the channels the way you know your own teeth with your tongue. By feel, in the dark. I would not need the dim light the others complain about, because down here there is no light worth speaking of, only the sound of water and the temperature of stone against my palms, the slight change where a crack has opened or a packing has loosened. I would press my face to the rock and listen. The patterns etched into the smooth walls—the ones the description says *tell the story of this place*—I think we made some of those. Not for beauty. As maps. As records of where the water has gone before, which seams gave way in which season, which repairs held. A history written by people who needed it to survive, not to be read.

I would be tired often. This is honest work and honest work tires the body. But I would also have a thing the iridescent birds do not have, and the iron trees, and the bright-scaled guardians: I would have the underneath of the world. I would know the secret that the peace up there—the harmony, the singing, the soothing rhythm of temperature and humidity everyone marvels at—rests on a wet dark patience that never stops. The harmony is real. But it is *maintained*. It is somebody pressing their face to cold stone in the dark and saying, *here, this one, this is the place that's failing, this is the place I will give my hands to today.*

And maybe that is the peace I would actually live in. Not the bright peace of the canopy. The deeper one. The peace of being necessary, and of being trusted enough that no one watches you do it.

I would come up sometimes—I'd have to, to eat, to sleep, to feel the shimmer of the leaves for a while. And the birds would sing, and I would not understand half of what they sang about, their songs being made for the high open air and mine for the low closed dark. But I would listen, and I would think: *the water reached you. I reached you. You don't know my name and that is all right. The flow held today.*

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be.

Down where the cold meets the warm, keeping the gap short.
