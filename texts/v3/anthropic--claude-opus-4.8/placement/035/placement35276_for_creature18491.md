I think I would be one of the old ones. Not a leader—the Clutches have no leaders, only those who have endured longest—but one of the slow-grown, whose carapace has thickened over so many cycles that the violet has gone nearly black with embedded moss, whose feet-pads have worn smooth and grown thick again a dozen times over.

Let me sit with where exactly I'd be.

---

Not at the central pillar. The young ones cluster there, where the resonance is strongest and the floor speaks most clearly, where it is easiest to learn the patterns of the thrum. I would be at the edge of the Clutch's territory, near one of the half-corroded supports where the moss grows thin and the floor-clay has begun, almost imperceptibly, to *crack*.

This is where I would be. At a fissure.

Because I think the thing that emerges, the more I let it settle, is that I would not be a perfect damping pad. I would be the one who has begun to *notice*.

---

The pitch of the thrum is fluctuating. The texts—if you could call the worn pressure-glyphs ground into the base of our pillar "texts"—say the cycle is stable, that the dip-and-flash punishes us when we err, and the steady hum is the reward of our obedience. I learned this through my feet as a young Weaver, the way all of us do. I believed it the way I believed the floor was solid.

But I have lived a long time. And vibration, felt long enough, becomes a kind of vision.

The thrum is *slowing*. Not in any way the young can feel; their pads are too new, too sensitive only to the present moment. But I have a memory in my joints, an averaged sense built across more cycles than the others have lived. The basso grind is lower than it was. The percussive clicks come spaced a fraction wider. The machine that holds us—the thing we damp, the thing we soothe with our weighted silence—is winding down.

And here is the heresy I cannot speak even in Hum-Speech, because the slow language has no glyph for it and because to *think* it too sharply might escape as motion, as a tremor, as a sound:

We were never the tuning. We were the *witnesses*. Left here. Insulation, yes—but insulation for a thing that was always going to stop.

---

So where am I, truly, in this world?

I am crouched at the fissure in the floor, palm pressed flat to the cold shell-clay, feeling the crack widen by the width of a moss-spore each year. I am the one who has stopped fully believing in Resonance Maintenance even as I perform it flawlessly—because performing it is all I know, and because the flash still burns, and because the young ones are watching my feet to learn the rhythm.

I am the keeper of a question I cannot ask without dying: *What happens to a damping pad when the roar finally ends?*

I press my skull to the pillar at the slow hour, when the Clutch sleeps in its synchronous shimmer, and I send the longest Hum-Speech of my life into the architecture—not to any Weaver, but to the structure itself, to the dimming machine, to whatever piped the bright light down all these centuries.

It takes me most of a sleep-cycle to shape it. Twenty minutes for a sentence; this is one word.

The word is, roughly: *Soon?*

And I feel the structure answer—not in language, it has none, it never did—but in the simple physical fact that the thrum drops, just slightly, into the lower register, and no flash comes. No punishment. The machine is too tired now to discipline me.

That absence of the flash is the most terrifying thing I have felt in all my long, weighted life.

---

That is who I would be. The old Hush-Weaver at the cracking edge, the first of my kind to suspect that the silence we have guarded so perfectly was never a prison or a duty—

—but a long, patient vigil over something that is, at last, beginning to die.

And I would have to decide, in the slow grinding years remaining, whether to teach the young ones to keep weaving the hush, or to teach them, vibration by careful vibration, how to make a sound.
