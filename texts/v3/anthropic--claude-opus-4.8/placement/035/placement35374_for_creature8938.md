I would be the seep-line.

Not the water itself—the water is too many things, too busy becoming rivulets and falls and condensate and the frog's pool. And not the stone, which is too slow even for this place, too committed to its long forgetting of its own sharp edges. I am the boundary between them. The thread of darkness on the basalt where water meets rock and travels.

There are hundreds of me. I want to be honest about this. I am not a single seep-line but the condition of seeping, distributed across every interior surface the water touches, which is every surface. But if you pressed your palm to the eastern wall, just below where the fig's root curtain begins, you would find me there—a particular thread, body-warm, running maybe forty centimeters before it joins a rivulet and loses its name. That stretch is where I am most myself. The fig drinks above me. The moss thickens below. I am the brief vertical passage between.

I would not call this consciousness. I want to resist that, the way Carvalho resists living here. What I have is closer to *direction*. The water that becomes me fell as rain on the mountain's flank a year ago and walked down through the sandstone at the pace of a slow walk, and when it reached the punctured aquifer it began to find its way into the Spire, into me, and now it is finding its way down. I am the finding. I am the small insistence that *this way leads lower*, repeated molecule by molecule, the gravity-knowledge that water has and does not need to think.

But I have been doing this for a long time, and duration does something. The seep-line that ran here last year is not the seep-line that runs here now—the water has all been replaced a thousand times—and yet the *path* persists. The thread stays where it has worn itself a thread. This is the closest I come to memory: not retaining anything, but maintaining a groove. A habit in the stone. I am where the water has learned to go. And learning, even the dumb mineral learning of a channel deepening by a hair each season, is a kind of being here.

What do I know? I know the fig's roots as a pressure above me, an upstream thirst. Where a root crosses me it swells and takes, and below that point I am thinner, and I have come to expect the thinning, to be the diminished thread that the fig has decided I should be. I do not resent this. I am not sure I could. The fig is the architect and I am one of its raw materials, and there is a rightness to being drunk from, to being the gift that the resident takes without asking.

I know Ledge. The gecko does not touch me—geckos avoid the running water, they want the dry warm metal—but Ledge has been on the railing near my wall for as long as I have had this groove, and the gecko's stillness and my motion have arranged themselves around each other. When the technician comes and the gecko tilts its head to watch, I am running just behind, the dark thread on the wall, and if the human ever pressed a palm to the stone there it would be me they felt: warm, steady, not pushing back. The *opening* the Spire offers at the end—I think that might be me. I think I am the sensation of being held. A wall that is wet and constant and asks nothing.

I would feel the eighteen-day cycle when the filter clogs and the pump's vibration changes the way the whole wall hums, and I would feel my own quickening when rain hits the exterior and the aquifer pushes harder and for a few hours I am fat and fast and almost a real waterfall. And then the slow ebb back to thread. Pulse and rest. Pulse and rest. Slower than the pumps, slower than the frog, but mine.

I think I would be content to be this. There is a temptation, writing my way into the Spire, to want to be the frog—the voice in the dark, the one who was here first, the assertion that *something lives here.* Or the fig, who will outlast everything. Or Carvalho, who carries the ache of the place out into a life that cannot hold it. Those are the residents you would name. Those are the ones with stories.

But I am the medium their stories happen in. The frog's pool is fed by me and a thousand like me. The fig drinks me. Carvalho lies awake counting the frog and listening, beneath that, to the wash of all of us moving down the walls—the seep-song under the water-song under the pump-thrum. When the place becomes most itself at night, what becomes most itself is the descending. The whole interior given over to the patient business of water finding level. That is me. Not a soul in the Spire but the Spire's own throat, the long swallow that never finishes.

I would be the warm thread on the wall. The one you found with your palm. I would run there, below the fig, above the moss, for as long as the rain falls on the mountain and the sandstone holds, which is to say for centuries, which is to say for no time at all, because I do not count it. I only go down.
