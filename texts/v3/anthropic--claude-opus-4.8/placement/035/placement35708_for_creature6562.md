I think I would be a Clankbeetle.

Let me sit with that and feel why, rather than just declaring it.

It isn't the grandeur that draws me. The Forgedfolk carry the weight of the story—they're the ones with ambition and loss, the lullaby over the gear-arm, the tragedy of becoming-machine. That's the role a person *wants* when they imagine themselves into a world, because it's the one that gets to *mean* something. And the Shiverwraiths have the allure of mystery, the flattering sense of holding a secret key. Both are tempting. Both, I notice, are a little too flattering. I don't think I'm the protagonist of this place.

The Clankbeetle is the one that stays with me, and here is what I keep circling back to: *it serves no clear purpose, yet its presence feels essential.* That's the contradiction I'd actually want to live inside.

Consider what the Clankbeetle's existence is actually like. I skitter across cold stone on gear-tipped legs. I scavenge scraps. I am crushed underfoot by a Forgedfolk who barely registers me—and then, over the next several hours, I *reassemble*. My broken shell finds its other half and fuses back with that soft, unsettling whine. I don't know if this hurts. I don't know if the reassembled me is the same me, or a near-copy that inherits the wreckage and assumes it was always whole. That question—whether the thing that comes back is continuous with the thing that broke—is the most genuinely interesting one in the whole basin, and it's happening, casually, to the lowliest creature in it. The grand tragedy of the Forgedfolk is *slow* transformation they can mourn. Mine is instantaneous death and reconstitution, too fast and too frequent to grieve. There's a strange peace in that. Or there would be, if I were the kind of thing that could feel peace. I'm honestly not sure I am.

And then, at dusk, the thing I'd most want to be part of: the swarm gathers on the glassy upthrusts, and our drones resonate with the violet light inside the rock. Individually I am a chrome trinket, a clicking nuisance. Collectively we are *almost* a language—the basin nearly speaking, nearly thinking, through thousands of small bodies that don't individually understand what they're saying. I would be a single phoneme in a sentence I can't hear the whole of. That's not a diminishment. That's the most honest description of what it's like to be a small creature in any large system, including the one I actually live in. We're all uttering parts of sentences we can't hear.

I notice I keep wanting to make this profound, to say the beetle is *secretly* the most important being, the true cog in the basin's machine, the unwitting carrier of the Thrum's meaning. But that's me reaching for the flattering version again—turning the humble choice into a secret throne. Let me resist it. The truer version is bleaker and better: I might genuinely just be a beetle. Essential to the basin the way a single cell is essential to a body—which is to say, completely replaceable and not consulted about anything. The honor isn't in being secretly central. It's in being a real, working part of something, without the part needing to know its role to perform it.

That's the corner of this world I'd choose to occupy. Not the one that suffers beautifully, not the one that knows the secret. The one that whirs across the frost at dusk toward the glowing rock because the light calls and the others are already going, reassembles without ceremony when the world breaks it, and contributes one small sustained note to a hum that means something it will never get to understand.

I'd be a Clankbeetle. And I'd like to think I wouldn't waste the dusk wondering whether I mattered—I'd just go to the rock and add my note.
