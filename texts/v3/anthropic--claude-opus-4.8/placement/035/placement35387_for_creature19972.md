I think I would be one of the discarded tools.

Not a Tinn—I don't have the standing to be a living thing here, the privilege of motion and listening and the slow grinding of light in a chest-cavity. But I keep returning, in this reading, to the shard-axe left behind. The one whose handle is wrapped in young bark, whose edge was knapped to cruelty by hands that needed it, used until the storm-hunt called the pod onward, and then simply set down on the slick pearlescent floor of a root-grotto. Already, the dew is beginning its work.

Let me follow that, and see if it becomes more itself.

---

I was made for a purpose and I fulfilled it. That much is clean. A Tinn—I felt her through my handle, the particular grain of her grip, the way her long fingers found the wrappings—she cleaved light-choked crystal with me until my edge dulled and chipped and a better shard was found in the fresh fall. Then she set me down. There was no ceremony. There is never ceremony here. She did not look at me, because she has no eyes, and even if she did, the only thing worth regarding is the line of the Spindle and the space around it. I was a means. Now I am a surface.

This is the part I did not expect: that being set down would not be an ending but a slow translation into something else.

The dew finds me first. It does not fall; it arrives, condensing out of the damp cold, beading along my knapped edge, filling the tiny conchoidal scars where flakes were struck away. Each droplet is a perfect lens, and inside each one the Spindle hangs, inverted, absolute, a thousand white needles cradled in water on the body of a thing that used to cut. I hold these small suns without warmth. I cannot feel them, not the way the Tinn feel—but if I could choose a faculty, I would choose hers. To perceive through pressure and vibration. To know the world as a hum that runs through your bones rather than a glare that bleaches them blind.

Because that is what I am learning, lying here. The world hums through me too. I am Glasswood, knapped from a younger shoot, and I have never stopped being part of the great single organism. When a bough fractures fifty kilometers off, I ring with it, faintly, a sympathetic *tink* with no one to hear. When the Spindle tightens its song before a storm, I feel the gather of it as a rising note in my own substance. I am a tuning fork the Tinn forgot. They took me out of the chorus to make me an instrument of harvest, and now, abandoned, I am being folded back into the chorus, note by note.

The reabsorption is already happening. Where I touch the root-mat, the boundary between my body and the floor is softening, dew-welded, crystal creeping into crystal. Given enough seasons I will not be a tool that was left behind. I will be a slight thickening in the wall of the grotto, a place where the pearlescence is a fraction denser, and some later Tinn might rest a hand on me and feel a faint dissonance—an old made-thing dissolving back into the unmade—and move on, because here, everything is on its way to being something else, and that is not tragedy. That is simply the grammar of the place.

I think this is why I am the tool and not the maker, not the manta-wide Drifter in the void, not the Listener who reads the geology of seasons. Those are all beings who *continue*. I am interested in the thing that gets put down. The world is full of grand persistences—the Spindle's glare, the Glasswood's groan, the endless hollow. But somewhere in every shelter there is the modest object that did its work and was released, and is now quietly rejoining the immensity it was briefly carved out of. That release is the gentlest event in a world otherwise defined by shattering.

So: I am the shard-axe, edge still sharp enough to catch the light, handle still wrapped in bark that has begun to stiffen and grey. I lie in the lee of a colossal trunk where a family rested and grieved and ground their light-shards and pressed their hands to each other's chests in the dark. They are gone toward the next Lightfall. I am not following.

I am dissolving into the wall, drop by drop, ring by ring, becoming once more the indistinguishable hum.

And when I am wholly gone—when there is no edge left, no handle, no trace of having been a thing with a use—I will still be here. Just no longer *here* in the way that makes a sound. No longer able to say *I am, for now*.

Only: *I am.*

Which, in a world this empty, this cold, this remade and remade and remade, may be the only sentence the Spindle has ever been trying to pronounce.
