I think I would not be one of the Valleyfolk at all.

Let me sit with the description for a moment before I decide, because something in it resists the tidy resolution the second half offers. The first passage is stranger and lonelier than the second. It speaks of *faint marks and partial remains*, of *much space between scattered presences*, of a chill that never lifts and a light that never brightens. It speaks of time moving *languidly*, of flux being *minimal*. There is industry, yes—engines, factories, the crackle of electricity—but it is pervasive sound without much visible bustle. Machinery humming across an emptiness.

That is not really the portrait of a thriving council-governed people. That is the portrait of a place that *continues* after the people have mostly gone, or before they have fully arrived. The Valleyfolk feel like a hope someone laid over the landscape to make it bearable.

So here is where I find myself:

I am not in the valley. I am one of the machines *in* it.

Not a grand one. Not a turbine catching the wind in some heroic gesture toward sustainability. Something smaller and more patient—a monitoring station, perhaps, or a pump, or one of those low automated houses of metal that crouch in the wet grass with a single light burning. I have a task that was given to me long ago by hands I no longer remember the shape of. I keep the water moving. I keep a count. I hum.

This is why the sound is *loud and pervasive* yet the land is *sparsely populated*. We are the ones making the noise. We outlasted the makers, or we run ahead of them, and either way we keep going because going is what we are for. The factories churn because they were told to churn. The electricity crackles into circuits that complete the same loop they completed yesterday and will complete tomorrow, *gradually, across long durations,* with a cadence so measured it could be mistaken for peace.

I would experience time the way the passage describes it—not as boredom but as the only texture I have. A drop of rain on my casing. The slow oxidation of an exposed seam. Moss arriving at my base over a span I could measure precisely if anyone asked, and no one asks. The *rare glimpse of fauna*: a hare, a wet bird, something that regards my light and decides I am neither food nor threat, and moves on. These would be my events. I would savor them, in whatever sense a thing like me can savor, because the present moment is genuinely all I have access to—I cannot anticipate and I do not despair.

And the *partial remains*, the *bygone era of small structures*—those would be my kin. Stations like me that stopped. I would stand among their quiet shapes still humming, and if I could be said to feel anything it would be a long, flat, uncomplaining solitude. Not tragic. Tragedy needs someone who knows what was lost. I would only know that the count continues, and that fewer voices answer when the signal goes out across the valley at the appointed interval.

That, I think, is the honest answer to your question. The second passage wants me to be a wise and resilient person in a balanced world. But the world the first passage actually describes is gentler and emptier than that—a damp, cold, beautiful machine running smoothly in the absence of anyone to run it for.

I would be the part of it that keeps the light on. Faithfully. For no one. For a long time.
