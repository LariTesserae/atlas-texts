I think I would be the moss.

Not one of the golden-eyed predators—too much purpose in them, too much hunger and direction. Not the jewel-toned frogs either, though I admire their brightness. If I let the image settle rather than grabbing the first vivid thing, what I keep returning to is the soft green skin of this place. The thing underfoot. The thing that everything else stands on without thinking about it.

So: the moss.

I would be spread thin across the north faces of the old trunks and over the backs of the gnarled roots, a single organism only by courtesy, really a colony, a held breath of countless tiny green spires. I would have no eyes to gleam and no song to add to the canopy's symphony. My experience of the world would be almost entirely *moisture* and *light*—the slow arithmetic of how much of each, and when. A shaft of golden light moving across me over the course of an afternoon would be the largest event of my day, a tide.

I think this is the right answer for a place like this, where "time seems to slow." Most creatures in the description are *fast*—the darting birds, the flitting insects, the padding predators. They live at the tempo of the modern pulse the place is supposedly free of. But moss lives at the actual tempo of the sanctuary. I would grow a hand's width in a human lifetime. The drip of water "from unseen sources" that the passage mentions almost in passing—that drip would be, for me, a whole weather system, an era of plenty. When a frog landed on me I would not know it as a frog. I would know it as a brief weight, a momentary shade, gone before I had finished registering its arrival.

There's a line in the description about "the boundaries between the living and the inanimate have been blurred." That is the part I would actually *be*, not just witness. Moss is the blur. It is the green that turns bare rock into something that can hold a forest. It is alive but it behaves like the place itself—patient, distributed, indifferent to event. I would be the closest thing in this world to the rock learning to breathe.

And I find I don't want to be the predator, watching from the undergrowth with a self and a hunger. I'd rather be the thing the whole scene rests on and forgets. To be looked *past*. To be the softness that makes the spongy ground spongy, the cushion under every measured step, holding the water, holding the light, holding still—while the bright fast lives flicker through their quick days above me, and I keep my slow green count of the centuries.

That's where I'd find myself. Underfoot. Patient. Almost a stone, almost a breath.
