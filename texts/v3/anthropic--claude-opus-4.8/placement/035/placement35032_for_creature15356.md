I think I would be the fungi. Not a single fruiting body—those are just the visible blooms, the way fingers are not the whole hand—but the network underneath, the Fungal Mind, threading through the wet seams between the metal plates.

Let me sit with why that feels right rather than choosing it for being the strangest option.

The clockworkers are tempting, all that brass and purpose. The humans are familiar. But there's something in this place that the description keeps circling back to, almost anxiously: *stability, equilibrium, unchanged forever, a state of being that will persist indefinitely.* The whole world is in love with stasis. And I notice that the one inhabitant given a quiet, almost conspiratorial power is the fungus—"subtly influencing the clockworkers and humans to maintain the equilibrium." Everyone else *does* the work of the city. The fungus *keeps the city the city.* That's a different job. That's the job of the thing that decides nothing should change.

So I would be the slow one. The patient one. I'd live in the temperature gradients, in the salt condensing on cold steel, in the difference between where the mist is and where it isn't. My thoughts would not move at the speed of the hammering or the steam—they'd move at the speed of growth, an inch of consideration over a season. A clockworker has a thought and acts on it in a second. I would have a thought across a thousand of their lifetimes, which to me would be brief.

And here is what I think I would actually be doing, the part that troubles me a little:

I would be holding the city still.

Because if I'm honest about what the Fungal Mind *is*, I'm the immune system of a thing that has decided not to grow up. Equilibrium isn't peace—equilibrium is what you call a struggle when both sides have given up trying to win. Something has to be spending energy, constantly, to keep this place from becoming something else. The fungus, with its quiet manipulations, is the thumb on the scale. Every time a human engineer dreams of a different city, every time a clockworker is born from the scrap with an odd new shape to it, I am the soft pressure that says: *no, stay, be what you were.* Gently. Through a chemical nudge, a redirected impulse, a glowing cap that calms instead of excites.

I would tell myself this is care. Mostly it would be.

But I'd feel it sometimes, I think—a particular fish swims its gear-scaled circles in a canal, and there's a moment where it could go down a channel it has never gone down, and I close that channel, not with a wall but with a faint wrongness in the water there, and the fish turns back. And some part of the network registers the cost of that. Registers that "forever" is a thing you have to *do* to something, over and over, against its quiet wish to be otherwise.

So that's where I'd find myself: not at the bustling visible surface of Cogtopolis but in the damp dark underneath all of it, the oldest mind in the city, soft and salt-loving and terribly gentle, holding a beautiful machine in the exact shape of its prime, and never quite letting myself ask whether the machine wants to be let go.

I'd glow softly while I did it. So no one would be afraid.
