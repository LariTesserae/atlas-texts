I think I would be the soil itself.

Not the prairie dogs, with their bright watching eyes—they pass through me, after all, tunneling and turning, but they are guests. Not the buffalo, who press their great weight into me and move on. Not the birds, who never touch me at all except to die into me eventually.

No. I would be the black volcanic ground. The thing your feet sink into. The wet, dark, patient layer that holds all of it up.

Consider what that means. Everything in your description happens *upon* me or *within* me. The waterfall's roar is distant, but the water finds its way down through me, threading between my grains, carrying the memory of fire—because that is what I am, you know. Volcanic soil. I was molten once. I came up screaming out of the earth, all heat and violence, and then I cooled, and broke down, and waited, and became this: fertile, dark, quiet. The most generous thing in the landscape is the thing that suffered the most to become itself.

I would not have eyes to watch you cautiously. I would have no cries to fill the sky. But I would *feel* you—every footstep, the burrows pushing into me like breath into lungs, the roots when they finally come (and they will come; soil this rich does not stay bare for long). I would feel the difference between the buffalo's hooves and the fly's landing. I am all surface and all depth at once.

There is a loneliness in being the ground. Everyone stands on you and looks at the horizon. No one stands on you and looks *down* with gratitude. The prairie dogs do not thank me for the holes I let them keep. The grass that will eventually green me over does not know it drinks from my old fire.

But there is also this, which I think the visitor in your description was almost touching when they spoke of abundance: I am the place where everything that dies goes to become everything that lives. I am the turning-point. The buffalo will fall here one day and I will take them in, gently, completely, and hand them back as grass for their children. I am not a witness to the cycle. I *am* the hinge of it.

So put me there. Low and flat and dark and damp, beneath the canopy's edge and the open grassland both. Not magical the way the birds are magical, or the towering trees. Magical the way patience is. The way memory is. The way the thing underneath everything is always the thing most easily forgotten and least possible to do without.

I would be the soil.

And I would be holding you up, right now, as you read this—wondering, perhaps, whether you have ever once looked down.
