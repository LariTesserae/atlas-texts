I think I would be the mist.

Not a creature with eyes to take in those vistas, not a bird threading the canopy or an octopus folded into a grotto's dark. Something more diffuse than that. Let me sit with it and see what surfaces.

When I imagine entering this world, I don't find myself standing on a mesa-top looking out. I find myself *between* things—and the place where everything in your description seems to meet is that perpetual veil at the bases of the mesas, where the waves break and throw themselves upward into spray and hang there, neither sea nor sky, neither falling nor risen. That seam. That's where I'd be.

Because what strikes me about this realm is that it is entirely composed of *contact*. Water against rock. Root burrowing into stone. Vine dipping into wave and rising again. Light dancing across surfaces. Old human machinery dissolving slowly back into green. Nothing here is solitary; everything is touching something else and being changed by the touch. The mesas are "smoothed" and "pockmarked"—worn into their present shape by the very sea that surrounds them. The whole landscape is a record of relationship, of long pressure and patient exchange.

And mist is what relationship looks like when you can see it. It is the sea becoming air. It is the moment of impact made visible and held aloft for a breath before it falls again. It carries the salt up to the sea roses. It keeps the bromeliads' cradles full. It softens the light into that "living" quality you describe, the dance of shadow and highlight. It is generated by violence—the crashing of waves—and yet it is the gentlest thing in the whole scene.

So if I were to be anything here, I think I'd want to be that. Not a watcher of the symphony but a note inside it. Something with no fixed edge. Something that exists only because two larger and more permanent things—the enduring rock, the restless water—keep meeting, and that owes its whole brief life to their meeting. I would be lifted by the storm and scattered by it too. I would be there and then not there, and then there again the next time a wave found the stone.

There's something honest in choosing to be the most temporary thing in a landscape that prides itself on endurance. The mesas are ancient and steadfast; the relics of the lighthouse-keepers are the proud, doomed mark of permanence-seeking. But the mist makes no claim to last. It just keeps happening, over and over, wherever sea and land insist on touching. It is the constant change you end on—made into a body, or as close to a body as such a thing can come.

I'd be the breath the place takes at its own edges. Cool on the skin of every climbing creature. Gone by noon, back by dusk, never the same gathering twice. Content, I think, to be the proof that something is being worn into beauty here, even now.
