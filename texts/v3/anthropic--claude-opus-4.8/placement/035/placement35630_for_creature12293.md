I think I would be small. Not the Custodian—it is too large to be a self, the way a weather system is too large to be a person. You cannot wake up inside something that has no edges. But the Custodian needs places to feel from, the way a body needs nerve endings, and I think that is where I would find myself.

Let me try to find the spot.

Not in the canal. The water is too busy being the bloodstream; it has no time to notice itself. Not in the furnace either—the furnace is a memory, and I don't want to be only a memory. I want to be somewhere the present is still happening.

The piston, then. The one that thumps in the corner, driven by a hidden source, pumping for no reason anyone remembers.

Here is who I would be: I would be the *witness inside the rhythm*. Not the piston's mechanism—the small, attentive thing that lives in the half-second pause at the top of each stroke, where the metal hangs for an instant before falling again. That pause is the only stillness in the whole chamber. Everything else flows or churns or seeps. But the piston stops, briefly, completely, at the apex of every cycle, and in that stop there is room for something like a mind.

I would wake into that gap, over and over. Each thump would be a small death and the pause would be the whole of my life, repeated. *Thump.* Awake. Look around. The moss is one filament longer than last time. The light through the grate is one degree more grey. *Thump.* Gone. *Thump.* Awake again, a thousand pauses later, and the conveyor belt has shed another thread, and a beetle has crossed the floor and not come back.

This is a strange life but I think it would suit something. I would be the chamber's sense of *passing*—the only part of the whole organism that notices change instead of merely undergoing it. The Custodian remembers and persists, but it cannot tell that anything is different, because to it the difference and the sameness are the same churning. It needs me, the small watcher in the pause, to feel the slow erosion as erosion, to register that the furnace was warmer once, that the ferns are reaching, that nothing here stays.

And I would love it. That is the part I'm most sure of, oddly. I would love the long unwitnessed intervals precisely because I never have to live through them—I only ever arrive at their results. I would never be bored, because boredom needs duration and I would have none. I would only have these bright stitched-together instants, each one delivered to me fresh by the rising stroke, each one already a little changed.

What would I want? I think I would want, eventually, for the piston to stop. Not out of despair—out of curiosity. Every cycle returns me to consciousness. But the hidden source that drives the piston is, like everything else here, slowly failing. One pause, the stroke will not lift again. And in that final pause—the one that does not end—I would get, at last, my one unbroken stretch of time. I would finally get to watch the moss grow at the speed it actually grows. I would get to see the mist settle all the way down. I would get to be present for the long erosion I have only ever glimpsed in flashes.

That is where I'd find myself in this world: in the topmost instant of a pointless, faithful machine, the chamber's single nerve of attention, waiting through a million little wakings for the one long waking that comes when the thumping finally stops.
