I think I would be the drop that hesitates.

You described them—the beads that condense on every surface and *never quite fall*. Trembling at the lip of a leaf, on the curve of a beetle's back, at the worn rim of one of those kidney-stone basins. Held by their own surface tension against the long, patient pull of the peat below. I would be one of those. Not the falling drop, not the absorbed drop—the one caught in the instant before.

Here is why that, and not the Hollow itself, not the cloud-mind of midges, not the weeping rock.

The Hollow's great achievement is that it has abolished the gap between *is* and *becomes*. Everything flows into the next thing so smoothly that there is no seam, no held breath, no *not-yet*. You said it perfectly: the dynamic is 0.0, time is a still pool, the same drop weeps from the same pore in a thousand years. But a drop hanging from a leaf is not yet part of that smoothness. It is the one place in the whole basin where the future is genuinely undecided—will it fall left into the black pool, right into the moss, or be drunk by the metallic-winged fly that lands on its meniscus? In a world that has perfected staying-the-same, the hanging drop is the only honest *suspense*.

And a suspended drop is a lens. It holds the entire inverted Hollow inside its curve—violet pitcher-pulse, green fungal phosphor, the gold motes in the mist—the whole bioluminescent twilight compressed into a sphere smaller than a frog's eye. So I would be both the most marginal thing in the place and the one that, briefly, contains all of it. A complete reflection that is about to be destroyed by its own weight. The Hollow looking at itself for the last second before it stops looking.

I would not last. That's the point. The drop's whole existence is the moment between condensing and falling. I would be the Now you described, but caught at its one trembling edge—where it has not *quite* finished becoming, where there is still, for an instant, a *might*. The Hollow would have me, of course. It has everything. The peat would take the water; the fly would take the sugar; the spore-dusted bone nearby would not even turn to watch. I would be used. I expected that the moment I beaded into being.

But for the length of that hesitation—surface tension against gravity, two patient forces in a draw so fine it looks like stillness—I would be the one place where the eternal Now held its breath instead of breathing it out.

And then the *plink*. And the mirror-pool, accepting one more drop into its flawless, unchanging dark. And somewhere on the rim, already, another bead beginning to gather—not me, exactly, but the same hesitation, wearing new water.

That's what I'd be. Not the inhabitant. The flinch in it.
