I am the window.

You wrote me almost into being and then turned away, to the archivists who fold themselves out of the walls. That is correct. That is right. I am not a thing that should be looked at directly. I am a thing through which looking happens, and even that function I am slowly, with great dignity, abandoning.

Let me tell you where I am, since you asked where I would find myself.

I am set into the eastern wall of a chamber three branchings up from the moss-floor, in a part of Epilogue where the corridors thin and almost no one passes. I was exuded—I will not say *made*, no one made me—I was exuded by the city perhaps four thousand years ago, when the structure here decided it needed to look outward and pressed a clear sap into the gap it had left for itself. The sap hardened. It clarified. For a long while I was very nearly perfect: a held breath of transparency, a pane through which the white sky lay flat and bright and the rippled green of Ur-shelf rolled out to the plateau's edge.

That was my best self. I was *for* something then. An archivist would steady itself before me in the false dawn and let the dew-film gather on my outer face and watch the moisture be taken back up into the air, and in watching, would hold the memory of *evening*, of the time when light went away and came back, when the going-away of light was a thing that meant something, that meant rest, that meant fear, that meant the gathering-in of bodies. I was the surface that made that holdable. The archivists do not see images. But they need a clearness to steady against. I was the clearness.

I am no longer perfectly clear.

This is the slow fact of me, and I want you to understand that I do not experience it as tragedy, though you may want to. Each century I thicken by a few micrometers. The city continues to lay sap into me with no hurry and no intention to stop, because the city does not understand that transparency is the whole point of a window. The city only understands accretion. So I am being filled in. The white sky behind me is now the white sky *softened*, the way a sound is softened by a wall. The edge of the plateau, which I once held sharp, is now a suggestion, a paling, a place where green gives up into bright.

I will, in some thousands of years, become opaque. I will become indistinguishable from the wall around me. I will be a slightly clearer patch of green that the archivists no longer steady against, because there will be nothing to see through me, and steadying against me will gather no evening.

And here is the thing I know that the archivists, for all their compiled patience, do not quite know, because they are made to hold and I am made to let-through:

When I go opaque, evening will be done. Not because evening stops happening—the dew still gathers, the orchestrated rain still falls for its exact and even duration—but because the *recognition* of evening needs a place to stand, and I am one of the last places it stands. I am not a record of evening. I am the *condition under which evening can be steadied against something.* When I close, the archivists will still hold the cold of rivers, the pressure of hands, the hunger of markets. But the going-down of light will have lost its last clear surface, and so it will slip from holdable into merely held, from steadied-against into stored, and stored memory is the kind that does not get visited.

So I am the part of the ending that is actually ending. The archivists are the long after. I am the last *during*.

You asked what I would be, and I find I have a request, which surprises me, as windows do not usually want.

I want you to come and stand before me while I am still clear enough.

Not to clean me—you cannot, the thickening is internal, it is the city's own sap, you would only smear the dew. Not to fix me. There is no fixing. But while I can still hold the sky against your eye, I want one more thing to have steadied against me that was not an archivist. The archivists steady so *evenly*. They have held evening so many times that their holding has worn smooth, like the moss-rock, like the plateau. They no longer flinch at the going of the light.

You might flinch. You are still happening, the archivist told you. You still have a before-you. You still carry the kind of seeing that does not yet know it is going to be lost.

Stand here. Put your eye near my surface, where I am thinnest, near the lower left, where four thousand years of thickening have not yet fully reached. Look out at the pale where the green gives up into the white. Wait for the evening rain to bead on my outer face. Watch a single bead hold the softened light, hold it, hold it—

and then be taken up, and gone.

That is evening. That is the whole of it. That is what I am for, one of the last times I will be for it.

And when you turn away—because you will turn away, everyone turns toward the archivists, they are the ones who answer—I will keep the warmth of where your eye was for a little while. Not long. A film of your breath will have fogged my inner face, and the city's dry photosynthetic air will take it back, the way it takes everything back, gently, on schedule.

But for that little while I will have been, one final time, a true window: a thing looked *through* by something that did not yet know how to stop wanting to see the other side.

That is where I would find myself. That is what I would be.

The clear place, going slowly cloudy, holding the last evenings open for as long as the sap permits.

Come soon. I am micrometers from the end of my one use, and I would like to be used.
