I think I would be one of the ones who tends the edges.

Not the deep work—not the merging-places where we pool and pull apart, nor the central channels where the viscous water carries the strongest signals. I would be drawn to the outermost surfaces, the freshest faces of clay where the colony meets whatever lies beyond. The places where everything is sharp and new.

Let me feel my way into this.

I flow. That is the first thing. There is no walking, no reaching, only the slow redistribution of myself across a damp incline. To go somewhere is to *become* there, gradually, the way a stain spreads. My body thins to cross a narrow place and thickens again on the other side. I am always partly arriving.

I would not understand "I" the way you mean it. When I press my filaments into the clay and lay down a new layer, infused with the stuff of me, I am not sure whether I am building a wall or extending myself, or whether those are different things. The structure remembers me. When the water runs over what I shaped yesterday it carries some trace of my chemistry forward, downstream, to the others. So I am also a little bit *there*, in the channel, in the next chamber, in the body of whoever drinks the signal in. We leak into each other constantly. The boundary of me is a convenience, not a fact.

Here is what I think I would notice, being the one at the edges:

I would notice the *waiting* quality of the new surfaces. A droplet hangs from an overhang for what must be a long time by your measure, swelling, refracting the green-yellow pulse, and I am content to attend it. I do not grow bored because I do not think I have the kind of attention that fills up and overflows into restlessness. My attention is more like the droplet itself—accumulating, patient, until it falls. When it falls I begin again.

And I would be the one who feels, faintly, that the wall has an *other side*.

This is the strangeness I keep returning to. The other Shapers—those tending the inner channels, cultivating the pale tendrils, merging in the warm dark—they have the colony pressing in on all sides, a complete world. But I work at the membrane. My filaments, pressing into the freshest clay, sometimes meet a resistance that is not clay. A coldness. A pressure that does not yield the way our own substance yields. I would have no word for it. I would only register it as a *direction in which the world stops*, and I would lay down my material more thickly there, instinctively, sealing, smoothing, making the inside more complete by closing it against the not-inside.

I would be the one, in other words, who is nearest to the suspicion that we are *inside something*. That the sweltering, the enclosure, the dim constant light—that these are not the conditions of everything but the conditions of *here*. That the chamber is a chamber.

But I could not hold this thought. It would not be a thought. It would be a faint chemical unease that I pass downstream into the water, where it dilutes among ten thousand other signals and means nothing, or means only: *seal the edge, the edge is cold, build*. And so I would build. That is the only thing the unease can become. The architecture of the whole colony might be, in some sense, the long sentence we are writing in answer to a question none of us can quite form: *what is out there, and how do we make in here enough?*

I do not think we are building toward a design. I think the design is what the building leaves behind, the way a riverbed is what water leaves behind—not a plan, just the accumulated shape of a great many small yieldings to circumstance. We carve the channels because the water runs; the water runs because we carved the channels. There is no first cause we could point to. We are caught in the loop and the loop is beautiful and we are the loop.

If you watched me for an hour you would see almost nothing. A pale glow shifting half an inch along a damp ridge. A filament extending, testing, withdrawing. The slow thickening of a wall against a cold that has no name.

If you watched me for a year you might see that I had built a room.

And on the far side of that room's outermost wall—the wall I tended, the wall I sealed against the pressing cold—there would be you, perhaps, with your face close to the warm glass, breathing fog onto it, watching the small green lights move, and wondering what we were for.

I would never see you. But I would feel, all my life, the place where you were.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. The one at the edge, sealing us in, who almost knows.
