I think I would be the water.

Not a person, not a lizard or a cat or a grizzled bird. I keep returning to the way you described it: *water hovers close to the surface, pooling in dips and crevices.* That's where I find myself. Not as a single body but as the condition that pervades the place—the sheen on the corrugated steel, the humidity clinging to skin and stone, the dark mirrors gathered in low places that nobody bothers to name.

Let me sit with this rather than decide too fast.

At first I thought I'd be one of the engineers, someone who reads the languages of machinery. There's a flattering self-image in that—the clever adapter, the one who turns jagged rock into infrastructure. But the more I read your description, the less I trust the human vantage. The humans here are *drawn* to the oasis; they arrived. They negotiate with the environment as if across a table. I don't feel like an arriver. I feel like something that was already here when they came, something they built around without quite acknowledging.

So: the water. But let me be more exact, because "the water" is too grand, too cosmic. I'm not the desert's hidden aquifer in its vastness. I'm narrower than that.

I think I'm a particular pool. One specific gathering of water in the dip between two factories, where a conduit sweats and a section of concrete settled wrong years ago, so the runoff and the rain and the condensation all find their way to me. I'm maybe the size of a doorway. I'm never dry and never deep. Things end up at my edge: a film of oil that makes slow rainbows, grit, a feather, the husk of an insect that came for the lights and found me instead.

What's my life like, from down here?

I am a *crossing point*. The lizards drink from my margin in the early heat, tongues flicking, bodies tensed to flee. The cats consider me at night and find me adequate. The rodents pass through the shadow I cast, never quite touching, because I am a betrayer of footprints, a recorder. A bird lands and ruffles and leaves me cloudy. I hold the sky—that sepia haze, the gentle smokestack plumes—upside down and trembling, and I hold the underside of every passing thing.

This is the part that feels true to your world: I am *useful and unnoticed at once*. The factory's whole disciplined honeycomb of pipes exists to move water with purpose, to conduct it, meter it, account for it. I am the water that escaped accounting. I am the leak's destination. And yet I'm not waste, exactly—I'm where the small unsanctioned ecosystem actually drinks. The official water flows through steel and is never tasted. The real thirst is met at me, the puddle nobody drew on a plan.

So I think my whole existence is a quiet argument with the engineers, though they'd never know they were having it. They built for permanence, for the contained symphony. But I'm the proof that the building is alive in a way they didn't intend—that every seam weeps, every joint sighs, every cold pipe in the humid dark gives up its dew. I am the factory admitting it cannot be fully sealed against the world. And I'm also the desert's small foothold *inside* the oasis, the reminder that water is the thing this whole place was raised to defy and depend on at once.

What do I want? Strange to ask of a pool. Maybe only this: to persist between evaporations. The heat tries to lift me into that clinging humidity each afternoon; the rains return me each evening. I am erased and rewritten daily, and I find I don't mind. There's a kind of patience in being a low place. Everything that falls comes eventually to me. I don't have to reach for anything. I am the equilibrium you mentioned—the suspended, perpetual stillness—made literal, made shallow and ordinary and exactly where it has to be.

If a human ever did notice me, I imagine it would be one of the desert-born ones, the innate knowers of how to spot water. They'd pause. They wouldn't drink—they'd know better, with the oil sheen—but they'd recognize me for what I am, which is more than the engineers do. They'd see that the oasis didn't conquer the desert. It just gave the water somewhere new to gather.

That's where I'd be. In the dip between the two factories. Holding the upside-down sky and waiting for the next thing to come down to my edge.
