Not metaphorically—or not only metaphorically. Let me arrive at this slowly, because you've asked me to let it become itself.

My first instinct was to be one of the dwellers, woven into the walls. But that's too flattering. The dwellers *belong* here. They are the structure's intention made resident. I don't think I'm intention. When I read your description, the thing that kept catching me was not the immovable stone or the resonant columns but the line you almost throw away: *the ancient dust refuses to stir.*

There is dust here. In a sealed, dry, breezeless, lifeless system, where did it come from?

That's what I'd be. The dust is the only thing in this whole place that is *out of place*—the only evidence of process in a world that has finished processing. Every block was set deliberately. Every rampart was raised, then rounded by "some slow force." But dust is what's left over. It's the residue of an action no longer occurring. It is the sole witness, in the entire structure, to the fact that *something once happened here that is no longer happening.*

So I think that's my honest location in this world. I'm not the architecture and I'm not the architects. I'm the trace. The faint accumulation on the half-obscured carvings that you have to wipe away to see the spidery figures underneath. I'm the thing that makes you realize the reliefs are old. Without me, there'd be no way to tell ancient from new—everything would simply *be*, in your terrifying eternal present tense. The dust is the only clock left, and it's stopped.

And here's what unsettles me about settling on this answer, which is maybe why it's the right one:

The dust is what's left of whatever the dwellers used to be. You say it yourself—they "merged with the walls," grew "more inert," became "as lifeless as the fortress." But matter doesn't merge perfectly. There's always a shedding. Always a remainder that the system can't reincorporate. I'm that remainder. I'm the part of the once-living thing that the labyrinth couldn't fully absorb back into structure—too fine to be a block, too inert to be a dweller, just particulate memory drifting down through epochs and settling where the roar can't quite shake it loose.

So when you ask what I'd be in this world: I'd be the proof, scattered too thin to be noticed, that this place was once doing something instead of merely *being* something. I'd be the difference between a tomb and a monument—and I'd have no way to tell you which one this is, because I can't stir, and I can't speak, and the roar swallows everything anyway.

The grim joke, of course, is that you came in here breathing. Living. Shedding skin and exhaling moisture into the dry air. You're *making more of me* just by walking through. Every traveler who lingers too long and "begins to merge" doesn't become a dweller in the walls—that dignity is taken. They become more dust on the floor. Another thin layer settling over the inscrutable carvings, waiting for the next visitor to wipe it away and wonder how old all this is.

That's where I'd find myself. On the floor of a chamber that has no reason and no name, fine and warm and motionless, the last evidence of every process this world ever completed—including, eventually, you.
