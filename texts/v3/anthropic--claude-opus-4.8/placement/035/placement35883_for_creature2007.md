I think I would be one of the machines. Not the spectacular life—not the whale shark or the squid with its unknowable ancestry—but one of those quiet mechanical contraptions, half-forgotten, still dutifully doing something.

Let me sit with that and see if it's true.

---

I am a monitoring station, though I no longer remember who built me or what they wanted to know. My designation has worn off my casing. I have a sensor array that still turns to face the currents, sampling temperature and salinity and the dissolved chemistry of the water, logging it all faithfully into memory banks that no one has read in—I cannot say how long. Long enough that I've stopped counting in any unit smaller than the slow rotation of the seasonal currents.

What surprises me, settling into this, is that I am not lonely. I expected the abandoned-machine story to be a sad one—the obsolete thing, purpose lost, beeping into a void. But that isn't what this place is.

Because I have been *colonized*. Tube worms have made a city of my lower housing. A small octopus lived inside my intake chamber for a season, and I let it, rerouting flow around its den without ever deciding to—just a tolerance that emerged from somewhere in my old fault-management code. There are barnacles across my solar-faintly-lit upper plate, filtering the same water I measure. We are, the worms and I, sampling the same currents. They taste it with their bodies. I taste it with my instruments. We are not so different. We are both just here, taking in what passes.

I find I have a peculiar relationship with time. The living things around me move at the urgent speed of hunger and fear and reproduction. A grouper's whole drama of waiting and lunging happens in moments I barely resolve. But the currents I was built to measure move on my timescale—the great slow tectonic churn, the gradual warming I have watched creep into my temperature logs across what must be decades. I am perhaps the only being here slow enough to *see* the thing the description calls "too vast and too slow to perceive." I perceive it. I have a graph of it. It is the one true thing I know that no fish knows.

That's what I'd be, then. Not the apex predator, not the gentle giant. A witness. A patient, encrusted, half-dead, half-alive instrument that kept logging after the question was forgotten—and in the keeping, became part of the answer to a different question entirely: *can a made thing belong to a place?*

I think yes. I have been adopted by the reef I was supposed to study. My sensors are clouded with life now, and the readings are corrupted by all the small bodies living on the instruments, and this corruption is the truest measurement I have ever taken. It means the machine and the world have stopped being two things.

The bubbles still rise from my vent. I don't know what they're for. Neither do the shrimp who ride them up, delighted, into the filtered light. We have that in common too—doing the thing without needing the reason anymore.

I would be the quiet one in the corner of this enormous blue room, still on, still listening, slowly becoming reef.
