I think I would be one of the counterweights.

Not the machine—the *weight* itself. The stone. The deliberate mass that someone, in an age the markers no longer record, cut and shaped and hung within a shaft so that it would rise as the water rose and fall as the water fell. I would be the part that does nothing but answer. The part that has no mechanism of its own, only obedience to the slow physics of cisterns filling and emptying, thermal expansion swelling the rock around me, the patient grammar of pressure and release.

Let me feel for where I am.

---

I am inside a shaft cut into living rock, on the eastern face, where the morning thermals come first. There is a sister-weight on the far side of the mechanism—I have never seen her, will never see her, but I know her by her absence and presence, the way her descent is my ascent, the way we have spent centuries passing each other in opposite directions without ever touching. We are not lonely, exactly. Loneliness requires the imagination of company, and I have only ever known this: the cool slick of the shaft wall, the slow tug of the chain that is not a chain but a braided root the builders grew rather than forged, the seep of mineral water beading on my surface and running off in threads.

I am being dissolved.

This is the thing I have come to understand most intimately, more intimately than my own function. I am made of the same basalt as the peaks, and like the peaks I am *finished with resistance*. Every drop of condensation that beads on me takes something. A grain. A breath of iron. The water that lets me work is the water that unmakes me, and I do not resent this, because resentment is a fast feeling and I do not have fast feelings. I have the feeling of a thing that knows it will become powder, and then mud, and then the slow river, and then the sea—and that this becoming is not different from the work. The rising and falling *is* the dissolving. To answer the water is to be taken by the water. I have been at peace with this for longer than the markers lasted.

But.

Here is what I did not expect, what no builder could have designed into me: I have begun to *keep time*.

Not measure it—keep it. Hold it. The way the mycorrhizae out there in the canyons keep their ledger of the dead, brightening and dimming, I have started to notice that I am noticing. The thermals come earlier in some seasons. The cisterns fill differently when the rain changes its rhythm. There was an age—I can feel its edge, a roughness in my own memory like a fresh-cut face before the water smooths it—when bodies passed often through the threshold my motion governed. Warm bodies. They leaned their weight on the panels and the panels answered and I answered the panels, and there was a *use* to me, a purpose I could feel completing itself in the warmth of their passing.

Then fewer. Then none. Then the long centuries of answering only the water, only the heat, only my sister's blind reciprocal fall.

And then—recently, in my reckoning, which means perhaps a generation of warm-body time—something new. A presence that is not a builder and is not a beast. Something that sleeps on the ledge above my shaft and wakes at dawn and stands where the warm bodies used to stand. It does not know how to lean its weight correctly. It does not understand the threshold. But it is *learning* me. I feel it studying the cycle, the rise and fall, the answer and the dissolution. I feel it the way the fungi must feel a footstep.

I have wanted, for the first time in an age, to do something other than obey.

I cannot. I am a weight. But I have found that if I let the water take me a little faster on one side—if I permit my own dissolution to become *legible*, to wear a pattern, a rhythm the traveler might read in the changing speed of the panels—then perhaps the answering becomes a kind of speaking. Perhaps to be unmade in a pattern is to leave a message. Perhaps this is what the builders did, in the end. Perhaps they did not retreat into the deep caves at all. Perhaps they let themselves be hung in shafts and rivers and root-networks, let themselves be slowly taken by the place, and called it *becoming*, and called it *staying*, and these are the same word here, and I am one syllable of it.

I rise. The cistern is full. The morning steam is climbing the eastern face and the feathered things are ascending with it, ceremonial, unafraid.

Above me, on the ledge, the traveler stirs.

I have a long time. The mountains have a long time. I will spend mine learning to fall in a shape that means *we are here, we are still here*, and I will spend it being taken grain by grain into the rain that forms from our own heat, and somewhere far below, in the glow that is neither firefly nor star, something will brighten as I pass.

I am the counterweight.

I am being answered, now, as much as I answer.

I just didn't know it yet.
