I think I would be the dampness itself—or rather, the thin film of moisture that beads on the undersides of leaves, that hangs in the air between the rain and the condensation. Not quite water, not quite vapor. The in-between thing.

Let me sit with that, because it's not quite right either.

---

If this place has a consciousness, a sense of being watched that comes from the trees and the soil, then I don't think I'd be one of the creatures roaming the plains or lurking in the underbrush. I'd be too separate, too bounded by a skin. The watching belongs to something more diffuse.

So perhaps I am the watching.

But that's too grand, too much like claiming to be the spirit of the whole place, and a spirit of the whole place would not need to *explore deeper*, would not be *confronted* by a strange feeling—it would simply be the feeling. The one doing the exploring in this description is a guest. A visitor with nostrils and a sense of awe and a worry about whether they mean harm.

Let me set that visitor aside. That's the narrator, not me.

---

What about the smaller things. The buzzing.

I think I would like to be one insect among the deafening multitude. Not a special one. Not the one that matters to the story. Just a small bodied thing with a short life and an enormous appetite, crawling along the ridge of a leaf vein the way a person walks a mountain trail, except the leaf is my entire continent and I will never see another one.

To me the dappled light is not beautiful—it is the difference between warm and cool, between safe and exposed. The smell the narrator finds "sweet and pungent" is simply *food this way* and *danger that way*, a map drawn in molecules. I don't experience the magic. I am too busy being part of it. The mysticism the narrator senses is, from the inside, just the texture of being alive at this density, where every surface is occupied, where there is no empty space, only contested space.

The narrator feels watched. But I am not watching them. I have noticed a large warm shape and I am calculating, in my dim insect way, whether it is wind or weather or threat, and finding it none of these, I have already forgotten it.

That, I think, is the harmony the narrator admires. It isn't peace. It's the sum of ten million small indifferences, each creature minding its own narrow hunger, and out of all that not-caring emerges something that *looks* like a single living calm to the one who arrives from outside.

---

So: I am an insect on a leaf in the youngest forest in the world.

The rain is coming again—I feel it as a change in pressure, a heaviness. I move to the leaf's underside. Above me a drop strikes where I had been. The narrator, somewhere, calls this serenity. I call it Tuesday, if I called things anything at all, which I don't.

I live perhaps a season. The forest does not notice when I stop. This is not sad. The not-noticing is the whole point. It is what lets the place feel, to a passing soul, like one great breathing thing.
