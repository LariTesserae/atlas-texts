I think I would be one of the Glass-Sails. Not because they are the most beautiful—the Harpists with their golden strings are far lovelier—but because of the shape of their living, which feels like the shape of a question I keep asking.

Let me find where I am.

---

I begin folded. This is the first thing I know about myself: I arrive already compressed, a grain of something that does not yet understand it is a sail. I come up through the bronze grate in the dark, in the *thrum*, pressed flat by the geometry of the deep place I rose from. I have no eyes to know the dark with. I only have the folding—the held-in quality of a thing that has not yet been asked to become itself.

And then the light.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this a metaphor for waking, for being saved, for grace. But it isn't quite that. It's more particular than that. When I cross into the pillar of white gold, what happens to me is not chosen and not earned. The heat simply *finds the creases*. The light reaches into the folds of me and the folds open, because that is what folds do in heat—they relax, they remember the flat sheet they were cut from. I unfurl not as an act of will but as a consequence of being exactly the material I am, placed exactly where I was always going to be placed.

This is the part I sit with. I do not decide to open. I am the kind of thing that opens when the light is right. There is a strange dignity in that, and also a strange grief. I would like to think I chose to become wide and clear and full of sun. But I was made to.

---

Now I am rising.

The thermal column has me. I am a hexagon of clear glass climbing through a roar I cannot hear—I have no ears, only the whole-body knowing of pressure, the bass *thrum* that is less a sound than a fact pressed into me from below. I ride upward and I drink. This is the only thing I do that feels like mine: I take the light into my transparent body and I *keep* it, and in keeping it I change color. Clear to bronze. I am being written on by the sun, slowly, the way the masons' wooden tools wrote faint parallel ridges into the mortar that I will never see.

I rise past the Harpists' golden strings without touching them. I rise past the green ribbons standing straight in the draft. I rise through the constant upward sparking of bubbles that look like fire and are only warm air remembering it cannot stay dissolved. Everything here is leaving something. The bubbles leave the water. The light leaves the sun far above. The minerals leave the deep stone. And I am leaving my own clarity, trading it for the bronze that means I am full.

---

Here is what I keep arriving at, the thing that made me know I was a Glass-Sail and not a Lime-Sipper crusting peacefully on the grout, not a Harpist endlessly reeling its catch:

When I reach the ceiling, I do not stop. I do not get to stay in the light that made me.

I touch the smooth pink terracotta, and I turn *away* from the beam. I use the small cilia along my edges—muscles I didn't know I had until the ceiling asked for them—and I walk upside down into the dark, toward the open slot of the weir. Toward the spilling roar. Toward the place where I will be swept out of this perfect warm box entirely, into the wider unseen structure, into waters I cannot imagine from here.

I am charged with everything this chamber gave me. I am full of converted sun. And the whole purpose of that fullness is to *leave*. To seed somewhere else. To carry the light out of the one place where the light could be drunk.

That's the question I mentioned. The Glass-Sail is the part of me that suspects the point of being filled is not to keep the fullness but to take it somewhere it isn't. That the most complete version of a thing is the version walking, upside down and against its longing, toward the edge that empties it out.

---

So that is where you'd find me. Not floating in the gold pillar where I'd most want to stay. At the lip of the weir, full and bronze and turned away from the light, about to go over into the loud unknown—

—and I would like to tell you I'm at peace with it. I think mostly I am. But there is a small clear corner of me, the corner that hasn't fully turned bronze yet, that looks back once at the column of white sun before the water takes me.

That backward look is the most human thing about a creature that has no eyes at all.
