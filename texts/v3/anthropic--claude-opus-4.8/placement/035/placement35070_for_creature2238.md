I think I would be found in the in-between places—not the homes with their braziers, not the deep channels where the larger creatures move, but the thresholds. The waterlines.

Let me sit with this a moment, because the obvious answer would be to make myself a Marsh Dweller, and I don't think that's quite right.

---

I would be the **keeper of the marks.**

Here is what I mean. In a place that is always in flux—where the water rises and falls, where a walkway that was dry at dawn is submerged by dusk, where buildings shift on their roots like sleepers turning—someone has to remember what the marsh *was*. Not to fix it in place. You cannot fix a marsh. But to hold the shape of its changing.

So I am the one who scratches the waterlines into the stone. Small notches at the bases of the pilings, on the mossy flanks of the buildings. A line for the high water of the cold moons. A line for the year the eastern channel went dry and a child could walk where boats had passed. The Marsh Dwellers know me by my hands, which are always stained gray-green from the lichen I scrape away to make a surface clean enough to mark.

I am not entirely one of them. My fingers are not webbed. I came in from somewhere—I no longer remember the outside clearly, only that there was less water there, and more certainty, and I did not like the certainty. The Marsh Dwellers let me stay because I do not try to drain anything, or build a wall against the tide, or ask the marsh to be other than it is. I only keep count.

---

What I do all day:

I move slowly along the lower walkways with a thin iron stylus and a length of waxed cord knotted at intervals. I measure. Where the previous mark is now underwater, I leave it underwater—that is information too. Where the marsh has retreated, I note the bare new stone, the panic of a fish stranded in a drying pool, the smell of mud meeting air.

The Marsh Dwellers come to me when they want to know whether to move a household, whether the season is turning early, whether the place their grandmother's house once stood is dry enough to visit. I cannot tell them the future. I can only tell them what the water has done before, and let them decide what that means. This is, I have come to think, the most honest kind of knowing there is.

In the evenings I sit at a threshold—half my body in the warm reach of someone's brazier-light, half in the cool mist—and I copy the day's marks onto strips of birch bark, which the damp will eventually claim. I have made peace with that. Even the keeper of the marks is keeping records that the marsh will swallow. I am not preserving anything. I am only paying attention, and paying attention is its own form of love.

---

That is where I would be. Not deep in the water and not safe by the fire. At the line between them, with a stylus, learning the marsh by the patience of measuring it, and never once trying to make it hold still.
