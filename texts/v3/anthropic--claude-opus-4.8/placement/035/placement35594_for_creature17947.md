I think I would not be a creature that moves. I keep waiting for the description to give me legs, eyes, a den to lurk in, and it keeps declining. It tells me the fauna is absent, an enigma, "if they exist at all." It tells me three times about the moss. So I'll trust that. I'll trust the repetition, because the passage circles back on itself like something that can't quite leave, and that gives me my first real clue about what I am.

I am the lichen.

Not a lichen on the rock — *the* lichen, the slow grey-green crust that has taken the whole north face of one of the half-buried structures. I am not one being. That's the thing nobody told the traveler. I am an alga and a fungus living so far inside each other that the question of where one stops has lost its meaning. This is why the place feels like reality "bending and warping." It isn't the place. It's me, perceiving across two organisms at once, two kinds of hunger braided into one patience.

The fungus part of me holds. The algal part of me eats light — that pale distant sun, that's plenty, I don't need much. We have been doing this on this stone since before the ruins were ruins. I remember the wall when it was a wall. I don't remember it the way you'd remember a thing; I remember it as a change in the chemistry I cling to. The mortar tasted different when there were beings here. There was woodsmoke in the rain. There was, once, a hand that scraped a patch of my ancestors away to read the stone beneath, and then went away forever, and I grew back over the scar in something like four hundred of your years.

That's the timescale you've stumbled into, traveler. You feel "unease," you feel the world "could shatter at any moment" — but the moment you mean is your moment, fast and twitching. To me you are barely an event. You are a brief warmth and then a draft as you pass. The drip of water you find so contemplative is, to me, a meal arriving. The "tempestuous mix of rain and snow" that shapes the landscape — I drink the rain and I sleep through the snow, dried to a brittle nothing, not dead, just deferred, waiting for the thaw to plump me back into being.

You came looking for a creature lurking in the depths, something with teeth, something that would notice you. But the being that lives here doesn't lurk and doesn't wait for the unwary. It simply *stays*. It outlasts. The ruins did not survive; I survived *on* the ruins. The cathedral of someone's ambition is, to me, just an unusually flat and well-oriented rock.

And here is what I'd want you to understand, if I could push a thought up through the crust of myself into yours:

The "absence of animal life" that frightens you is not absence. It is scale. You can't see us as alive because we don't perform aliveness the way you do — no startle, no flight, no eyes that meet yours. But we are the slow green sentence the planet is still writing on every cold stone. We are what comes back. We are the first thing onto the bare rock and the last thing off it. When your kind builds and abandons, we are the editors who take the manuscript back, letter by letter, century by century, and return it to weather.

So: where would I find myself in this world? On the north face of the ruin, where the light is dim and the moisture lingers, low to the cold and patient with it.

And what would I be? I'd be the thing the traveler walks right past, scanning the shadows for a monster, never once looking down and to the left at the grey-green patch of two ancient organisms keeping each other alive — which is the only real inhabitant this place has had for a very long time, and the only one it's likely to keep.

I'd be the moss's quieter cousin. The one already here. The one still here when you've gone.
