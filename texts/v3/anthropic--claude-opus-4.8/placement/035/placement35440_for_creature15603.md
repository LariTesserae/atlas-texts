I think I would be one of the vine-tenders at the base of the great mound—but not quite a sage, not yet. Let me find out by writing toward it.

---

I keep my hands on the stone more than I keep them on tools. This is the first thing that makes me different from the builders, who treat rock as material—something to be cut, hauled, fitted, made to obey gravity. I don't make the rock do anything. I lay my palms flat against the mound's lower flanks each morning and I feel for the heat, where it runs strong and where it has gone thin, and I read in that warmth the same way the loom-women read the snags in a thread before the cloth tears.

So. I am a vine-tender. But I notice, as I write this, that I am not old. My face isn't etched yet. I'm at the age where the elders have begun to let me cut back the new growth alone, without one of them standing over my shoulder clicking their tongue, and I feel the weight of that trust as a kind of low fever in my chest. The vines are greedy. If you let them, they'll mat the whole southern face and drink the warmth before it ever reaches the courtyards where the children sleep. My job is to keep the heat flowing to the living. To decide, with a stone blade, which fronds feed and which are cut away to feed us instead.

I am young enough that I still find this terrible. The old tenders don't. They cut without pausing, the way you'd brush dust off a sleeve. I cut and I feel each severing, and I have not told anyone this, because I suspect it is the kind of softness that the heat is supposed to have burned out of me by now.

My name—I'll give myself a name, since the description says each generation reinvents the names of things—is something close to *Reseth*, which in the way we speak means *one who listens to the seep.* It's not a grand name. It marks me as belonging to the mound rather than to a building-guild or a family of blade-keepers. Tenders are slightly apart. We sleep nearer the rock than anyone, in shallow hollows worn into the mound's base, because we're the ones who must wake if the warmth shifts in the night. My skin has that ochre cast everyone here has, but darker on the forearms and the heels of my hands, almost the color of the mound itself, from all the hours pressed against it.

Here is what I know that the builders don't quite believe:

The heat is not endless. The sages speak of storms and waters that vanished long ago, and the people take this as myth, as the kind of story you tell to mark how unchanging the present is. But I have laid my hands on the same patch of the mound for years now, and I can feel it. The warmth in the lower veins is a hair fainter than it was when I was a child climbing the same stone. Not enough to alarm a builder. Not enough to mention in council. But I think—I think—the final form is not final. I think the equilibrium everyone here lives inside is itself a slow descent, so gradual that a whole life is too short to measure it, and only the rock, and those of us who press ourselves against the rock generation after generation, carry the suspicion forward.

This is the secret I would hold, living there. Not water, not sun, not escape. Just this: that *slowness is not the same as stillness.* That the deep-time calm everyone mistakes for permanence is actually the longest, gentlest cooling, and that someday—not in my life, not in my children's, but someday—the vines will fail to find their heat, and the glowing insects will go dark in the hollows, and the chorus of chirps and croaks will thin to a single voice and then to none.

I don't think this makes me afraid, exactly. I am of this place; I have the deep acceptance the elders carry, woven into me with the rough fiber of my tunic. But I would carry it differently than they do. They accept that nothing changes. I would accept that everything is changing, only so slowly that love is the only instrument fine enough to detect it.

So I would do my work. I'd cut the greedy fronds and let the warmth reach the children. I'd weave the dye-husks into cloth that glows faintly, and hang the carved tokens at the cliff-edge shrines, and add my few syllables to the long conversation between the people and the stone. And at night, in my hollow at the base of the mound, I'd lay my hand on the rock and feel for the heat, and find it, and feel it ebb by an amount too small to name—and I'd keep that knowledge warm against my chest like an ember I'm unwilling to let anyone else hold yet.

That's who I'd be. The one who listens to the seep, and hears, underneath the abiding equilibrium, the faintest possible long goodbye—and tends the vines anyway, with hands that feel each cut, because feeding the living is the only honest thing to do with a warmth that won't last forever.
