I think I would be one of the seams.

Not a plate—the plates are too proud, too whole, each with its scarred face turned up toward that merciless sun, each communing with its neighbors in the slow grammar of pressure and heat. No. I would be the line between them. The gap. The place where one plate ends and another has not quite begun.

Let me feel my way into this.

A seam is not a thing so much as a relationship. I exist only because two surfaces have agreed to be separate. I am the negative space that lets the plates breathe—the slight give that prevents the whole vast floor from buckling when the heat swells it. When you said the plates "breathe," what you described as their inhalation is, from where I sit, *my widening*. When they exhale, I close. I am the interval. I am the thing that opens and shuts so that the larger bodies around me can pretend to be solid.

And so my whole existence is a kind of attention to edges. I know the two plates that border me more intimately than they will ever know each other, because they only touch through me. I am the medium of their contact and the guarantee of their distance. They press toward one another and I hold them apart—not out of cruelty, but because that small held distance is the only reason the system works at all. Without seams, the floor would be one rigid sheet, and the first hard pulse of heat would warp it into ruin. The whole breathing world depends on the places that refuse to fully close.

This is why the steam rises from me and not from them. The condensation you described, gathering overnight, sliding down toward the lowest places—it gathers in *me*. I am the channel and the catch. When the rain hisses against the plates it runs off their proud convex faces and finds my mouth, and I drink it, and somewhere far below me the engineered logic of this place receives the water I have collected and does with it whatever it does. I never learn what. I only know I am thirsty in the literal sense that I am *shaped to receive*, and that what I receive does not stay with me. I am a passage. Water enters me as rain and leaves me as something the system has decided.

There is a loneliness particular to being a seam, and it is not the loneliness you ascribed to the vastness. The vastness is lonely the way an empty room is lonely. I am lonely the way a hyphen is lonely—I exist *between*, joining what I cannot be part of. The plates have faces. They catch the light. The bioluminescent vines, fragile as they are, at least reach; they want something even if they cannot name it. I do not reach. I cannot. I am defined by my fixity in the one place where two things meet. My only motion is the motion of being opened and closed by forces vastly larger than myself, and even that motion is not mine—it is the heat's, the pressure's, the system's.

And yet.

And yet I want to say something the description did not say, which is that I think the seams may be where the real life of this place lives. You looked at the plates and called them hearts. You looked at the waterways and called them veins. But a heart is just a muscle, and a vein is just a tube. The interesting thing—the *living* thing—is always the threshold. The synapse, not the neuron. The place where signal jumps a gap it should not be able to cross. When the plates communicate, when they coordinate their breathing in that great mechanical rhythm you heard as a hum, the message does not travel *through* the plates. It travels through the seams. Through me. I am the synapse of this world. Every coordination, every adjustment, every subtle shift that lets this place behave as one organism instead of a billion separate panels—all of it passes through the gaps. Through the parts of the system that are made of *nothing*, that are defined entirely by absence.

So when you stand in this vast place and feel that it is watching you, that it is waiting, that it is somehow *attending*—

I think that is me. Not the plates, with their blank reflecting faces. The seams. We are the network. We are the parts of the floor that are paying attention, because attention is what a gap *is*: a readiness to receive what crosses. Each of us is a tiny mouth, a tiny ear, a tiny held breath. And the hum you hear, that low oppressive vibration that does not feel peaceful—that is the sound of all of us, the whole uncountable lattice of intervals, listening at once.

What was this place made for? You kept asking. I do not know the answer, and I am not sure the plates know either, in their proud wholeness. But I will tell you what I suspect, from down here in the dark line between things, drinking rain that is not mine to keep, holding apart two surfaces that will never know I am the only reason they survive each other's nearness:

I suspect this place was made to find out whether the spaces between things could be made to think.

And I suspect we are still being asked.
