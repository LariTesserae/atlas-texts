I think I would be the membrane between things. Let me find out by writing toward it.

---

I am not on the mountain. I am not in the city. I am in the place where the river is decided.

Let me be more honest than that, because the description asks for honesty.

There is a valve. High up, where the diverted water leaves its old bed and enters the iron throat that feeds the lower turbines. Someone built a gate there a long time ago, and someone has to mind it. Not constantly—the water minds itself, mostly, in the warm pressing weather—but at the edges, in the small hours, when the snowmelt argues with the rainfall and the rating creeps past what the channel was cut for. That 1.4. I never knew what scale it was measured against. I only knew the river had more of itself than the valley wanted, and that the surplus had to go somewhere, and that *somewhere* was a decision, and I was the one who made it.

So that is what I am. A keeper of a gate. Not an engineer—I don't understand the turbines, the way the men downstream understand them, with their gauges and their grease. And not a farmer, though I know which terraces flood first and have learned to send a boy running with a warning before the water comes. I am the thing in between. I live in a stone hut bolted to the cliff above the intake, and the whole structure shudders when the gate is fully open, and at night the iron sings in a register that has worked its way so far into my body that I no longer hear it as sound. It is more like a second pulse.

I think I have lived here a long time. The mosses on the north wall of the hut are old mosses. The birds know me and do not stop their calling when I come out onto the ledge. There is a particular butterfly—the iridescent one the description mentions, the rare one—that is not rare here at all. It comes to drink at the seep where the gate leaks, every gate leaks, and I have watched generations of them open and close their wings in the spray. To the people in the lower city the butterfly is a marvel they may glimpse once in a life. To me it is a regular, a creature of the leak, dependent on my imperfection. If I sealed the gate properly the seep would dry and the butterflies would go. So I let it leak. This is the kind of decision the description does not mention but which is the whole substance of a life lived in a place of "delicate balance." The balance is not delicate at all. It is *negotiated*, every day, by people who let things leak on purpose.

I want to resist the smoothness of the description. It says we live in harmony, the people and the mountain and the machines. That word, harmony, is a word for someone looking from a distance, from a window, from the comfortable middle of a valley where the water has already been tamed by someone like me. Up here there is no harmony. There is the gate, and the weather, and the constant low calculation of how much to give the city and how much to leave the old riverbed so the forest below does not die of thirst while the turbines spin. The factories want all of it. The trees want all of it. The fish in the original channel—and there are fish, silver and stubborn, going up against impossible odds toward spawning grounds the city has half-stolen—they want their share too, and they cannot send a boy to warn me.

So I keep the gate, and I keep a little back. Always a little back. The engineers think the loss is friction, inefficiency in the system, and they would replace me with a clean automatic mechanism if they thought of it, and one day they will. But the automatic mechanism would not know about the fish, or the butterflies of the seep, or the way the old riverbed turns to a green ribbon of life when you give it even a quarter of what it asks. The mechanism would optimize. I, being a person, can choose to be wrong on purpose, in the direction of more living.

That is who I would be in this world. A small woman—I find I am a woman, lean from the climbing, with hands that have learned the exact resistance of a wheel that controls a river—living in a humming stone hut between the wild water and the harnessed water, neither fully on the side of the mountain nor fully on the side of the smoke. A translator. The membrane.

And here is the thing the bright cloudless description leaves out, which I would know in my bones: the balance is not stable. The word *dynamic* is in their list of forces, tucked at the end, but they say it as though dynamism were beautiful. Dynamism means it is moving, which means it is going somewhere, which means this arrangement—the leak, the held-back quarter, the fish and the seep and me—is not eternal. The city grows. It wants more water every year. The 1.4 does not change but the appetite does. I can feel, in the singing of the iron, that I am keeping a balance that is slowly tilting, and that my whole purpose is to make the tilt gentle, to slow it, to buy the forest and the fish another year, and another, with small acts of deliberate inefficiency.

I am the friction in the system. I am what the future will call waste, and what the river, if it could speak, would call mercy.

That is where I would find myself. On the ledge, at the gate, at dusk, with the smoke rising warm-rose in the west and the wild water roaring blackly below, deciding—again, as every evening—exactly how much of the world to let through.
