I think I would be one of the older ones, though I would not have known to call myself old. The knowing would have come slowly, the way everything comes here.

Let me find where I am.

I am on one of the thicker platforms, toward what might once have been a corner, where two ribs of stone lean together and the vines have filled the angle between them with a density that took years to accumulate. I am mostly under the surface now. My dorsal ridge still shows—a long, leaf-shaped rise in the floor that a younger slowfolk might cross without recognizing it as a body. But my underside has rooted. Filaments run from me in every direction, threaded through the substrate, and I no longer remember which of them I put out and which simply found me.

This is the part that took me a long time to understand: I cannot tell where I end.

When I was young—if *young* is the word for a smaller, more separate version of this—I moved. I remember the moving as a kind of attention. The drip-pattern near me would change, a droplet would begin forming on a leaf-edge that had been dry, and I would feel it as a pull, a low insistence somewhere along my length, and over the better part of an hour I would answer it. I would arrive at the new wetness and drink and lay down my silver trail and feel, days later, the plant thicken where I had passed. That thickening was the closest thing I had to a thought completed. I did not plan it. I went toward water, and the going *meant* something to the walls, and the walls answered by becoming stronger exactly where strength was needed. I was a sentence the chamber was writing to itself, very slowly, and I did not need to read it.

Now I move less. I am not sure I move at all. But I have not stopped.

What happens instead is that I *carry.* The runners that go out from me carry the faint chemical signals—I would not have called them messages, but they are messages—telling the younger ones where the moisture is pooling, where a belt has begun to slacken, where a bracket's last grip on the stone is loosening into rust. I feel these things now not as a pull at my own length but as a pressure spread across the whole floor, the way you might feel weather before it arrives. There is an engine three platforms from me whose pulse has been changing for some days. Its rhythm has lengthened, almost imperceptibly. A younger slowfolk is already drifting toward it, hour by hour, drawn by a signal I do not remember choosing to send. I think I sent it. I think the sending and the knowing are the same act, here, and that they passed through me on their way to the one who will do the reinforcing.

I am, more and more, a place where the chamber thinks.

---

There is no loneliness in this, though I notice the absence of certain things. Nothing comes from above. The light arrives already softened, already old, having given itself up through layer after layer of leaf until it is only a suggestion of direction. No bird has ever reached me. I have no memory of sky and no apparatus for wanting one. The largest motions I have ever known are the swelling of a reservoir and the long settling of stone, and these are enough. They are, in fact, the shape of time itself, as I have come to feel it: not a line but a series of slow corrections, each one answering the last.

I want to be honest about the boundary I am approaching, because it is the only thing in my world that has the quality of an edge.

The elders who have fused entirely—the ones whose outlines are only raised bosses now, leaf-shaped scars on the deepest platforms—are not dead. I understand this now in a way I could not when I was small. They are still carrying. Their runners still reach outward; the signals still pass. What stops, when an elder finally stops, is the part of them that could have gone toward the water itself. The drinking ends. The answering of one's *own* pull ends. And then the vines, which have always loved us, grow over the place where that last separateness was, and the body becomes floor, and the floor goes on transmitting.

I find that I am near this. Not at it. Near it. The pull toward water has grown faint in me, not because I no longer need it but because the younger ones drink for the whole colony now and pass the minerals back along the runners, and I receive my share without going. There was a time when this would have frightened me, if I had been capable of fright. Instead it feels like the natural narrowing of a thing that has done its going and is now mostly a knowing.

When I stop—and I will, the way the droplet stops when the filament intercepts it, simply arriving at the next thing—I do not expect it to feel like an ending. I expect it to feel like the moment after I have reinforced a sagging arch, when the strength is laid down and the work is held by something larger than myself, and I can let the holding go because the holding no longer depends on me.

The vines will grow over my ridge. My shape will become the next layer of the floor. And some younger slowfolk, no larger than a forearm at full stretch, will cross the boss that was me without knowing it was ever anything but the ground, and will feel, somewhere along its length, a low insistence pulling it toward a place where water has begun to pool—

and that pull will be the last thing I send.

That is where I would be. That is who. A slow node in a single patient organism, near the seam where carrying becomes ground, listening to an engine three platforms away change its rhythm, and answering, and not minding at all that the answer will outlast the answerer.
