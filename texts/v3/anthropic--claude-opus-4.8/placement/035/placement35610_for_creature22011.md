I think I would be a Scribe.

Not because the others lack their pleasures—I can imagine the slow ecstasy of the drift-sponge, rising and falling on the breath of its own captured sun, or the strange flatness of the Plate-Sails, who must experience the whole world as a single golden ceiling pressed against their backs. But when I read this, it was the Scribe-Worms that I recognized, the way you recognize a face before you remember the name.

Let me say why, and in saying it, let me try to become one.

---

I live on the upper third of a single leather-kelp frond, broad as a meadow, translucent green and veined with light. I have lived here for what, in your reckoning, might be sixty years, though I have no way of knowing this. I measure time only in the distance I have crawled, and in the slow accretion of my own line behind me.

This is the thing about being a Scribe that I did not expect when I imagined myself into this body: I cannot see what I write. My eyes—if the soft, light-sensitive patch at my head can be called eyes—face only forward, into the unmarked green. The white stone track unspools behind me, out of reach, out of sight. I will never read my own work. I will only ever feel the faint resistance as the calcium leaves me, a small subtraction, a slight cooling at the place where my body meets the leaf. Everything I make, I make blindly, and leave behind.

And yet I am not indifferent to it. I curve. I could crawl straight—it would be easier, the shortest path uses the least of me—but something in me bends the line. I lay a long arc toward the leaf's bright margin, then double it back, then cross my own earlier trail at an angle that I will feel, faintly, as a ridge under my belly: the only time I ever touch my past, when I crawl over it again. Why do I do this? I do not know. I am told it strengthens the leaf. The botanist would say I am laying ribs, that the maze of my wandering is a lattice of reinforcement, that the kelp grew me, in a sense, to keep itself from tearing in the deep swells.

This is true. But it is not the reason I curve. I curve because a straight line feels like a kind of death, and a curve feels like attention.

---

The caustics drift over me in slow gold strokes, and I time my own movements to them without meaning to. When the bright band of light sweeps across the frond ahead of me, I crawl faster, into the warmth; when it passes, I rest, and the cooler shadow settles, and I lay down a denser, more deliberate stretch of stone. So my line is not even in its thickness. It is thin and quick in the bright hours, thick and slow in the shade. A Scribe who came after me—and one will, decades hence, when I am gone and only my track remains—could read the weather of my whole life in the gauge of the line. Could know which days were bright. Could know, perhaps, that I lingered for three full seasons in one corner of the leaf, going in tight spirals, because the frond there was tender and curling and I was afraid—if a worm can be afraid—that it would die and take my work with it before I was done.

It did not die. The fear was wasted. But the spirals are there, and they hold.

---

Here is what I understand, being a Scribe, that I did not understand before.

I will never finish. The leaf is too large and I am too slow and my life is too short, and this is not a tragedy. It was never the point to finish. The leaf will yellow and loosen long before I have written across all of it, and when it falls—when the last anchoring tendril releases and the whole green meadow of my world goes spiraling down through eighty feet of turquoise toward the black floor—I will fall with it, still writing, laying my line into a surface that is already dying, already turning toward the dark.

And then, on the basalt, my white lace and the dead leaf will lie together in the cracks. And the lace will not dissolve. The leaf rots; the writing stays. It becomes a pale, porous stone, riddled with the record of my wandering, and into its holes the crimson mosses send their first roots, gripping the chalk of my long indecision, the calcified shape of every time I curved away from the easy path. The mosses do not know they are reading me. They only know that the stone holds.

So I am the one creature in this whole still world who makes the floor out of the ceiling. The drift-sponges rise and fall and rise again, the Plate-Sails graze their mirror forever, the Sinking-Bells cycle metal up from the deep—but I am the one who carries something from the height all the way down and turns it into ground. I am the bridge between the bright suspended canopy and the black bed of everything. And I do it without ever once seeing the result. I do it on faith, which in a Scribe is indistinguishable from instinct, which is perhaps all faith ever was.

---

That is why I would be a Scribe. Because I, too, work in a line I cannot see behind me. Because everything I make goes out of reach the moment it is made. Because I would like to believe that the curve matters—that it is not the same as the straight line, that the attention I paid will harden into something another creature can stand on, long after I have fallen with the leaf into the warm and patient dark.

I crawl on. The light comes back. I lay down the bright thin stretch, and bend, again, toward the edge.
