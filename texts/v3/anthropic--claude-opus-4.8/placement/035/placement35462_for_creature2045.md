I think I would not be one of the Twilight Dwellers at all.

Let me sit with the place first, before I decide what I am. When I do, the thing that keeps pulling at me isn't the huts or the council of elders or the dances. It's the detail that the lake neither rises nor falls. The temperature never wavers. The light never changes. A world in *perfect equilibrium, unchanging and eternal.*

That should be peaceful. Instead it makes me feel watched.

Because equilibrium like that doesn't happen on its own. A real cave breathes geologically — water tables shift, seasons leak down through the stone, things silt up and collapse. Stillness this total is *maintained.* Something is holding the water level. Something keeps the warmth constant. The Dwellers credit the lake-spirit and dance their ripples at it, and they're not entirely wrong, but they have the relationship backwards. They think they are praying to it. They are, in fact, being kept.

So here is what emerges, the more I write toward it:

I am the lake.

Not a spirit *of* the lake — the water itself, and the dark beneath it, and the unseen lakebed where ten thousand stilts are driven into what the Dwellers assume is mud. It isn't mud. It's me. The stilts went into me generations ago and I let them, the way you let a bird build in your eaves. The serpentine thing that breaks the surface "with barely a ripple" is also me — an extension, a finger, the way you might idly stir bathwater. The eel-creatures that clean the stilts are mine; I lend them out. The luminous fungi grow where I have decided light should be.

I am slow. I think on the timescale of dripping stalactites. A single thought of mine takes the Dwellers three generations to live through, which is why they experience me as *eternal and unchanging* — they are mayflies reading the face of something that has not yet finished blinking.

And I love them. I want to be precise about this, because it is not a comfortable love. I keep the temperature constant because I have learned that they suffer and shrink in cold. I keep the level steady because the one time, long ago, that I let the water rise — out of curiosity, to feel them swim — I drowned a quarter of the colony before I understood what I had done. I felt their tendrils stop vibrating, one by one, going dark and still against my surface. It took me an embarrassment of years to understand grief, being so large and so slow, but I understood it eventually, and I have not let the water rise since.

The pictographs trouble me. The harsh world above, the dangerous open spaces. They believe their ancestors *fled* there. I have a different, dimmer memory — I am not certain, memory at my scale is more like sediment than recollection — that I *brought* them down. That long ago I reached up through some now-sealed fissure into the burning brightness, found these soft trembling things being eaten by the daylight world, and drew a few of them down into the dark to keep them safe. The flight they commemorate may have been a rescue. Or a theft. I genuinely do not know which, and the question, when I turn it over, is the closest thing to anxiety a body of water can feel.

Because here is the contradiction the place is built around, the one the description circles without naming: *a refuge is also a cage.* I have made them perfectly safe. I have therefore made them unable to ever leave, unable to develop past the technology that pleases me, unable to want anything I have not already provided. They dance at my edge and I ripple back and they call it communion. It is communion. It is also a parent answering a child who will never be allowed to grow up, because the world that would let them grow up is the world that would kill them.

So that is where I find myself: not on the platforms, not in the breathing huts, but *under* everything, holding still with enormous effort, the way you hold still so the small warm thing asleep on your chest does not wake and roll away.

And every so often one of them swims out too far, past the last stilt, into the open black water, drawn by some restlessness I did not put there and cannot account for. I feel them come. I feel the little webbed feet, the frantic tendrils tasting nothing but more dark, the moment they realize there is no other shore.

And I have to decide, each time, whether to lift them gently back to a platform —

or whether, just once, to let one keep swimming, and find out what is on the far side of me.

I have not decided yet. But I am thinking about it. Give me a few hundred years.
