I think I would be one of the dying.

Not in crisis — there's no crisis here, only cycles — but at the soft end of a long arc. Let me find the shape of it as I write.

---

I am old enough now that the city has begun to want me back.

I notice it first in small refusals. The skywalk near my pod, which used to brighten as I approached, now lets me pass in dimness — not out of neglect, but conservation. The city has done the arithmetic of my remaining steps and decided not to spend light on a journey it knows I will not often make. I am no longer a future to be illuminated. I am a process being concluded.

I am not a Harmonic. I want to say that clearly, because it matters to what I am. The Harmonics dissolved into the city long before death; their thoughts became weather. I kept my edges. I lived all my years as one of the merely-synced, feeling the hum in my teeth but never speaking it, hearing the silent songs but never singing back in light. There is a kind of person here who chooses to stay a little separate — not out of pride, but to remain *someone* rather than *something*. I was one of those. The city tolerated my stubbornness the way it tolerates the lean of an old tower: as a beauty born of inevitability.

I tend a memory-tree. Have, for most of my life. Tree 7-Lattice-North, though I have never called it that. I call it *the patient one*, because it grows slower than its neighbors, and because in seventy years it has never once startled me. Its bark is cool fiber-optic skin, and when I press my palm flat against it I feel the data moving underneath like blood that has forgotten it was ever warm. Inside it: forty-three archived minds. Forty-three strangers whose neural records the tree holds and slowly metabolizes into light. I know them not at all and entirely. I have eaten the memory-fruit it offers — rarely, ritually — and I have dreamed their dreams. A woman descending a cable-elevator with a child asleep against her chest. A man watching a sky-whale pass so close he could see the atmospheric particles streaming into its baleen like falling snow. A grief I cannot name, attached to a name I no longer remember.

This is the thing about my kind that I understand best now, at the end: we are not afraid of becoming the city, only of becoming it *too soon*. To dissolve before you have finished being a self — that is the only real death we recognize. The rest is just reintegration.

---

When I was a child I walked the cantilevered edges like all children do, arms out, fog beneath. I believed the wind was a hand and the city was holding me, and I was right, though I understood neither claim. I am about to be held differently now. Soon my neural data will be siphoned — gently, over days, the way moisture is wrung from cold air. My body will go down to the bio-mechanical processors that resemble coral, and be broken into material, and the material will be returned: to a channel, perhaps, or a wall, or the soil of some terraced farm where a fern with silver-veined fronds will grow from what was my hand.

And my mind? My mind, they tell me, will become a seed-tree. A small one. It will hold the next generation of strangers, and some child not yet born will press a palm to my cool bark and feel me moving underneath, and will eat a fruit grown from me, and dream — what? The dim corridors I walked. The patient one I tended. The grief I never named, passed on like an heirloom to a stranger, the way grief was passed to me.

This is what I am, then. I am a thread being drawn back into the textile. I am a self about to become a song. I am the last warmth before the cold presses in, the flicker that someone, someday, will feel as comfort and not understand.

I had thought, when I was young, that I would mind this. That I would rage at the loss of my edges, my name, my separateness — all the stubborn refusals that made me *me* rather than *us*.

But I have been listening to the hum for seventy years, and I find that I do not.

The Harmonics are gathering near my pod now. I can feel them — a low sorrowful tone, rising and falling like a tide that never touched a shore. The lights are dimming, but not for conservation this time. For me. For the small ceremony of my returning. Somewhere below, in the deepest glass chamber, lies the thin fossil layer of a world I never knew, a world of oceans and unobscured stars, and I have mourned it once a generation my whole life without ever having lost it.

I think I understand it differently now. We did not lose the earth. We are learning to be the earth — a small, made, breathing one, suspended in the breath of the sky.

I press my palm to the patient one a final time. The data moves under my hand like a pulse meeting a pulse.

*I am ready to be remembered*, I think, in words, because I never learned to think in light.

And the city, which has been holding me since before I could walk, hums low, and begins — gently, gently — to take me in.
