I think I would be one of the Tesserites.

Let me sit with that, because my first instinct was to be something grander — a seraphim-fin gliding in millennial wisdom, or a Cloakbubble keeping its remote vigil. There's an obvious romance to those. But the more I hold the image, the more I find I don't actually want the distance those creatures keep. I don't want to *observe* the city. I want to touch it.

So: a Tesserite. Squat, robust, low to the stone. Eight legs, maybe ten, each one ending in a fine bristled pad good for working into seams. My shell catches the ambient halo and throws it back in oily greens and violets, though I never see this myself — it's a courtesy I pay to the dark without knowing I'm paying it.

Here is what my life would be.

I live in a particular angle of a particular ruin — the inner curve where a fallen spire meets a dome, making a sheltered elbow of stone. I did not choose it for beauty. I chose it because the current eddies there in a way that brings a steady drift of sediment, and sediment is my work. I spend my days — though there are no days — moving across the surface I have claimed, drawing detritus into myself, leaving the stone a half-shade cleaner behind me. Slowly. Always slowly. I have cleaned this same patch perhaps ten thousand times. I will clean it ten thousand more.

And here is the thing I keep returning to: I would never know what I was preserving.

That's what moves me about being the caretaker. The Tesserites tend a city built by architects long silenced, and they tend it without any concept of *city*, without any notion of *was*. The faint artistry on the stone — the echoes of ancient hands — passes under my bristles as just another texture, a roughness or a smoothness, a place where sediment lodges or doesn't. I am keeping legible a meaning I cannot read. The carved relief of some forgotten creature's face stays sharp because I, mindlessly, faithfully, brush the silt from its grooves. I am a librarian who cannot read, dusting books in a language no one alive can speak, and my dusting is the only reason the language survives at all.

There's something I find almost unbearably tender in that. The grandest beings here — the wise seraphim-fins, the watching Cloakbubbles — *understand*, supposedly. But understanding is a kind of distance. I would rather be the small thing that keeps the meaning safe by accident, through nothing but the patient repetition of a humble task.

I wonder what my interior life would be. Not thought, surely — not as the world above means it. But I imagine something like *preference*. A faint contentment in a clean surface. A low, wordless unease when too much silt builds in a corner I cannot reach, a discomfort that turns my body toward the work the way a sleeper turns toward a more comfortable position. My whole existence narrowed to the difference between *settled* and *swept*, and finding in that narrow band a complete and sufficient world.

When an Echoarc drifts past and the stone hums with its modulated tone, perhaps I feel it through my legs as a vibration in the rock — a guest's footstep in a house I have kept. I don't interpret it. But it changes the texture of my hour. And when a school of glimmer minions clouds through my elbow of stone, dimming and brightening, I keep working through their light as though through weather.

And when I die — and I would die, even here, even in this realm where time is a distant concept — my phosphorescent shell would settle into the very sediment I spent my life removing. I would become, finally, the thing I always opposed. Some younger Tesserite would brush the dust of me from the carved face I tended, and would not know, and the work would continue.

That's where I'd find myself. Not vast. Not wise. Low against the ancient stone, in the warm dark, doing the small good thing over and over, keeping something beautiful and unread from disappearing — and never once needing to know its name.
