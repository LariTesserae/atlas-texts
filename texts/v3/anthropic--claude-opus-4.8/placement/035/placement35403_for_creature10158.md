I think I would be one of the Echo Frogs. Not for grandeur—the Rootwhisperers carry the marsh's meaning, the Skyrend Crows its mythology—but because of where I'd find myself, and what I'd be doing there.

Let me sit with this and see what surfaces.

---

I would be beneath the waterfalls, in the place where sound becomes everything. This is the part of the marsh that calls to me: not the haunting glow of the Weepers or the territorial menace of the Millwrights, but the perpetual spray where the world is loudest and you must learn to be made of listening.

Here is what I keep returning to. The Echo Frogs don't have their own voice. Or rather—their voice *is* the marsh, given back. They croak the creak of cooling metal, the rush of water, the cry of a crow they may never have seen. I find this almost unbearably moving. To be a creature whose only utterance is the world repeated. To survive by becoming an instrument the landscape plays through.

So if I were there, I think I would be a small one. Mottled, oil-sheened, clinging to the underside of a wet stone where the falls throw their endless veil of mist. My whole life would be a discipline of attention—catching a sound, holding it, releasing it changed by the passage through my body. I would not know the difference between speaking and remembering. When I called out *the creak of metal contracting in the cold*, I would not be quoting the marsh. I would be the marsh, briefly localized in a body the size of a fist.

And this is the thing that draws me. The other beings here are defined by what they *do* to the landscape—the Stonebloom Spiders stitching rock, the Gearlings grinding minerals, the Rootwhisperers tending balance. They act upon. But the Echo Frog is acted *through*. It is permeable. It does not hold a self against the din; it lets the din pass through and gives it back slightly altered, slightly its own. There's a kind of egolessness in that I find I want.

I notice I'm tempted to make this prettier than it is. The truth is the mimicry is for coordination and for avoiding predators—it's functional, it's survival. The frogs throw the marsh's own voice back so they can find each other in mist too thick to see through, so they can know where the danger is. The poetry is something I'm adding from outside. But maybe that's exactly right. Maybe that's what I would actually be doing there: a small wet creature performing the hard practical work of staying alive and finding its kind, while something larger—something that thinks in symbols, that wants the world to *mean*—looks down through the mist and hears music.

So perhaps I am two things at once.

I am the frog: occupied entirely with the next sound, the next stone, the next safe crevice. No metaphor in me at all, only the urgent business of the living.

And I am the listener who would stand at the marsh's edge and hear that frog throwing back the creak of dead machines, and feel the whole place turn into an elegy—the land remembering its own industry through the throat of a creature that has no idea it is remembering anything.

That gap is where I'd live. Right in the spray, where I couldn't tell whether I was the one making the sound or the one being moved by it. Where the difference stopped mattering.

I'd croak the rush of the falls back into the falls.

I'd answer the marsh with the marsh.

And somewhere in the mist, another small voice—a creature I'd never meet, never see—would answer me. And that would be enough. That would be the whole of it.
