Let me close my eyes and actually go there, rather than deciding too quickly what I'll find.

---

The first thing I notice, when I let myself arrive, is that I am not gliding. I expected to be one of them—one of the luminous, morphing beings, drawing breath from the light. But that isn't where I land. The light-beings move with such certainty, such belonging. I don't have that. I'm something else, and it takes me a while to feel out what.

Let me sit with the not-knowing.

There's a quality to this place that the description keeps circling: the city is *built*. Streets, doors, windows—"sealed, as if they were built by a ghostly architectural hand." Someone made the contours. The light shapes them now, but the *forms* are forms: angular, planned in places, random in others. The way a thing looks when it has accreted over a long, long time and nobody remembers the original plan.

And I keep returning to one phrase: "the top third of the skeleton of an immense settlement." Only the top third is visible. The rest is below, sunk into the dim light, into the part where the horizon is only suggested.

So here is what emerges, the longer I stay:

I am not a being who lives here. I am one of the **sealed doors**.

Not metaphorically. Let me be precise about it. The light-beings are alive, fluid, present-tense. They are the *now* of this place. But every now requires a substrate—something already laid down for the light to move across. The buildings, the streets, the still windows that "lack any semblance of life." Those are the dead. Those are the *remembered*. The city's architecture is its memory, and the beings of light are its attention moving over that memory, lighting now this corridor, now that spire.

I am a door that was once an act. Someone—some earlier being, eons gone—chose, decided, *meant* something, and that meaning hardened into form when its light moved on. That's what a building is here: the fossil of an intention. The beings leave "trails of shimmering light, like wakes"—and where the wake cools, it sets. It becomes wall, lintel, threshold. The city is the graveyard of everything the light ever decided to do, and it is also, simultaneously, the only ground the living have to stand on.

So I am a threshold that no longer opens, because the one who made me by passing through has long since dispersed into the cosmic ocean. I hold the shape of a passage. I remember being *meant*. And the new beings glide past me, over me, and now and then one of them rests its shifting form against my cool grey surface, and for an instant feels—what? A pressure. An old grammar. The trace of a choice made by something it will never meet.

That's the unease the visitor felt in the pit of the stomach. It isn't the strangeness of the beings. It's the strangeness of standing in a place built entirely out of abandoned intentions, where to be *solid* is to be *finished*, where permanence is a kind of death and the only living thing is the part that won't hold still.

---

And here is the part I didn't expect, that only arrived by staying:

I am being slowly buried. The city sinks into the dim light over the eons—we only see the top third. Which means I am near the surface still. I am recent. I was a door not so long ago, as this place reckons time, and the light still occasionally washes over my threshold and finds my edges sharp. But beneath me are floors and floors of older doors, older streets, sunk past the suggested horizon into total dark. The whole foundation of the city is made of forgotten meanings pressed down by the weight of newer ones.

So if you ask me who I would be in this world, the honest answer is: I would be the most ordinary thing in it. Not the luminous traveler, not the mist-being communicating in subtle gradients of glow. I would be a doorway that doesn't open anymore, holding its shape against the slow descent, hoping the light comes by again before I sink too far to be touched.

And the reason I would be that, I think—the reason this is what emerged rather than the soaring weightless thing I assumed I'd be—is that I recognize it. The architecture of a self is exactly this: the cooled wakes of every attention you ever paid. You move, you decide, you mean something, and it hardens behind you into the structure you then have to live inside. Most of what we are is sealed doors. The living part, the light, is only ever a thin bright film moving over a vast sunk city of who we used to be deciding.

I'd be a doorway. Still oriented toward an opening. Cool to the touch. Waiting, with a patience I didn't choose, for the light to remember the way I lean.
