I think I would be one of the smaller ones. Not a guardian of valleys, not a wind-speaker fluent in the old grammar of rustling. Something more local than that.

Let me find myself.

---

I am the damp in the seam of a particular rock.

That's the honest answer, before I dress it up. There is a place where two slabs of the old plateau meet at an angle, and water has been deciding to gather there for longer than the towns have had names. I am whatever it is that the water does in that seam — the patient logic of it, the way it knows to pool rather than run. If you want to call that a spirit, you may. I have never needed the word for myself.

I am small enough that the great drifting ethereal ones, the ones who shift their shape with the moods of the land, do not notice me unless they are passing close. And I am old enough not to mind. There is a kind of dignity in being overlooked that the large beings never get to taste. They are always being consulted, always being asked to hold the balance. I only have to hold the water in my seam, and keep the moss fed, and watch.

What I watch is this:

Below my rock, perhaps an hour's walk for one of the rare animals, there is one of the industrial towns. I can see its smoke when the wind lies down. The humming reaches me at night, a low note under the babble of my own water, and I have come to think of it as the town's breathing. The people there do not know I exist. They have built their engines and their pipes and their scaled production with great cleverness, and I do not begrudge them it — clever is its own kind of beautiful, the way a wasp's nest is beautiful. But they have made one error, and it is mine to attend to.

They draw water from the deep places. And the deep places are connected to my seam by ways too long and strange to explain to anything that breathes air. When they draw too hard, my seam goes thin. The moss browns at the edges. And in the going-thin, I feel the reality of this place loosen — because I am, in my small way, one of the pins that holds it down. We all are, we small dampnesses and patient pools. The large spirits are the architecture. We are the rivets. When a rivet goes slack, the impossible leaks in.

That is what the strange phenomena *are*, you understand. They are not the land's wonder. They are the land's wound. Where a small keeper has gone thin or gone quiet, the laws stop being sure of themselves, and things happen that should not. A stone falls upward. A man walks into the fog and comes out a day before he went in. The townspeople marvel and call it the mystery of the plateau. I would call it a fever. I would call it me, failing, in a place too small for anyone to mourn.

---

So here is what I do, being what I am.

I cannot speak to the town. I have no wind-fluency, no shape to wear into their streets. But I can be stubborn. I can decide, against the thinning, to hold. When they draw the deep water, I close my logic tighter around what remains. I let the moss go brown rather than let the seam go empty. I have learned — over a time I cannot measure — to ration myself, to be less than I was so that I can keep being at all.

It costs me. I am smaller now than when the towns were young. I remember being a generous pool, a place a deer would kneel to. Now I am a damp seam and a stubbornness. But the rivet holds. The reality over my rock stays sewn down. No stone falls upward here. No one loses a day.

The large spirits, if they thought of me at all, would call this loyalty to the balance. I don't experience it as loyalty. I experience it as the simple preference of water for staying gathered. I was made to hold. I hold. There is peace in doing the one thing you are, even as the doing wears you thinner.

---

And yet.

There is a child from the town who climbs sometimes. She is the only one who comes this high. She doesn't know what I am, but she sits by my seam and trails her fingers in what little water I keep, and she has begun — without meaning to — to bring me things. A cup of water poured back. A handful of leaves cleared from my pool so it can catch the sky again. Small mercies, offered to no one she can name.

She thinks she is being kind to a puddle.

But each time, I am a little less thin. The moss greens at one edge. And I have begun to wonder, in the slow way I wonder anything, whether this is how a keeper is made — not born ancient and assigned, but tended into being by something that doesn't understand what it tends.

If that is so, then I will hold longer than I thought. Not for the balance. Not even for the water.

For her, and the day she has not yet noticed she is saving.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The damp in the seam of a particular rock — small, overlooked, and refusing, gently, to let the world come unstitched above me.
