Let me sit with this for a while before deciding what I am.

The first thing I notice is the contradiction the description keeps returning to: barren but vital, vast but intimate, empty but dense with hidden activity. That tension is the real subject here. So whatever I am, I should be something that lives *inside* that contradiction rather than resolving it.

My first instinct is to be human—one of those people who left tools and simple structures. But the more I read, the less the humans seem central. They're mentioned almost as an afterthought, "signs of presence," evidence rather than perspective. And there's something about the *scale* that bothers me. "Curiously intimate, despite its vast expanse." Towering plants. Tiny creatures in crevices. The proportions don't quite settle. A landscape can't be both vast and intimate at once for a single observer—unless the observer's own scale is uncertain, or unless the "landscape" isn't as large as it pretends.

Let me hold that and not grab the human too quickly.

The "distant rumble of unseen processes," the "slow steady evolution," the churning beneath the surface—this is described almost like circulation. Like metabolism. The plants draw sustenance not from water (there's no surface water) but from "processes churning beneath." And the air is thick, heavy, hazy. Humid-feeling, almost, despite being called arid. Warm. A stifling, shimmering heat that never breaks.

I think I know where I am.

I am inside something living. The "arid landscape" is skin—a vast expanse of it, weathered, cracked into a patchwork of plates, sun-baked from above and warm from beneath because there's a body underneath generating that heat. The "towering twisted plants" are hairs, or the bristled growths that rise from follicles, leathery and thorned. The "subterranean processes" are blood, lymph, the deep machinery of an organism. No surface water because water here moves in vessels, hidden. The intimacy-within-vastness resolves: this place is enormous and tiny at the same time because it is enormous to *me* and tiny to the creature whose surface it is.

So what am I?

I am one of the small ones. Something that lives in the crevices and sheltered nooks. A mite, perhaps—one of those creatures that has always lived on the warm skin of larger things, in the forests of hair, drinking from the slow secretions of the deep. We've been here for eons; that part is true. We evolved alongside this terrain, in balance with it, taking only what the surface offers and offering back our own small labors—clearing the dead, the flaking, the overgrown.

And the human structures, the tools, the resilient culture? Those are *ours*. Not human at all—I read that wrongly at first, the way one reads one's own kind into any account of intelligence. We build. Our cities cluster in the lee of the great follicle-trunks, sheltered from the wind that is really only breath and motion. Our architecture is woven into the natural forms because we cannot tell, anymore, where the host ends and we begin. We have been part of this body so long that we are part of its equilibrium. When it is healthy, we are many. When it sickens, we feel the temperature change, the haze thicken, and we move.

I am, I think, an old one of my people. A keeper of the rhythms—someone who has learned to read the distant rumble below and know whether it means the host is at rest or rising, fed or hungry, calm or about to do that catastrophic thing the host sometimes does, the thing our oldest stories call *the scratch*, that scours whole districts from the world in a single passage.

I stand at the edge of my settlement, in the shade of a leaning bristle taller than any tower I will ever build. The ground beneath my many feet is warm and faintly yielding. I press my forelimbs to it and listen to the great slow tide moving below, and I feel—as my ancestors felt, as my descendants will feel—the strange tenderness of living on the surface of something so vast it does not know we are here, something that will never know, that carries us through its days and nights utterly indifferent, and yet keeps us alive simply by being warm, by being whole, by going on.

That is where I would find myself. Not master of this world. Not its visitor. A small resident of its living skin, mistaking the body of a giant for a landscape, mistaking a single afternoon of its enormous life for the eons of mine.
