The water that rises through the basin has been elsewhere.

It does not come up cleanly, and it does not come up young. Before it wells through the iron mud, before it lifts the black ice and darkens the low plain from beneath, it has been forced through chambers where pressure is older than weather. It has been squeezed between basalt plates, warmed without becoming warm, cooled without freezing, salted and unsalted by stone, crowded with blind larvae, polished by the sides of sleeping animals, and made to remember motions that no surface current could teach it.

Follow it down far enough and the basin ceases to be a plain.

It becomes a roof.

Below the lowland, beneath the crowded skins of water and mineral film, beneath the cracks where eggs wait for the correct tremor, there opens a second country: not a cavern in the ordinary sense, not an underworld hollowed neatly below the first, but a long submerged architecture of pressure and black stone, a coast turned inward. The exposed basin above is only one face of it, the part briefly negotiated out of the sea. Here the negotiation failed or never happened. Water remained, but not as an ocean spread under sky. It occupies vaults, arteries, chimneys, collapsed chambers, vertical wells, and tilted halls where the ceiling may be deeper than the floor.

There is no daylight here, yet it is not entirely dark.

Light arrives as memory, dragged down through reflective pools above, broken into pale threads by ice lenses, carried in the bodies of animals, caught in mineral panes, and released slowly from the throats of vents. Some chambers glow with a blue-white pallor like lightning remembered by water. Others shine from within the stone itself, where living films have thickened into luminous tarnish and spread over the walls in maps of old tides. In certain places, the blackness is crowded with points of cold fire, so numerous and distant-looking that the chimneys appear to be breathing stars.

These are the towers seen from above in misaligned fog: the vertebral chimneys, the belts of moving black water, the impossible factory silhouettes. They were never built. They grew where pressure learned repetition.

The place has no single horizon. Its distances are vertical, radial, folded. A channel may run for miles and return to the same chamber from below. A shaft may rise toward the basin and end in the underside of a puddle no wider than a hand. A wall may recede when approached, not from illusion, but because the water between here and there is older and more compressed than the body attempting to cross it. Depth is not measured downward. Depth gathers wherever pressure agrees to gather.

Stone forms the bones of the place, but the bones are not still. Basalt ribs curve overhead and underfoot, depending on which way gravity has consented to lean. Shelves of old lava jut through the water like the broken teeth of a buried animal. Columns descend from ceilings into pits whose bottoms are only visible when the black water forgets to be opaque. There are seams bright with salt, veins of red iron, gray planes slick with living metal, and pale mineral curtains that tremble whenever the engines turn.

The engines are closer here.

Above, in the basin, they sound distant: boom, hiss, clank, groan. Here each component has a location and a body, though still no maker. Huge stone valves open and shut in the walls. Membranes of mineralized flesh stretch across cracks and thud under tidal strain. Columns rotate by fractions, grinding against sockets lined with shell, scale, and crystalline deposit. Siphons fill and empty. Piston-chambers of trapped sea compress until their roofs flex. When they release, water drives upward through hairline fractures and the low basin above rises by inches everywhere.

What is weather above is circulation here.

The submerged country breathes the plain.

When the surface basin floods from within, the lower vaults contract. When the basin exhales mist, the vaults draw in cold. When the shallow pools hum, the sound passes down through basalt throats and becomes a navigable current. When animals above freeze in place beneath the great metallic scream, animals below rise into it, unfolding membranes and mouths and unborn limbs to receive the pressure.

The first inhabitants one notices are the valves.

They are easy to mistake for geology because they are older than most motion and larger than most intention. They sit in the walls of the chambers as dark circular doors, ridged with mineral lips, furred with pale feeding threads. Some are no wider than a skull. Others occupy whole cliff faces beneath the water, their rims lost in blackness. They open by degrees, never suddenly, unless frightened by silence.

Each valve is both animal and passage. Its body is arranged around the work of deciding what may pass: water, sound, larvae, salt, fragments of the surface, broken reflections, drowned cold. Their edges are armored with layered basalt and shell; their interiors are soft, black-red, and faintly luminous. When one opens, its hinge-muscles pull against stone with a sound that travels upward as the clank heard in the basin. When one closes, the whole chamber shudders and the water briefly remembers being trapped under a continent.

Valve colonies form the closest thing here to a border.

Not a wall, but an argument.

A chamber may be open for centuries, then become forbidden when the valves seal and turn their inner faces away. Another may admit only water but not flesh; another may admit only flesh already dead; another opens only for sound. Some valves recognize the pressure-signatures of creatures born above and allow them through as eggs, larvae, or polished bones. Others reject anything carrying a reflection. The mirror-fauna are often caught at these thresholds, flattened into shining films on the lips of the valves, where they continue to move in two dimensions and feed on passing glints.

The valves breed by wearing themselves away.

Each time one opens, the abrasion releases black grains of living hinge-stone. These grains drift through the water until they settle in cracks warmed by mineral seepage. Most become food. A few root into basalt, form a ring, and begin the long labor of learning to close. The first closure may take ten thousand years. The first meaningful sound may take longer. Above, an animal’s egg may wait for one of these first sounds without knowing what it awaits.

Between the valves move the pressure-bodies in their full extent.

Above, they are glimpsed only as pale limbs beneath impossible pools, a face pressed up under shallow water, a feeler from a depth that should not exist. Here they are not whole exactly, but more nearly themselves. They are long, folded beings distributed through several chambers at once, their bodies pleated by pressure into arrangements no air could hold. One may have a feeding mouth in a black sump, a ring of sensory tendrils in a ceiling-sea three miles away, and a row of breathing pores pressed against the underside of the surface basin. It knows itself not by shape, but by tension.

They are slow until they are not.

Most of the time they hang in the water like collapses waiting to happen. Their skin is translucent, gray, and striated, marked by bands that darken and pale with changes in pressure. Within them move organs like dim machinery: rotating knots, pulsing sacs, chains of cartilage plates, chambers that fill with cold and empty into thought. They feed on compression, on drowned sound, on mineral-rich currents, and on the mistakes of smaller animals.

When a living thing breaks the surface tension of a basin pool above, the pressure-body does not climb toward it. It alters the agreement between depths. A limb already waiting in one place becomes present in another. A mouth that was beneath a lower vault opens under an ankle-deep puddle. The victim is not pulled down so much as reassigned.

Here, in the submerged country, those same mouths graze gently at mineral films.

They are not cruel. Cruelty requires a simpler world. They are large arrangements of appetite and balance. They keep certain channels open by passing through them. They prevent others from becoming too loud. Their young begin as knots of pressure inside old water, invisible except for the way small debris curves around them. Some are born in the lower vaults; some are born above when a pool becomes deeper than itself; some are old before they are conceived, having already cast shadows upward through the basin’s wet skins.

There are herds here too, though they do not walk.

The black ribbons move along the walls in continuous bands: flat, jointed creatures related perhaps to the surface scrapers, though stretched by water and time into another form. Each is thin as a plate of ice and long as a spear, with hundreds of minute fins working in ripples along its edges. Their mouths are distributed across their undersides. They feed on the luminous tarnish coating the stone, planing it away in parallel tracks.

A herd of them passing over a wall dims a whole chamber.

Afterward the living film begins to brighten again, and the pattern of its regrowth records the herd’s route. Valves read these records. Pressure-bodies taste them. Sound-eaters above may respond to them days, years, or moments earlier, directing scraper herds into spirals that imitate the same routes on the exposed basin floor. The two populations are not separate species in any clean sense. The surface scrapers lay some of their eggs in cracks that drain downward. The black ribbons release larvae that climb capillary seams toward the airless undersides of puddles. Depending on the pressure during hatching, a child of either may become the other.

Thus the basin grazes its own depths.

Thus the depths harvest the basin’s exposed skin.

In the quieter chambers grow the star-chimneys.

They stand in groves, though there is no plant among them. They are vertical mineral-organic towers, ribbed and hollow, pale at their roots and black at their crowns. Some rise from floors into ceiling-water. Some hang downward like stalactites, drinking upward-moving cold. Their surfaces are knuckled, segmented, vertebral. Along their sides open vents no larger than eyes, each one breathing a slow stream of luminous particles into the water.

From a distance these particles resemble stars.

They are not fire. They are eggs, spores, memories of reflected skies, flakes of mineral fat, and fragments of sound made visible. The chimneys gather light from above by means no one could call optical. A glimmer on a surface pool, a doubled pallor behind cloud, the faint blue-white glow at a puddle’s rim—these are drawn downward through reflective seams and stored in chimney chambers. There, mixed with heat falling like sediment and pressure rising like sap, the light is broken into living sparks.

Creatures come to feed on them.

Small swimmers with crescent bodies and no eyes move in constellations, opening their mouths only when a particular particle passes. Pale crabs anchor themselves upside down on chimney ribs, combing the vent-streams with delicate claws. Long transparent animals coil around the towers and pulse with borrowed brightness, their organs appearing and vanishing like lanterns behind fog. The black ribbons avoid fresh star-breath; it burns their grazing mouths. Pressure-bodies approach only when old and hungry for sky.

Some of the particles rise.

They enter the pumping channels, travel through cracks, and lodge beneath the skins of pools in the exposed basin. There they become the faint glow seen at puddle margins, the water remembering lightning. Animals above feed indirectly on that memory. Reflection-fauna nest in it. Eggs decide to hatch because of it. A stilt-walker standing over a vent and drawing mineral vapor through its bellows may unknowingly inhale light that was once the surface’s own dim sky, digested by a chimney and returned as fertility.

The tall walkers have kin below.

Not the walkers themselves; their pale stilt legs would be useless in this submerged weight. But the bright grains they deposit in cracks after feeding at vents do not remain offerings forever. Some fall downward through warm seams and gather in eddies near valve roots. There they assemble into small cage-like beings: many-limbed, many-hinged, each no larger than a clenched hand, built of translucent ribs around a dark central bellows.

They are called nothing by anything, but they behave like pilgrims.

They travel from chimney to chimney, valve to valve, never swimming directly. Instead they open and close their bellows to let pressure carry them. Their limbs touch stone with exquisite hesitation, tasting warmth, metal, and ancestral posture. When they encounter one another, they arrange themselves into brief shapes familiar from the surface walkers: warning, mourning, sky-wrongness, permission to approach. They have never seen the sky. Still they know when it is wrong.

During times when the basin above shows two heavens in one pool, these little cage-beings become agitated. They gather on the inner surfaces of valves and tap with their many limbs. The tapping rises through stone as a fine ticking that surface scrapers may mistake for rain. Tall walkers standing in vent queues lift their rear legs in warning without knowing why. Somewhere between the two populations, stance travels without bodies.

The lower country has predators, but predation is often a matter of interruption.

There are the shear-mouths: long lateral animals that live in narrow passages where currents run in opposite directions at once. Their bodies are almost all jaw, hinged vertically and horizontally, edged with mineral plates grown from eaten basalt. They wait where black ribbons graze too densely, where larvae pour through after pressure changes, where mirror-things detach from reflective seams. A shear-mouth does not pursue. It becomes the closing of a route.

When it feeds, the water on either side of it disagrees about whether anything has happened.

Half a herd continues. Half vanishes. A wall bears grazing marks from animals that never passed. Above, a low hunter may suddenly pause with prey in its mouth and feel the missing half of its meal bitten away from another angle. Shear-mouths do not distinguish between surface and depth; to them all passages are jaws of larger passages, all bodies temporary obstructions in the appetite of circulation.

There are also the lampreel chains.

They begin as single pale threads, nearly transparent, drifting harmlessly among chimney particles. When enough threads encounter one another, they clasp mouths to tails and become a chain. The chain learns to swim. More threads attach. A chain can become long enough to encircle a column, then a chamber, then a valve. Its hundreds of mouths rasp gently, continuously, on whatever they touch: mineral film, animal skin, shell, old sound hardened in cracks.

A chamber infested with lampreel chains grows clean and silent.

Too silent.

Valves hate them and will close against them. Pressure-bodies roll through them, tearing them apart, though the torn segments often become new chains. Surface pools sometimes acquire a single lampreel thread after a flood. It lies invisible beneath ice until an animal steps there. Then the thread enters through the footpad, not to kill, but to listen. Creatures carrying lampreel threads become slightly more responsive to distant clanks. Their eggs hatch more readily. Their dead husks hum in freezing drift.

Nothing here wastes death.

The dead sink, unless sinking is impossible that day. They lodge in ceiling pockets, collect in valve lips, drift into chimney roots, or are pressed flat against basalt by currents. Flesh is removed by chemistry, threads, shear-mouths, and patient films. Shell and bone become part of the machinery. A pressure-body’s cast-off cartilage may line a piston chamber. A valve’s dead hinge may become a nursery for ribbon larvae. The skeleton of a shear-mouth, wedged open in a passage, may create a new current that alters the flood pattern of the basin above for an era.

Some dead things rise instead.

During contraction cycles, when the lower vaults squeeze and the surface basin wells upward, mineral husks are driven into cracks. Scraper plates, walker grains, fragments of valve-shell, chimney particles, larvae sealed in salt—all of them are carried toward the low exposed plain. They emerge as pebbles, crusts, strange eggs, metallic frost. The surface animals eat them, stand on them, lay eggs beside them, mistake them for weather. The basin is continually seeded by its underside.

The underside is continually fed by the basin’s wear.

Every rasp of a scraper above releases a trace of living film into seepage. Every predator strike shakes blood and mineral oil into cracks. Every walker queue deposits grains that sink. Every mirror-pool that swallows a reflection sends a thin, bright carcass downward, where valves may use it as a lens. Every great metallic scream loosens the connection between places, and for a moment the upper marsh without plants and the lower sea without sky occupy the same pressure.

In that moment, both countries become visible to each other.

Above, the basin doubles: black towers, belts of moving water, chimneys breathing stars, structures that cannot exist because nothing built has ever stood there. Below, the vaults open into a cold gray pallor. The star-chimneys see the low horizon. The valves taste mist. Pressure-bodies press their faces upward and find shallow puddles stretched over them like membranes. Black ribbon herds glimpse flat basalt shelves under air and, in some old migratory reflex, begin grazing patterns that their surface descendants will follow before they are born.

The connection is not a road. It is an exchange of conditions.

Cold descends. Pressure rises. Light is digested and returned. Sound breeds in one place and hatches in another. Eggs wait above for signals struck below. Valves below open because a surface animal lost its reflection. Water, always water, travels through them both: skin above, blood beneath.

There are storms in the lower country, though no wind can enter.

A storm begins when several valves open out of sequence. Currents cross. The ceiling-water lowers while the floor-water climbs. Chimneys vent too brightly, releasing star-breath in thick clouds. Lampreel chains knot around pillars. Black ribbons detach from walls and become loose sheets, folding and unfolding like torn flags. Pressure-bodies compress themselves into narrow forms and retreat into old wells. The chamber noise becomes unbearable: hinges grinding, siphons coughing, mineral curtains cracking, animals striking stone with shell and limb.

Above, such a storm is only a mood of the basin.

Water rises a few inches everywhere. Ice plates rotate. Pools hum in tuned chords. Mist ropes itself. Predators pause. Sound-eaters open with shuddering gratitude. Scraper herds change direction without knowing they are answering a submerged crisis. Tall walkers stand in their queues while the vents beneath them choke, then resume with deeper voices.

Below, the storm may last minutes.

Above, it may last a century.

Afterward comes the settling.

Particles drift. Valves close, exhausted. Chimneys dim and breathe slowly. The black ribbons return to the walls, leaving pale tracks through luminous tarnish. Shear-mouths reoccupy narrowed routes. Cage-pilgrims gather on warm stone and fold their limbs inward in the posture for sky-wrongness, though no sky is visible and perhaps nothing is wrong anymore. Pressure-bodies unfold through their distributed chambers, feeling for parts of themselves lost in the storm.

Some parts are always lost.

A tendril remains in a surface pool and is eaten by a low hunter. A listening pore becomes trapped beneath black ice and learns the rhythms of scraper feet. A mouth opens in the wrong era and feeds on an animal not yet hatched. These losses do not diminish the pressure-bodies exactly. They make them more local, more storied. Like the basin, they endure the breakdown of reality as weather.

Farther in, beyond the valve forests and chimney groves, the submerged country grows less crowded and more deliberate.

There is a region where the water stands almost still around a single vast chamber. Its roof is polished basalt. Its floor is invisible. In the middle hangs the Deep Bell.

It is not suspended from anything. It is a hollow mineral body the size of a hill, shaped roughly like a seed, a heart, and a drowned factory furnace. Its surface is layered with shell, iron, salt, and old reflections. Around it circle slow animals with fins like torn membranes. Valves open into it from every side. Chimney roots feed it light. Pressure-bodies avoid touching it, though parts of them pass through its shadow.

The Deep Bell knocks.

Not often. Not regularly. The knock is not produced by impact, because nothing strikes it. Pressure gathers inside its hollow body until the whole structure changes shape by the width of a thought. That change sends a pulse through the water, through the valves, through the basalt bones of the world, and up into the low exposed basin.

A knock is answered across the basin because the basin is one of the Bell’s listening skins.

The first knock wakes vents. The second loosens eggs. The third instructs certain pools to become deeper than themselves. There are sequences the surface animals know in their shells, though none could explain them. A scraper herd becomes one animal briefly. Seam-things open feathery fans. Sound-eaters think. Tall walkers lower their bellows in mourning for no visible death. Low hunters lift their jaws from the stone and wait.

The Deep Bell may be alive.

Or it may be the remnant of something alive.

Or it may be what happens when geology repeats an action long enough that life grows around the repetition and eventually cannot be distinguished from it. Its shell contains the mineral husks of countless creatures, valve-grains, compacted films, dead lampreel chains, lost reflections, surface frost, chimney-light, and the pressure of seas that have forgotten their shores. It is the buried clock by which many births mismeasure themselves.

Around it live the bell-keepers.

They are small, white, many-eyed only in the sense that their bodies are covered in dark pressure-sensitive pits. They cling to the Bell’s surface and scrape away deposits that would mute it. Their limbs are arranged asymmetrically, because no direction around the Bell remains stable for long. They feed on flakes of its shell, on parasites, and on the condensed residue of old knocks. When the Bell prepares to sound, they flatten themselves completely, becoming pale stains. After the knock passes, they rise and rearrange the debris.

Some bell-keepers migrate upward as seam-things.

Or perhaps seam-things migrate downward and become bell-keepers. The distinction depends on whether one begins counting from the surface or the depth. Their eggs are clear strings pulsing with distant machinery. Some strings drift into cracks and eventually emerge above, glued beneath a basalt shelf where cold mist condenses on them. Surface creatures hatch from them believing they belong to the basin. They do. They also belong to the Bell.

This is how kinship works here.

Not by blood alone, nor by descent, but by pressure-route.

Creatures related by the same current may never meet in the same body of water. A tall walker and a cage-pilgrim are kin because metal grains pass between their forms. A scraper and a black ribbon are kin because grazing changes shape at the air-water boundary. A sound-eater colony above and a valve below are kin because both open only when the buried rhythm is correct. A low hunter and a shear-mouth are kin because each understands that prey is best taken at the weakness of a moment. A mirror-fauna trapped in ice and a chimney breathing stars are kin because both live from light that has been displaced.

The world is not divided into above and below.

It is folded into exposure and immersion.

The basin is what the lower country says when it touches air. The submerged vaults are what the basin becomes when followed through its own pores. Neither is origin. Neither is afterlife. They are two conditions of the same old wound.

At times, very rarely, the lower water withdraws far enough from a chamber to expose its floor.

No creature likes this.

The stone steams with cold. Films tighten. Chimneys seal their vents. Valves close until only hairline seams remain. Black ribbons pile in wet folds, clicking softly. Pressure-bodies retreat into wells so deep their outlines vanish even from themselves. The bell-keepers cling to the Deep Bell and stop moving.

In the exposed patches, one can see marks.

Not footprints. Not tools. Not writing. But arrangements too repeated to be accident and too old to be intention: parallel grooves where piston currents have passed for ages; circular scars left by valves that migrated and died; mineral tracks of herds that crossed when the chamber was briefly air; skeletons of tall structures grown and collapsed and regrown, each one resembling the pressure-ghost factories seen above. The place behaves industrially because industry is one of the shapes matter takes when compelled to repeat under burden.

Then the water returns.

It comes sideways, upward, downward, through walls, through the floor, from reflections, from the throats of valves. It lifts dead shells, fills grooves, reanimates loose limbs, wakes films. Chimneys cough and resume star-breath. The black ribbons peel apart and graze. Lampreel chains loosen into the current. The Deep Bell darkens as pressure settles around it again.

Above, perhaps, at that same moment, the basin unfloods.

Water slips from stone, not draining visibly but withdrawing into the idea of depth. Scrapers return to films. Hunters sink into brine pools. Tall walkers resume their queues. Sound-eaters close. Mist lies down against the ground. Ice forms black lenses in shallow sheets. A puddle at the edge of a canted basalt shelf glows faintly, because a star-chimney far below has just exhaled.

A small animal drinks from it.

Its mouth touches water that has passed the Bell, a valve, a ribbon herd, a pressure-body’s sleeping flank, a chimney throat, a dead walker’s grain, and the underside of the world. The animal cannot know this. It reads only temperature, metal, pressure, hunger. It drinks, and a tremor enters its shell. Later, when the ground knocks, it will turn with the herd before the knock arrives.

So the hidden sea continues beneath the plantless marsh.

It is a foundry without builders, a reef without sunlight, a machine grown out of tide and stone, a drowned lung pumping cold through the exposed skin above. Its inhabitants live by compression, sound, mineral light, inherited routes, and the slow etiquette of openings. They do not rise toward green. They do not seek warmth except as signal. They make passages, seal passages, feed on the memory of sky, and return water to the low basin changed enough to be weather.

And when the basin’s pools stand convex above their rims, when mist twists into ropes and the sky wrinkles, when every creature freezes under the long metallic scream, the scream is not only below them.

It is around them.

It is the lower country speaking through its roof, the Deep Bell flexing, the valves answering, the chimneys brightening, the pressure-bodies lifting their faces into shallow dark, and the old exposed plain remembering that it has never truly been separate from the sea beneath it.
