Beyond the locks that admit the seals, the sea has a mouth.

It is not a beach.

It is a coast built as an organ: a long armored reach where the outer ocean is gathered, strained, slowed, accelerated, measured, and swallowed into the interior sea under the skin of the world. From far off, in fog or stormlight, it resembles a chain of low black islands and squared cliffs. Closer, it becomes legible as machinery too large to hide: intake towers standing offshore like fortresses, spill aprons broad as plazas, caisson walls, baffle fields, trash racks, breakwaters, service causeways, acoustic buoys, light masts, gantry rails, warning pylons, sluice mouths, and enormous gates that open in the drowned base of the wall.

Here the world’s skin meets weather.

There is a sky, and because the walled sea has none, the sky feels extravagant. Clouds move without rails. Rain falls from above and is not condensation. Wind has room to gather itself and cross the water in visible darkening bands. Gulls lean on it. Foam tears sideways from wave crests. The sun, when it appears, makes real glitter on the outer swells, a wild brightness unlike the long engineered strips glowing over the interior basins.

But even here, under open air, nothing is wholly natural.

The shoreline is poured, plated, armored. The tide runs against schedules. The surf breaks on concrete teeth. Beneath the waves, the actual seabed has been replaced for kilometers by sloped revetments and layered blocks, by sacrificial aprons and vibration mats, by vast grilles set into the submerged wall. There are warning lines painted on structures no human can reach except at dead low maintenance tide. There are depth numbers stenciled on pylons and half-erased by salt. There are ladders descending into water so violent that no one uses them. There are red lenses, blue lenses, white strobes, all crusted with salt and bird droppings, all still obeyed by creatures that never learned their meanings in words.

This place is called the Mouthworks by those who service it, though the animals know it by force.

The outer ocean comes in green and gray, with kelp torn from distant beds, plankton blooms, fish eggs, sand, crab larvae, drowned insects, jelly bells, fragments of shell, storm wrack, coldness, salt, and the immense pressure of elsewhere. It meets the intake field and hesitates. Not visibly, not as a mind hesitates, but as water does when confronted with architecture: it piles, shears, turns, plunges, divides. Some of it breaks over the outer teeth in white sheets. Some slides along the breakwater faces. Some is pulled downward through submerged mouths where the grilles hum and the inner sea begins drinking.

At certain hours the whole coast inhales.

The surface lowers by centimeters along the intake wall. Floating weed turns and aligns. Fish schools elongate into silver ribbons and are drawn toward shadow. Foam patches stretch, thin, and vanish. The buoys lean inward. The kelp forests growing on the outer baffles bow all at once, fronds streaming toward the gates like banners in a wind no air can feel. The sound changes too: surf overlaid with a deep interior suction, a low continuous note traveling through the concrete and into the bones of birds standing on the rails.

Then the pumps slacken, or the gates close, or the pressure equalizes somewhere far inside, and the Mouthworks exhales.

Water comes back out through outfall tunnels warm from machinery, clouded with microbubbles and powdered mineral waste, faintly metallic, sometimes slicked with biological richness from the interior basins. It does not pour like a river at first. It blooms. Great pale plumes open beneath the surface, rising from the wall’s lower slots and spreading outward into the tide. In cold weather the outfall steams where it meets the air, making low fog that crawls across the breakwaters and turns the warning lights into red moons.

The coast therefore has two climates laid over one another.

One belongs to the true ocean: tides, storms, seasons, moon, wind, migration, winter cold, summer plankton, the long slow breathing of the planet. The other belongs to the machine behind the wall: intake demand, pump rest, filter purge, emergency overflow, heat exchange, aeration, maintenance lock cycling. Where they interfere, the living world has become clever.

Kelp grows thickest not on the oldest rocks, because there are few old rocks left, but on the roughened seaward faces of the baffle fields. The fronds are dark olive, gold at their torn edges, heavy with snails and amphipods. They rise and fall in ordinary swell, then flatten suddenly when the intake pull begins. Generations of kelp have been ripped loose by unannounced draw cycles; the ones that remain grip deep into drilled anchor pits and cracks in concrete armor. Their holdfasts lace around bolts and lifting eyes. Their forests are artificial in foundation and wild in motion.

Eelgrass has found the quieter water behind the service islands, in lagoons formed accidentally between causeways and abandoned cofferdams. There, sediment has gathered: sand from the outer shore, pulverized shell from the grilles, concrete dust from the interior sea, rust, fish bones, gull feathers, fragments of insulation, black crumbs of old rubber. The grass roots in this mixed silt and softens the hard geometry. Small fish hide there when the intake warning strobes begin. Seahorses, thin as bent wire, cling to stems and ride out the currents. Crabs bury themselves with only their eyes showing, facing the direction from which food will come when the outfall starts.

The rocks that are not rocks are crowded.

Barnacles cover the lower walls so completely that the concrete has become pale and stippled, a living abrasion. Mussels hang in blue-black masses from the grilles, fattened by the endless passage of water. Limpets graze clean ellipses around rust stains. Anemones bloom in the eddies behind ladder rungs. Sea stars move slowly over hazard stripes, prying at shells. Oysters have colonized the old inspection steps, sealing the seams so densely that workers have to cut footholds when they descend. Every flat surface becomes contested. Every edge becomes food.

The intake screens themselves are reefs and execution grounds.

On calm days fish gather before them in enormous shifting veils. Some feed on plankton trapped against the mesh. Some pick at shredded weed. Some wait for smaller prey dazed by turbulence. The screens hum gently when flow is low, violently when the pumps wake. Experienced fish feel the change early and peel away into side currents. Young ones remain too long. They strike the mesh in flashes, recover, or do not. Crabs climb the grilles after draw cycles to feed on what has been pinned there. Gulls stand above, watching through the water, screaming when the tide drops enough to expose a harvest.

The animals here do not merely endure the machine. They use its appetite.

Cormorants hunt in the slick water above the intake mouths, folding themselves into black arrows and vanishing. They know the moments after a gate change, when small fish are disoriented and rise too close to the surface. Terns patrol the outfall plumes, where warm water lifts larvae and oil-sheened insects into feeding reach. Gulls have learned the difference between a maintenance alarm and a purge alarm. The first brings men, noise, and danger. The second brings scraps.

The seals belong to this coast more than to the interior sea, though some have made the walled basins part of their range.

They haul out on the outer breakwaters, on sloped armor blocks warmed by the sun, among old bollards and tide gauges. Their bodies shine with seawater and scars. Pups are born in the lee of acoustic towers where the surf is muted by baffling fins. The older animals know the lock cycles the way their ancestors knew sandbars and tidal races. At slack intake they nose near the grilles, bright-eyed, waiting for stunned fish. When certain green pilot lights brighten beneath the water, the boldest slip into the approach channels and ride the controlled current toward the inner locks.

Those that return from the sea with walls smell different.

They surface at the Mouthworks coated not only in salt but in the faint industrial tang of the interior: oil traces, ozone, cold metal, antifouling chemicals, the mineral breath of concrete vaults. Other seals sniff them. Pups follow them. Some carry new scars from grilles or drone encounters; some have learned to hunt under false dawns and return heavy with fish that never saw the sky. These specialists are restless in ordinary tide. They sleep on the breakwater but turn their heads when the wall begins to hum, as if hearing a second ocean call from behind the first.

There are fish that make the passage both ways.

Small silver schools are born outside in the open plankton and vanish inward through the intake fields, many unintentionally, some by habit. Their young grow under roof-light and return months later through equalization flows, altered by diet, size, timing, luck. They enter the outer coast in warm bursts, spilling from outfall slots with the interior’s powdered sediment in their gills. Predators wait for them: seals below, cormorants above, larger fish in the mixing layer where warm machine-water slides over cold sea.

Eels understand the connection best.

The conduits under the Mouthworks are not as endless as those inside, but they are older in use. Eels move through tide drains, cable sleeves, cracked outfall lips, weed-dark crevices between armor blocks. Some enter the interior sea young and pale, grow fat among service trenches, then reappear years later at the outer wall, scarred, darkening, ready for some ancestral migration no engineer designed. They pass through pipes intended for pressure relief and through gaps no inspection map admits. To them, the wall is not a border. It is a complicated reef with a hollow behind it.

Octopuses live in the armor field.

They are mostly unseen until they choose otherwise. They occupy cavities between concrete blocks, old sensor housings, drowned junction boxes, empty pipe collars, the shadowed backs of acoustic pylons. Their dens are decorated with crab shells, mussel shards, bottle glass, ceramic tags, bits of red gasket, and the polished screws of maintenance drones. They hunt where the outfall plume meets the kelp edge, spreading themselves over metal and stone with equal confidence. One may change color to match rust streaks, another to match barnacle crust, another to the pale warning paint on a submerged ladder.

They are the coast’s private intelligence: not masters, not symbols, simply excellent at making use of complexity. When the suction builds, they seal themselves deep. When the purge releases warm cloudy water, they emerge and take what the machine has stunned.

There are larger presences farther out.

Whales do not come close often. The low frequencies of the pumps trouble them. The acoustic deterrents were built for their safety and therefore became part of the coast’s hostility. Still, in certain seasons, when fish gather thick along the mixing lines, dark backs roll beyond the buoy field. Their breaths stand white in morning air. Their songs reach the submerged wall as immense fragments, entering the concrete, traveling inward, becoming faint tremors in the walled sea’s outer basins. Fish inside may startle at a sound from an animal they will never meet.

The Mouthworks is also a place of remains.

Storms throw things against it. Driftwood from real rivers, broken floats, dead birds, plastic crates, kelp rafts, fish boxes, lost nets, the ribs of small boats, the bodies of seals, once even the swollen carcass of a whale that lodged against the outer teeth until cranes came. The machine catches the world. Its grilles take what the sea gives, and not all of it can be cleared before the next cycle.

There are wrecks in the baffle field: maintenance skiffs sunk in sudden draw, an old inspection submersible crushed against a side screen, crane hooks lost in storm, autonomous cleaners that failed and became reefs. Barnacles write over serial numbers. Kelp threads through steering handles. Crabs occupy battery compartments. Fish pass in and out of cockpits. A dead machine here does not become silent; it becomes porous.

The living things read color here as they do inside, though the code is mixed with weather.

Red strobes on the outer pylons mean suction or exclusion. Gulls rise. Cormorants lift off the intake lip. Seals either withdraw or, if they are specialists, move to their chosen eddies. Blue lights mean aeration or chemical balancing; fry gather in the bubble fields despite the risk. White strobes mean gate movement, sudden turbulence, a reversal strong enough to turn weed into rope and rope into hazard.

But there are also signs no machine sends: the falling glass-green wall of a storm swell, the blackening line of rain, the moon-drag of a spring tide, the pressure change before wind. Life here must know both languages. A mussel that thrives under predictable intake may be torn free by winter surf. A fish that understands tide may die in a pump-start. A gull that follows purge alarms may still be taken by a wave.

The humans of the Mouthworks are more visible than those inside, because the sky exposes them.

They live in low service stations built into the high wall, in weatherproof rooms above the gate galleries, in modular houses on the causeway where everything tastes faintly of salt. Their windows face both directions: outward to the real sea, inward through thick glass to control chambers lit by diagrams of flows. They wear orange, gray, and black. They speak over wind. They know which rails are slick with algae, which ladders are weakened by corrosion, which alarms are serious, which are old electronics complaining about gulls.

Some are engineers. Some are divers. Some are screen crews who cut away mussel masses before they become obstructions. Some are animal monitors, because the law or habit or guilt requires counting what the machine swallows. They tag seals, rescue birds oiled by minor leaks, record whale passages, sample larvae from intake baskets, scrape bacterial films from outfall lips, and note, with professional unease, how quickly the coast adapts to every adjustment.

There are also unofficial people.

Fishers anchor beyond the exclusion buoys when patrol drones allow it, because the mixing water is rich. Shell gatherers come at low maintenance tide to harvest mussels from approved sections and steal from unapproved ones. Salvagers search storm wrack against the armor blocks. Children from the service settlement dare one another onto old platforms where gulls nest and warning paint flakes underfoot. Everyone who lives near the Mouthworks learns the calendar of the pumps, because the wall feeds, kills, employs, warms, deafens, and sometimes floods them.

At night the place becomes less like a coast and more like an instrument panel laid across the edge of the sea.

Lights blink in long lines over black water. The towers show red at their crowns and green at their access doors. White strips glow along catwalks, making runways for rain. Underwater lamps reveal columns of bubbles, kelp streaming, fish flaring and gone. The interior sea’s artificial dawns sometimes leak outward through open lock throats as pale pulses beneath the surface. On those nights the seals gather near the channels, watching light rise from under the wall.

The outfall fog catches every color.

It drifts low across the armor blocks, smelling of salt, warm metal, algae, and something mineral, like a wet building cut open. Gulls stand inside it and appear headless. The horns sound. The pumps shift. Far inside, under the world’s skin, fish turn beneath ceiling lamps and crabs climb hazard signs; outside, under cloud and stars, kelp bows toward the same force.

What lives here is not the same life as in the walled sea, but it is kin to it.

The interior sends out warmth, larvae, stunned fish, mineral dust, chemical traces, altered migrants, scraps of machine, and animals carrying habits learned under roofs. The outer coast sends in eggs, plankton, seals, eels, spores, storm wrack, coldness, hunger, and the old ocean’s endless attempts to enter every opening. Between them, the gates do not make a clean border. They make a throat.

On one side, a young machine is becoming a sea.

On this side, a shore is being taught to breathe through machinery.

The Mouthworks lives as kelp gripping concrete teeth, as seals listening for lock cycles, as cormorants diving into pump-slick water, as octopuses denning in sensor housings, as mussels fattening on what the grilles cannot keep out. It lives as gulls reading alarms, as eels treating infrastructure as migration, as people counting losses in notebooks damp with rain. It lives as two oceans meeting: one with weather above it, one with a ceiling; one ancient and restless, one poured and bolted and newly full of fish.

And the current between them never stops for long.
