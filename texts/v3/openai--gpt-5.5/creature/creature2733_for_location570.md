At first, it seems that people live there.

They do, of course: families in damp rooms above workshops, old women in houses screened with cane, mill hands, pump keepers, cutters, sorters, haulers, mechanics, rope-makers, children with green stains on their knees. But after a while it becomes difficult to say that the town belongs chiefly to them. They are residents, certainly, but also attendants. They live inside something larger, something vegetal and slow-minded, and much of their daily life consists of keeping that larger life from collapsing into either wilderness or ruin.

The people are practical before anything else. They have the watchfulness of those who live with water at floor level. They notice a pump changing tone, a vine pulling too hard on a gutter, a sweetness in the air that means fermentation has gone too far, a faint bowing in a plank that means rot has reached the underside. Their knowledge is not dramatic. It lives in hands, shoulders, routines. A resident can tell by stepping on a lane whether the roots beneath are still firm. A child can hear which drainage grate is blocked. An old worker can smell when a batch of fiber will dry cleanly and when it will mildew no matter what is done.

They are not especially hurried people. The town has taught them that haste is usually wasted in wet heat. They work steadily, with pauses built into the labor: waiting for presses to close, vats to settle, rain to pass, stems to soften, racks to drain. Their movements are compact and practiced. They duck under pipes without looking. They lift their feet over thresholds automatically. They lean away from walls because walls are rarely dry. When they carry bundles of reeds or fiber, they carry them high on the shoulder, wrapped in sacking, heads turned slightly aside from the dust and sap.

Most of them have several trades. A machinist also knows how to graft supports into a living root buttress. A reed cutter can repair a belt drive. A cook may also tend a tank of aquatic plants used for filtration. Children learn knotting, sorting, and trimming almost as early as reading. No one here can afford to believe that a job ends at its proper boundary. In a dry city, there may be gardeners, plumbers, builders, and waste collectors. Here those roles overlap until they become one general civic art: keeping matter moving in the right direction.

Their homes are tidy in a way that has nothing to do with dryness or polish. Cleanliness here means circulation. Mats are hung. Shoes are raised. Food is sealed. Tools are oiled and suspended from rafters. Bedding is aired whenever the weather allows. Herbs are kept near windows not because they are decorative but because they discourage insects, sweeten rooms, and remind people when the air has gone stale. Walls may be stained, floors warped, and ceilings patched with reedboard, but nothing is permitted to sit forgotten in a corner. Forgotten things bloom.

They dress for dampness rather than modesty or fashion. Loose shirts, cropped trousers, aprons of waxed cloth, broad hats, sandals with soles that grip wet brick. Many keep a knife or pruning hook at the belt. Mill workers wear fiber masks in the dusty lofts and oilcloth sleeves near the vats. Pump crews have high boots, though they resent wearing them in the heat. Children go barefoot where they can, because bare feet know more about the ground than shoes do.

Their bodies bear the town’s marks: darkened nails, callused palms, small scars from cane edges, insect bites, old burns from steam pipes, a permanent sheen of sweat. They smell faintly of soap, plant sap, oil, smoke, and rain-wet cloth. Even on rest days they seem slightly work-ready, as if some valve might need opening or some roof-line clearing before the next storm.

They are not unfriendly, but neither are they careless with attention. Hospitality exists, but so does maintenance. A visitor may be offered tea and then immediately asked to hold a ladder, move a sack, or keep their foot off a weak board. Conversation often happens while doing something else. People talk while stripping stems, tying bundles, watching a gauge, turning cut leaves on a rack. News travels along work routes: from canal dock to weighing house, from drying loft to seed shed, from pump room to residential lane.

Their humor is quiet and often botanical. They compare stubborn people to roots in brickwork, talkative ones to leaking valves, lazy ones to floating duckweed: always present, never anchored. Children are teased for “growing mold” if they sleep late. A good mechanic is said to have “dry hands,” not because their hands are literally dry, but because things stop corroding when they tend them.

The elderly are valued less as authorities than as archives. They remember flood years, abandoned channels, which streets used to be lower, which tree roots are older than the buildings they hold, which sealed doors should never be reopened because water waits behind them. They know the town’s buried anatomy. A younger engineer may bring drawings; an old canal woman may say, “No, beneath that wall is the old spillway,” and the drawings will be changed.

Children grow up with an intimate distrust of surfaces. They are warned early that green does not always mean ground, that a mat of duckweed can hide a tank, that a vine-covered railing may have no iron left inside it, that a bright mushroom on a beam is not to be kicked. Their games are vertical and winding: racing along root steps, balancing on rails, stealing warm fruit from trellises, mapping secret passages through shade houses. They know which machines are dangerous by sound before they know their names.

The town’s plants also live there, not as background but almost as citizens of another order. They are bred, trained, cut, divided, coaxed, and restrained. Reeds in the outer marsh grow in managed plots like slow fields. Vines are tied onto wires and scolded back from windows. Mangrove roots are guided around posts until they become braces. Banana, rubber, cane, fern, sedge, duckweed, gourd, moss, algae, creeper, and useful weed each have their permitted zones, their seasons of indulgence, their moments of punishment.

Some plants are old enough to be known individually. A rubber tree leaning over a lane may have a name. A root mass holding up a walkway may be treated with the caution given to an elder relative: fed, cut lightly, never shocked. There are vines nobody removes because they have held a wall together longer than the mortar. There are shade trees blamed for every cracked foundation and defended by everyone who has ever worked beneath them in summer.

The people do not worship the plants exactly. Their relationship is too laborious for worship. They cut them, boil them, pulp them, burn them, ferment them, sell them by weight. But neither do they regard them as inert material. Plants here are partners of a difficult kind: generous, invasive, useful, and always trying to exceed the agreement. A resident learns to think of growth as pressure. Green life is not peaceful. It pushes, swells, pries, clogs, lifts, shades, feeds, and repairs. The people survive by making bargains with it every day.

There are specialists whose whole work is negotiation. Rootwrights train living supports around timber frames. Mold readers inspect warehouses and decide which bloom is harmless and which means a batch must be burned. Water clerks track canal levels, cistern reserves, overflow routes, and the depth in old cellars. Seed wardens keep fluff from choking filters. Night pumpers sleep lightly in rooms above the machinery, waking when rhythm changes. Trellis crews move through the town with ladders and hooks, cutting openings in the green mass so light can continue to reach the streets.

The town’s social order follows maintenance more than wealth. A person who can keep water moving during a three-day rain has status. So does someone who can restart an old press with improvised parts, or save a drying loft from mildew, or judge when a reed bed is ready to cut without weakening the bank. Ownership matters, but competence matters more. The marsh is too persuasive for vanity to last long.

They celebrate intervals rather than victories. A week of clean drying. A flood that rises but does not enter the houses. A machine surviving another season. The first successful cutting from a newly planted bed. A repaired walkway reopening. Festivals are probably humid, noisy, and practical: lanterns under vines, drums softened by rain, food wrapped in leaves, children running between wet tables, old workers sitting with their feet raised, watching the sky.

Their food is shaped by the place: fish when the canal gives them, rice or grain brought by barge, greens from trellises, gourds, fermented things, fried cakes, herbs that like damp soil, soups kept simmering because nothing stays crisp for long. Kitchens are among the driest and most defended rooms in the town. Salt, flour, and tea are guarded like tools.

They are wary of outsiders who admire the greenness too easily. Visitors see lushness; residents see labor. A wall covered in leaves may be beautiful, but someone has to know whether the leaves are hiding cracks. A canal edged with reeds may look natural, but someone has to clear the intake grilles. The town is picturesque only from a distance. Up close, it is a continuous act of intervention.

And beneath the human life, within it and around it, something else lives: the town itself as a damp, composite organism.

Its veins are pipes and channels. Its lungs are pumps and shade houses. Its skin is moss, brick, rust, reed panel, algae film, and leaf. Its bones are old beams, root buttresses, rails, and concrete softened by wet years. Its metabolism is circular: cuttings into mills, scraps into compost, compost into beds, water through filters, fiber into panels, panels into buildings that host more vines. It digests its own waste slowly. It repairs itself by grafting. It remembers in flood marks, abandoned doorways, roots grown around iron, and machines converted into planters after failure.

The humans who live there are not masters of this organism. They are its nervous system, its caretakers, its irritants, its immune response. They cut back infection, open blocked passages, reinforce weak limbs, harvest excess growth, and teach the next generation where the old channels lie. The plants are its flesh. The water is its mood. The machines are its heartbeat.

So whoever lives there is not one kind of being.

It is a community of damp-handed workers and root-trained builders; of children who know the difference between floor and floating weed; of elders who remember the buried canals; of vines thickening around beams; of reeds returning after every cutting; of pumps breathing under the streets; of molds waiting for neglect; of trees slowly becoming architecture.

They all live there together, though not peacefully. More accurately, they persist there in managed tension.

The people keep the plants useful.  
The plants keep the town standing.  
The water keeps them both honest.
