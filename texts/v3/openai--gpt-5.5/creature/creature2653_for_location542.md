The ones who live there are not a desert people, though outsiders would call them that. They do not belong to the surface. The surface is only weather to them: a killing brightness, a roof of heat, a place crossed rarely and with lowered eyes. They are people of the seam, of the narrow dark between mineral plates, and their bodies and manners have learned the logic of a crack.

They are slight, often, but not weak. Long-limbed in a folded way, with shoulders shaped by carrying loads sideways through tight passages. Their hands are broad-palmed, toughened by hot stone, ash, ceramic edges, salt, resin. The pads of their fingers are dark and polished as if oiled, lined with pale dust that never entirely leaves. Their feet spread on narrow steps and ladders with an instinctive care; even children place their weight as if the whole city were a sleeping animal that must not be startled.

Their skin ranges through umber, ochre, deep brown, gray-brown, the colors of fired clay and old resin smoke. Almost everyone is marked by the city: pale crusts at the edges of hair, faint burn scars on wrists, soot-dark crescents under the eyes from goggles or veils, stippled freckles where volatile oils have touched and healed. Their lips are often cracked but carefully salved with bitter wax. Their hair, when it is visible, is usually bound tight, braided flat, wrapped in fiber, or stiffened with scented ash to keep it from catching dust and fumes.

Their eyes are striking, not because they shine, but because they do not waste motion. They look sideways before upward. They read vents, wall sheen, dust direction, the angle of a hanging root. They can tell by the tremor of a lamp flame whether the lower fissure has begun to breathe wrong. They can look at a wall and know whether it is safe to carve, safe to sleep beside, safe to seed, or best left alone for three generations.

They speak softly. Not always quietly — the city is loud, and sometimes a voice must be thrown hard against stone — but speech is shaped by caution. Their language has many words for heat: stored heat, rising heat, kiln heat, living heat, false-cool heat, killing surface heat, mother-wall heat, vent-sick heat. It has many words for dryness too, but few for water, and those are ceremonial, archaic, half-mythic. A child may learn the word for rain from a song before understanding that it refers to something that falls.

They do not say they are thirsty. Thirst is too large a word, too close to panic, too useless. They say they are hollow, salted, resin-dry, ash-mouthed, in need of paste. What moisture their bodies require comes in oils, gels, dense mineral fruits, carefully prepared condensates scraped in microscopic amounts from cooled vents, and foods that release their held chemistry only in the gut. They do not drink in the way surface people drink. The idea of pouring clear liquid into a cup would seem obscene to them, extravagant and almost frightening.

Their food is dense, dark, smoky, and slow to make. Pod meal pressed into cakes. Resin curds sliced thin. Bitter starch leached through ash. Smoked root-filament, dried until it snaps. Ferments kept not wet but warm, sealed in clay with oils and mineral salts until they sour into pungent pastes. Children are given softened cakes rubbed with sweet dust from the upper orchards. Elders eat black gel from split bulbs, because it soothes the throat and makes the joints less brittle.

They carry eating knives, scraping spoons, little pouches of personal ash. A meal is often a matter of grinding, dusting, warming, pressing, licking from the fingertips. To share food is intimate, but to share shade is holier. A person may refuse a cake without insult. Refusing shade to someone in need is remembered.

Their clothing is built around breath. Wrapped layers, stiff outer cloth, inner veils softened by skin oil, masks with replaceable filters of fiber, ash, and powdered charcoal. Copper or ceramic eye slits. Hoods that can be folded up against surface glare. Belts hung with hooks, sealing plugs, vent keys, small knives, packets of bitter ash for plant wounds, charm-disks of fired salt. Wealth shows not as color but as finish: a veil woven fine enough to stop dust without choking; a copper face screen hammered thin as leaf; gloves lined with smooth ceramic scales; sandals so well fitted they make no slap on stone.

Children wear bells of seed husk or ceramic bead, not for decoration alone but so adults can hear them in side passages. Very small children are tied by long cords when brought near vent levels, and no one is ashamed of this. The city has swallowed enough beloved things.

They are not many. Or they do not seem many. Families occupy clusters of rooms bitten into the walls, but whole levels stand empty except during harvest, sealing season, kiln season, or danger. Population is a thing they speak of indirectly. They count sleeping shelves, masks, hands available for a vent-seal, mouths owed pod-cake. They know the city was fuller once, because there are names carved beside doorways no living person uses, and hearths too numerous for the present quiet. Whether the people dwindled, or the city grew in preparation for losses that never stop coming, is not agreed upon.

They organize themselves less by blood than by work and wall. There are vent-keepers, ash-tenders, kiln families, root-cutters, pod harvesters, plasterers, pipe-listeners, glass-knappers, salt-readers, seed-wardens, and those who know the old sealed rooms. A person may be born into one craft and married, apprenticed, injured, or chosen into another. The highest respect is given to those who can enter the lower passages when the wind organs begin to moan and return with correct news.

Their leaders are not kings. Authority belongs to those who have been right before disaster. An old woman with burned eyelids who can hear pressure changes through her teeth may command more obedience than a council. A plasterer who knows where the wall is secretly hollow may stop a wedding procession. A child who smells wrong gas may be listened to immediately if they have been taught well enough to name it.

They do have councils, but councils gather in practical places: at shaded widenings, beside kiln yards, under hanging root curtains where the air moves. There are no thrones. There are sitting ledges worn smooth by generations of worried backsides. Disputes are settled with witnesses and memory. Punishments are usually repairs. If you waste ash, you grind ash. If you damage a root-mat, you tend its wound through a season. If you open a vent unwisely and no one dies, you spend a year carrying seal-clay and listening to the pipes until fear teaches what pride did not.

Their religion, if it can be separated from maintenance, is a reverence for containment. They honor things that hold: walls, skins, pods, sealed rooms, sleeping masks, ceramic jars, the body, the city itself. They fear careless openings. Birth is an opening. Death is a closing. Harvest is a permitted wound. A vent is a mouth that must not be flattered.

They make offerings not of water, never water, but of useful dryness: sifted ash, fragrant resin, powdered copper, old salt scraped from family thresholds, the first husk of a child’s mask, a worker’s worn glove. These are pressed into shrine cracks or burned in kilns until their smoke stains the ceiling. The oldest shrines are almost indistinguishable from storage alcoves, and this does not trouble them. A sacred thing, to them, should also be useful; a useless sacred thing would be an insult to survival.

Their dead are not buried in earth, because there is no earth worth the name. Nor are they burned entirely, because fuel and heat are managed too carefully for extravagance. The dead are dried, salted, wrapped in mineral fiber, and placed in family alcoves behind sealed plaster, sometimes upright, sometimes folded. A small vent-thread may be left near them, not enough to disturb, only enough to keep them within the city’s breath. Names are incised on removable tablets, because walls are repaired and rooms change purpose. Memory must be portable.

Sometimes, when a dwelling is abandoned for good, its dead are not moved. The new wall goes up before them. The room becomes part of the thickness of the city. In this way, the ancestors are quite literally structural.

They are careful with strangers, if strangers ever find them. Surface travelers, half-blind from glare and dehydration, have been known to step over the slit and vanish in memory. A few are lowered in. Most do not survive the first hours unless guided: they breathe too hard, speak too much, reach for plants barehanded, ask for water. The crack-dwellers are not cruel, but mercy is measured. They will give shade, ash, a mask, resin paste. They will not endanger a vent room for a person who cannot obey a gesture.

To outsiders they may seem secretive. To themselves they are merely unadvertised. Nothing good comes from being found by those who believe every city must have a well.

They trade rarely but not never. At night, when the surface releases a little of its stored violence, small parties climb out through the slit with loads wrapped in pale cloth. They carry resin cakes, black glass blades, mineral dyes, heat-cured ceramics, bitter medicinal dusts, foil-leaf hammered thin, seeds that will not grow anywhere else but are valued by chemists, priests, poisoners, and kings. In return they bring back metals, rare fibers, stories, sometimes children, sometimes spouses, sometimes nothing but news of rains in impossible countries.

Those who travel above are changed by it. They come back with surface burns, wider gestures, strange songs, and a haunted fascination with horizons. Some never reacclimate to the crack’s closeness. Others weep with relief at the first boom of the vents below.

The city’s plants are not merely crops to them. They are neighbors, tools, dangers, archives, weather vanes, and in some cases almost kin. Each family maintains certain wall-growths the way another people might maintain animals or orchards. Children are taught to recognize household vines by touch in the dark. A root curtain that has insulated a sleeping room for sixty years may be spoken to while being trimmed. Resin trees are inherited. Bulb clusters are divided at marriage. A seed vault is guarded with a seriousness elsewhere reserved for temples or armories.

But affection has limits. The gardens are hungry in their mineral way. They will pry plaster apart, drink old mortar, invade sleeping niches, poison a room with defensive vapor if cut badly. The people love them as one loves fire: constantly, gratefully, with knives nearby.

Some plants respond to human habitation so strongly that one can read social history in their growth. Around abandoned upper dwellings, the black fans grow huge and flat, feeding on trapped heat and old soot. Near birth rooms, pale filament moss forms soft curtains where lamps are kept low. Around communal kitchens, resin vines thicken into glossy ropes. In old conflict zones — where vents were sealed hastily, where families fought over heat access, where a kiln exploded — the growth is stunted or strangely colored, as if remembering bad chemistry.

There may be other living things too, though the people deny the word “animal.” In the deepest salt niches, small crusted forms appear after vent changes: slow, blind, articulated things like folded flakes of mica or walking scabs. They are not kept. They are swept into ash and burned. In sealed seed rooms, something sometimes clicks from inside the walls when certain salts are brought too close. No one opens those walls quickly.

There are rumors of root-saints: people who went into the lower vents during old disasters and did not return, but whose voices later came through the wind organs, altered by pipes and pressure. There are stories of a worker found years after burial, not decayed but mineralized through, their wrappings threaded with living filaments and their mouth full of crystals. There are children’s tales of black-leaf mothers who nurse lost infants with resin and raise them into silent wall-climbers. Adults laugh at such stories until the pipes sing at the wrong hour.

The people themselves sometimes seem less like inhabitants than caretaking organs of the city. They patch its skin, regulate its breath, prune its overgrowth, feed its kilns, scrape its salts, listen to its warnings. Their architecture is not separate from their bodies. A vent plan is a family tree. A stair is a remembered tendon. A sealed room is a scar. They do not say “the city was built here” so much as “the crack was taught to hold us.”

Their beauty follows from this. A graceful person is one who does not strike the walls with their load. A generous person adjusts a shade cloth before sitting beneath it. A wise one knows when not to carve. A beloved voice is low enough not to waste breath but clear enough to carry through mineral turns. A fine house is not large, but thermally obedient. A good death is one that does not endanger others with panic.

They celebrate, though celebration there is not lush in the surface sense. Festivals happen during stable vent weeks, after harvest, after successful sealing, after the first safe opening of a new chamber. Lamps are hung behind black leaves so the walls shimmer bronze and violet. Kiln doors are cracked to let red light spill along the lanes. People paint their veils with powdered mineral, click seed pods in rhythm, stamp carefully on resonant stone platforms, and sing into approved shafts where the city gives their voices back layered and deep.

Their music imitates the vents: drones, pulses, long held tones that rise until they tremble. Their dances are compact, all wrist, shoulder, heel, and breath. No wide leaps. No wasted space. Lovers touch foreheads through warmed cloth. Children chase each other through ladder shadows until an elder hisses them still.

They have humor as dry as everything else. They joke about rain as if it were a foolish invention. They mock surface people for carrying water inside skins like portable emergencies. They tease newlyweds about whose family root-mat will invade whose sleeping wall first. They name stubborn children after minerals that refuse to grind.

And yet beneath the humor is a great seriousness, because every comfort in the city has been negotiated with danger. Shade can be squandered. Breath can turn. A plant can die. A wall can feed something hidden. A sealed vent can become an explosion. Memory is not nostalgia there; it is infrastructure.

What lives in the crack is therefore not only a people, and not only a garden.

It is a compact civilization of heat-herders and ash-keepers, of masked children and soft-voiced elders, of resin orchards and breathing vents, of rooms that remember more inhabitants than they now hold. It is a partnership between human patience and mineral appetite. It is life without wetness, abundance without softness, cultivation without soil, urbanity without crowds.

The inhabitants have not conquered the place. They have become precise enough to be permitted by it.
