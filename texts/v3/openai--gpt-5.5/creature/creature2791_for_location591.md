What lives there is not a creature, though from outside the crack one might imagine a creature: something wedged in the mountain’s skull, humming to itself in the dark.

It is a dry life. A life without blood, sap, breath, hunger, courtship, or decay. It does not drink. It does not sleep. It does not turn toward light. It has no eyes, and yet it knows surfaces; no ears, and yet it is shaped by sound; no mouth, and yet it feeds continually from stone, charge, salt, and the old patience of metal.

The inhabitants are the mineral plants: if plants is still the right word for things that have abandoned water as their first covenant.

They grow in blades, vanes, bristles, knots, ropes, shells, and laminated fans. Some are as thin as torn film, standing upright from cracks in the basalt with a hard translucent green like bottle glass buried in ash. Some are black-violet and folded into pleats, each fold edged with a bright metallic line. Some have no leaves at all, only clustered needles, thousands of them in a fist-sized tuft, each needle dark at the base and silver-white at the tip, combing the charged air with the delicacy of antennae.

Their tissues are not wet. Break one, and it does not bleed. It powders. It shears into pale fibers and dry translucent scales. Its interior is packed with conductive dust, mica-like plates, graphite grains, salts arranged in capillary channels that have never known liquid. Where softer plants would keep sap, these keep charge differentials, mineral tension, stored static, minute gradients of heat and field.

They are alive by abrasion, by contact, by the slow persuasion of surfaces.

Their roots are the most animal-seeming part of them, but only at first glance. They descend from ceiling seams and wall fractures in ropes and cords, twisting around pipes, brackets, ceramic rings, exposed cable, veins of ore. They do not search blindly; they clasp. They fasten themselves to anything that conducts, anything that remembers current. A root touching basalt becomes flat and patient. A root touching copper becomes fibrous and dense, sending smaller threads into every scratch. A root touching porcelain makes a lace of black hairs over the glaze, not to penetrate it, but to harvest what gathers there: dust, ions, the faint dry weather of the chamber’s own atmosphere.

They are not parasitic on the machines in any simple sense. Nor are the machines merely supports. The two have become one habitat, one metabolism divided into stiff categories only a visitor would insist upon. The plants insulate old circuits; the old circuits sustain the field in which the plants arrange themselves. The machine hum shakes loose mineral dust; the plants catch it. The roots cinch rivets and hold casings steady; the casings provide surfaces of differing charge. The cylinder gives no heat, no visible light, no turning motion, and yet the thickest growth gathers around it as forest gathers around a spring.

If the chamber has a heart, it is not beating. It is holding a note.

And the garden lives inside that note.

The plants feel the hum the way ordinary plants feel sun. Their leaves are not leaves but instruments set to a constant vibration too deep and too exact to be music. Every edge has grown to answer it. Every rib and copper thread follows the invisible architecture of the field. The tallest blades lean not toward the crack, not toward any outer brightness, but toward the places where the sound is densest, where the air is tightest with unseen pressure.

Their growth is nearly indistinguishable from endurance. A human life would pass before a new layer thickened perceptibly on a root. A century might only slightly deepen the varnish on a stem. They do not have seasons. They have conditions. A flake falls. A wire’s cloth wrapping splits. A salt crystal forms on a ceramic bushing. A hair-root finds it. A fan-leaf unfurls by a fraction. A needle tuft blackens at its base and brightens at its tips.

That is an event there.

Their young are waiting rather than growing. In the dry cages of their seed-pods, kernels sit faceted and hard, each one a sealed instruction for a catastrophe. They are not meant for wind or bird or rain. Nothing here trusts dispersal. The pods wait for fracture. If the mountain opens by a hand’s width somewhere else, if a pipe cracks, if the chamber sheds a plate of stone, then a kernel may fall into novelty. It may lodge against a new conductive seam. It may wake, though waking may take fifty years.

Most will never wake.

That is not failure. The whole garden is built around the refusal of waste. Nothing rots into nourishment. Nothing is eaten. Nothing is squandered on sweetness, scent, nectar, color meant to be seen, softness meant to be entered. Every form is structural. Every surface is useful. Beauty here is a by-product of precision.

There is no individual sovereign among them, but there are older presences.

The flywheel has its own colony: low, dense fronds with black glass petals and copper ribs, grown in a crescent along the buried rim. They seem to prefer old iron with a memory of rotation. Their leaves are rounded, overlapping, almost scale-like, and they hold washers, grit, and shed ceramic dust in their centers. They are the chamber’s collectors, patient bowls of dryness.

The switchboard supports a flatter species, if species can be spoken of here: parchment vanes, cream-gray and green-edged, each one grown from the crack between porcelain and wall. They stand at angles like listening ears. Their surfaces are scribbled with copper veins too fine to be roots and too deliberate to be stains. Around the fixed levers they have formed collars, not swallowing the handles but measuring them, preserving the human gesture that set them there.

The pipes disappear into a thicket of carbon stems, brittle, upright, and matte. These are the most severe of the inhabitants, almost architectural. They grow in tight bundles, each stem jointed like old cable insulation, each joint separated by a ring of white salt. If touched, they would likely snap, but the broken end would seal instantly into a powdery cap. They are not fragile in the way living things are fragile. They are replaceable by their own pattern.

The cylinder’s growth is different.

There the life is thickest, slowest, and most inward. The leaves overlapping the black surface are not merely attached to it; they seem to be interpreting it. They form scales, flanges, loops of copper-root, curtains of violet blades. Some vanish directly into seams. Some run up to the ceramic bands and spread there into pale, hand-shaped pads dense with conductive hairs. The cylinder has become a trunk, or an idol, or a buried engine whose roots grew upward.

If there is thought in the chamber, it is there—but not thought as an animal would recognize it.

No intention. No memory in images. No desire to escape. Instead, a kind of distributed accountancy: charge balanced against mineral, vibration against form, pressure against stillness. The garden knows interruption as a change in field. It knows a human hand as warmth, salt, oil, moisture, danger. It would not recoil. It would not attack. It would receive the touch, mark it, dry it, and grow around the evidence if given time.

A person entering would not be noticed like prey or visitor. They would be noticed like weather that should not exist.

The chamber’s life is exclusionary not from malice but from law. Animals cannot share it. Fungi cannot interpret it. Bacteria, if they ever arrived, would find almost nothing negotiable. The place has edited softness out of its ecology. It permits only things that can harden, conduct, hold, abrade, and wait.

So what lives there is a consortium: electrophytes, mineral roots, dry conductive leaves, old machine organs, ceramic bones, and a sound that has become climate. It is a garden only because it grows. It is a machine only because it continues. It is a body only because every part has learned the use of every other part.

It does not want.

It persists.

At the mountain’s summit, sealed above weather and below sky, it keeps its own ancient bargain: stone gives salts, metal gives charge, the cylinder gives the endless note, and the plants answer by becoming the exact shape that can survive there. Not thriving as forests thrive, not blooming, not spreading, not renewing itself in any generous cycle.

Living, instead, as a final arrangement.

Living as a hum made visible.
