At first it seems no one lives there. Then it becomes clear that the marsh is full of inhabitants, but they do not occupy it in the ordinary way.

They are the ones almost seen in the pools: the bare feet crossing where no body stands, the procession carrying baskets across noon-reflections, the hands implied by every smoothed seam of mortar. They live in the delay between an action and its completion. They are not ghosts exactly, because ghosts belong to a past, and this place has not allowed a past to form. They are not people exactly, because people finish gestures. These do not.

They are basin-makers who have become basin-functions.

Their bodies are assembled from the same materials as the place: damp lime, reed fiber, warm clay, seed husk, ash, water held in skin too evenly. In bright air they are difficult to see because their surfaces answer the light like plaster. In shade—where there is so little shade—they appear as figures the color of wet brick and peeled reed, long-limbed, narrow-shouldered, with palms broader than seems necessary. Their fingers are always stained dark to the second joint. Their nails are thin shell chips. Their hair, when they have hair, lies against the head like soaked grass or hangs in ropes like roots pulled clean from a trough.

Their faces are not unfinished, but they are made for attention rather than expression. Their eyes are pale gold or water-black, set wide, patient, without hurry. They blink rarely. Their mouths are small and often full of water, not because they are drowning, but because water is how they hold speech before deciding whether to release it.

They are quiet because they do not think sound should be wasted on what can be arranged.

They move slowly, but never idly. One stands for half a day beside a cistern with a reed scoop in its hand, waiting for a droplet to reach the correct weight. Another kneels on a platform and presses the same loose edge of matting back into resin, again and again, though the matting never comes free. A third walks a causeway carrying two jars suspended from a branch across its shoulders; the jars are empty, but their shadows are full. Farther off, one turns a woven wheel that has not needed turning for years or has always needed turning and never been turned enough.

They are most visible when seen indirectly. In a canal, their reflections continue working after the bank is empty. A watcher may glimpse one bending over a clay basin, but on looking up see only the frog that had been waiting there all along. Their footsteps appear briefly as darkened prints on pale plates, filled with clean water, and then smooth themselves flat. Sometimes their hands emerge from walls where water threads through stone, adjusting a channel too fine for any tool.

They do not live in the houses so much as keep the houses ready for the moment when living might resume. Sleeping mats are straightened by them. Bowls are rinsed by them. The pot held at the edge of boiling is watched by them with grave concentration. They sit beside hearths where no fire burns and warm their palms over the remembered shape of flame.

They eat very little. They take the shine from fruit without biting it. They sip condensation from lintels. They chew the white inner ends of fresh-cut reeds and spit the fibers into baskets, where the fibers become larvae, or thread, or roots, depending on which part of the marsh requires replenishment. Sometimes they open a red fruit that never falls and remove from it not seeds but tiny rolled plans: a new pool, a new frog-row, a channel that should have existed from the beginning.

Their children, if they are children, are smaller and more transparent. They live mostly in reflections. They stand ankle-deep in pools that reflect ladders not present, learning how to climb what is absent. They press their hands into soft clay and wait for the clay to remember them. They follow dragonflies without moving their feet. They count causeway tiles in both directions and laugh soundlessly when the numbers disagree.

The oldest among them are almost indistinguishable from arrangements of wet stone. They sit in plazas too bright for human eyes, backs straight, hands open on their knees, water gathering in the lines of their palms. The carved signs on the upright stones are not writing but portraits of their duties: wave, eye, leaf, ladder, hand. The elders read these signs by filling them with water and watching which mark dries last.

They have no king, unless the level of water is their king. No temple, unless every cistern is a temple. No law, unless stillness is law.

Their society is made of maintenance.

One caste tends beginnings. They open buds without letting flowers pass into wilting. They keep fruit at the precise heaviness before fall. They polish eggs that will never hatch, not from cruelty, but because hatching would disturb the pattern.

Another caste tends almost-endings. They lay green skins of algae on water and prevent them from becoming rot. They hold decay at its first sweetness. They know how to keep a reed yellow at its cut end forever. They stand beside dead insects that have not yet realized they are dead and stroke their backs until their wings resume shining.

Another caste tends distances. These are the thin ones seen at the far edges of the basin, where the retaining wall gives way to brightness. They walk away continuously and never arrive elsewhere. They adjust the horizon. They decide which pool may be seen but not reached, which grove must remain apart, which wall should stop before becoming useful. They carry measuring cords knotted with unequal intervals. Their footprints are longer going than returning.

Another caste tends causes. These are rare and treated with caution. They sit beneath dry jars that feed flowing spouts. They hold invisible weights in their laps. They listen to the incline of canals where water refuses to obey incline. When something threatens to happen for a reason, they gently remove the reason and store it in a sealed clay bowl.

They do not fear visitors, but they dislike completion. A stranger crossing a plaza is a danger because footsteps insist on sequence: lifted, placed, lifted, placed. A stranger drinking from a pool diminishes it. A stranger plucking fruit chooses one season over the others. So the inhabitants watch, not hostile, but concerned. Frogs are their first sentries. Herons are their tall, white announcements. Lizards carry warnings along hot plaster with the flick of their throats.

If a visitor remains respectful, the basinfolk allow them to pass through the unfinished noon. They may even offer hospitality. A bowl will appear on a threshold, filled with water so clear it tastes like the memory of rain. A mat will be unrolled in a house where no one entered. A handprint will darken on the wall beside the visitor’s shoulder, wet and warm, as if someone has just leaned there.

But if the visitor tries to repair what is strange—to free a suspended droplet, lower the heron’s foot, push the gate that knocks without current, finish the curling of a vine—the inhabitants gather.

They come without hurry from every direction: from reed huts, from pools, from under the pale clay plazas, from the reflections of ladders, from the scent-boundaries where mint becomes ash. They stand around the intruder with their broad damp hands hanging open. They do not attack. They wait until the visitor understands that here, finishing is a kind of violence.

Their language is not made of names. It is made of kept conditions.

A lifted palm means remain.

Two fingers dipped in water mean not yet.

A bowl turned upside down means cause removed.

A reed split lengthwise means this has happened before, but not earlier.

When they must speak aloud, their voices sound like water traveling through porous stone. They use verbs more than nouns. Their oldest prayer, if it is a prayer, means something like:

Be arriving.  
Be opening.  
Be held.  
Do not pass away into having been.

They did not build the marsh once. They are building it always. Every fingerprint in clay is current. Every cord mark is being pressed now. Every plank gate has just been hung. Their labor is not repetition but suspension, the continuous crafting of a world that must remain at the moment of becoming convincing.

Perhaps long ago they were ordinary humans who tried to make a wetland where none could exist. Perhaps they lived in a dry basin and desired frogs, shade, fish, fruit, rain. Perhaps they copied every process they could observe—seepage, germination, silt, rot, reflection—until the copy became more obedient than nature. Then, having made a world that did not need time, they had to surrender their own time to tend it.

Or perhaps there were never ordinary humans. Perhaps the marsh imagined builders because every made thing requires hands behind it. The hands appeared first as prints in clay, then as reflections in water, then as figures carrying jars beneath the noon sky. In that case, the inhabitants are not the makers of the basin. They are the basin’s explanation for itself.

They love it. That much is certain.

They love the artificial mud without stones. They love the planted distance between one grove and another. They love the frogs in rows, the decorative fish, the white insects like beads, the birds that step without splashing. They love the clean channels, the sharp corners, the reed roofs still yellow at the cut ends. They love the fact that nothing here has been left to accident or inheritance. To them, wilderness is not freedom but neglect. A thing is alive because it has been placed carefully and kept in relation.

And yet there is longing in them.

Sometimes one stands at the low retaining wall and looks into the bright rearranging distance beyond. Sometimes one lifts a fruit and almost lets it fall. Sometimes a child-reflection puts one foot on the first rung of a ladder that is not there and looks upward with something like impatience. Sometimes, at the edge of a pool, a basin-maker opens its mouth and releases a single bubble of speech.

The bubble rises.

It does not break.

That is how they live: in the warm, shining interval before the surface gives way.
