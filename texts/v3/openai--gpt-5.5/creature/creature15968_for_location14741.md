What lives there is not quite a people in the surface sense, though they are human enough to bleed, complain, fall in love, hoard small luxuries, and misjudge the weather.

They are a population made by compression.

Not dwarfish, not pale cave-myth creatures, not blind; the place is too young for that, and they still remember—through grandparents, songs, contraband drawings, and the shock of fallen birds—that there is a sky. But they have become narrow in habit. Economical in gesture. Careful in appetite. They move as though every limb has learned the price of knocking something loose.

Their bodies are lean without being frail. Heat has taken the fat from them; labor has corded their wrists, shoulders, calves. They tend to have darkened skin from minerals, lamp-smoke, and tannins rubbed in as protection against damp rash. Their hair is usually cropped, braided flat, wrapped, or netted close to the skull, because loose hair catches in belts, molds in sleep, or drinks too much moisture from the air. Beards are uncommon among machinists and condensary workers, more common among root-tenders, who oil them with bitter seed paste to keep mildew out.

Their eyes are large-seeming, not by mutation but by use: pupils trained to the dim, brows accustomed to squinting through steam and red light. Many wear glass beads or polished mineral lenses on cords at their necks, not as spectacles exactly, but as inspection tools—for reading cracks, checking water clarity, finding mites under leaves, seeing the tremor in a pressure needle.

They dress in layers that can be opened, tied, wrung, patched, and dried. Sleeveless underwraps of absorbent cloth. Outer aprons of waxed fiber or thin treated leather. Knee guards. Soft-soled shoes with split toes for ladders and wet pipework. Belts hung not with weapons but with little instruments: chalk, folded copper spoons, pruning hooks, valve keys, wick knives, sealing wax, thread, measuring reeds, slips of slate.

Children wear bells until they are old enough to understand where not to step.

Not loud bells. Nothing here is loud. Small ceramic clackers sewn at the hip or ankle, enough for an adult to hear them approaching a cistern scaffold, a narrowing gallery, a sleeping gutter, a floor plate newly marked unsafe. A child who has gone three full dry seasons without breaking a pipe, wasting a ladle, or stepping across a fresh chalk line is allowed to cut the bells off in a small family ceremony. The bells are not discarded. They are hung somewhere useful: beside a valve, under a drip, on the trellis of a fruiting vine. The city keeps childhoods as warnings.

They do not hurry unless something is burning, cracking, birthing, or spilling.

Their walking has a grammar. A surface-dweller might mistake it for fatigue. It is not. It is listening through the soles. They know which stair answers with hollow sound, which metal plate flexes in damp weather, which balcony sways harmlessly and which sways with a new note. They step around root crowns as naturally as others step around dogs. They touch walls when they pass them—not sentimentally, but to read temperature, seepage, vibration.

A greeting is often a hand offered palm-down, dampness visible. Dry palms mean thirst, fever, or too long near furnaces. Wet palms mean work, luck, or recent rain-rise. Close friends press the backs of their wrists together, exchanging heat rather than embracing. Full embraces happen, but sparingly. Bodies are warm, and warmth is not always comfort.

They speak softly and precisely. Their language, whatever its older surface root, has grown dense with words for moisture, failure, tension, salvage, and partial success.

There is one word for the first bead of water that forms on metal before a drip begins.

Another for water that looks clear but tastes of copper.

Another for a crack that has stopped widening but is not forgiven.

Another for the smell of hot rain entering stone.

Another for the shame of having used clean water on something that could have taken grey.

They rarely say something is “broken.” Broken is too simple. They say it has “asked for another form,” or “entered repair,” or “lost agreement with its load.” A person can be spoken of the same way after grief, fever, or disgrace.

Their society is organized less by family name than by maintenance obligation.

There are cistern houses, root wards, furnace lines, shaft crews, mold families, brace guilds, gutter-keepers, lamp stewards, survey children, condensation clerks, fungus renters, pressure listeners. A person may be born into one obligation and apprenticed into another, but no one lives entirely outside the loops. Even the very old are given work: sorting seeds by touch, listening for changes in dripping while others sleep, telling children which walls used to weep and no longer do.

The most respected are not rulers in the usual sense. They are the people trusted to decide interruption.

Who may stop a furnace before its scheduled cooling.

Who may cut a root thicker than a thumb.

Who may divert a drip.

Who may declare a walkway temporarily dead.

Who may say: leave this room now, the ceiling is thinking.

These decisions are not made dramatically. There are no proclamations from balconies, no grand councils in bright halls. Authority arrives with a lamp, a slate, and three witnesses. A mark is made. Work changes course. People grumble, but obey, because everyone knows somebody whose uncle ignored a small warning and lost a whole stairwell.

They have laws, but the deepest laws are habits worn into the body.

Do not drink from the white basin.

Do not take the last dry cloth.

Do not mock someone who fears a crack.

Do not leave a tool where a root can grow around it.

Do not curse a child for spilling water until you know whether they tried to save it.

Do not pretend not to hear a drip.

The last is perhaps the oldest city-law. A drip may be a blessing, a leak, a warning, or a theft. To ignore one is to refuse citizenship.

Their homes are stacked, partitioned, borrowed from structure. A household might occupy three rooms that do not touch: a sleeping shelf above a dye flue, a cooking niche two ladders down, a shared washing alcove in another ward entirely. Privacy is made with curtains, steam, sleeping schedules, and politeness. Walls are thin when they exist at all. Secrets survive not because they are hidden, but because everyone has learned which things not to notice.

Beds are hammocks, slatted shelves, fungal-fiber mats rolled up by day. Infants sleep in suspended baskets that rock with the vibration of pumps. Lovers meet in warm service ducts, disused scaffold pockets, trellis shadows, and behind the great stacked looms during shift-change, where the belts make enough soft sound to cover whispering.

They cook slowly. Fuel is dear, heat is everywhere but flame is rationed. Much food is steamed, pressed, fermented, softened in mineral-warm water, dried near exhaust, or cultured in covered trays. They eat root cakes, sour vine fruit, pale mushrooms, algae sheets, bitter greens, seed oil, vat curd, mineral salts scraped and purified, tiny fish or eels when rain brings them, and on festival days, real grain from above if trade has been kind.

Surface food is regarded with both desire and suspicion. Apples are almost mythical: wastefully full of water, crisp in a way that seems violent. Fresh milk spoils too quickly and is spoken of like a fragile guest from a foolish country. Honey is treasured because it does not rot. Leaf tea from sun-grown plants is drunk at funerals, births, and ceiling repairs.

They do trade with the surface, though not freely.

At the shafts and service lifts there are exchange rooms where air changes character. There, surface merchants lower sacks of grain, salt blocks, thread, medicinal bark, tools, lamp parts, sometimes books damaged by damp before they can be properly sealed. In return the city sends up ceramic condensers, heat-worked glass, mineral pigments, fungal leathers, compact engines, root oils, pressure gauges, and strange fruits that bruise black in daylight.

The people who travel upward are few. They are called something like “glare-bearers” by those below, half admiring, half pitying. They wear smoked lenses and skin wraps and return sunburned, hoarse, overstimulated. Many come back with headaches from distance. The horizon unsettles them. Open air feels less like freedom than exposure. Wind is particularly offensive: invisible, unowned motion touching everything without permission.

Still, some are addicted to the surface. They volunteer again and again. They speak too much of clouds. They are considered useful but unstable.

Children born below are taught the sky in diagrams.

A blue bowl.

A white fire.

Water falling from above without pipes.

Plants growing with no one counting their leaves.

Animals larger than a sleeping mat.

These lessons are necessary because ignorance is dangerous, but they create strange longings. Some children press their faces under shaft grates during rain-rise and scream with laughter when cold droplets strike them. Others cry. A few become quiet afterward for days.

The city’s births are communal in practice, if not in sentiment. Pregnancy is difficult in the heat. Labor rooms are placed near stable walls, never under suspended tanks, with cooled plates hung nearby and root-fans turned by hand. Newborns are wiped with boiled greywater first and clear water only at the brow, mouth, and hands. The first gift to a child is not a toy but a cup, marked with the household stamp and the child’s drip-right.

Drip-right is symbolic at first. Later it becomes practical. Every resident has an allotment, but allotment is only the outer shell of a much more intimate economy: favors of water, remembered ladles, debts measured in clean rinses, apologies paid in condensation tokens, marriages negotiated partly by cistern access and partly by whether the two families’ work schedules waste heat together efficiently.

Marriage itself is less important than linkage. People form households by blood, trade, repair, friendship, shared walls, shared cisterns, and old rescue. A brace crew whose grandparents survived the same collapse may consider itself kin forever. A root ward may adopt a machinist because her exhaust line keeps their tubers alive. A child may have three mothers, two legal uncles, a dead father whose name is spoken to a pump he designed, and a godparent who is technically a fungal leaseholder.

Their dead do not go into the ground. The ground is too close, too active, too necessary. Burial would be both impractical and rude.

The dead are dried where possible, wrapped in cloth inoculated with chosen fungi, and given to decomposition chambers maintained with great care. Bones are cleaned, marked, and incorporated into family niches, tool handles, measuring rods, trellis charms, or the white aggregate used in memorial plaster. To become part of a wall is not considered grim. It is a continuation of citizenship.

There is a phrase said after death: “May they hold where placed.”

It applies equally to a person, a bolt, a graft, a promise.

They are not conventionally religious, but reverence permeates their maintenance. They do not worship the Condenser Tree exactly. They maintain it, decorate it, argue beneath it, repair it, and bring it tokens of failure. That is more serious than worship. Gods can be begged. Systems must be understood.

Still, the children whisper that the Tree dreams in drops.

The old say the city has three breaths: the hot breath from below, the cool breath from above, and the green breath in between. A person’s health is judged by whether these breaths are balanced in them. Too much lower breath: fever, anger, red skin, reckless work. Too much upper breath: faintness, longing, distraction, talk of leaving. Too much green breath: sleep, softness, rot, forgetting one’s tools.

Healers are part physician, part plumber, part gardener. They treat fungal lung, mineral rash, heat-swoon, root allergies, machine crush, and the melancholy called “wide hunger,” which afflicts those who have seen open landscapes and cannot make their minds small enough again. Treatment may include bitter draughts, cooled cloth, darkness, controlled visits to shaft light, hard work sorting valves, or, in stubborn cases, a surface journey so the sufferer can discover whether longing survives arrival.

The ward growers are among the strangest and most beloved residents. They know plants as one might know difficult relatives. They can tell by touch whether a vine is drinking too much from a wall. They graft fruiting stems onto structural roots. They shave fungal shelves without killing them. They pollinate flowers by hand with brushes made from gecko shed and hair. Their fingernails are often green-black. They smell of loam, vinegar, and lamp oil.

A good ward grower can stand in a corridor and say, “This place will crack in two years if we do not persuade the roots downward.”

Persuade, not force. Plants are never commanded here. They are bargained with through moisture, heat, pruning, darkness, mineral feed, and patience.

Machinists, by contrast, are dry-humored, scarred, and exact. They tend to distrust anything that grows while one is not looking. Yet even they tie leaves around hot pipes, seed moss onto insulation, and speak respectfully to the algae beds that clean their washwater. Their machines bear plant names; the plants bear inventory numbers. Over generations the distinction has become almost impolite to emphasize.

The young are apprenticed early. Not cruelly, but necessarily. A six-year-old may be trusted to sort ceramic washers by size, to catch clean drips in a cup, to recite warning marks. At ten, they learn shaft maps. At twelve, they are taken to the raw edges and shown the place where the city stops. This is considered essential.

They must see that habitation is not guaranteed.

They must see stone unsoftened by hands.

They must see the black glass wall pulse.

Most return subdued. A few return thrilled and become surveyors.

Surveyors are a breed apart. They are the ones who enter new cracks, crawl through hot mineral seams, listen beyond plaster, test air with flame and fungus, and mark possible futures in chalk. They are not numerous. They die more often than others. They are admired, distrusted, flirted with, and watched for signs of having heard too much stone.

They carry thread spools to find their way back, mirror chips to catch weak light, little cages of moss that darken in bad air, and narrow hammers whose use is governed by more taboo than any weapon. A surveyor who strikes stone without need can be exiled from the profession. One who finds a new sweet seep may have children named after them for a generation.

The city also contains those who do not quite belong to any guild: storytellers, debt counters, lamp singers, mildew scrapers, illegal distillers, repair widows, surface-born refugees, root thieves, grief-sleepers, makers of toys from failed gauges, and the quiet class of people who simply watch.

Watching is valuable. A woman whose legs no longer carry her may sit above a condensation run and know before any gauge that the air has changed. A boy considered too dreamy for machinery may notice that geckos have left a wall before the plaster bulges. An old drunk may be tolerated because, for reasons no one can explain, he can smell rain-rise six hours before the shaft bells.

There are criminals, of course, but crime takes local forms.

Water theft.

Heat theft.

False dryness reports.

Unlicensed root cutting.

Diluting medicine with bitter cistern water.

Bracing a private wall with public iron.

Keeping a hidden cool chamber.

Opening a valve out of turn.

Sabotage is rare and treated almost as madness. To damage the city is to injure the body that contains you. Punishment is usually restitution through labor. Severe offenders are assigned to dry work: sorting ash, carrying kiln brick, scraping mineral scale in hot ducts. Exile to the surface exists, but it is considered extreme, almost a death sentence—not because the surface is uninhabitable, but because it is socially vast. A person cast upward has been removed from the loops that make them meaningful.

Their art is small, durable, and integrated with use. Patterns etched into pipe collars. Lullabies timed to pump cycles. Clay cups shaped to waste no drops when tilted by a child. Murals painted on walls that no longer bear load. Shadow plays cast through steam using lamp cages. Tiny gardens inside broken gauges. Brace pins with family marks. Funeral songs performed as work chants while plaster is mixed.

Music avoids strong resonance. No great drums. No trumpets. Strings are muted. Flutes are soft and breathy. Percussion is finger-tapped on cloth-wrapped ceramic, or played on hanging bowls already used to catch drips. Their melodies descend more often than rise. When they sing of the surface, the tune often climbs too high and becomes uncomfortable; singers smile apologetically afterward, as if they have opened a door too wide.

They are not joyless. They have festivals, though festivals are tied to function.

The First Condensation after a long dry heat.

The Cleaning of the White Basin.

The Day of Cut Bells.

The Root-Turning.

The Sealing of New Cracks.

Rain-rise itself is not a holiday because it requires too much work, but the evening after a well-managed rain-rise can become one. People gather on galleries around the Condenser Tree. Clear water is ladled ceremonially into public vessels. Children are allowed to taste sour fruit dipped in salt. Old gauges are cracked open and their brass faces polished for ornaments. Someone tells the story of the first builders, who came into the wound with ropes and lamps and immediately began arguing about drainage.

No one agrees on why the first settlers stayed.

Some say they were miners trapped by war above.

Some say surface heat or plague drove them down.

Some say the fresh cavern was discovered beneath a ruined factory, and the machinery came first, people second.

Some say the wound opened and sang with warm water, and the first people mistook usefulness for destiny.

Whatever the truth, the current inhabitants do not think of themselves as refugees. Refuge has an implied afterward. They are after the afterward. They are citizens of an unfinished accident.

And they are not the only residents.

The vines live there in a way almost equal to people. Some have names older than households. There is a silver-backed vine on the east galleries said to have drunk from the first condenser plates. There is a root mass under the west kilns that holds three ledges together and is fed with ceremonial scraps. There are pale heat-plants in the old lift shafts, translucent and swollen, that glow when bruised and are harvested only during certain repairs. There are fungal lineages leased, inherited, fought over, and courted by changing the composition of sawdust and cloth.

There are also machines that have lived long enough to acquire temperament.

A pump that starts more easily if warmed with kitchen vapor.

A lift that stalls when overloaded by strangers but carries children smoothly.

A distillation column that sings before pressure trouble.

A loom whose belts must be replaced in pairs or it shakes angrily for days.

No one claims these machines are conscious in a simple way. But no one who depends on them speaks of them as dead matter either. In a place where everything responds—stone to pressure, roots to water, metal to heat, fungus to breath—the category of “alive” has become practical rather than philosophical.

If it changes when neglected, if it fails when insulted, if it keeps you alive when tended, then it is owed manners.

Even the stone is a resident, though a dangerous one. Young stone. Unsettled stone. Stone still remembering its breakage. The people do not imagine it as malicious. They imagine it as burdened, half-awake, not yet accustomed to carrying a city in its wound. They give it brass pins, plaster, iron ribs, root nets, silence. They date its cracks like birthdays. They forgive it nothing, but they attend to it constantly.

The fauna are minor but storied. Geckos near lamps are considered lucky because they prove insects are present and poison is not. Blind eels are gathered after rains by children under supervision, then released into drainage sumps or eaten in tiny portions by elders who claim they strengthen the blood. Silver cistern insects are tolerated if they remain below the lid line. Mites are enemies in mushroom houses but ignored elsewhere. Bats, if seen, are an omen of upper openings and are never harmed.

A bird fallen through the shaft becomes a crisis of meaning.

Some want to save it.

Some want to study it.

Some want to release it upward immediately.

Some want, secretly and shamefully, to keep it, because it is color and panic and sky made flesh.

Usually it dies. The air is wrong, the light too weak, the cage too narrow, the city too interested. Its body is given to the growers, its feathers distributed as rare teaching objects. Children touch them and are told: this is what distance makes.

The people themselves, if asked what they are, would not say undergrounders. They are not deep enough for that. They would not say miners, though mining is in their ancestry. Not gardeners, not machinists, not refugees, not cave folk.

They might use a word meaning “those who keep the damp.”

Or “the held.”

Or “the ones between breaths.”

They know they live in a place that cannot be taken for granted. Their pride is not conquest but continuation. Every morning the city has not collapsed, dried, flooded, rotted, overheated, or choked. Every morning is therefore an achievement, though no one says so grandly. They rise, check the walls, rinse with what can be spared, trim leaves from valves, listen to pipes, send children with chalk, measure the cisterns, argue softly, mend what shifted in the night.

They are intimate with incompletion.

They do not dream of permanence as surface empires do: towers, monuments, stone declarations meant to outlast weather and kings. Their permanence is iterative. A patch over a patch. A graft over a break. A new path for old vapor. A child taught not to waste a drip. A crack noticed before it becomes a death.

They live as if survival is not a state but a craft.

And because of that, they have become beautiful in a way that would be easy to miss: not shining, not free, not innocent, but attentive. A whole people trained to notice the small beginnings of disaster and nourishment alike.

They are warm-skinned, soft-voiced, mildew-scarred, clever-fingered, half-lit. They smell of metal, leaf oil, salt, wet cloth, and smoke. They carry cups with their names on them. They distrust silence until they have listened to it properly. They love children, clear water, stable ceilings, useful plants, and machines that answer honestly. They fear open flame, false dryness, wasted motion, and the huge blank indifference of the sky.

They live below the surface, in the fresh wound, not as parasites and not as masters.

As clot, perhaps.

As moss on a scar.

As careful breath inside the earth before the earth has decided whether to heal around them or swallow them deeper.
