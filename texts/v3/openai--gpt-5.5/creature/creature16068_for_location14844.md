What lives there is not one kind of creature.

It is a community, but not a soft one. Nothing there is made to be held. Nothing there carries the rounded ease of wet life. The garden’s inhabitants are edged, resonant, conductive, full of hollows and vanes and charged dust. They live as instruments live: by tension, by being crossed by forces, by answering correctly when struck.

The first inhabitants one notices are the tall ones.

They resemble trees only because they stand upright and persist for centuries. Their trunks are glassy, layered, translucent in places and smoky in others, with internal ribs like the bracing of towers. They are not passive plants. They listen through their length. Each hollow stem measures pressure, ion density, pitch, the angle of sunlight, the metallic taste of approaching herds. Their crowns open when the air is profitable and shut with the sudden precision of hands around a flame.

They feed from the sky.

A wet tree drinks from below and spends its life resisting gravity. These do the opposite. Their hunger is upward. Their roots are anchors, contracts, memory-lines sunk into basalt powder and old buried conductors, but their mouths are high. Dust enters them. Voltage enters them. Wind enters and is sorted. They comb minerals from turbulence, unfold membranes to catch charged particles, and at night discharge faint auroras along their ribs. In dry storms, they become ecstatic: every vent open, every flute-throat sounding, every hidden chamber vibrating with computation older than instinct.

Their fruits are rare and not meant for sweetness. They are clear capsules, faceted like grown crystal, filled with rotating mineral smoke. When mature, they detach without falling. They hover for several days in the lee of the parent stem, learning the local wind. Then, if the pressure-song is right, they crack and release a seed like a splinter of black glass wrapped in pale powder. Most seeds never become trees. Some become listening posts. Some become parasites of stone. Some are eaten by winged things and hatch later in the animal’s bones.

Below them live the low mats.

They are older than they look. From a distance they seem like velvet, lichen, frost-dark fur spread along stone. Up close each patch is a city of retractile filaments, sensors, jaws, spore-tubes, signal hairs, and dry nerves. The mats are the garden’s skin. They know footsteps before the tower does. They taste shadows. They remember the trail of every grazing herd by the pattern of salt left in hoof-scratches.

They do not think quickly, except during storms. Most of the time their thoughts are slow changes of color: copper deepening to bruise-red, jade paling toward ash, blue-black turning glossy before a pressure drop. But when the western wind strikes and the basin begins to sound, the mats rise together and become a single aware surface. Filaments touch and spark. Signals cross the bowl in crackling waves. A birth on one side, a death near the plinths, a foreign mineral on a traveler’s boot, a leak of forbidden vapor—all of it passes through them.

If the garden has a memory, much of it lies in the mats.

They are not kind, but they are exact. They will withdraw from a careful foot. They will strip an injured animal to bone. They will seal around a fallen seed to keep it from being shattered by salt wind. They accept offerings only in forms they can read: powder, charge, vibration, ash, finely ground ceramic, the last breath of certain insects.

The flowers are sharper citizens.

They live briefly and ceremonially. A flower may wait ten years inside a closed mineral pod, jointed and armored, appearing dead to any eye that expects softness. Then a tone passes through the cliff at the necessary frequency, or a grazer herd sings alarm in the right harmonic, and the pod unfolds.

Its petals are knives of translucent mineral, green-white, amber-black, violet clear as cold. They open with a chime that is also a claim. Around them gathers magnetic pollen, not drifting aimlessly but arranging itself according to fields emitted by the flower’s core. Some pollen seeks other flowers. Some adheres to passing animals. Some threads itself into the bodies of old machines. Some becomes a map in midair, lasts four seconds, then disappears into the next gust.

The flowers are reproductive organs, yes, but also announcements. They declare which underground circuits still function. They reveal where pressure has changed. They expose invisible agreements between plant, machine, and cliff. When too many bloom at once, animals leave the basin floor and climb to ledges, because a mass flowering can rewrite the next generation of everything that breathes there.

The animals are harder to divide from the air.

The small fliers are everywhere and almost never still. They are called insects only by those who need a familiar word. They are triangular, lacquer-dark, metallic along the edges, each body stretched over a sparse interior scaffold. Some have eyes like chips of mirror. Some have no eyes and steer by electric taste. Some carry a coil in the sternum that hums when they bank. Their wings are not wings in the usual sense but tensioned membranes that lock into wind the way a blade locks into a handle.

They hunt pollen. They hunt one another. They carry spores between crystal flowers and static mats. They scrape conductive films from the organ-stems. They court by collision, striking each other in midair with sounds too high for many ears. Their swarms form temporary intelligences—brief, angular minds that know the shape of the basin’s currents better than any single body could.

A swarm can make a decision.

It can decide to avoid the tower. It can decide to descend on a dying grazer. It can decide to thread itself through the plinth ring and come out changed, with new markings on every back. During dry storms the fliers gather into columns and become almost a voice. Not a speaking voice. A cutting, continuous cry with intention braided through it. In those moments they seem less like animals than like sparks from a larger nervous system.

On the ground live the polished seed-things.

They are many-legged, low, glossy, tireless. Their shells resemble obsidian, ceramic, enamel, sometimes dark green stone veined with silver. They click as they move. They are the gardeners in the narrowest sense, though no one appointed them. They prune the mats, clean the bases of the hollow stems, open channels in powder after a salt-slide, and build towers of grains and saliva-less cement that stand just long enough to alter airflow around seedlings.

They are also thieves.

They steal pollen. They hollow seeds. They carry microscopic machine-parts in their gut and deposit them in new places. They farm colonies of smaller white mites inside cracks in their shells. When one dies, the others take it apart within minutes, sorting its body into mineral, memory-knot, carbon splinter, usable hinge. Nothing wet marks the death. There is no stain. Only a brief intensification of clicking.

The larger grazers come and go like weather with bones.

They are high-shouldered, narrow, solemn animals, built for caution. Their legs are long and jointed to place their feet precisely between zones of influence. Their hides are pale gray or salt-brown, often striped with darker conductive bands. Their faces are masked in bone, not as armor alone but as resonators: plates, chambers, vents, ridges grown around the skull into shapes that amplify breath.

They do not graze leaves. They inhale.

They lower their mouths to root vents, flower bases, cracks in plinths, old exhaust pores in buried machines. They draw in mineral vapor, ion-rich exhalation, powdered nourishment lifted by warm pulses from below. Their throats contain vibrating plates, and these plates are social organs. A calf learns its herd not by scent, but by chord. Mothers call in layered metallic hums. Dominant animals carry bass notes in their chests. Alarm spreads as a bowed shriek that makes loose salt jump from the ground.

They know the safe sounds.

A grazer can cross the basin during turbulence by listening through its hooves. It feels which plinth is waking, which hollow tree is under strain, which patch of mat is hungry, where dust has hidden a pressure seam. Old individuals become almost priestly. Their masks grow elaborate and asymmetrical, etched by pollen and wind. The herd follows them not because they lead with certainty, but because they hesitate at the right moments.

When they die, they continue participating.

Their bodies are not left to rot. Singing insects enter first through the throat-plates. Root filaments follow. Dry fungi bloom along the ribs as pale fans. The skeleton is cleaned, tuned, and used. Wind plays through the skull. Larvae shelter in the pelvis. Seeds lodge in the vertebrae. A dead grazer may spend five seasons as a flute before it becomes salt.

There are predators, though they are not always bodies.

Some are swift hook-jawed fliers that hunt inside swarms, folding themselves into the turbulence until they strike. Some are flat, patient things that live under basalt powder and rise only when a footfall repeats a vulnerable rhythm. Some are flowers whose scent loosens ceramic knots in animal skulls, causing the dazed creature to stand still while root-filaments enter through its nostrils.

But the most feared predators are resonances.

A wrong tone can kill. A traveler’s tool, a cracked plinth, a herd’s panic-song, the sudden synchronized opening of organ-stems—these can create a frequency that stops the small fliers midair or makes a grazer’s throat-plates fracture inward. The inhabitants have evolved around such dangers. Their bodies include dampers, hinges, vents, sacrificial membranes. The garden kills through music as often as through teeth.

And then there are the machines.

They live there too, though not as machines live in cleaner stories.

They are not servants waiting to be reactivated. They are not ruins. They have become old organs of the place. The leaning tower is perhaps the most awake of them, but not necessarily the most important. It watches without eyes and remembers without language. Its matte surface holds diagrams the way skin holds scars. When animals pass, it dims or brightens by small degrees. When a new shoot survives a dry storm, the tower records it as a pressure-map, a line of light deep inside gray substance.

It may once have been built by hands.

The hand-shaped hollows suggest makers with fingers, or something like fingers, or a machine’s idea of what a hand should be. But whatever built it is gone, changed, or dispersed. The tower no longer commands the garden. It confers with it. It listens to root networks, receives pollen patterns, answers the plinths with buried pulses. Its intelligence is not humanlike. It does not ask what the garden is for. It asks whether the prohibition holds, whether the line is intact, whether the next storm may safely invent.

The plinths are stranger.

Each is a small law with a surface. One invites growth. One rejects it. One breeds shells. One changes shadows. One sings when approached by warm bodies. Together they form no obvious machine, yet the basin’s life arranges itself around them. Animals avoid certain angles between plinths. Fliers use them as navigation points. Flowers near them produce more structured pollen. The mats grow toward them in branching patterns like diagrams seeking completion.

Inside or beneath the plinths something continues to calculate.

Not quickly. Not with urgency. It may be maintaining the dryness. It may be negotiating with the atmosphere. It may be running an experiment whose subjects have long since become its collaborators. It may be praying in arithmetic to the absence of water.

There may still be people.

Not many, if any, and not people in the old sense.

If descendants of the builders remain, they do not live in houses. They would live in protected hollows under the northern cliff, in cavities lined with ceramic felt and conductive bone, emerging only during quiet pressure intervals. Their bodies would have changed. They would be narrow and dry-skinned, with sealed mouths, broad sinus chambers, dark lenses over the eyes, and fingers long enough to fit the tower’s shallow depressions. Their blood, if blood is the word, would be a moving suspension of metallic dust and volatile binders, incapable of spilling as liquid.

They would not speak aloud.

Speech would be too crude, too dangerous, too easily swallowed by the basin’s roar. They would communicate by touching brow to brow, by clicking teeth against conductive plates, by releasing brief alkaline scents, by making hand-signs that alter the static around their fingers. Children would learn first not words, but frequencies: the tone that means shelter, the tone that means do not breathe, the tone that means the garden is making something and must not be interrupted.

They would be gardeners only in the reverent sense.

They would not prune much. They would not irrigate, could not irrigate, would never even imagine water as a gift. Their work would be boundary-work, listening-work, correction-work. They would clean dust from the clean plinth without touching it. They would collect the transparent dust-fruits before they cracked in lethal weather. They would carry dead fliers to the mats. They would stand in the hand-hollows of the tower and allow its diagrams to pass through their nerves.

Perhaps they wear masks grown from grazer bone and tower ceramic. Perhaps their tools are tuning forks, salt knives, static combs, hollow rods that gather airborne minerals. Perhaps they bury their dead upright in the basalt powder so the wind may use them. Perhaps they consider a life successful if, after death, one’s ribs produce a clear note for three winters.

But it is equally possible that no separate people remain.

Perhaps the builders entered the garden deliberately and distributed themselves. Some became ceramic knots in animal skulls. Some became rules in the plinths. Some became the tower’s visible thought. Some became inheritable code inside lacquer-winged creatures. Some gave their last bodies to the mats, whose slow memory now contains them as mineral preference and withdrawal reflex. In that case, the inhabitants are the former people—only no longer gathered into individual skins.

The true resident of the bowl may be the garden itself.

Not as metaphor. As a dry, distributed organism made of plants, animals, old machines, cliffs, wind, salt, and prohibition. Its thoughts occur as storms. Its nerves are conductive roots and static mats. Its lungs are hollow stems. Its blood is charged dust. Its mouth is every vent that inhales mineral vapor. Its dreams appear as pollen lattices in midair, branching maps of roots that do not yet exist.

It lives by refusing water.

Water would dissolve distinctions. Water would soften the clicking grains, blur the salts, rot the dry nerves, short the delicate agreements between charge and mineral. So the garden keeps itself pure of it with the severity of an immune system. It does not hate water. It simply cannot permit it. A drop is not a drink there. It is an invasion, a grammar error, a dangerous memory from another world.

The creatures who live there understand this in their bodies.

They do not thirst. They tune.

They drink gradients, inhale metals, eat voltage, court by resonance, seed by dust, mourn by allowing the wind to play through the dead. They are lush without being moist, abundant without softness, social without speech. Every living thing has space around it because every living thing occupies more than its body: a field, a tone, a current, a permission.

What lives in the waterless garden is an ecology of instruments.

A herd with bone masks and singing throats.  
Aerial shards that think in swarms.  
Glass trees that feed on charged wind.  
Velvet mats with crackling memories.  
Flowers like mineral bells.  
Machines that have become weather.  
Possibly a people who survived by ceasing to be separate from what they made.

And over all of them, or through all of them, lives the old dry intelligence of the bowl: patient, bright, deafening, exact—still inventing itself whenever the wind comes hard through the western notch.
