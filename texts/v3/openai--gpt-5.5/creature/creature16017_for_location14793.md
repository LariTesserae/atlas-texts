Nothing there lives upward.

Everything that survives on that tableland has learned the same grammar: flatten, thicken, store, wait. Life presses itself against stone as if the sky were a weight. It does not flourish in sprays or towers. It accumulates in mats, plates, herds, seams, chambers, slow-moving belts of flesh and root and shell.

The largest creatures are the ones first mistaken for geology.

They are broad, low animals, built like pieces of the plateau that have decided to breathe. Their backs are armored in dull keratin plates the color of wet basalt, each plate rough enough for lichens to root in. Old individuals carry whole weather maps across their hides: orange crusts along the shoulders, black moss in the seams of the neck, pale mineral blooms where frost has repeatedly melted and refrozen. When they lie down among stones, only the occasional fogging of their nostrils gives them away.

Their bodies are immense but economical. The legs are short, jointed close to the ground, ending in wide padded feet that spread over frost crust and gravel without cracking either loudly. Their heads are heavy, blunt, almost shovel-like, made for pushing into mats of moss and scraping cushion plants without uprooting them. They graze as glaciers cut: slowly, shallowly, returning again and again without destroying the source.

They do not have cries in the ordinary sense. Their communication passes through the ground—low chest-throbs, pressure pulses, a trembling of warmed soil beneath the herd. A calf knows its mother by the heat of her flank and the rhythm she makes when lying down. Adults turn together not because one leads, but because the same vibration passes through many bodies at once.

Farther out move the great migratory bands: taller, leaner, draped in dense coats that hang in rigid panels when frozen. Some carry flattened antlers or cranial fins, broad dark vanes threaded with blood vessels. In sunlight, when there is any, these fins gather heat; in motion they bleed off excess warmth without steam betraying them too brightly to predators. Others have translucent sacs along the throat and chest, dimly amber beneath the skin, where warmed air and blood pass close together. At night these sacs glow faintly through mist, not with light of their own but with retained body heat.

Their migrations are not seasonal in any simple way. They follow thaw-lines, mineral licks, fungal blooms beneath snow crust, the slow recovery of grazed mats. A herd may spend three years completing a circuit that no individual fully understands. The route exists in hooves, milk, scar tissue, and the memory of the oldest females, who know where ice forms treacherously over hollow ground and where wind strips the lichens clean by midwinter.

The young are born dark and wet into hollows warmed by many bodies. They stand quickly, but not eagerly. Nothing here wastes eagerness. For their first weeks they remain in the middle of the herd, nearly invisible beneath the hanging coats of adults, drinking milk so rich it cools into wax on stone.

The predators are almost the opposite: narrow, pale, and patient.

They do not dominate the land. They haunt its margins. Along ridgelines, in fog, beside the old channels, they lie with their bodies stretched in the direction of the wind, fur flattened, eyes half-lidded against ice dust. Their limbs are long but not made for explosive speed. Sudden pursuit is too expensive here. Instead they inherit weather.

They know where a crust will hold them but not a heavier grazer. They know which slopes glaze first when mist freezes after dusk. They know how to keep a herd uneasy without scattering it, how to turn one old animal slightly away from the warmth of the others, how to follow for thirty hours until a limp becomes a fall.

Their mouths are dark inside. Their teeth are less like knives than cold chisels, meant to open hide stiffened by frost. Some have whiskers along the forelegs as well as the muzzle, sensitive to tremors through stone. Some can lower their body temperature for long waits, becoming almost indistinguishable from the ridges they occupy. When they kill, they eat first in silence, then withdraw before the smaller processors arrive.

Those smaller creatures are everywhere.

The plateau is alive underneath.

Beneath the moss mats and frost-sorted pebbles are burrow nations: compact, blunt-clawed animals with insulated bodies, shovel teeth, and skin thickened against mineral abrasion. They are not individually impressive. One held in the hand would seem all nose, muscle, and heat. But in number they are one of the true powers of the place.

They tunnel through thawed seams, root beds, carcass shadows, and ancient soil trapped between stone plates. They drag down shed hair, dead moss, splinters of bone, dung, feathers, fish scales, flakes of oxidized ore. Nothing remains unused for long. Their chambers are sorted not by thought but by instinct refined over ages: warm chambers for digestion, cold chambers for storage, wet chambers for fungus, mineral chambers where they gnaw grit to maintain their teeth and gut cultures.

Their ventilation holes rim themselves with frost. At dawn and dusk, when temperature differences are strongest, vapor breathes from them in faint pulses. Seen across a basin, the ground appears to be idling.

They have attendants and parasites: blind white mites that live in their fur; small black beetles that follow their tunnels; root-filament fungi that grow in the walls and are harvested without ever being planted. If there is anything like agriculture here, it is older than intention. The burrowers do not build a civilization. They metabolize one.

The beetle-things on the surface are hard, dark, and numerous. They move in files between carcasses, mineral seeps, dung fields, and the warm undersides of sleeping herds. Their shells contain metallic flecks taken from the land, making them dullly reflective in certain angles of light. They rasp bone. They polish antler. They carry spores in grooves beneath their wing-cases, though many have no wings left in any functional sense. Flight is mostly useless on the plateau; the wind owns the air.

There are birds, but they are poor proclaimers of themselves.

Most are wingless or nearly so, low-bodied, with insulating down under smooth outer feathers that shed rime without rustling. They walk delicately through beetle trails and burrow vents, stabbing for larvae, seeds, softened moss tips, and flakes of frozen fat. Their beaks are precise instruments. Their calls are clicks, throat pulses, or sounds too low to separate from the ticking of stone.

A few true flyers exist along the canyon seams, where updrafts are reliable. They are long-winged, ash-colored scavengers that rarely flap. They appear out of fog as if extruded by it, circle once above a kill, and vanish into the blue-gray distance. Their nests are not constructions so much as selected ledges where feathers, bones, and frozen droppings accumulate over generations.

The tarns hold another population entirely.

Under the black water are heavy-headed fish with slow tails and wide mouths that browse films from submerged rock. Their blood resists freezing; their eyes are reduced, suited to dimness and clouded ice. They spend winter in a state close to suspension, hovering above mineral seepage where the water is fractionally warmer.

Clinging to stones below them are jointed filterers like armored hands. They open and close fans in the seep currents, taking in mineral-rich water, bacteria, and the fine organic dust that slides down from the plateau. Some are pale. Some are black as old oil. When disturbed, they fold into themselves and become pebbles.

At the tarn edges live amphibious grazers, squat and seal-smooth, with blunt snouts for breaking new ice. They haul themselves out onto frozen margins to rest in clusters, silver-eyed, steaming faintly. Their young develop in pockets beneath ice shelves, feeding first on microbial films, then on the soft undersides of water plants. Adults remember breathing holes and return to them with exactness, even after storms bury the surface.

The plants are not passive decoration. They are the oldest citizens.

Lichens crust the stone in iron, soot, green-black, and sulfur-yellow. Some grow so slowly that a patch the size of a palm may have begun before the oldest living herd animal was born. They digest rock by patience. They drink mist directly. They hold minerals in their bodies and release them only when scraped, eaten, frozen apart, or buried.

The mosses are black, red, and deep green, dense as felt. They grow in hollows where breath from herds or vents adds a little moisture. Pale grasses thread through them, each blade short, waxed, and curled inward against the cold. Cushion plants make tight domes with leaves like scales, warm at the center, outer surfaces dead or half-dead as insulation for the living core. Many flower only after rare sequences of thaw and mist, producing tiny colorless blossoms close to the ground, pollinated by crawling insects that never see a summer in any generous sense.

There are fungi too, though mostly hidden: under carcasses, inside burrows, between roots, beneath old dung crusts. Some stain the soil blue-black. Some live inside the lichens. Some wake only when a dead animal warms the ground beneath its collapsing body. For a few weeks after a kill, an entire hidden garden rises under the hide: threads, nodules, beetles, worms, larvae, bacteria, all reducing flesh into heat and mineral.

The worms are pale and cold-slow until they find warmth. Beneath sun-warmed stones or recently abandoned herd beds, they braid together in masses, digesting fine organic matter and mineral dust. They are one of the reasons the thin soils do not vanish entirely into wind. They bind the plateau’s meager fertility in their bodies, then surrender it again.

None of these lives are separate in the way they first appear. The place is a circulation system.

Moss becomes grazer. Grazer becomes predator, beetle, worm, vapor, bone meal. Bone feeds lichen. Lichen takes stone into itself. Burrowers carry surface death below, and vents carry underground warmth upward. Tarn creatures filter what seeps down from the mineral shelves. Birds stitch carcass to burrow, burrow to tarn, tarn to ridge. The herds do not simply cross the plateau; they move nutrients across a continent too wide for weather alone to mix.

Even heat is part of the ecology. Warmth gathers under bodies, in throat sacs, in burrow chambers, beneath dark stones, around vents, in the mud below tarn ice. Many creatures survive by locating warmth rather than shelter. A sleeping herd is a temporary climate. A carcass is a furnace. A burrow colony is a buried organ. A south-facing basalt face may support an entire miniature world no wider than a cloak.

If anything intelligent lives there, it does not make monuments.

It would be easy to miss because intelligence in such a place would not announce itself by building upward or carving signs into stone. It would conserve. It would remember routes, pressures, thaw cycles, breathing holes, safe fog, dangerous ice. It would exist in herd decisions, predator patience, burrow ventilation, the chemical bargaining of roots and fungi. The plateau does not reward command. It rewards attunement.

So the inhabitants are not masters of the land. They are continuations of it.

They are stone-colored grazers with lichened backs. Heat-throated herds moving like slow weather. Pale predators that hunt with terrain instead of speed. Burrow colonies breathing through frost holes. Beetles like filings given legs. Wingless birds with silent feet. Fish turning beneath black ice. Amphibians exhaling steam at tarn edges. Lichens older than memory, mosses dark as old blood, cushion plants clenched around their own small warmth.

Together they make a world that seems empty only because almost nothing wastes motion, height, color, or sound.

The plateau is inhabited by patience wearing many bodies.
