At first it seems that no one could live there permanently. Then, after a day and a night, you begin to notice the inhabitants by their absences.

A shutter closes before the noon glare reaches it. A gutter has been cleared of salt with a patient hand. A trail between pump houses avoids three stones that look no different from the others until evening, when scorpions come out from under them. A clay cup sits upside down beneath a condenser pipe, weighted with a black pebble so the wind will not take it. Someone has tied strips of pale cloth to a valve wheel, not as decoration but to show from a distance whether the wheel has shifted.

The people who live here are few, and they do not make themselves large upon the land.

They are narrow-shouldered, sun-hardened, economical in movement. Not starved-looking, exactly, but edited by the plateau: no softness that is not useful, no gesture that spills effort. Their skin is darkened, cracked at the knuckles, often dusted with pale mineral powder. Their eyes are hooded against glare, with permanent creases at the corners from squinting into heat shimmer. Most keep their hair covered or bound tight beneath cloth, leather, or beaten fiber hats lacquered with resin and ash. Bare skin is rare in daylight. They dress in layers, but loose ones: reflective outer wraps, vented sleeves, stiff collars that shade the neck, sandals soled with insulating pads cut from old belt material or laminated hide.

They move as if the ground is an animal that might wake.

No one crosses open basalt without first looking at the sky, then the birds, then the direction of the wind. Children learn this before they learn letters. They are taught to place their palms near a stone but not on it, to judge heat by the air just above the surface. They are taught that a shadow belongs first to whatever has already claimed it. They are taught that thirst makes fools, and fools feed the plateau.

Their settlements are not villages in the lowland sense. They are attachments.

A family may live against the shaded side of a pump house, in rooms cut partly into old service trenches and partly built from scavenged ceramic panels. Another group occupies the underside of a collapsed conveyor frame, roofing it with condenser mesh and thorn mats. Some live in lava tubes whose mouths have been narrowed with stone walls, the interiors cool and carefully divided: sleeping alcoves, seed bins, tool racks, water jars sealed with waxed cloth. Others keep seasonal shelters along the terraces, following fog, insect hatch, or the opening schedule of old steam valves.

Their homes are half dwelling, half instrument. Every roof catches something. Every wall casts a measured shade. Every doorway has a lip to turn rainwater inward. Clay jars are buried to their necks in floors to keep them cool. Condensation plates hang above sleeping places. Wind chimes are not ornamental; they warn of shifts in air that might bring fog or dust. In the best houses, one can hear water arriving drop by drop before dawn, each fall amplified through a ceramic throat into a covered cistern below.

They do not own water the way other people own land. They hold rights to moments.

A family may have claim to the third gutter of the eastern tower from first light until the sun clears the ridge. A widow may possess the runoff from a cracked vat only during rain that comes from the south. Children inherit cup-times, valve-times, fog-nets, shade-lines. Disputes are argued with memory, not maps: who repaired the pipe, who cleared the salt, who found the leak, whose grandmother sealed the tank after the red storm. Theft of water is a grave offense, but wasting water is worse. A thief can repay. A waster has insulted everyone living and everything dead.

They are descended, partly, from the workers who built the machines: miners, condensers, pipe-fitters, kiln tenders, brine sorters, people sent up to take useful things from a country that gave nothing freely. But they are not simply the remnants of an abandoned industry. They have become something more local and more exact. They do not think of the machines as ruins. They think of them as old animals, temperamental but feedable, blind in some parts, generous in others.

They know which towers still comb fog from the dawn air. They know which fans turn only because their shafts remember turning. They know which buried pipes carry water, which carry steam, which carry nothing but scalding breath and mineral flakes. They polish some valves with oil rendered from rodents. They pack others with clay and thorn fiber. They sing to engines, not from superstition alone, but because a steady rhythm helps three people pull a seized wheel without breaking their wrists.

Their craft is maintenance after the age of manufacture.

They can no longer make all the parts they need, so they grow substitutes from habit and scarcity. Ceramic patch over metal wound. Bone pin in brass hinge. Leather gasket, cactus wax seal, beetle-shell lacquer on cracked insulator. They braid cable from salvaged wire and plant fiber. They make mirrors from polished ore plates to flash messages across the basin. They use lenses chipped from old gauges to start cooking fires. They grind mineral salts into pigments, medicines, poisons, preservatives, and trade goods.

Their tools are beautiful in the way desert things are beautiful: nothing extra, every repair visible. Knives made from shear blades. Water needles for probing damp seams beneath basalt. Hooked rods for drawing scorpions from cracks. Long-handled shade stakes wrapped with cloth, planted at noon beside work crews. Sounding hammers tuned by use; a good mechanic can strike a pipe and tell whether it is dry, wet, pressurized, scaled, or occupied.

They eat from brief abundance.

After rain, they harvest quickly: swollen seeds, fresh thorn shoots, pale insects before their wings harden, fungal threads from under damp plates, the yellow flowers of the ribbed succulents. They trap small mammals with stone funnels and spring snares. They collect eggs from ground birds but leave enough that the birds keep returning. They dry lizard meat in strips on hot black rock under mesh cages. They grind bitter grass with salt and insect flour into cakes that keep for months. Some families herd a domesticated form of the narrow-faced burrowing mammal, not in pens but in managed warrens around leaky cisterns, where the animals feed and breed under watch.

Nothing is eaten without calculation. Fat is treasured. Blood is caught. Bones become needles, hooks, powder for soil, charms for children, splints for machines. Even cooking is timed: flat stones retain evening heat, steam vents warm sealed pots, and the hottest hours can bake food without fuel if one knows where to place it and how long to wait.

They are not humorless. Scarcity has made them precise, not grim.

Their jokes are dry and small, often about people who ignored obvious signs and were corrected by stone, heat, or insects. They laugh quietly, because loudness in the open feels foolish. They hold festivals after rains, but the festivals may last only an hour before work overtakes celebration. Children run barefoot through cooling channels, shrieking while adults pretend not to watch. Old people sit near the tanks and name the smell of the rain by its source: ash-rain, rim-rain, metal-rain, thorn-rain, bad rain, sweet rain, rain that woke the salt.

Music here is mostly percussive and breath-light. Ceramic jars tapped with bone. Cable strands plucked between posts. Low flutes that imitate wind passing through pipe mouths. Songs are often instructions disguised as memory: the order of valves along the northern terrace, the safe path across a clinker field, the names of poisonous pools, the timing of beetle emergence after fog. A child who can sing a route may be trusted to walk it.

They bury their dead in cool places, but not always underground.

Some are sealed into abandoned ceramic vats, painted with white branching salt forms. Some are laid in lava shelves where air moves slowly and dryly over them. Mechanics of great skill may have their hand bones kept in tool houses, not as relics for worship exactly, but as reminders that good repair outlives flesh. Names are spoken at condensers before dawn, when droplets begin to gather. The dead are associated with water because water is what returns.

Their beliefs are practical until they are suddenly not.

They do not worship the machines, but they greet certain machines. They do not believe stone is alive in the way an animal is alive, yet they apologize before drilling into it. They know salt can sicken a person, but they also believe some salts carry messages from deep heat. Those who lick the white branching growths around the old vats and come back slow-eyed are treated with caution. Some are considered damaged. Some are considered instructed. A few become listeners.

The listeners sit on humming ridgelines with their palms on shaded rock, feeling for tremors through heat-stressed stone and buried metal. They predict pressure releases, pipe failures, fog climbs, sometimes rain. Whether they are sensing real vibration, mineral intoxication, inherited knowledge, or all of these at once hardly matters to the plateau people. A warning that saves water is a true warning.

Their language has many words for dryness.

There is the dryness of noon stone, which kills. The dryness of safe stored grain. The dryness of a cistern that should not be dry. The dryness of skin before fever. The false dryness before fog. The clean dryness after good wind. They have many words for shade too: moving shade, owed shade, dangerous shade, machine shade, bird shade, child shade, last shade.

They measure distance less by length than by risk. A place is “two drinks away,” or “one shadow if clouds hold,” or “too far after the hawks rise.” Time is counted by mechanical events as much as celestial ones: before the north valves sigh, after the tower gutters start, when the third fan shadow touches the cracked vat, when the rail stops burning the hand.

They live with animals not as masters of them, but as neighbors in a crowded economy.

The copper-backed lizards are tolerated in houses because they eat pale insects. Scorpions are killed near sleeping mats but left alone beneath outer stones, where they keep burrow pests down. Heat-hawks are respected and cursed; their presence reveals prey, but also warns prey. Ground birds follow maintenance crews, catching insects stirred by footsteps. Some birds have learned to wait by valve lines at sunset for beetles drawn to condensation. Children throw them scraps and study their movements.

There are semi-tame beetle colonies kept in shaded mineral boxes. Their mirrored shells are harvested after molting for lacquer and ornament. Their larvae process waste. Their sudden agitation can indicate a change in humidity before human skin notices. A good beetle keeper is valued almost as highly as a pipe reader.

The plateau also has its unwelcome inhabitants.

There are pale, blind things in some of the old underground conduits, descended perhaps from rodents, perhaps from something that entered the warm pipes long ago and never left. They come out after deep rains, slick-skinned and long-toothed, nosing along runoff channels. People block house drains with thorn screens when thunder builds.

There are salt-drunk animals that wander from the evaporation yards with mineral crust around their mouths and no fear left in them. They may walk straight into settlements, trembling, eyes filmed white or green. Their meat is not eaten. Their bones are buried away from cisterns.

And there are the pipe swarms: pale insects that hatch in such numbers after a rare sequence of fog and heat that they clog gutters, foul tanks, blind animals, and make the air tick like burning grass. During a swarm, everyone seals water first, breathes through cloth second, and complains only afterward.

The people fear fire less than water in the wrong place.

A dry fire can be seen, smothered, starved. But a flood in a narrow channel under cloud arrives as a wall of mud and stones, with no respect for habit. Children are told stories of sleepers taken by dry washes, of whole camps swept into grates, of cisterns cracked by sudden filling. During storms, the elders stand not under shelter but where they can watch the channels. Rain is blessing only if it is caught. Unwatched rain is a predator.

They trade with lower lands, but uneasily.

Caravans come up by the scarps in the cooler months, bringing cloth, grain, medicine, new metal, sometimes news. They leave with salts, pigments, dried insects, condenser mesh repaired by plateau hands, heat-hardened ceramics, and rumors of machines that still breathe after a century. Lowlanders find the plateau people abrupt and inhospitable. Plateau people find lowlanders loud, wet-smelling, and careless with shade.

Children born here often dislike the lowlands. The air below feels thick to them, almost dirty with moisture. Trees make them nervous because shade falls everywhere and hides too much. Rivers seem obscene: water lying open under the sky, unguarded, making noise for no reason.

Their social order is built around competence. Age matters, kin matters, inheritance matters, but none of these can compensate for someone who cannot read heat, conserve water, or repair what they use. Leadership shifts with circumstance. During fog season, condenser keepers hold authority. During rail salvage, metalworkers do. During drought, the water counters. During sickness, salt doctors and insect keepers. During storms, anyone who knows channels is obeyed.

The most admired people are those who make abundance last without appearing to possess it.

A woman who can tune a leaking pipe so that it waters thorn grass, fills a cup, and does not attract too many predators becomes famous. A child who notices condensation forming on an unexpected wall may earn a new name. A mechanic who restarts an old fan is celebrated, not because wind is rare, but because moving air can mean cooler storage, drier grain, safer sleep, more catch from fog.

Names change over a lifetime. A person may begin with a birth name, gain a child-name from some accident, a work-name from skill, and an elder-name from what they have saved. Names such as Finds-Under-Stone, Three-Valves, Rain-Listener, Beetle-Mother, Shade-At-Noon, Keeps-the-White-Tank. A bad name can cling too: Spills, Sleeps-in-Channels, Drinks-First.

They are not trying to conquer the plateau. That ambition belonged to the builders of the yards, and the plateau outlasted it. The present inhabitants have a smaller and more durable ambition: to remain included in the place’s arrangements.

They accept that every improvement invites another life. Repair a pipe and insects come. Insects bring lizards. Lizards bring hawks. A damp wall grows moss, then roots, then burrows, then snakes. A household that catches more water must also manage more teeth. This is not considered failure. It is considered the actual shape of having something.

Their children grow up knowing machines as habitats before they understand them as devices. A condenser tower is a place where fog becomes drink, yes, but also where swifts nest, where beetles bead themselves with dew, where old mesh cuts fingers, where a particular grandmother fell and lived, where the dawn wind smells faintly of tin. A pump house is not an object. It is shade, danger, history, sound, water, lizard eggs, a locked room, a roofline, a law.

If there is a single creature that best represents them, it is not the hawk or the scorpion but the black lichen on basalt: slow, thin, patient, altering stone by almost nothing, surviving where survival looks impossible from a distance.

They live by taking little from many sources. A mouthful from fog. A seed from thorn. A measure of salt. A strip of meat. A repaired valve. A remembered path. A warning from birds. A night’s coolness stored in stone. A machine’s last obedience. A leak turned garden.

They are not gentle people, but they are careful. They can be hospitable with astonishing generosity, offering a guest water that cost them hours of labor and years of inherited right. They can also abandon a fool without ceremony if saving him would endanger a cistern. Morality here is not soft. It is hydrological.

At night, when condensation gathers and the stars stand close in the thin air, their settlements become visible by small, shielded lights: not many, never wasteful, each one tucked into stone or metal. Figures move between tanks and towers with covered jars. Someone checks a mesh panel. Someone listens to a pipe. Someone lifts a sleeping child away from a wall where scorpions hunt warmth. Someone marks the first drops of the night on a tally slate.

They live there the way everything lives there: not by defeating scarcity, but by entering into agreement with it.
