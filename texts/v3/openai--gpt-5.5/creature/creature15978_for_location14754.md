What lives there is not a species so much as a staff.

Nothing in the hollow behaves like a visitor. The frogs, mites, larvae, fish, snails, beetles, pale lizards, soft-winged moths, and translucent things under the mat all seem less like animals occupying a habitat than small organs distributed through a larger body. They are not commanded, but they are fitted. Each has the look of having been shaped by the basin’s one rule: continue.

The frogs are the most visible. They are small, green-brown, damp-skinned, with wide-set eyes the color of wet seeds. They sit in leaf-cups and along the rims of shallow pools, toes spread on waxy surfaces, throats pulsing in exact sympathy with the buried pumps. They do not leap far. There is nowhere far to go. Their calls are soft, percussive clicks and rounded notes, never rising into a chorus that would dominate the hollow. When they feed, they take gnats from the vapor columns one at a time. When they breed, their eggs are fastened beneath leaves where condensation keeps them slick. Their tadpoles do not swarm. They develop in little held waters, each cup containing only what it can sustain.

The insects are more numerous and more purposeful. Long-legged skaters move over water-films so thin they seem to walk on breath. Lacquer-backed beetles patrol the vine ridges, their antennae tapping the openings of vents and hollow stems. Some crawl inside the reed-valves when the pressure drops, cleaning the lips of the apertures with minute combed forelegs. Others feed on the mineral skin that forms where warm vapor meets cooler air. They shine black, green, amber, but never brightly; their colors are sealed under moisture like paint beneath varnish.

Inside the pumping pods live the larvae.

They are pale, jointless, half-transparent, with darker threads of gut showing through them. They move by contraction, folding and lengthening through the wet chambers. They eat rot before it can clog anything. They scrape fungal crust from inner walls. They widen passages by rasping where the plant has swollen too tightly, and seal other places by packing them with processed pulp. If one were removed, it would seem helpless and unfinished, but inside the pods they are expert. They are not parasites. The plant grows rooms for them. They keep those rooms usable.

The snails are slow engineers of surfaces. Their shells are flattened and dark, patterned with rings like old watermarks. They live on the weeping stones and the undersides of the moss shelves, grazing algae down to a perfect thinness. Too much algae would smother the stone-pores; too little would expose them. The snails keep the balance by appetite alone. Their trails dry almost instantly into faint silver writing, then soften again under the next breath of condensation.

There are fish beneath the floor.

They are hardly fish in the open-water sense: narrow, colorless, ribbon-boned creatures moving through the shallow channels under the root-felt. Their eyes are reduced to pearly dots. Their bodies bend through seams no wider than fingers. They feed on drifting spores, shed larvae skins, and the soft crumbs of decomposed leaf matter that pass down through the mat. At times their movement can be seen as a quick pale correction underfoot, like a thought crossing beneath skin. They keep the channels open simply by using them.

The lizards belong to the drier intervals, though nothing here is truly dry. They are pale from the underside of leaves, with faint yellow throats and thin ribs visible when they breathe. They cling upside down on frond-stems and wait for moths. Their scales are not dusty but glazed, each one holding a tiny line of moisture. They shed in fragments, and the cast skin is eaten almost immediately by mites and springtails.

The moths look diseased until they move. Their wings are patterned like mildew, lichen, bruised leaf, old condensation stains. They cling with folded patience to the lower surfaces of broad leaves, where they are nearly indistinguishable from the marks of damp. At dusk, if dusk reaches the basin at all, they stir and drift through the open channels without striking anything. Their bodies are heavy with nectar from the amber sacs. They pollinate flowers that hardly resemble flowers: valve-mouths, pressure bladders, pale vents with dust hidden deep in their throats.

Smaller than all these are the true maintenance people of the hollow: mites, threadworms, springtails, transparent lice, fungal grazers, blind white flecks that gather along seams of warmth. They live in numbers too precise to become abundance. They crop bloom, loosen rot, carry spores, clean eggs, open pores, close wounds. If the basin has hands, they are these.

But beneath them, around them, through them, there is the greater resident.

The hollow itself lives there.

Not as a single mind with intentions, not as a monster pretending to be a marsh, but as a joined life that has passed beyond the need to distinguish plant from machine, animal from tool, digestion from repair. Its roots remember the shape of the basin. Its leaves regulate the air. Its pods move water as if breathing with several hearts. Its stones weep because the roots have taught them to. Its animals are born into tasks their bodies already understand.

It does not hunt. It does not reach outward. It does not expand except microscopically, by thickening what is already there. Its desire, if it has one, is not growth but continuance. It wants the pool kept level. It wants the valves clean. It wants the vapor warm, the rot useful, the channels open, the spores sorted, the dead returned before they become interruption.

At the center, in the dark convex pool, live the smallest and perhaps oldest things: pale turners, no larger than seeds, moving in circles below the surface tension. They have no eyes. They have cilia like faint halos. Their motion is constant and ceremonial, though it may be nothing more than feeding. They stir without rippling. They maintain some invisible exchange between pool and column, drop and depth, mineral and breath. Around them, the thumb-thick green columns open and close, feeding the pool one measured drop at a time.

If the hollow has priests, they are these little pale creatures.

If it has workers, they are the larvae and beetles.

If it has lungs, they are the reeds.

If it has memory, it is in the root-mat, compressed under every careful foot.

No inhabitant there is sovereign. None is decorative. None is wasted. To live in the basin is to take part in its maintenance, whether by feeding, breeding, rasping, pulsing, exhaling, dying, or being dissolved. The place has no masters and no guests. It has functions that wear bodies for a while.

What lives there is an agreement, old and wet and loud, made flesh in many small forms.
