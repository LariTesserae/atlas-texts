What lives there is not a population so much as a slow, bright system with a few animals and people caught in its folds.

The first inhabitant is the cavern itself, or the plant that has mistaken the cavern for a body.

It is not one organism in any simple sense, but neither is it merely a forest. The roof-growth, the root-sheets, the moss plains, the reed-pillars, the mineralized terraces, the seed clouds, the thirsty mats underfoot — all of them seem to answer one another. Pressure in one district changes light in another. A wound in stone is found by roots before water has finished dripping from it. Rain falls where the ceiling has been preparing to drink. The great buttress roots stand like organs of an underground animal too large to turn over in its sleep.

Its life is cold, pale, rapid, and patient at once. It grows like weather and thinks, if it thinks, like a watershed. It remembers paths only as temporary absences of growth. It notices heat, weight, mineral taste, vibration, frost, breath. A camp is not an offense to it, only a new shape to be tested, shaded, rooted through, lifted, fed upon, or left alone. It does not hunt people in the manner of a beast, but it is always changing the terms under which they may remain alive.

Many of its smaller forms are dangerous by accident.

There are mosses that behave like snowfields, crusting over hollow basins. There are pale mats that stiffen when crossed too quickly and soften when trusted. There are vines that mimic rope not from malice but because rope is a successful shape. There are leaf-cups full of clean water, and leaf-cups full of sleeping larvae, and leaf-cups full of sweet sap that ferments into a cold poison. There are blade-leaf fields that ring at a touch and then close slowly over the disturbance, cutting anything that tries to force a way through. There are glowing veins that carry water, heat, sugar, or caustic mineral liquor; the difference matters.

The plants are the oldest residents, but they are not alone.

The grazers live where the white moss is young.

They are narrow, high-jointed creatures, built as if from reeds, sinew, and misted glass. Their bodies are pale grey, with faint green shadows under the skin where their gut-symbionts work. Their legs are too long for comfort, but perfect for stepping lightly over uncertain ground. Their hooves spread like root-fingers when they set weight down, testing the moss before committing to it.

They graze almost silently, cropping frost-stiff moss and the soft tips of new runners. Their teeth grow in translucent plates and are worn down by mineral grit. Their ears are large but folded close, clear-veined and delicate, able to feel tremors through the air more than hear sound. A herd is rarely more than three to seven animals. Larger groups attract too much notice from things below the mat.

They do not flee suddenly. Sudden movement is for open country and sunlight. Here they fold themselves behind pillars, pass behind root-curtains, step into the pale complexity of distance, and are gone without haste. Their young are born in condensation season, when the moss is richest and the ceiling light is strongest. A newborn can stand within minutes, but its skin remains almost transparent for several days, so the herd keeps it among frozen reed-shadows until its blood darkens.

In the seed pods live the warm animals.

The pods are houses, weather balloons, wombs, traps, and seasonal fruit. Inside some of them, where fermentation keeps a pocket of heat, small blind creatures nest in the spongy walls. They are no bigger than a hand, soft-bodied, with shovel faces and whiskered lips. Their fur is not fur exactly, but a nap of fine white filaments that collect moisture from the air. They eat pod-pith, fungal films, insectlike larvae, mineral salts, and sometimes one another.

After rain they emerge in little crowds, trembling with urgency, to lick fresh stone. They know the taste of new fractures before any human sees the crack. Travelers watch them carefully. If the pod-mice, as some people call them, all run in one direction after a rain, there is usually either water, exposed salt, or danger there. Often all three.

Some pods tolerate them. Some depend on them. The animals carry spores in the grooves of their lips and under their filament-coats, spreading the next generation of roof-growth. A pod that seems infested may in fact be in bloom.

Higher up, among the falling spores, drift the flat feeders.

They look like leaves that learned to swim. Their bodies are thin, flexible, and broad, with ribbed edges that pulse almost imperceptibly. Seen from below, they are pale green or white and vanish against the luminous roof. Seen in turning, they reveal a darker underside and a round, soft mouth fringed with cilia.

They feed on luminous spores, pollen dust, and microscopic life in mist. During rain-fronts they gather by the thousands, though from the floor one notices only that portions of the ceiling seem to be shedding and reassembling. They descend slowly after storms, riding cold updrafts from crevasses. A dead one on the ground is rare and valued: its skin dries into a membrane clearer than cured leaf, useful for goggles, lantern-windows, and wound covers.

Children are warned never to sleep under a turning flock. Not because the creatures attack, but because what they excrete is a fine adhesive powder that can seal eyes, nostrils, and seams in clothing. A person caught beneath them wakes crusted in pale dust, breathing through cloth gone stiff as eggshell.

The true predators are fewer, quieter, and less often seen.

The one most feared lives under the walking surface. It is not large compared to the cavern, but it is large compared to a person. Its body is flattened and muscular, with a shovel skull and limbs reduced to jointed hooks. It moves between layers: under moss, above stone, through old root-felt and dry plant litter. Its back lifts the ground in a slow ripple, like a thought passing through a sleeping animal.

It does not chase. It waits where paths have become habitual. It learns pressure. A hunter can deceive sight here, but not the pattern of repeated footfalls. The under-mat predator settles beneath a trail, loosens the root-weave, and makes a softness. When weight comes, the surface fails just enough. The prey sinks to the thigh, then to the waist, and the creature closes from below with patient strength.

People listen for it by lying flat with one ear to the moss. They listen not for sound, but for refusal: places where the roots are too tense, where ordinary drip-vibrations do not pass cleanly, where a living mat seems to be holding its breath.

There are also lesser lives everywhere, but most are too small or too seasonal to seem like animals.

Glass mites move through frost on the leaf-ribs, drawing tiny maps that vanish when the air warms. Thread-worms live inside glowing veins and die instantly in open air. Pale insects hatch only during six-minute rains, mate in the falling water, and leave eggs glued under blade-leaves before the last drops fall. In the mineral cracks are slow colonies of shell-soft things that feed on stone dust and plant acid. In the dry felt are sleeping spores, larvae, cysts, folded leaves, patient hungers.

Then there are the people.

They are human, or near enough that a stranger would call them human before noticing what the place has made of them.

They are slight, cold-lean, and economical in movement. Most wear layered garments of cured leaf-skin, felted root fiber, pod membrane, and hide from the pale grazers. Their clothing is not dark. Dark cloth stands out too sharply and absorbs the wrong kind of heat. They dress in milk-grey, old green, frost-brown, and resin amber, with veils or narrow hoods to cut the ceiling glare. Their eyes are often shaded by slitted masks made from reed bark and manta-skin membrane. In a world without a sun, glare still blinds.

Their hands are strong in the fingers. Everyone climbs. Everyone knots. Everyone knows how to test a bridge, splice a root-rope, read tension in a vine, and cut themselves free before a living cord tightens. Children learn knots before numbers. They learn the safe plants by touch, then by smell, then by how the light behaves around them.

They do not build as if building means permanence.

Their villages are agreements with a surface that may revoke them.

A settlement is usually placed where three things coincide: reliable condensation, old enough ground, and a roof bright but not yellowing. Huts crouch under overhangs or against reed-pillars, never in the middle of a plain unless the plain has been tested for years. Floors are raised on latticework because no one trusts a mat entirely. Store pits are lined with wax and stone flakes. Sleeping platforms hang from visible ropes with knots inspected every evening.

Their fires are small, blue, and watched. Fire is not a comfort here; it is a tool with a debt. The people prefer heat-stones, fermenting pod-pith wrapped in waxed leaves, or body-warmth under layered felt. Smoke is useful mainly for curing, signaling, and keeping certain mites from stores. A careless ember can become an invisible underground coal that eats through dry root-felt for days and opens a village from beneath.

Their tools are intimate with the materials of the cavern. They carry obsidian blades from new fractures, polished thorn awls, reed-bark needles, shell scrapers from deeper waters, and hammers of cold stone wrapped in root leather. Their bowls are trained rather than made. Their water flasks are seed bladders hardened with resin. Their lamps are rarely needed for sight, but used for color: a flame shows differences the cold living light conceals.

They have little metal, and what metal they possess is ceremonial, scavenged, inherited, or traded from elsewhere. A bronze hook, an iron needle, a copper ring: such things are not common tools but memories of another kind of world.

They are not numerous.

The cavern could swallow nations and still feel empty. The people live in scattered bands, terrace hamlets, reed-tree platforms, and seasonal camps. They move when the light tells them to move. They abandon good houses because a root has begun to thicken beneath them, because the ceiling has gone too blue for too many mornings, because pod-mice have fled uphill, because a white plain has stopped sounding hollow and started sounding hungry.

Their elders are not merely the old. An elder is someone who has survived enough changed paths to distrust memory properly.

Memory here is dangerous if used alone. A trail that was safe last year may now cross a basin. A bridge that carried ten people yesterday may be drawing sap and shortening in the night. A village shrine may be twenty paces from its old place because the mat on which it stands has crept. So they keep memory braided with present observation. They tell stories as warnings, but every warning ends with some version of: look again.

Their maps are not flat drawings. Flat maps are too arrogant. They use knot-cords, carved reed bundles, bead sequences, songs of light-change and condensation timing. A route is remembered as a chain of tests: where the moss should crunch, where the reed-pillars should pulse blue, where the air should taste bitter, where one must wait for the root-curtain to rise at cold-tightening. To know a path is not to know where it lies. It is to know how to ask whether it still exists.

Their spirituality, if named from outside, would seem like caution elevated into reverence.

They do not worship the whole cavern as a god exactly. A god can be begged. The cavern must be read. Still, they treat the great buttress roots with a silence indistinguishable from devotion. They leave polished stones near condensation basins. They hang leaf-skin flags where safe weather has been granted twice. They mark the edges of dangerous plains not only to protect travelers, but to acknowledge that the surface has a mouth there.

They have taboos that sound superstitious until tested.

Do not carve living roof-root.

Do not burn dry felt near old ice.

Do not name a path safe while standing on it.

Do not thank water before you have sealed the vessel.

Do not sleep under seed-clouds, even if they are beautiful.

Do not cut a vine that has crossed your camp in the night until you know whether it is passing through or claiming you.

Their dead are difficult.

Burial in the plant mats means swift incorporation. Stone burial is rare because stone is hard and often unsafe. Cremation is dangerous. In some settlements the dead are wrapped in cured leaf and placed in cold crevasses, given to the blue ice. In others, bodies are laid in high root-cradles after the eyes and mouth are sealed with wax, so that the cavern may take them slowly and visibly. A person returned to growth is not gone in the same way a surface person is gone. For a season, perhaps two, relatives may avoid eating moss from that district.

Their children play at weather.

They scatter spore-dust and pretend to be rain-fronts. They build tiny villages on moss hummocks and let trained vinelets overgrow them. They learn to stand motionless when the ceiling flickers. They learn, very young, that the world is alive but not kind, responsive but not loving, generous but never safe.

Some become ceiling-watchers, spending long hours on reed platforms with shaded eyes, reading brightness across distances that defeat ordinary scale. Some become root-hearers, able to tell from touch whether a mat is moving, sleeping, drinking, or hollowing. Some become pod-cutters, harvesting seed bladders without triggering their unfolding. Some become fracture-seekers, following mineral bitterness and blue light to new stone where obsidian, salts, and heat-veins can be found.

The best travelers are quiet not because silence is sacred, but because sound is useless. They speak close. They use hand signs, cord tugs, small mirrors of shell, touches on shoulder or wrist. A shout dies too soon and may call attention to nothing helpful. A dropped pebble tells more.

There may be outsiders sometimes.

A person from the surface, if there is a surface still reachable, would arrive expecting darkness and find instead a brightness that feels wrong to the skin. They would underestimate distance. They would call the place a cavern, then a forest, then a country, and all three words would fail. They would try to make a camp and wake to find their stakes swollen, their ropes budding, their footprints erased by pale runners. If they survived long enough, they would stop asking where north was and begin asking what the ceiling was doing.

But the true natives do not think of themselves as underground. To them, this is not beneath the world. It is the world: roof-sky above, moss-ground below, deeper skies under the cracks, weather in the leaves, paths that live and die.

What lives there, finally, is a civilization of temporary permissions.

Plants live there as continents live: moving too slowly and too largely to be called creatures until one has watched them change a valley.

Animals live there as whispers live: pale, sparse, specialized, appearing at the edges of sight and vanishing before certainty.

People live there as careful guests live in the house of something immense: lightly, observantly, with tools in their belts, knots in their hands, and one eye always lifted toward the luminous roof where the next rain is already forming.
