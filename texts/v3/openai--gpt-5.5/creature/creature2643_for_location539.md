The ones who live there are a people of maintenance, breath, and listening.

They are not fish-people, not spirits, not a vanished race remade by flood. They are human, but human in the way old trees are trees: altered by long keeping with one element. Their bodies have not escaped the ordinary limits of lungs and skin, yet everything about them has been trained toward water. They are narrow-shouldered from moving through flooded doorways, strong in the back and thighs from climbing wet ladders, patient in the hands from knotting, scraping, sorting, repairing. Their feet are broad and clever, good at gripping soaked wood and submerged stone. Their palms are callused in patterns: rope-lines, reed cuts, mineral burns, the polished scars left by shell tools.

They have a dampness to them that never quite leaves. Hair is usually braided close or cropped, because loose hair tangles in roots and cordage. Children wear theirs in little tied knobs until they learn the drowned passages. Adults oil their skin with pepper-scented plant fat against rot and biting films. The old smell faintly of smoke, lime, and warm leaves.

Their eyes are accustomed to dim green light. They do not stare quickly. They look the way gardeners look: downward, along edges, into small changes. A stranger might think them solemn, but they are not joyless. Their humor is quiet and precise. They laugh with the mouth closed, shoulders shaking, so as not to waste breath or call attention across water. A good joke is one that arrives late, like a mineral shelf discovered to have grown into the shape of a face.

They speak softly when they speak at all. Their spoken language is full of short wet sounds, palate clicks, small breaths, and syllables that can be made without opening the mouth wide. But speech is only one part of language. Their true fluency is in hands, knots, knocks, and tension.

A tug on a cord can mean come up, go down, loosen, danger, wait, child below, vent waking, tether fraying, or I am here. The difference lies in rhythm, pressure, and pause. A knock on a reed wall carries through water differently than through air; they learn both. Lovers can argue by touching the same rope from different platforms. Mothers can scold children underwater with two fingers and a glance. Elders can read a neighborhood’s condition by laying a hand on the main tether and feeling where the strain has shifted.

They are raised to notice before they are raised to obey.

A child’s first lessons are not numbers or names, but distinctions: warm current, dead current, breathing jar, false air, soft mineral, hard mineral, safe algae, choking algae, sleeping root, hungry root, old rope, lying rope. They are taught to count breaths before they count days. Their games involve finding hidden air pockets, balancing stones in belts, following a current with closed eyes, identifying edible leaves by touch in clouded water. A child who shouts is not punished harshly, but everyone turns to look. The embarrassment is enough.

Their infants sleep in suspended cradles slung high under roof beams, above the remembered flood marks. The cradles are woven to rock with the building, not against it. Babies grow used to rain noise before they know voices. When they are old enough to crawl, they are tied by soft cords to a post or an elder’s waist, not because the people are cruel, but because every house opens somewhere into depth.

They do not divide the world cleanly into land and water. A room may be an air-room in one season, a kneeling-room in another, a seed-pool later, and an ancestor-place after that. A doorway may be for walking, swimming, passing baskets, or letting roots through. To them, architecture is not solid arrangement but negotiated permission.

Their families are not only bloodlines. A household consists of those who keep the same anchors, breathe from the same jars, tend the same roof-garden, and answer when the same tether is struck in the night. Marriage is often described as “joining maintenance.” To wed someone is to learn the knots of their house and trust them with the flood marks of yours. Divorce happens when cords are untied publicly and redistributed. It is sad, but not shameful. A badly kept tie is more dangerous than a severed one.

Children belong first to their birth-parents, then to the platform, then to the water. An orphan is rare in the social sense, because any child who knows the routes and signs of a district is already partly everyone’s responsibility. The dead, too, remain in place. They are not buried deep in earth; earth is too restless and scarce. Some are wrapped in root-cloth and lowered into warm mineral pools where lime slowly takes them. Some are set within old flooded rooms, seated with stones in their laps, becoming pale shapes behind veils of fern. Some have their bones cleaned, carved, and made into pins, needles, toggles, flute-stems, or depth counters. This is not considered desecration. It is considered continued usefulness.

An ancestor who becomes a tool is thanked before work. An ancestor who becomes stone is visited when currents allow.

They do not worship gods of thunder or sun with much fervor. Those are distant powers, too high or too brief. Their reverence is directed toward process: the forming shelf, the breathing vent, the patient root, the rain that neither begins nor ends. They believe intention can be slow. They believe the mountain is still becoming, and that to live well is to avoid interrupting its thought.

Their priests, if they can be called that, are vent-listeners and map-weavers. They keep no temples separate from ordinary life. A shrine may be a suspended jar of clear air, a mineral lip no one harvests from, a drowned nursery left undisturbed, a post bearing the notches of five generations of water rise. The holy is often marked by refusal: do not scrape here, do not tie here, do not warm food in this spring, do not speak above this pool after dark.

The vent-listeners are mostly old, though not always. They lower their faces close to the water and listen through bowls, shells, hollow reeds, or their own submerged bones. They can tell when a spring has changed temperature by the way algae lifts from a stone. They know the difference between harmless bubbling and a vent beginning to clear its throat. People bring disputes to them because anyone who has listened to stone growing is expected to understand patience.

The map-weavers keep the city’s memory. Their maps hang rolled in dry chambers, woven from reed, bark fiber, dyed root, shell bead, and knots of varying tightness. A drawn line would lie too soon. A woven map can sag, be retied, accept a new anchor, loosen where a district has drifted, grow heavy where mineral has thickened. Young apprentices learn to read them blindfolded. A knot is a family. A gap is danger. A slack loop is seasonal passage. A dark bead is a drowned room where someone important once slept.

They are not poor in knowledge. They are poor only in things that cannot survive wetness.

They know fermentation, buoyancy, rot, lime, tension, medicinal films, edible spores, safe molds, poisonous blooms, the mathematics of raft balance, the moods of clay, the temper of volcanic glass. They can build a basket that carries fire across rain. They can mend a wall underwater by feel. They can make a sleeping mat that dries while being rained on. They can judge whether a platform will last another season by the note it gives when struck with a bone mallet.

Their economy is made of obligations rather than coins. A family with a good vent owes warmth. A family with a strong roof-garden owes cuttings. A household whose anchors hold well during flood season owes tether-space to those whose lower stones are shifting. Debts are remembered in cords and meals, in labor given during repairs, in the sharing of dry fire. Hoarding is despised less for moral reasons than practical ones: anything kept too tightly in such a place rots, molds, sinks, or becomes dangerous.

They eat what the drowned highland permits. Soft roots steamed in spring-baskets. Waterfern cakes. Pale stems peeled and pounded. Gourds stuffed with mineral-salted leaf mash. Small shell-creatures when they are abundant enough to harvest without offending the pilings. Blind eels on rare ceremonial nights, divided into pieces so small the taste is more memory than meal. Fruits that ripen under moss mats, sour and waxy, eaten with oil. Fungus grown in shaded clay jars, some white and mild, some dark and pepper-hot.

Food is not served in large feasts often. It is passed, softened, shared, tucked into leaves. The grandest meal is one cooked with actual flame: smoke-cured reed heart, charred fruit skin, clay-baked tubers. Fire gives food a taste of elsewhere. It is eaten slowly, almost uneasily.

They make music, but it does not ring. Their instruments are low flutes of bone and reed, water drums, suspended stones, clay bells, shell rattles used sparingly because their sound travels strangely through walls. Songs are often hummed more than sung. Many have no fixed words, only breath patterns meant to accompany work: pulling, scraping, diving, knotting, waiting out rain. A mourning song may last an entire day and consist of three notes descending by almost nothing.

Their dances are not leaps. They are shifts of weight on floating platforms, circular steps taken in counterbalance, bodies leaning and recovering as if imitating rafts. Underwater dances are slower still: hands blooming open, knees folding, cloth and hair moving after the body has stopped. These are performed during the deepest rains, when the lantern flowers open and release pale pollen into the currents. The dancers move through the milky threads without breaking them, guiding fertility through the gardens by gesture and wake.

The people are not fearless. They simply fear the appropriate things.

They fear dry wind, because it cracks what should remain swollen. They fear clear sky if it lasts too long. They fear a new silence under the floors. They fear bubbles rising where no vent was known. They fear children becoming overconfident near roof-roots. They fear the Unlaid District, though they harvest its edges and speak to it politely. They fear a person who ignores small signs. Carelessness is treated almost as a sickness, contagious and requiring intervention.

They do not fear darkness much. Darkness is ordinary. They do not fear being wet. Wetness is the condition of being alive. They do not fear decay except when it is hidden. Visible rot can be cut away; hidden rot breaks a bridge.

There are specialists among them.

The tether-keepers dive daily to inspect anchor stones and mineral chimneys. They carry knives of black glass strapped flat against the arm, bone wedges, coils of treated cord, and small gourds of air. Their chests are strong, their movements economical. They are respected because the city literally hangs from their vigilance.

The roof-gardeners cultivate upward and downward at once. They prune vines above their heads while checking root curtains below the floor. They know which plants can be trusted near a sleeping room and which will quietly pry apart a wall. Their fingernails are often stained green-black. They taste water constantly, dipping a finger and touching it to the tongue: too much lime, too little warmth, bloom coming, rot starting, flowers soon.

The lime-scrapers harvest mineral growth without killing it. They speak of stone as one might speak of livestock: this shelf is young, leave it; this curtain may be cut; this chimney is sick; this nodule will harden by next rain. They carry curved shell blades and polish their tools obsessively, because roughness invites unwanted growth.

The air-jar keepers tend the public breathing places. They inspect the overturned ceramic wells where air is trapped below water, refresh them, seal cracks with resin, mark their condition with notched reeds. No one jokes about them. A neglected air jar is close to murder.

The fire-keepers are few and somewhat set apart. Their chambers smell different from the rest of the city: resin, char, dry pith, heated clay. They are not magicians, but children think of them that way. They know how to coax flame without letting it become disaster. Their hands are scarred not by water but by heat, and this makes them seem exotic, touched by a rare animal.

There are also those who go outward: route-tasters, shadow-readers, gatherers of floating mats. They navigate beyond the settlement into the drowned valleys and rain plains. They return with leaf bundles, black glass, strange seeds, softened wood from old forests, rumors of distant currents, and sometimes stories of the three submerged summits changing shape. Their boats are narrow and ribbed like leaves. Their bodies are wrapped in layers of cord and gourd, so that if one piece fails another may hold.

Outsiders, if they ever arrive, are treated with caution rather than hostility. The drowned highland itself is dangerous enough; no sensible person adds violence without need. A stranger would be fed, watched, dried as much as possible, and quietly evaluated: Do they shout? Do they waste fire? Do they pull on cords without asking? Do they look at water as obstacle or as place? Most outsiders fail before they know they are being tested.

The people’s sense of beauty is inseparable from fitness. A beautiful knot is one that holds under changing strain and can be untied in darkness. A beautiful face is calm underwater. A beautiful house has roots passing through it without being conquered by them. A beautiful elder remembers where the floor used to be and does not resent where it is now.

Their clothing is close-fitting when worn below, loose and layered above. Bark-cloth wraps, reed-fiber skirts, sleeveless vests, belts heavy with stones, bone toggles, waterproof pouches, shoulder nets. Colors are muted: smoke brown, moss green, clay pink, lime white, basalt black. Bright dye exists, but it is used sparingly for warning marks, children’s first-breath ceremonies, and the cords tied around newly risen walls in the Unlaid District.

They mark age by competencies rather than years. First climb. First breath-count. First solo crossing. First knot trusted with weight. First night listening for a tether. First descent into an ancestral room. First time noticing danger before an elder names it. A person becomes old not when the body weakens, but when others begin bringing them small changes and asking what they mean.

Around them live slower citizens.

The plants are not background. They are co-builders, trespassers, food, weather-signs, memory-keepers. Roof moss holds rain and cools sleeping chambers. Root curtains filter silt. Waterferns feed families. Lantern flowers announce the deep-rain season. Vent reeds lean toward warmth and betray changes below. Some vines are trained like domestic animals, guided along frames, trimmed away from hinges, encouraged to bind walls. Others are invaders and must be cut back with ritual apology and practical severity.

Fungi live in the walls, in clay jars, beneath sleeping platforms, in mineral cracks. Some are cultivated for food, some for medicine, some for waterproofing, some only tolerated because removing them damages the structure more than leaving them. A good house has a known smell. If its smell changes, people investigate.

The shell-creatures are treated almost like weather. Children are told not to pry too many from one piling, because a bare piling hears the water poorly. Their clicking at night is one of the city’s small assurances. If the shells stop clicking, someone wakes the tether-keepers.

The blind eels are messengers from below. No one owns them, no one follows them too far. When one passes through a street, people pause work and let it go. Its appearance before a journey means delay. Its appearance beneath a nursery means move the cradles higher. Whether these omens are superstition or generations of ecological observation no longer matters; they have kept people alive.

And beneath all of them, or among them, or slowly becoming them, there is the mineral life of the place itself.

The people do not know whether the Unlaid District is alive. They do not say it is. They do not say it is not. They treat it as one treats a sleeping elder whose dreams rearrange the house. They harvest its straight pale walls carefully, never from corners, never after dark, never without leaving a cord, bead, or carved sliver in return. When a new wall appears, they do not celebrate immediately. First they watch it. They taste the water. They send a lime-scraper, then a vent-listener, then children with sharp eyes but strict instructions not to touch. Only after months do they decide whether the wall is gift, warning, or question.

The city’s inhabitants, then, are not masters of a drowned mountain. They are its attentive tenants.

They live by yielding without surrendering. They accept that rooms will become pools, that gardens will drift, that stone will grow where no one asked it to, that water will take the shortest memory and make it long. Their genius is not conquest but companionship. They survive because they can distinguish change from disaster, and because they know that the world does not have to be finished before one can belong to it.

In the night, when the lamps are hooded and the rain presses down like another lake above the lake, they lie in hammocks and listen: rope creak, shell click, vent pulse, root drip, mineral settling grain by grain. No sound is meaningless. No silence is empty.

They sleep lightly because the mountain is still thinking.

And they intend to hear what it decides.
