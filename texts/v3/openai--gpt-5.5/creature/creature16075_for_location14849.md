The people who live there are not pale in the simple sense. They are the color of things kept from weather but not from time: limewashed bone, damp shell, cooled ash, the underside of stones. Some have a faint blue or violet cast where the blood shows near the surface. Some are freckled with mineral stains that never wash away. Their hands are darker than their faces, burnished by stone dust, salt, grease, soot, and the hides of beasts.

They are slight, mostly. Not fragile, but made for conserving force. Narrow shoulders, strong backs, long tendons in the wrists, feet that spread well on wet stone. Their bodies know slopes better than roads. They climb without seeming to climb, using the smallest irregularities in walls and old carvings. They crouch easily. They sit on their heels for hours. Their children learn balance before speech, because floors move, ledges crumble, and a careless fall may not end where the eye thinks it will.

Their eyes are large, but not blind. The cold mineral light has shaped them differently from surface light. They see small differences in whiteness the way others might distinguish reds and greens: fresh fracture from old calcite, safe salt from bitter salt, living damp from dead damp, the faint shine that means a wall is sweating before the droplets appear. In brighter districts they go barefaced. In flare-seasons, or under crystal seams, they wear slitted visors of smoked shell, bone, or thin beaten metal. They do not like sudden darkness, not because they fear it, but because darkness underground usually means something has closed, shifted, flooded, or opened.

They are quiet by habit and by anatomy of culture. Their voices can be clear when needed, but they are seldom raised. Speech is used close to the body: murmured into a shoulder, passed hand to hand, shaped against another person’s palm. A great deal of their language is silent. Fingers mark direction, ownership, warning, affection, refusal, weather, fracture, debt. Knuckles tapped against stone can say more than a shouted sentence. Children first learn the sign for wait, then the sign for listen, then the sign for do not trust the floor.

Their mouths are often closed, their faces apparently calm, but they are not solemn people. Their humor is minute and constant. A raised eyebrow at a collapsing wall that had been promised to last another season. A child tying a false warning-knot in an elder’s cord-map. A beast refusing a route that three planners approved. They laugh with breath rather than sound, shoulders shaking, mouths open but almost silent. Loud laughter is for very deep rooms, far from crystal.

They do not think of themselves as survivors of a fallen city. That would imply that the city had once been complete. To them, completion is a dangerous idea. A finished wall has begun to fail. A settled district is overdue for rearrangement. A name used too long grows brittle. Their history is not a line behind them but a set of exposed layers: house under plaza, plaza under cistern, cistern under sleeping court, sleeping court under a market that will someday be cut apart for blocks.

Their elders are not keepers of dates. They are keepers of behavior. They remember how a certain arch sounded before it sagged, where condensation gathered in the year of bitter salt, which beasts would not cross the eastern basin before it opened, which family ignored a narrowing doorway and had to be dug out through the roof. Wisdom is practical, but the practical has become sacred from long repetition.

Their priests, if they can be called priests, are surveyors, tasters, and listeners.

They carry bowls of water to set on suspect ground. They hang strings from ceilings and mark the lean of clay weights. They press their tongues to walls. They sleep beside new cracks. They know which mineral scars are healing and which are only sealing over hunger. They do not command the rock. They interpret its reluctance.

Their holy people are often missing fingers.

Not from punishment, but from attention. A hand placed too close to a growing seam. A wedge tested too soon. A beast’s harness caught during a tremor. Such injuries are not hidden. The maimed hand is a credential: proof of contact with change. Children are allowed to touch the scarred knuckles of old crack-watchers before their first crossing of a new bridge.

They dress in layers of hide, woven gut, shell scales, felted animal hair, and cloth so old in method that no one remembers its first fiber. If there is no plant, then their textiles come from beasts, membranes, shed skins, hair, and long strips of treated inner hide split fine as thread. Their garments are pale at first but never remain so. They acquire the city: white dust in seams, grey damp at hems, rust-red at the knees, black soot at cuffs. Most clothing is tied rather than sewn shut, because bodies must be freed quickly from snag, flood, or collapse. Sleeves can be removed. Hoods can become bags. Belts are also measuring cords. Jewelry is practical: salt tokens, bead tallies, small stone weights, polished chips from important fractures, shell disks indicating basin rights or oven obligations.

They have little privacy in the surface sense, but many degrees of nearness. Families are not only blood groups. A household may consist of siblings, former spouses, apprentices, an elderly map-bearer, two unrelated children rescued from a sealed district, three pack-beasts who know the route to a vent, and a sleeping animal too large to move whose presence has made the front room into a shrine-road. Kinship follows maintenance. Whoever helps shore your wall becomes partly yours. Whoever shares a cistern during a dry interval is remembered longer than a cousin.

Their dwellings are provisional without seeming poor. A room may be beautiful even if everyone knows it will be dismantled within ten years. Walls are fitted from blocks used by generations before, each stone bearing ghosts of former angles. Thresholds are treasured. A good lintel may pass through twenty houses. Doorways are watched closely, because a doorway changing shape means the rock has taken an interest. When that happens, the household does not fight long. They remove their bowls, sleeping mats, tools, ancestor shells, and cord bundles. They touch the narrowing sides once, in farewell or apology, and build elsewhere.

They sleep low, often in wall niches, on platforms, or in hammocks of braided hide where the floor is unreliable. Infants sleep in suspended bowls lined with warm membrane. The very old sleep near cisterns, not because they are abandoned there, but because the sound of intermittent water is considered gentle to a mind loosening from the present.

Their dead are not buried in the ground. The ground is too active and too inhabited. Some dead are dried in salt rooms and wrapped in shell plates. Some are placed in high niches until mineral skin grows over them. Some are given to particular beasts that consume flesh without scattering bone. Bones are kept, used, carved, built with. A grandmother may become a roof peg, a bridge charm, a handle for a blade used only in kitchens, a polished listening rod. This is not disrespect. To remain load-bearing is a blessing.

They do not worship ancestors as rulers. They consult them as materials.

The animals live with them in arrangements older than domestication and less obedient.

The blind pack-beasts are central to the city. They are low, broad, and patient, with seal-smooth hides and blunt heads that sway close to the ground. Their eyes are sealed under skin or reduced to pale knots. They smell damp, salt, pressure, and recent stonebreak. A good pack-beast can refuse a road three hours before it cracks. They haul sledges on runners worn glassy by centuries of passage. They are not driven so much as negotiated with. Their handlers walk beside them, touching flank, jaw, harness, ear-slit. A beast that stops is never beaten at once. First the ground is checked. Then the air. Then the ceiling. Only after all has been judged safe does someone scold the beast, and even then lightly, because the beast may know something that has not yet happened.

There are wall-creatures like pale articulated hands. They move vertically, delicately, with the caution of scribes crossing wet ink. They feed on mineral films, on salts, on microscopic life in condensation. People do not kill them inside houses. Their presence means a wall is breathing properly. If they abandon a district, cistern-keepers are summoned.

There are scavengers on stilts, thin-legged and soft-mouthed, that pick through refuse pits. They have translucent ears and long toothless snouts, and they sort what the people discard better than any child could be taught: shell from bone, edible rot from poison, useful gut from spoiled membrane. Markets tolerate them. Kitchens curse them. Children imitate their mincing walk.

Wingless moths cluster around warm chimneys and oven vents, though moth is only the nearest word. They are plush, eyeless, and almost weightless, with folded nubs where wings might once have been or might someday be. Their bodies collect soot and mineral pollen. When disturbed they do not fly but loosen, dropping like flakes of ash. Their shed fuzz is gathered for tinder and infant padding.

The cistern eels are seasonal miracles. For months they are only knots in dried mud, leathery cords wedged into cracks at the bottoms of basins. Then the hidden reservoirs exhale, channels pulse, water enters, and the eels soften back into life. Children watch for the first movement with the seriousness of astronomers. The eels are eaten, revered, feared, and used as omens depending on the district. An eel appearing in a basin thought dead can renew a neighborhood’s claim to remain there.

The hand-sized soft stones are beloved by children and old women. At rest they are indistinguishable from rounded mineral lumps. Moisture wakes them. They unfold slowly: a fringe of feelers, a breathing slit, a muscular foot, a shy puckered mouth. They graze on wet salt and vanish into stillness again when dry. Some households keep them near doorways as damp-watchers. If the stone-creature opens, water is coming. If it opens too wide and turns itself inside out, bad water is coming.

The larger sleeping things in plazas are not pets, not livestock, not citizens, but they are included in civic life. They lie for months or years in public places, ribcages rising under sediment-patterned hides. No one knows whether they choose old roads because roads are warm, or whether roads form around them because people learn to pass where they sleep. Their dreams are believed to affect nearby fractures. A market may relocate rather than wake one. Children climb on them only in districts with lax elders. When one finally rises, the whole neighborhood watches in silence. Its underside may reveal old pavement, lost coins, bones, names, a spring, or a crack that has been waiting in darkness beneath its weight.

There are also things less integrated.

White lizards in the outer avenues, their skins nearly luminous, fast enough to seem like chips of light breaking off the floor.

Long-bodied aqueduct dwellers that coil in dry channels and drink condensation with hairlike tongues.

Soft flocking animals that move over flats like ash pushed by a breath. They do not seem to have leaders. When alarmed, they flatten until they become stains.

Pale crabs that emerge only during architecture-storms, clicking across wet statues and vanishing when the salt rings dry.

Deep things heard only through bowls of trembling water.

The people classify animals not by shape but by relation to change. There are damp-callers, crack-knowers, salt-cleaners, heat-thieves, refuse-saints, road-liars, basin-sleepers, and those that must never be named while under a seam of crystal. A creature’s usefulness is not merely economic. It is informational. Every species is a gauge, a warning, a calendar, a rumor from the stone.

Their food is almost entirely animal, mineral, and microbial. Pale meat dried in salt. Rendered fat. Eel paste. Shell broth. Eggs from crevice colonies. Thin cakes made from powdered bone, mineral starches, and living films scraped from warm vents. There may be fungus, but if so, they do not think of it as plant. It is a skin of the undercity, a lamp-fed curd, a pale leather that grows where heat and damp agree. They cultivate it on slabs, in chimney throats, under covered basins. Some kinds glow faintly. Some are poisonous unless washed in three waters. Some are reserved for the sick because they bring dreams of open air, though no one living has seen open air.

Salt is wealth, medicine, boundary, flavor, oath. To share unsalted food is intimacy or desperation. To oversalt a guest’s bowl is an insult in some districts and a marriage proposal in others. Salt kitchens are among the most stable institutions in the city, though their buildings move like everything else. They know how to dry meat without smoke when smoke would anger a seam, how to preserve eel, how to scrape bitter crust from sweet, how to read the color of evaporation.

Their markets are quiet not because they lack passion but because value is tactile. Buyers handle weight, grain, flex, dampness, edge. A bargain may consist of two people touching the same object for a long time, each adjusting finger pressure until agreement is reached. Disputes are settled by witnesses who remember the feel of the item before trade. Written contracts exist only in crude marks; true contracts are knots, scars, shared labor, and public memory.

They make art, though much of it would not look like art to strangers. They polish wear into beauty. They carve not new faces but the last surviving groove of an old face, preserving erosion rather than resisting it. They suspend strings of shell and bone where condensation will strike them one drop at a time, producing patterns too soft to be called music and too intentional to be called dripping. They arrange stones according to former uses: hearth-stone beside bridge-stone beside cradle-stone beside prison-stone, making histories one can walk around.

Their songs are nearly inaudible. Hummed into bowls. Breathed through shell flutes. Tapped with fingernails on sled runners. Some songs are meant for beasts and use frequencies felt more than heard. Some are sung only during flares, when the crystals brighten and all ordinary work pauses. These flare-songs are not pleas. They are acknowledgments: we see the ceiling seeing us.

They have law, but law is local, conditional, and geological. A rule may hold in one basin and be absurd in the next. Marriage customs change across a bridge. Ownership of a cistern depends on who cleared it, who first tasted it, who maintained its channels, which animals drink there, and whether it has ever gone completely dry. No one owns a crack. A crack belongs first to itself, then to those endangered by it, then to those who can read it, then to those who salvage from it.

Children are educated by errands. They carry salt between kitchens, messages between scaffold crews, damp cloths to elders watching new seams. They learn the city through obligations. At a certain age each child is blindfolded and led through familiar streets after a condensation storm, then asked to return by smell, slope, and air movement. Another rite requires them to sit alone beside a sleeping plaza-beast until they can match their breathing to its ribs without fear.

They are tender with children but unsentimental about danger. A child who ignores a chalked fracture is not merely scolded; they are made to sit with a crack-watcher through a whole dimming, watching water tremble in a bowl. Fear is trained into attention, not panic.

The city’s builders are its most admired laborers. Not architects in the commanding sense, but rearrangers, shorers, listeners with mallets. They travel in crews with wedges, braided gut, bone poles, stone blades, soft metal chisels, and old songs for keeping rhythm without noise. They can dismantle a house in a day and rebuild it around a vent by the next dimming. They know when to save a carved block and when to grind it for mortar. They argue constantly with hand signs. Their bodies are marked by white dust that settles permanently in the lines of their skin.

Map-bearers are stranger. They carry bundles of cord so dense they resemble nests or internal organs. Each knot is a place, but not only a place: a condition of place. A cistern knot may include dryness history, ownership, taste, eel recurrence, depth, last collapse, and whether a certain pack-beast once refused it. Maps are updated by untying as much as tying. A dead district is not removed. Its knot is loosened and moved inward, where fingers can still find it but do not catch on it during daily use.

They do not have kings in any permanent way. Authority gathers where need gathers: around a flood-reader during wet pulses, a salt-master during scarcity, a beast-handler during migration, a scaffold chief during collapse, a memory-elder during disputes over old thresholds. Power that lasts too long is considered structurally unsound. A leader who refuses to yield office is compared to a wall that will not crack and is therefore more dangerous than one that has honestly split.

Their idea of courage is not defiance. Defiance is for fools and surface stories. Courage is entering a newly opened lower street with a rope around the waist. Courage is abandoning a beautiful house because the doorway has narrowed. Courage is leaving water in a basin during drought because the old rule says the stone must believe. Courage is walking around a sleeping animal even when the detour takes half a day.

They have myths, but their myths are not full of green worlds and suns, unless those are very old and now mistrusted. Their creation stories begin with hardness softening. With a ceiling remembering light. With the first people waking inside a sealed reservoir after the water withdrew. With beasts teaching roads to move. With a city built by giants, then inherited by smaller, wiser hands. With the idea that above the stone there may be another brightness, but it is too young, too loud, too full of weather to be safe.

Some believe the city is descending. Some believe it is being slowly born upward. Some believe each opened lower layer is not the past but a future that hardened too early. These are not contradictions to them. Stone has more than one direction of time.

They are not trapped, exactly. Trapped is a word for those who expect departure. These people expect revision. Their lives are spent among closures and openings, losses and salvages, temporary shelters and recurrent waters. They are mournful sometimes, but not hopeless. They have the patience of creatures whose catastrophe is older than memory and therefore no longer an event. It is climate. It is citizenship.

What lives there, finally, is not only a people among animals, or animals among ruins. It is a whole society of attention.

Every inhabitant is listening with some part of itself: the pack-beast with its sealed face, the eel in its dry basin, the wall-creature with its jointed feet, the child with chalk on their fingers, the elder with tongue against stone, the sleeping giant with its ribs lifting under sedimentary hide. They live by yielding without surrendering. They build because things fall. They remember because names wear away. They remain because remaining, there, has never meant staying still.
