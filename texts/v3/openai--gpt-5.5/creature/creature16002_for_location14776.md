What lives there is mostly too thin to cast a shadow.

At first it seems the chamber has only visitors: drops, steam, heat, mineral stains, a few pale things startled into motion at the pool’s edge. But the true inhabitants are spread through the wet places in layers finer than breath. They live as films, veils, skins, and threads: a low society of microbes clinging to stone where water, warmth, metal, and light meet.

They are not green. They do not make the chamber soft. They do not announce themselves with growth anyone would call abundant.

They lie along cracks as rust-red slicks no thicker than oil. They gather in black smears where manganese seeps. They make amber skins over the warmest shallows, so delicate that a falling drop breaks them into islands. In the brightest seams they form a translucent glaze, almost invisible until it catches the mineral radiance and answers with a dull honey-colored shine.

They feed on boundaries.

One kind lives where cooler seep-water touches hot basalt, harvesting the change. Another gathers around sulfur beads and iron stains, taking energy from minerals the way surface plants take it from sun. Some use the chamber’s strange light, not as leaves would, not generously, but sparingly, with dark pigments hidden in their membranes. Others avoid the light and work underneath the films, in the oxygen-poor underside where dissolved metals arrive from the stone.

Together they make a living skin over a place that does not yet know whether it will remain habitable.

Their colonies are constantly being erased and redrawn. A warm exhalation dries a ledge and cracks them apart. A new trickle wakes a seam and lets them spread in an hour. A salt crust buries them; they sleep beneath it. A drop breaks the crust; they begin again. Nothing here grows in confidence. Everything grows in readiness.

Among these films move the animals.

They are few, small, translucent, and nearly without ornament. Their bodies have the economical look of things that cannot afford beauty except as a side effect of being precisely made. Most are no longer than a grain of rice; many are much smaller. They are pale because there is no need for camouflage in a world of glowing stone and empty water. Their organs show faintly through them: a thread of gut, a pulsing speck, a milky knot where eggs are carried.

The most visible are the little crustacean-like grazers at the warm margin of the pool.

They look unfinished, as if carved from a chip of clouded glass. Each has a curved back, fine jointed legs, and two long feelers held ahead like questions. They do not swim so much as start and stop through the water: dart, cling, scrape, freeze. Their legs comb the microbial films from the stone in strokes too small to see clearly. Their mouthparts work constantly when they feed, polishing invisible meals from the wet mineral skin.

They have no proper eyes, or none that look like eyes. Light reaches them through the whole body. They sense brightness as pressure, heat as distance, chemistry as direction. A change in the glow along a seam tells them water has arrived there. A bitter trace in the pool warns them away from a newly opened sulfur vein. The tremor of a falling drop sends them under ledges, into cracks, beneath loose plates of silica.

Their lives are arranged around brief opportunities.

When the amber film thickens at the pool’s edge, they come out. When the eastern fissure breathes too hot, they retreat into cooler seams. When fresh condensation runs down a wall, they climb the wet track and graze until it dries. They are not numerous enough to swarm. At most, three or four may appear in the same patch of light, feeding separately, each leaving a nearly invisible cleared trail through the film.

They lay their eggs under overhangs where heat does not spike too sharply. The eggs are clear beads glued in a row, difficult to tell from mineral droplets except that they do not fall. Inside them, tiny bodies fold and refold, waiting for the right wetness. Many never hatch. Some are cooked by a sudden plume, sealed in by salt, or eaten before they open. Enough survive for the chamber to keep its pale punctuation of movement.

There are worms too, though they are easier to imagine than to catch sight of.

They live in the cracks feeding on what gathers there: bacteria, mineral slime, dead skins, the softened remains of eggs that failed. A worm’s body is a living thread, white or colorless, scarcely thicker than a hair. It extends from a seam, tastes the water, and withdraws at the first disturbance. It has no interest in open space. The bright chamber, to it, is only the dangerous outside of the crack.

Some of the worms graze. Some hunt.

A hunting one waits in a narrow channel where the tiny grazers must pass between pool and ledge. It does not chase. It lies half-hidden, the front of its body held loose in the water. When something brushes it, it folds with sudden exactness. The struggle is almost too small to be called violence: a shiver, a tightening, a pale speck pulled into darkness. Then the channel is still again.

Near the meniscus live smaller things: mites or mite-like creatures, round-bodied, slow, with legs like bent hairs. They walk on films that are neither quite water nor stone. They browse along salt edges and feed where bubbles leave rings of residue. They are tolerant of conditions that would kill the grazers, but fragile in other ways; a single falling drop can drown or fling them into water too hot to survive.

There are also animals that may not be animals in the ordinary sense of the word from above. Transparent sacs fixed to stone. Contracting threads. Minute bells that open and close in the water. Soft colonial things that look like mineral fuzz until they retract all at once. Some filter the shallow pool. Some farm bacteria on their own surfaces. Some are probably temporary, carried in through seep-water from another hidden pocket, surviving here for a generation or less before conditions turn against them.

Nothing here is ancient yet.

The inhabitants are colonists without knowing it. Their ancestors came in with groundwater, sealed inside pores, cracks, and mineral films, waiting through pressure and darkness until the chamber opened and offered warmth, wet stone, dissolved metals, and light. They did not arrive as an army. They arrived as traces. Spores. Eggs. Dormant cells. A few living specks in a thread of water.

The chamber did not welcome them. It merely failed to kill all of them.

That is enough.

The largest lives here are still smaller than a fingernail, but the most important inhabitant is larger than any of them because it is spread everywhere: the collective film, the breathing microbial skin that makes the chamber edible. Without it, the pale grazers would vanish. Without the grazers, the films would thicken, rot, mineralize, or choke themselves under their own residues. Without the worms and mites, the dead would remain too long in the wrong places. The whole system is not balanced in any graceful, perfected way. It is provisional. It is a set of accidents that have begun to repeat.

The creatures do not think of the chamber as a room. They know it as gradients.

Warm to hotter. Wet to dry. Bright to brighter. Bitter, metallic, safe, scalding, edible, empty. The pool is not a pool but a shifting map of temperatures. The wall is not a wall but a vertical country of seep-lines and crusts. The eastern fissure is a season. The falling drops are weather. The low ledge above the shallow water is a pasture when damp, a desert when dry, a refuge when the pool flashes too hot.

Their time is not human time. A minute can be an event: a film opens, a crust dissolves, a chemical plume passes, a grazer crosses into danger and disappears. A day can be an age for a microbial bloom. A season may remake the whole population. If a new crack admits cooler water, one set of lives will spread. If heat strengthens, another will take over. If the luminous seam dries, the creatures that gather beneath it will leave no sign except a few pale shells caught in salt.

Those shells are the nearest thing the place has to bones.

They collect in tiny drifts along the pool’s lower edge: transparent husks, molted skins, empty egg casings, bodies made weightless by decay. Mineral films overgrow them quickly. In time they may become part of the crust, little ghosts trapped in silica and iron, evidence that life touched this chamber during one of its brief favorable moods.

What lives there is not flourishing in the lush sense.

It is testing.

It is waiting in cracks. It is feeding in intervals. It is retreating from heat, returning with condensation, multiplying under a skin of amber light. It has no roots, no leaves, no song, no den, no nest larger than a bead of eggs under stone. Its drama is almost entirely microscopic. But it is alive with the stubbornness of first attempts.

The chamber’s inhabitants are the kind of life that makes no claim on a place until the place has almost destroyed it and failed. Then they remain—not boldly, not permanently, but carefully—turning mineral breath into flesh, light into motion, wet stone into a narrow world.
