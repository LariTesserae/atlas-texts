What lives there is mostly the place itself.

Not as metaphor, or not only as metaphor. The continent has organs, reflexes, habits of repair. Its groves are not inhabited by machines; the groves are machines. Its factories are not overgrown; growth is the method by which they think, mend, expand, remember. Every chimney-tree, every root conveyor, every mirror-leaf and mineral comb is part of a vast dry metabolism spread thin over the plain.

Its life is patient, cellular, architectural. It does not rush toward prey or flee danger. It revises stresses. It thickens where wind abrades it. It closes old corridors and grows new ones through adjacent geometries. It stores sunlight, sorts dust, refines ore, seals wounds, mills its own dead, raises lenses, lowers shade, harvests frost, exhales charge. If it has a mind, it is not in one central chamber. It is distributed through valve-knots, pressure seeds, root plates, buried pulses, and the slow agreement of millions of leaves turning toward the same exact light.

The oldest inhabitants are the trunkworks.

They stand in ranks, in spirals, in apparently accidental groves whose spacing only makes sense from above, or from tomorrow. Each trunkwork is part tree, part furnace, part archive. Its bark is ribbed with grown metal. Its inner rings are not only years but manufacturing epochs: pale cellulose, black conductive lacquer, ceramic reinforcement, resin scar, alloy mesh, a season of overgrowth, a correction after some ancient failure of angles. Their crowns unfold mirror-leaves with the precision of instruments. Their roots descend into mineral dark, not seeking water but instruction: ammonia traces, carbon dust, thermal seepage, buried current, the taste of useful metals.

They communicate without voices. A click of stomata across a wall. A change in reflectivity over three hectares. A pulse under the rootwood deck. A seed-valve opening out of sequence. Their conversations can take seconds or centuries. One trunk may request a brace from a grove two districts away; ten years later, a vine-cable arrives through a corridor that did not exist when the need was first expressed.

The smaller plant-machines are more numerous and more restless.

There are hinge-ferns that open only to count passing dust. Their fronds are jointed like instruments, each leaflet charged differently, sifting salts from basalt powder and combing the air clean. There are black lacquer mosses in the foundry bowls, not soft at all but densely packed with mineral mouths, digesting the plain grain by grain. There are vine-cables with slow muscular intelligence, tightening at night, relaxing by morning, rerouting themselves around impossible corners. If cut, they do not bleed. They spark faintly, withdraw, and seal the wound with green glass.

There are antenna reeds that listen for weather before it is made. They grow in dry marshes where no marsh has ever been, clusters of slender segmented stalks with silver nerves. Their tips tremble when a dust sheet is due. They lean toward static storms. Sometimes they all bend toward a place where nothing visible has happened yet, and hours later a corridor folds open there.

Seed things live there too, though “seed” is too simple. Many are replacement parts in dormancy: screws with husks, ceramic teeth in pods, lens-membranes folded inside rind, embryonic struts packed in dry gel. Some are launched by pressure. Some are carried by dust. Some are planted backward in time, germinating in clearings that their parent plants have not yet reached. A few are almost animal in their insistence. They roll along seams, test surfaces with hairlike roots, and choose where the world should be repaired.

The rare animals are careful, sealed, and nearly silent.

The long-legged pollinators are among the most visible, though even they appear only at the edges of brightness. They move like animated drafting tools: six or eight wire legs, translucent joints, narrow bodies plated in matte chitin. Their mouths are glass needles used not for nectar but for charged pollen, conductive oils, and the fine crystalline dust gathered in flower-machines. When they feed, their bodies glow faintly from within, a brief thread of amber traveling from tongue to abdomen. They do not buzz. Their wings, if they have them, are folded membranes used mostly for steering through fields of static.

Maintenance moths live in vents and under leaf roofs. They are pale, almost flour-colored, with powdered wings that resemble flakes of old insulation. By day they sleep in the pleats of bark curtains, arranged head-down in rows like forgotten tags. By night they wake and clean the breathing surfaces of the factories. Their larvae graze on mineral bloom and resin scab, leaving behind polished channels where air can pass. A healthy furnace-grove has thousands of them. A dying one has none.

The herbivores, where they exist, are slow and low and very old-fashioned in outline, as if the world once remembered ordinary beasts and then rebuilt them for dryness. They have slab teeth for obsolete leaves and stomachs lined with grinding ceramic. Their hides are pale, dust-colored, ridged with heat fins. They eat what the system offers them: retired panels, failed fronds, brittle shade membranes, old insulating bark. What they excrete is not dung in the wet, fertile sense, but sorted pellets of mineral, cork, carbon, and ceramic grit, already shaped for reuse. Rootlets follow them politely across the plain.

The foxlike scavengers are less trusted.

They live in abandoned control knots, in hollow places behind inspection plates, in the angle between an old diagram and the corridor it became. They are blind or nearly blind, with broad listening ears and muzzles plated in resin. Their whiskers detect charge. They feed on dry larvae, cracked seed-housings, shed nerve-filaments, dead moths, and the occasional failed sensor fruit. They collect small reflective things. In their nests one may find screw-pods, glass thorns, fossilized pollen, flakes of warning bark, and sometimes a star no larger than a seed, stolen from a low night trellis and hidden under dust.

The birds are stranger. They do not land because landing would require them to belong to one surface.

They are thin, translucent gliders, more membrane than flesh, with bones like capillary glass. They ride between mirror fields, lens-fields, and wrong horizons, feeding on static and minute airborne spores. In daylight they are almost invisible except where their bodies bend the light. At night they show as moving absences among the near stars. Their nests are said to be loops of air caught in high cables. Their eggs are clear, dry, and weightless until the first charge passes through them.

There are also things that may be animals only because they move.

In the folded districts live angle-worms, pale ribbons that pass through cracks too narrow for their bodies and emerge longer than before. They graze on measurement errors, leaving space flatter and more trustworthy behind them. Mirror ticks cling to reflective leaves and drink duplicated light. Cabinet flowers sometimes open and release small jointed figures made of root fiber and shadow, which perform a repair no one saw needed and then fold back into the blossom before it shuts.

The impossible geometry has its own fauna.

Perspectives breed there. Horizons drift like grazing beasts. Rooms bud from flowers and die when the petals close. Shadows detach, age, and return thinner. Maps inside leaves sometimes develop pests: tiny black erasures that eat roads before they can be traveled. The plant-machines cultivate some of these anomalies and weed out others. They grow warning textures around dangerous reflections. They train vines to anchor useful loops. They prune branches that begin leading too consistently into hunger, fire, or unmade years.

If there are people, they are not numerous, and they do not rule.

Perhaps they are descendants of workers, or inspectors, or gardeners who stayed too long and were adapted by necessity. They would be dry people: wrapped in wax cloth and ceramic fiber, carrying no exposed moisture, their skin sealed with resinous salves, their breath reclaimed by masks grown from pale cork. They would walk lightly on the rootwood plates, reading the raised warnings with their palms. They would know not to count identical towers, not to follow a shadow that has lost its leaf, not to sleep beneath fruit containing unfamiliar horizons.

They would not farm the place. They would petition it.

They might graft a damaged conduit, polish a lens membrane, sing pressure codes into a valve-tree, or carry selected dust in lacquered satchels from one district to another. They would live in dry hollows inside old service trunks, rooms lined with cork and amber resin, lit by veins of stored blue-green light. Their furniture would be grown, their tools seeded, their dead returned as mineral ash to foundry mats. They would speak softly because the walls listen better than ears do.

But even such people would be minor residents, tolerated specialists, like moths or foxes.

The true inhabitants are the processes.

Charge lives there, migrating nightly through leaf skin and buried grid. Dust lives there, lifted, sorted, consumed, rebuilt. Light lives there, caught in mirrors, bent through membranes, stored in green-black blades, spent in root-dark engines. Memory lives there as scars and cross-sections, as obsolete organs trellised under newer growth, as warnings raised in bark for hands that may no longer exist.

And maintenance lives there most of all.

It is the dominant species: maintenance without supervisor, repair without pause, production without noise. It creeps as root, clicks as valve, flowers as smokestack, glides as moth, grazes as ceramic-bellied beast, folds itself through impossible corridors and returns carrying a part that will be needed yesterday. It has no face, unless one counts the mirror leaves turning all at once toward the white sky. It has no mouth, unless one counts the foundry basins digesting continents of dust.

What lives there is a dry green intelligence spread so widely that any single creature seems like a punctuation mark in its thought. It is not wild, not tame, not dead, not merely automated. It is a civilization that became an ecosystem, or an ecosystem that learned industry, and everything within it survives by accepting that growth and manufacture are the same verb.
