What lives there is not a single people, but a small, damp civilization made of many hungers.

The most visible inhabitants are the ants.

They are dark, polished, quick, and purposeful, each one no larger than a moving punctuation mark. They come out of the mud corridors in narrow files, disappear beneath roofed galleries, reappear on root-bridges, under leaves, along the dry underside of the fallen branch. Their bodies catch the green light in brief flashes: head, thorax, abdomen, then gone into shadow. They do not wander in the human sense. Even when one seems lost, it is reading the world with its feet and antennae, tasting the air, following or questioning a line of scent laid down by others.

They are the street traffic, the messengers, the repair crews, the bearers of impossible loads. One carries a green shaving of leaf twice its own length, held upright like a sail. Another grips a pale larva in its jaws, carrying it with a care that looks almost ceremonial. Others move empty-mouthed but not purposelessly, transporting information instead of matter: alarm, food, dampness, breach, death, new passage, unsafe passage, queen-scent, fungus-scent, rain.

Their city is partly inside them. The roads are not only mud tubes and cleared pads; they are chemical paths renewed by walking. The walls are not only clay and chewed fiber; they are maintained by memory distributed among thousands of bodies. No ant knows the city, but the colony knows it. No one designed the plazas, yet litter gathers at the edges and nurseries rise where the dampness is tolerable. No ruler stands in a tower. Deep below, hidden in a chamber no predator has yet reached, the queen is less a monarch than a warm origin, an egg-laying center around which the city arranges its urgency.

The workers are small and numerous, the color of wet bark or burnt sugar. Their jaws are fine tools: scissors, tongs, clamps, shovels. Their antennae are always touching—walls, ground, one another. They pause face to face in doorways and exchange more than greeting. Food passes mouth to mouth. Messages pass in odor. A worker arriving from the wet margin may be sampled by three others and redirected before it has taken ten steps. A newly collapsed road is not announced; it is smelled, touched, encountered, and then answered.

The guards are heavier-headed, slower until they are not. They wait near openings where shadow thickens. At the first tremor of a beetle’s foot, they appear like knots forming in a thread. They do not defend an idea of territory; they defend warmth, brood, fungus, stored food, the continuity of traffic. If the threat is too large, they become architecture. Their bodies fill gaps until mud can be brought. They are doors that bite.

Beneath and beside them live the paler builders: termites, secretive and soft-bodied, more like animated grains of rice than citizens of the surface. They are the hidden masons of the fallen branch and the mud-dark underside of things. Where the ants make visible traffic, the termites make concealed transformation. They inhabit wood from within, turning dead branch into chamber, chamber into food, food into bodies, bodies into soil. Their tunnels are warm, close, almost airless. Their world is touch and vibration. Light is not merely unnecessary to them; it is a wound.

They move through galleries plastered with earth, saliva, and excrement, laying a skin between themselves and the drying air. Their workers are blind or nearly blind, pale as root tips, with bellies full of the invisible helpers that let them digest wood. Inside them live other lives—microbes and protozoans turning cellulose into sustenance. The termite is already a settlement before it joins the larger city.

Their soldiers, when they appear, seem specialized almost to absurdity: heads too large, jaws shaped for closing one kind of argument. Some stand at broken walls while workers repair around them. They are not brave in the way a solitary animal is brave. They are organs of the mound, extruded briefly into danger.

Between the ants and termites is no simple peace. They share the hollow because the hollow is rich enough and complicated enough to permit overlap. They raid, avoid, seal, exploit. The ants patrol the outside of structures the termites hollow from within. The termites wall themselves away from ant smell. Sometimes a breach opens and the two cities meet like incompatible liquids. Then there is panic, violence, rapid masonry, bodies dragged backward into darkness. An hour later, the wall is closed and the mud surface bears only a damp scar.

But the city is not only its builders.

On the shining skins of water live springtails, minute and elastic, creatures of surface tension and sudden disappearance. They gather where leaf-cups hold rain, grazing on films too thin for larger mouths to notice. They seem to have been made from dust, soot, and nervousness. At a shadow they release themselves into the air with hidden springs, vanishing and landing elsewhere, unharmed by a scale of physics that would drown another animal.

Mites move everywhere, though they are easy to miss. Some are red as sparks. Some are translucent. Some cling to beetles, ants, fly legs, snail flesh, riding larger animals as weather systems. To them, the city is not streets and towers but moving continents: a beetle’s knee, an ant’s neck, the furred underside of a caterpillar. They are parasites, scavengers, passengers, cleaners, predators of eggs smaller than grains of pollen. Their lives occur almost below the threshold of intention.

Threadlike worms occupy the wet films in the soil, curling and uncurling through mineral darkness. Nematodes pass between grains where there seems to be no room for passage. They feed on bacteria, fungi, roots, each other. They are among the true old residents of such places, though no tower is built for them. When the mud dries they withdraw into waiting. When water returns they uncoil and resume the hidden commerce of rot and digestion.

There are larvae in the pools: mosquito young hanging head-down from the surface, breathing through the thin ceiling between water and air. They twitch when disturbed, dropping and rising like commas in clear glass. Other fly larvae work in the wet rot under leaves, soft, pale, blind mouths moving through the beginning of decay. To the ants they may be food. To the pool they are inhabitants. To the air, later, they will become brief wings.

Beetles move through the districts as armored weather.

Some are scavengers, nosing through refuse edges for softened fragments. Some are hunters. A predatory beetle at the mouth of an ant road is a catastrophe with legs: slow, deliberate, polished, smelling of danger. Its jaws enter openings. Its antennae test seams. Its feet leave marks in the raw soil that remain long after it has passed. The city feels it before it arrives. Traffic pulls inward. Guards accumulate. Workers carrying larvae turn back. A wall that was a passage becomes a barricade. The beetle does not hate the city; it reads it as a concentration of meat.

Spiders live there too, though they do not join the traffic. They wait in the angles: between two root hairs, under a curled leaf, beside a fungus rib, against a clod where their bodies become another speck of earth. Their patience is a kind of architecture. A web may be no more than three threads arranged across an ant path, or a loose trap strung in the air above a puddle where flies rise. Some spiders hunt without webs, stepping with unbearable delicacy over the mud roofs, pausing when vibrations pass underfoot.

The flies are temporary citizens, nervous and iridescent. They settle on wet bark, lift, settle again, rub their forelegs together as if considering the place. Fungus gnats drift in the dimness, their larvae hidden in wet organic seams. Tiny midges rise from pools at dusk. To the ants they are interruptions. To the spider they are announcements of supper. To the city itself they are pollinators, egg-layers, future corpses, brief visitors whose bodies will be sorted into use.

The snail is slower than anything else except growth.

It crosses the outer district at night, carrying its house with the grave authority of a wandering hill. Its body is a wet muscle, its eyestalks soft questions. It leaves behind a bright road that ignores all zoning, crossing ant avenues, mud roofs, seedling stems, refuse piles. The ants investigate the slime with agitation and distaste. Springtails gather at its edges. Fungal spores adhere to it. The snail does not belong to the city, yet for the hours of its crossing it changes the map. Its track catches light after the animal has gone, a silver street no builder intended.

At the margin waits the frog.

Small as a coin, colored like wet leaf and shadow, it arrives when the evening cool gathers in the mud. It is not a citizen but a god of the outskirts, sudden-mouthed and silent. Its throat pulses. Its eyes hold the whole miniature settlement without understanding it as a settlement. To the frog, the city is a place where food concentrates. Ants, flies, springtails, small beetles—each is a possible flicker taken into the mouth before sound can happen.

The frog’s violence is almost too fast to be an event. An ant walking along a cleared pad simply stops being there. Another vanishes from the lip of a pool. The city responds not with grief but with adjustment. Lines shift inward. Alarms bloom chemically. A route is abandoned until the frog moves on. Loss is one of the conditions under which the place is organized.

There are also the rooted inhabitants, slower and more absolute.

The seedlings are not citizens because they do not move aside. They are laws. A young root descends and the city must bend. A fine root hair enters a corridor and divides traffic. A grass blade falls and becomes a bridge, then an obstacle, then a damp wall, then food, then structure. Ferns shade districts before they unfurl fully. Liverworts claim the wettest ground in dark green tongues, making slick territories where ants step carefully and springtails thrive.

Fungi live there as both housemates and future conquerors. Pale threads run through the fallen wood, mapping it in silence. Some fungi are tended, encouraged, grazed, carried in spores from chamber to chamber. Others are enemies of brood, bloomers of dangerous mold in places too wet or too still. The city keeps shade, but not too much shade; moisture, but not drowning; decay, but not abandonment. Fungal life is everywhere, patient, capable of turning every architecture into food if maintenance falters.

And beneath all of them live the smallest residents: bacteria, yeasts, spores, algae films, invisible communities slicking the mud grains and leaf skins. They make the smells of the place—the sweetness after rain, the sourness under bark, the mineral breath of wet clay. They soften the dead. They feed the feeders. They digest what jaws only begin.

So the inhabitants are layered.

At the surface: dark ants, quick and social, carrying the city in scent.

Under the bark: pale termites, blind and communal, turning wood into corridors and flesh.

In the water films: springtails, larvae, worms, microbes, creatures of trembling lenses and mud pores.

At the edges: beetles, spiders, mites, snails, frogs, each using the city without belonging wholly to it.

Through everything: roots and fungi, slower powers around which all animal urgency must arrange itself.

None of them lives alone, even when solitary. Every body is part of another body’s weather. The ant is a builder, prey, carrier of mites, mover of seeds, wall-maker, corpse-to-be. The termite is a wood-eater, soil-maker, ant-food, host to gut symbionts. The snail is a grazer and road-layer. The fungus is food, infection, architecture, rot. The frog is predator and future nutrient. The city is less a collection of residents than a tense agreement among scales.

They live by repair.

They expect collapse the way trees expect wind. Rain will cave in roofs, open chambers, drown young, erase scent, turn avenues to streams. Then the bodies come out. Ants carry larvae upward. Termites seal light away. Workers taste the wet edges and decide where mud must go. Springtails scatter and return. Worms rise into the new films. Fungi swell. Beetles investigate the damage. The city is wounded into activity.

They do not build monuments because permanence would be a misunderstanding. They build continuities: a usable tunnel, a dry chamber for eggs, a sealed breach, a covered path from food to brood, a wall that will last until the next rain and then be made again.

What lives there, finally, is a many-bodied attention.

It is a population of tiny animals and slower green beings, all tuned to dampness, shade, touch, vibration, odor, and the weight of a single drop. It is a city of workers without tools, hunters without names, gardeners without plans, architects with mouths full of mud. It is nervous, fertile, provisional, and exact.

It lives close to the ground because the ground is its sky.
