What lives there does not live by growth.

The basin permits only forms of life that can endure without insisting on becoming more than they are. Anything hungry in the ordinary way would fail. Anything warm-blooded would spend itself against the official cold and leave only a temporary stain. Anything that depends on season, pursuit, nesting, flowering, migration, or decay would find no bargain to make with the place.

So the inhabitants are patient to the edge of nonexistence.

At first they seem like errors of attention: a darkening against a terrace, a height where there should only be stone, a hunched outline among frost-polished slabs. They resemble animals because the mind has few other categories for a thing that is present, silent, and not entirely architectural. Elk-sized, perhaps, though too low in the shoulders; cairn-shaped, though too balanced; wrapped in a surface like wet slate, lichen, hide, and old shadow all occupying the same skin. They stand with their foreparts lowered toward the ground, as if listening through their knees.

They do not graze. There is not enough to graze. They do not hunt. There is not enough motion to hunt.

They attend.

Their heads, if they have heads, are blunt and narrow, without antlers, without ears in any familiar arrangement. Along the front of them are pale seams or pits that may be eyes, though they are never seen to blink. Frost gathers along these seams and does not melt. When the roar shifts, the seams open a little wider, showing not flesh but a dim interior like clouded ice over deep water.

They feed on continuance. On the pressure of sound held too long in stone. On the unused violence of avalanches that never fall. On all the withheld events of the basin: rain that does not fill pools, echoes that return before their causes, distances that refuse completion. They stand where the law is thinnest and draw sustenance from contradiction.

This is why they are always far away. Nearness would require too clear a decision.

Their tracks, when they leave any, are the three narrow marks found in mud by the frozen seep. But the spacing is wrong because they do not walk step by step. They occupy a sequence of positions, and the ground remembers only a few. A track may be fresh, rimmed with wet mineral shine, and also ancient enough for frost to have grown into its bottom. Sometimes the prints cross slopes too steep for weight. Sometimes they begin in the middle of a gravel flat and end at a pool whose surface has not been broken.

They move most often when unobserved, but not secretly. Secrecy implies intention. Their stillness is not concealment; it is their native state. Motion is something they suffer briefly, like weather.

If one watches them too long, they become more like stone. If one looks away, the knowledge of them becomes animal again.

There are smaller lives as well.

Under stones live the white flat insects, thin as flakes of old ash. They have no urgency. Their bodies are almost translucent, with dark threads inside them like cracks in ice. They do not scurry so much as translate from one shadow to another. They eat the undersides of lichen mats, the mineral bloom on wet rock, perhaps the tiny black seeds that form from upward-falling drops near the central ring. They leave no shed skins. They may not molt. Their lives are held at a single stage, neither larval nor adult, each one an unfinished thought preserved indefinitely.

In the moss under overhangs are slower things: rootlike worms or wormlike roots, pale and cold, braided through the felted green. They respond to sound rather than moisture. When the roar deepens, they tighten. When the high metallic keening passes overhead, they loosen and extend microscopic hairs into the stone. They do not digest in the ordinary sense. They take in vibration, pressure, mineral bitterness. They make the moss possible. The moss makes them hidden. Together they form a kind of listening carpet in the dark creases of the basin.

The pools contain inhabitants too, though not swimmers.

Look down long enough into the tin-colored water and small dark flecks appear beneath the surface. They hang there without rising or sinking. Their outlines are circular, then angular, then like folded wings. They are not reflected overhead; they are in the water, or in the layer of world the water touches. They gather around reflected versions of the stone ring, around lower skies, around clouds that are not present above. If disturbed, they do not flee. The disturbance passes through one arrangement of the pool while they remain in another.

Perhaps they are seeds. Perhaps eyes. Perhaps the basin’s way of counting itself.

The bird that sometimes crosses above is not a resident in the same way. It belongs partly to the outer world, where hunger and direction still matter. Yet even it has been altered by passage over the hollow. Its wings are too long, its body too narrow, its flight too cleanly incised. It does not call. It leaves behind that black seam in the air because the basin does not immediately accept the fact of crossing. For several minutes the path remains, a wound made by motion. The bird may be the same bird every time. Or every bird that enters may become, for the length of the crossing, the one bird the basin allows.

But the true inhabitants are the ones that built without tools.

They are not seen directly. They live in the alignments.

They are in the knee-high walls that enclose nothing, in the huts made for bodies that do not correspond to any animal, in the black slabs with cup-marks full of ice. They are in the cairns whose stones face inward. They are not ghosts, because ghosts imply a prior living people. They are not spirits, because spirits imply a separation between matter and will. They are arrangements that learned preference.

At some point, if “point” is a word that applies there, stone began to answer sound. Gravel began to sort itself according to pressure. Blocks leaned toward one another and discovered shelter. Frost marked hollows, hollows collected echoes, echoes deepened the hollows. Repetition became habit. Habit became form. Form became almost intention.

Those are the builders: not beings with hands, but local agreements among stone, cold, thunder, and damaged distance. They live as architecture lives when no one uses it and yet it refuses to be ruin.

The three shelters at the gorge mouth are their clearest organs.

One opens onto the basin from a higher elevation, though its back wall should meet the mountain. One becomes cold water to the touch without wetting the hand. One holds a palm-sized night sky in the middle of the day. These are not decorations. They are apertures. The shelters are ways the basin senses its own impossible positions. Through them, it compares versions of itself: above and below, solid and fluid, clouded day and star-struck night.

The inhabitants tend these openings by remaining absent from them.

Nothing lives there that would sit by a fire, mend a tool, sharpen bone, cook meat, bury dead, or speak across a threshold. But the shelters are occupied all the same. Their emptiness is not vacancy. It is posture.

Near the central ring lives something larger than the silhouettes.

Not inside the ring exactly. Not beneath it. The distinction is unreliable. It resides in the incomplete circuit of dark blocks, in the warmer air that is only less cold, in the trembling beads of condensation, in the drops that fall upward and sideways, in the second rhythm beneath the roar.

It may be the basin’s heart, though it does not pump.

It may be a mouth, though it does not open.

It may be a sleeping animal so large that the surrounding mountains are not its walls but its ribs, and the ring of stones is the place where its breath comes nearest the surface. If so, it is not asleep in preparation for waking. Waking would be change, and change is not the law there. It sleeps as a glacier sleeps, as bedrock sleeps, as a word sleeps inside a language no one speaks anymore.

The silhouettes on the terraces may be its surface-thoughts, extruded briefly into animal-like form. The white insects may be its flakes. The pools may be its eyes looking sideways through time. The gorge may be its throat, roaring with all the motions it has refused to perform.

It is alive only if life can mean maintenance without progress.

It does not want visitors. It does not reject them. Want and rejection are too directional. A visitor entering the basin becomes another temporary condition to be resolved into permanence. A footprint, a shout, a displaced pebble, a remembered fear: all are accepted, stripped of ownership, and assigned a place. The basin does not kill in order to feed. It preserves until the distinction between arrival and always-having-been-there grows thin.

This is the danger of its inhabitants. Not attack. Incorporation.

Stand too long near the ring and your thoughts begin to settle into terraces. Your memories stop leading to one another and instead lie side by side, equally present, equally cold. Your name becomes a sound that might return from the gorge later in a voice older than yours. Your body remains yours, perhaps, but the mud may already have decided your prints are ancient. A stone you touched may turn its flattest face inward. A wall may acquire one more necessary block.

The creatures of that place are not numerous. They are not rare either. Number is difficult where distance folds and moments lie pressed against lateral moments. There may be seven of the hunched listeners, or one seen through seven arrangements of approach. There may be a colony of ash-insects under every stone, or a single insect repeated wherever shadow permits. The builders may be extinct, unborn, geological, or continuous with the structures themselves.

What lives in the basin is what has made a truce with impossibility.

It has no need to flourish.

It remains.
