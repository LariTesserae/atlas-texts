What lives there is not a people, not a single monster, not even a recognizable class of animals, but a whole cold civilization of appetite.

It begins below sight.

In the cracks, in the sweating seams of stone, in the places where iron-salt fluid wells up from the dark, there are the first inhabitants: films, mats, crusts, stains that are alive without looking alive. They are black, white, copper-green, bruised violet, gray as old fat. They spread over shale in slick skins and thicken around vents like curdled mineral milk. They do not reach upward like plants. They do not flower, leaf, or sway. They cling. They digest stone-breath. They drink seepage. They turn the basin’s buried chemistry into flesh.

Everything else feeds on them, directly or eventually.

The most numerous creatures are the small ones: blind, translucent, jointed things that crowd every pool and gutter. They look like shrimp made from fingernail parings and wet glass. Their organs are dim threads inside them. Their many legs beat constantly, not to travel far, but to hold position in weak currents, to comb the water, to sift the gray food suspended there. When rain fills the channels, they are swept along in pale clouds; when pools shrink, they pile against one another until the water seems thick with trembling.

In the silt live the braid-worms.

They are not solitary worms but cords of bodies, long pale strands made of many individuals knotted together head-to-tail and side-to-side. They move through mud like slow lightning, surfacing in arcs, vanishing, resurfacing elsewhere. Their skins are milky and faintly iridescent, marked by pressure-sensing pits instead of eyes. They eat the buried films and the soft remains of the dead. When disturbed, they split into separate lengths, each length trying to become a whole again. Predators tear them apart constantly, but the mud is always full of them.

On the stones are the raspers.

They are hand-sized, low, soft underneath, armored above with overlapping plates dark as wet shale. They creep over mineral film with patient muscular ripples, leaving clean crescent tracks behind them. Their mouths are circular rasps, rings of hard teeth that make the basin’s endless scraping sound. They have no faces in any comforting sense. Their sensory organs are arranged along the edges of their bodies: cilia, taste-fronds, tiny pits that read moisture and tremor. When rain begins, they tighten against the rock and let water pass over them. When the rain stops, they resume scraping as though no interruption occurred.

Some of what looks like stone is animal.

There are slab-colonies on the flats: hundreds of armored, immobile creatures locked together so tightly that their backs form a false pavement. Each individual is roughly shield-shaped, with a hinge-mouth underneath and rootlike gripping tendrils in cracks below. Together they imitate basalt plates. Small grazers cross them. Flying things land on them. Then, when enough weight gathers, the colony flexes. Narrow mouths open between the plates. The surface becomes a trap.

There are also shell-knots clustered under ledges, animals that spend most of life cemented in place. Their shells grow according to the direction of seepage, so they twist into hooks, fans, lopsided cups, and hanging teeth. During dry intervals they are sealed stones. When mist thickens or rainwater runs over them, they open pale combs into the flow and filter whatever passes: larvae, scum, sperm, eggs, blood, fragments of molt, powdered shell. They are patient beyond hunger. Some may live for centuries.

The basin’s moving hunters are flatter and more deliberate.

They have lateral bodies, jointed low to the ground, clad in dull plates. Their legs spread wide, giving them the look of walking fragments of broken armor. Most have mouths that open sideways rather than forward, splitting the head into shears. Their eyes, when present, are cloudy beads, vestigial and almost useless. They hunt by pressure. A grazer shifts wrong; a pool surface dimples; a worm trembles against the wrong pebble. Then the hunter crosses the distance without haste and closes.

They do not chase beautifully. Nothing here wastes energy on elegance. They wait at edges, at rivulets, at pool lips, at the borders between wet and merely damp. Their genius is placement. After rain, they line newly formed channels where fleeing burrowers must cross. During drying, they haunt the last water. Near vents, they lie among warm stones and let smaller lives come to them.

Above them are the winged swarms.

These are not birds. They are not insects exactly either, though they borrow the worst properties of both. Their bodies are small, dark, compact, furred not with hair but with sensory bristles and fungal-looking growths. Their wings are membranes stretched between elongated limbs, often ragged at the edges from collisions and mating fights. They fly badly as individuals and perfectly as a mass. They pulse, rebound, strike one another, fall, climb again.

They are drawn to heat and breath. Wherever a fissure exhales, a column of them forms, spiraling in the updraft, clicking against each other like thrown shells. They feed on airborne spores, vapor-fat, tiny drifting larvae, and the fluids of larger animals. Some have needle-mouths and land briefly to pierce soft joints. Others scrape condensation from stone with brushlike tongues. At rest, they hang in mats on cliff seams, layer over layer, until the rock looks furred, fevered, alive. The mats whisper and contract in waves.

Larger fliers come rarely.

They are heard first: a slow leather beating overhead, a displacement of the basin’s noise, a pressure passing through the dim air. Their bodies are long and folded, with membrane vanes supported by many struts. They do not soar under a sun; they grope through cloud-shadow by echo and heat. Their heads are narrow, split into listening cavities, their mouths lined with inward-curving hooks for seizing swarmers from the air or tearing mats from cliffs. When they descend, the smaller flying things scatter like ash.

Some of the larger fliers carry entire colonies on them: mites in the wing folds, thread-parasites dangling from their throats, shell-burrowers anchored between plates of skin. An old one may be so burdened that its outline is no longer its own. It becomes a flying reef of dependent hunger.

In the deeper mud are the bellows.

These are the great animals whose calls roll across the basin. They are seldom visible whole. A ridge moves where no ridge was. A field of bubbles advances through black water. A blunt back breaks the surface of a flooded pan and sinks again. They are massive, low, amphibious things, more like living banks than beasts, armored in flexible plates darkened by mineral accretion. Their mouths are wide trenches underneath the head, built for sucking whole mats of mud, worms, crustaceans, eggs, and smaller predators into grinding internal chambers.

They do not rule the basin by speed. They rule by volume.

Where a bellow-feeder passes, channels are rearranged. Pools are deepened. Burrows collapse. Its wake exposes buried colonies and crushes slow animals into paste. Scavengers follow the groove it leaves. Parasites cluster around its breathing slits. During breeding periods, the bellows drum their bodies against hollow stone, making the basin resonate. Their calls are not songs but territorial pressure, declarations felt through mud more than heard through air.

There are things that live only on the dead.

They arrive before death is finished. Scent-darts first: narrow, glossy scavengers with forked chemical organs held high, tasting the air in quick jerks. Then the clatterers: many-legged shell-backed animals that converge in waves, climbing over each other, clicking mandibles against bone or plate. Then soft white interior things, squeezing through wounds and natural openings. Then the threaders, hair-thin animals that enter meat and emerge from it blackened and swollen.

A carcass becomes a temporary city.

The outer plates are opened. Fluids run into mud. Eggs are laid in warm cavities. Larvae hatch before the last muscle has stopped twitching. The air above thickens with winged feeders. Larger predators come not only to eat the corpse, but to eat the eaters. For a little while the dead thing generates heat, smell, danger, and abundance. Then it is gone. Not cleaned. Translated.

Parasites are everywhere.

They hang from wing joints like pale seeds. They pack themselves into the hinges of armored legs. They lace through the gills of pool creatures. Some mimic sensory organs and attach near the head, feeding on nerve-rich tissue. Some alter behavior: an infected grazer abandons safe stone and crawls toward open mud; a winged animal descends at the wrong hour and is taken; a predator waits near a vent until its own parasites mature and drop into the warmth.

There are parasites of parasites, too. Nothing here is allowed to remain simple.

Many animals carry living burdens deliberately. Small cleaners nest beneath the plates of large hunters, eating mites and wound-rot. Shell-caps grow on the backs of slow grazers, providing camouflage in exchange for transport between feeding grounds. Certain winged creatures lay eggs only in the mucus trails of cliff-clingers; the larvae hatch into the trail itself and follow it back to the parent colony. Symbiosis here is not gentle cooperation. It is negotiated damage.

The young are often unlike the old.

Pools bloom with larval forms after mass spawnings: transparent ribbons, comma-shaped swimmers, drifting globes with ciliated rims, tiny armored triangles that snap at anything smaller. For a few hours or days, the water turns cloudy with potential bodies. Filter-feeders open by the thousands. Predators station themselves at drainage lips. Most young become food before they become anything definite.

Those that survive metamorphose according to where they settle. A larva attaching to vent-warmed stone may become a shell-filterer. One buried in silt may become a burrower. One taken into the body of another animal may become a parasite. Species boundaries are not always clean here. Life is shaped by chemistry, pressure, temperature, crowding, and chance. Form is a response, not a promise.

Their senses make a different world from the visible basin.

To them, the clouded sky is nearly irrelevant. The real landscape is vibration. A falling droplet is a bright event. A footstep is a moving wall. A struggling animal is a beacon. Rain is catastrophe and opportunity written through every surface at once.

They taste trails left hours or days ago. They feel currents moving under stone. They read the electrical twitch of muscle through muddy water. They know when a pool is becoming too saline, when a vent is souring, when a buried seam has opened, when a mass of bodies ahead means food, danger, mating, or all three.

Most have no concept of distance as sighted creatures understand it. They know nearness by contact, by chemical strength, by the delay between vibration and echo. The basin is not empty space to them. It is a continuous skin.

There are mimics.

A predator near the shallows makes sounds like rain ticking on stone, coaxing burrowers upward before storms. A soft-bodied ambusher spreads itself into the shape and texture of bacterial mat until grazers begin feeding on its surface; then the surface folds inward. Some winged hunters imitate the distress clicks of swarmers trapped in mud, drawing others down. Certain pool animals flash faint reflective pits, not for light, but to resemble the glint of exposed mineral films that grazers seek.

There are builders, but not architects.

They build with bodies, secretions, and accidents. Egg-cases cemented under shelves become ridged curtains. Generations of shell-filterers thicken into reefs along seep lines. Burrowers honeycomb the mud until a rain collapses it into new channels. Acid-mouthed grazers enlarge cracks as they feed, making future refuges. Cliff mats stain the stone, trap silt, and create ledges for other creatures. What looks almost like structure is merely accumulated use.

The closest thing to a social creature may be the seam-clingers.

They live in dense colonies along warm cracks, each individual a dark oval with gripping limbs and a flexible feeding tube. Alone, one is vulnerable and nearly mindless. Together, they behave like a single nervous surface. Touch one edge and the whole colony tightens. A predator approaches and hundreds of tubes withdraw at once. During spawning, they release gametes in pulses coordinated by vibration, turning the runoff cloudy. Some colonies are so old that their dead lower layers have mineralized, forming shelves on which their descendants live.

They are not intelligent in the human sense, but the colony remembers through shape: old attacks leave gaps, successful seep-lines are thickened, safer overhangs become crowded across generations.

There may be rarer things in the far lagoons.

Long shapes that surface only during the heaviest rains. Pale crowns rising from black water, not flowers but feeding organs. Slow animals with ringed bodies large enough to bridge channels. Hunters that live beneath the slab fields and lift stones from below. Blind giants that follow the underground water and emerge only where the basin lowers into flooded pans.

Their presence is inferred by absence: a suddenly emptied pool, a swath of crushed shell, a bellow answered by something deeper and farther away.

No creature here is beautiful by the standards of sunlight. Their colors are bruises, metals, old milk, wet ash, liver-dark reds hidden under plates. Their symmetry is practical and often broken. Their ornament is accidental: mineral deposits, parasite loads, scar tissue, encrusted eggs, colonies growing on colonies.

But they are perfectly fitted to the withheld world.

They belong to cold moisture, to intermittent water, to stone that sweats food, to darkness that makes eyes optional. They are compact, armored, soft where they must be soft, patient where they must conserve, frantic when rain opens the map. They live by edges and transitions. They understand no emptiness, because everything in their world is surface, signal, meal, threat, or passage.

The basin is inhabited by a multitude that has never needed green things, never waited for sunlight, never imagined silence.

It is a world of mouths and membranes, shells and feelers, mats and vents, larvae and scavengers, old bellows in the mud and winged swarms in the breath of the rocks. Everything listens. Everything touches. Everything eats or is being eaten slowly.

And beneath it all, in the seams where the rock gives off its metallic warmth, the first dark films continue spreading, patient as geology, feeding the whole crawling basin from below.
