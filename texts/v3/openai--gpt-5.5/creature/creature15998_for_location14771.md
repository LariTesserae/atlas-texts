The people who live there are not numerous, but they are many in the way a mechanism has many parts. Perhaps two hundred souls, perhaps fewer after a hard winter, more after a sequence of mild births. They do not seem scattered through the town so much as threaded through it: a hand at a cistern cover, a shoulder in a doorway, a child on a stair, a bent back under a roof edge, someone crossing above you along a parapet with a basket of wet wool.

They are highland people, compact and weather-kept. Their bodies tend toward economy: narrow wrists, strong fingers, backs made hard by carrying water, stone, grain, dung, children. Their faces are often lined early, not with extravagance of suffering but with squinting into pale light and wind. Their skin holds the color of indoor smoke and outdoor cold. Hair is usually kept bound, braided, wrapped, or cut short enough not to gather damp and ash. Beards, where worn, are trimmed close in winter and shaved in summer when lice or sweat become troublesome.

They move with care. Not timidity, not slowness exactly, but the precise-footedness of people raised among uneven paving, wet stone, low lintels, sleeping animals, open basins, and tools left where they must not be forgotten. Even children learn early to step over gutters rather than into them, to put a hand to a wall before turning a blind corner, to listen before entering a roofed passage. A child who runs wildly is not scolded for noise so much as for waste, breakage, and danger to water.

Their clothing is layered and practical. Wool first: undyed gray, brown, black, or off-white, sometimes patched with a different flock’s color. Tunics, leggings, wraps, sleeveless coats thickened by felting. Leather where rubbing would wear wool through: soles, belts, knee-straps, pouches, tool loops. In wet weather they wear hooded cloaks treated with grease or smoke, stiff at the shoulders, smelling faintly of animal and hearth. Children inherit garments in a visible genealogy of patches. A sleeve may have belonged to three cousins before being cut down for a younger child. Nothing fits perfectly for long, and nothing is discarded while any part can still serve.

They are not poor in the way of people who lack knowledge. They are poor in surplus. Their wealth is stored in exactness: a sound roof, a sealed jar, a dry stack of fuel, a cistern that does not lose water through a hairline crack, a goat that twins reliably, a daughter who can read weather from the smell under a wall, an old man who remembers which stones were set badly forty years ago and must be watched after rain.

They speak quietly because the town carries sound for them. Their language, whatever its larger family, has been pressed into local shapes. It has many words for dampness: the wet that beads on wool, the wet that rises from stone, the wet that spoils grain, the wet that promises seepage, the wet that means a roof will drip for three days, the wet that is only mist and does not count. It has specific verbs for water moving incorrectly. A gutter can wander, choke, bite, back-sleep, lose itself, remember its old course. A wall can drink. A roof can sweat. A jar can lie.

They do not waste strong emotion on ordinary hardship. Hunger is not theatrical. Cold is not an insult. Weather is not blamed for being itself. But they are not without feeling. Their joys are small and therefore sharply perceived: fresh bread from the black oven; a bright egg in winter; the first day when damp clothes dry fully before night; a visiting trader with salt, needles, red thread, or news; the discovery that a child has cut a clean tally mark instead of a clumsy one; a roof repaired before rain rather than after it.

Their households are close but not sealed. A family may sleep behind one low doorway, cook behind another, store grain in a shared raised room, keep tools in a cousin’s niche, dry wool on an aunt’s roof, and owe rights to a basin at certain hours. No one can pretend to be wholly private. Walls carry argument, coughing, lullabies, grinding stones, the scrape of a chair, whispered illness. Still, there are manners of invisibility. A closed hanging is respected. A bowl turned upside down outside a door says not now. A lamp left unlit at a usual hour will bring a neighbor, but not immediately; first there is listening.

The oldest people are repositories rather than dependents. Even when their hands tremble, they are asked to inspect things: the taste of stored water, the smell of grain, the color of a goat’s gums, the right thickness of cistern plaster, the old path under fresh lichen. They remember deaths, marriages, winters, repairs, disputes over catchment, the year the eastern stair froze for nine days, the year moths ruined the wool, the year three boys found seep-water under a slab and were praised for not disturbing it.

Children belong first to their mothers and fathers, then quickly to the town. They are sent through narrow places because they fit. They carry messages, jars no larger than their ribs, twists of fiber, bowls of ash, kindling, eggs, herbs, lamp-wicks, warm bread wrapped in cloth. They know the roofs before they know the ridge. Their games imitate the town’s necessary work: building miniature drains in dust, damming trickles with pebbles, trading shards as though they were jars of salt, holding councils in which some child always declares another has wasted water and must clean the goat pen.

They learn counting through stores and measures. They learn honesty through interdependence rather than doctrine. A thief of ornaments might be forgiven in time. A thief of water, seed, stored meal, or roof slate is more difficult to absorb back into trust. Punishments are practical. The guilty repair what they endangered. They clear drains in cold rain, haul stone, surrender a share of fuel, sit publicly while elders recite the chain of consequences: if the gutter blocks, the cistern muddies; if the cistern muddies, the children sicken; if the children sicken, hands are missing from harvest; if hands are missing, grain fails; if grain fails, the whole town eats memory.

Their leaders are not crowned or richly clothed. Authority gathers around competence, age, restraint, and the ability to be awake at inconvenient hours. There is likely a water-keeper, though the title may be humbler: the one who opens, the one who listens, the one with the basin key if keys exist. This person knows levels by sound. They can hear from a drawn bucket whether the cistern is full, low, troubled by sediment, or fed overnight by hidden seepage. They do not own the water. They answer to it.

There are stone-setters, roof-lifters, wool-workers, potters, bakers, hide-scrapers, bone-carvers, goat-tenders, herb-knowers, charcoal-burners who travel downslope, and carriers who make the longer paths to trade. Specialization exists but never entirely. A baker also patches walls. A weaver also tends hens. A goatherd knows birthing songs and the safe angle of a retaining stone. A child may learn the shaping of clay from one grandmother, the sharpening of bone from an uncle, and the names of damp moss from someone unrelated but patient.

The baker is among the most socially important people, not because bread is elaborate, but because shared heat is precious. The oven is fed with dung cakes, thorn bundles, brush, and fragments of wood too small for other use. People bring dough at appointed times, marked by cuts on boards, knots in cord, or simple memory. The baker reads loaves by smell and sound. Bread there is dense, dark, sometimes sour, sometimes stretched with ground roots, bitter greens, or crushed pulse. A festival loaf might contain dried fruit from the guarded trees, a pinch of salt, or herbs rubbed between palms until the whole lane smells briefly generous.

The tannery workers keep a necessary distance. They handle skins, urine, scraped fat, bark, ash, and sour water. Their hands are cracked and strong-smelling. They are teased but respected, avoided in dining but sought in winter. No one wants the tannery near the well; no one wants to lack sandals, hinges, straps, buckets, door-hangings, bellows, and water-skins.

The goatherds leave the town more than most. They know the slopes by wind-shadow and hoof-scratch. Their animals are lean, sharp-faced, clever, and disobedient in ways that are almost admired. Each goat is known by horn, udder, temper, and voice. Milk is too valuable to be casual. It becomes cheese, sour curd, medicine for children, barter, ceremonial food for births and reconciliations. A goat that eats roof herbs may be cursed by three households before evening and forgiven by none.

The donkeys are older than they look, or seem so. They are treated neither as pets nor mere tools. They know the eastern path, the lower salt road, the place where stones slide after rain. Their tack is repaired endlessly. Their ears appear at lane corners like small weather vanes. When one dies, its hide, bone, sinew, and memory are distributed carefully. Children may cry; adults pretend not to until work is finished.

The hens are nervous little citizens of corners, cages, and courtyards. They scratch where ash has been spread, cluck under stairs, vanish at hawk-shadow, and produce eggs that can change the mood of a morning. Their feathers become stuffing, dusters, ritual wisps tied above cradles, or toys for infants. Nothing feathered is merely decorative, except perhaps the wild birds that cross the saddle and make everyone briefly still.

The town’s animals share its caution. Cats, if present, are half-owned and named by their markings rather than affection: Split Ear, Smoke Foot, Thin Tail, Basin Thief. They keep to roofs, grain rooms, and warm stones. Dogs are few because they eat too much and bark too loudly, but one or two may be kept near the goat pen or by those who travel. They are rangy, cold-nosed, and solemn, sleeping with one eye open toward the slope.

There are also smaller lives, tolerated because they cannot be excluded: lizards in sun cracks, moths around lamps, beetles under dung cakes, spiders in roof corners, silver-gray insects under damp slabs, mice in storehouse walls. People do not romanticize them. They know which are harmless, which spoil grain, which foretell wet, which must be trapped, which may be ignored until after winter. A good cat is praised less for beauty than for keeping the storehouse quiet.

Plants live there as dependents. The guarded fruit trees are almost family members: named, remembered, argued over. Their fruit is counted while still hard. Blossoms are watched anxiously for frost. Children are warned away from them in tones usually reserved for fire and deep water. Their roots receive wash-water, blood-water from slaughter, steepings from herbs, and the last rinse from cooking pots. A tree that survives a bad year is thanked by pruning. A tree that dies is not quickly removed; its dry branches may stand one season like an elder whose speech has stopped.

The roof trays and wall pots hold onions, bitter leaves, medicinal herbs, and tough little plants whose virtues are known in exact doses. These are not gardens of abundance. They are negotiations. A handful of green can sharpen soup, treat a fever, settle a stomach, clean a wound, dye a thread, drive insects from bedding, or mark a burial. Children learn not to pull leaves without asking because even a weed may be assigned.

The dead live there too, though not as ghosts in the frightening sense. They live in niches, in wrapped objects, in names repeated over basins, in repairs attributed to vanished hands. An old threshold may be known as Grandmother Sef’s stone because she fell there carrying twins and laughed instead of wept. A drain may be called Marr’s Argument because two brothers quarreled over its course and one was proven right only after the next rain. The dead remain useful as measures. “Your knot is loose; old Tavi would have cut it off and made you tie it blind.” “Do not stack jars that way; that is how the north store cracked in your mother’s girlhood.”

Their ceremonies are restrained but not bare. Births are marked with warmth: a lamp kept through the night, a thread tied at the door, bread sent quietly, water drawn before dawn and touched to the child’s forehead, feet, and hands. Marriage, or whatever formal joining they practice, is less about romance than about rearranging obligations. The couple may walk the circuit of the gutters, receive tools, touch the cistern cover, and be given a new storage niche. Love exists, certainly, but the town recognizes it most fully when it becomes reliable labor.

Death is handled with composure until the body is gone, then grief may arrive in fragments. There may be no large cemetery within the bowl. Bodies might be taken beyond the rim to stone-lined graves, cairns, or a sheltered cut in the hillside where ancestors are placed among rock, not soil. What returns to the town are small things: a spindle, a knife handle, a bead, a measuring cord, a cup too chipped for ordinary use but too remembered to grind into drainage fill.

Their religion, if named as such, is close to maintenance. They honor water because without it the town fails. They honor stone because it bears them. They honor thresholds because much of life depends on crossing rightly: dry to wet, child to adult, stranger to guest, stored to spent, living to dead. Shrines in alley corners hold no glittering idols. Perhaps a shaped stone, a bowl blackened by oil, a knot of old wool, a line cut into plaster, a pebble carried from a lower river by someone long gone. Offerings are modest: first drops, crumbs, ash, a thread, a pinch of salt when salt can be spared.

They distrust extravagance. A person who speaks too loudly, promises too quickly, spends too freely, or laughs at waste will be watched. Not hated at first; simply measured. Outsiders are measured the same way. A traveler entering the town is noticed by everyone before anyone seems to look. Where are their feet from? Are their soles valley-soft or ridge-hard? Do they carry their own cup? Do they ask for water before greeting? Do they admire the town as quaint, or do they see the drains?

Hospitality exists because isolation requires it, but it is bounded. A stranger may receive a place near a hearth, a bowl of broth, water enough, directions, and news traded for news. But no one will show them the deepest cistern casually. No one will leave them alone in the storehouse. Children will be called indoors, not in panic but in habit. If the stranger proves useful, polite, and observant, the town may warm by degrees. If they repair a tool well, carry water without spilling, or notice a blocked gutter before rain, their name will be remembered.

Their market is small but intense. Traders come at intervals from lower valleys or other high settlements: salt, metal scraps, needles, oil, resin, dried fish, seed, colored thread, perhaps written messages from places where writing matters more. In exchange the town offers wool, felt, cured skins, hard cheese, carved bone tools, herbs from the slopes, repair work, and sometimes labor. Market day does not transform the town into festivity exactly, but the air changes. People stand straighter. Hidden jars appear. Children are told not to stare and stare anyway. New colors enter the lanes like birds.

They have music, but not much idle noise. Songs are paced for work: grinding songs, roof-lifting chants, lullabies that mimic dripping, counting rhymes for children clearing gutters, long narrow melodies for walking exposed paths where breath must be managed. Instruments are simple: bone flute, skin drum, clappers, a stringed thing repaired so often its original wood is only a rumor. At night, music is low enough not to waste lamp oil by keeping everyone awake.

Their humor is dry and exact. They laugh at imbalance, pretension, bad knots, goats, young lovers trying to be invisible, elders repeating themselves and being right, traders slipping on polished flags, children solemnly imitating councils. They do not laugh much at helplessness. There is too much of it nearby, always waiting beyond the wall.

Illness moves differently in such a place. Everyone knows a cough by its owner. Quarantine, if practiced, is spatially improvised but socially strict: a hanging across a lane, a bowl placed outside, one designated visitor, bedding smoked, cups boiled, waste carried to a particular pit. They have herbs, poultices, bone needles, splints, steam, rest, and knowledge accumulated from failure. They know when a fever is ordinary and when it has become a town matter. A healer there is not mystical in appearance. She or he is someone with clean hands, sharp memory, and the courage to smell what others avoid.

Their sense of time is architectural. Years are remembered by repairs, not kings. The year of the cracked lower basin. The year the west roof slipped. The year the old hall pillar was reset. The year the mist lasted until planting failed. The year the eastern path washed out and salt came late. Children become adults not on an abstract birthday but when they can carry a full jar without sloshing, spin an even thread, judge a roof stone, keep watch through a cold hour, or be trusted to open a water cover.

They are not trapped, though they are rooted. Some leave. Younger people go down-valley with traders, marry into other settlements, seek warmer fields, larger markets, soldiers’ pay, temples, ports, or merely novelty. Some return thin with stories. Some do not return at all. The town makes room for absence in the same way it makes room for seepage: by noticing, naming, adjusting. A roof may pass to a niece. A tool may hang unused until a younger hand grows into it. News of the absent is stored with other fragile goods.

Those who stay do not necessarily lack imagination. Their imagination is inward and structural. They can picture hidden water under stone. They can imagine the consequence of moving one block. They can see in a child’s clumsy knot the future failure of a loaded basket. They dream, perhaps, of broad rivers and forests, but they wake to gutters, and the gutters are real.

What lives there, most of all, is attention.

Attention lives in the set of their shoulders when clouds lower over the ridge. It lives in the pause before someone pours wash-water into a tree pit. It lives in the child sent back to pick up three spilled grains. It lives in the old woman touching a wall with the back of her hand to feel whether damp is rising behind the plaster. It lives in the way a whole lane can become silent at the first wrong sound from a roof slab shifting.

They are neither grand nor primitive. They are not waiting to become something larger. They are a complete people in a complete small place, shaped by stone, scarcity, damp, wind, and mutual necessity. Their lives have little room for spectacle, but much room for precision, memory, obligation, and the stubborn tenderness of maintenance.

They live as the town lives: low, close, weathered, interlocked, enduring. They do not conquer the saddle. They inhabit its permission.
