What lives there has learned to be difficult to see.

Not invisible exactly. Not shy in the ordinary sense. More as moss is shy, as seep-water is shy: present everywhere, withdrawing from any one name. The basin’s inhabitants do not offer themselves as figures against a background. They are folds in the background. They are warmth moving through warmth, a pause in a chorus, a bead of water that climbs when it should fall, a root that changes direction for no visible reason.

The smallest things rule first.

In the moss live whole pale nations: springtails with glassy bodies, mites red as clotted mineral, hair-thin worms that taste stone and fungus through their skins. There are colonies of translucent crabs no larger than fingernails, their shells clouded like old ice, their claws delicate enough to comb spores from moss filaments. They climb roots after rain and gather in the seams of ancient structures, not worshipfully, not intelligently perhaps, but with the regularity of a rite. Their eggs are carried under the bellies of the females in soft blue clusters, and the larvae hatch into water films so shallow they seem impossible as habitats.

The insects are the basin’s weather.

Some are nearly soundless: flat black beetles that live beneath wet bark and move only when the pressure changes; moths with fur like damp ash; long-legged flies that stand on pool surfaces and drum messages downward with their feet. Others make the darkness dense. Cicada-like things hidden in root hollows vibrate until the stone answers. Crickets with translucent throats pulse in alternating bands, one patch answering another across the basin in patterns too complex to be mating alone. There are blue-bellied beetles whose light is not meant to illuminate but to signal briefly under leaves, a shuttered glow passed from individual to individual like a secret.

Some beetles cultivate fungus. They carry spores in grooves along their backs and plant them under leaf-curled shelters, trimming the growth with their mandibles so that it remains hooded and dim. The fungi glow only when bruised, or when warmed by the beetles’ bodies. In that brief glow the beetles appear silver-backed, solemn, almost ceremonial, gathered around their low gardens. They seem to know the old black panels better than any other animal does. When one steps on the dry warm surface of a panel, the panel ripples, and the beetle pauses as if listening through its feet.

The amphibians are more numerous than they sound, and they sound innumerable.

Most are small, no longer than a finger, with skin the color of wet stone, dead leaves, cream fungus, root-shadow. They live in the impossible places: in water held between layers of moss, in the throats of hollow trunks, in the rain-filled cups of plants, in the thin slick depressions of old rock. Their eyes vary. Some have large black eyes like polished seeds, useful for catching the briefest insect glimmer. Others have eyes reduced to cloudy beads beneath skin. A few are entirely blind, long-toed and milk-white, hunting by vibration and the electrical tremor of prey.

Their voices make the basin larger than it is. One knocks like wood tapped underwater. One whistles downward, as if falling. One produces a soft bubbling note that seems always to come from behind the listener. Another has learned, over generations, to pitch its call to the rain-hum of the leaning posts. When storms begin and the posts answer, these frogs stop calling until the suspended beads of water form their trembling lattices; then, one by one, they join the vibration, not imitating it but fitting themselves into its gaps. Their throats pulse with old machinery.

The plants are not passive here.

The mosses breathe visibly in the wettest hours. Their mats rise and slacken by fractions, taking in water, exchanging salts with the stone, feeding hidden fungi that pass messages through the basin more slowly than thought and faster than root growth. Some moss refuses the bare slick stones absolutely, leaving them clean as if grazed by an unseen mouth. Other moss grows in graded bands around artifacts, darker where invisible fields thicken, taller where drops hang too long in the air.

The ferns are warm. Touching them is like touching the flank of a sleeping animal. Their black-green fronds unfold around seep-holes in exact circles, and at night—if night can be said of a place without day—their undersides release yellow dust that clings to insects, salamander feet, the damp fur of passing mammals. Vines hang from unseen branches and listen through tension. They do not snare prey, not deliberately, but they recoil from voices, from certain repeated sounds, from metal struck on stone. Footsteps do not bother them. Breath does.

Fungi occupy nearly everything that has stopped moving for long enough.

There are floury white blooms on fallen leaves, velvet black shelves along bark furrows, green films inside hollow trunks, orange nets under root mats, and one slow blue species that grows only along the seams of the waist-high arch. That fungus never crosses onto the central membrane of the arch. It forms a border, a living acknowledgement of a boundary older than itself. In rain it swells and releases a smell like bruised pepper and old paper, attracting moths that dust it with the spores of another fungus entirely. The basin’s decay is not a downward path. It is a set of exchanges, each death immediately becoming architecture.

The birds are low-voiced, practical, and strange.

They do not sing into open air. They knock, click, hiss, and pipe from within the flattened crowns of the squat trees. Many have wide mouths and soft whisker-like feathers around the beak for catching gnats in darkness. Some sleep hanging upside down beneath branches, wings wrapped tight, heads tucked into damp breast feathers, waking whenever a pool exhales insects. Others creep along limbs like bark come loose, tapping for grubs in the gardens that grow from the tree furrows.

Their feathers are mostly matte: charcoal, wet brown, lichen gray, a green so dark it is almost a temperature rather than a color. A few carry hidden markings visible only in beetle-blue flashes—bars and throat patches that flare briefly when they court. Their nests are woven from root hair, moss fiber, insect silk, and strips peeled from the inner bark of hollow trunks. They line them with moth fur. Their eggs are warm to the touch and faintly speckled with mineral stains from the rain.

There are mammals, or creatures near enough to mammals.

Small ones first: sharp-faced tree-shrews with slick fur and long gripping toes, living in the high rootwork and low branches. They quarrel constantly in dry clicks, mark their routes with bitter scent, and feed on beetles, fern-spores, frog eggs, and the pale fruits that grow close to bark. They know the safe paths over polished stone. Their young are born nearly hairless in hollows warmed by old panels or by fungal compost. They are fierce, clever in the quick animal way, and almost impossible to catch looking directly at you. You see them mostly as heat crossing heat.

There are heavier things too.

One of them moves through the basin rarely enough that every smaller voice recognizes it. It is not enormous, but in that place it has the gravity of a large event. Its body is low, broad-shouldered, furred in dark waterproof bristles except along the belly, where the skin is gray and folded. Its feet are wide and soft, with spreading toes that grip slick rock and lift from mud with that slow suction heard before silence. Its head is blunt. Its ears are small. It smells of wet leaves, musk, and iron-rich clay.

It feeds without hurry. It turns over moss mats with the side of its snout, laps larvae from shallow pools, breaks open rotten wood for grubs, and sometimes stands motionless beside the central pool while gnats settle on its back. It knows not to step between the humming posts during rain. It knows the arch as an obstacle, or a presence, or both. Calves of its kind, if they can be called calves, are born in root-shadow near the eastern seep and stay hidden for weeks, their bodies striped with pale damp bands that fade as they grow. When threatened they do not run far. They fold themselves into stillness and become another wet stone.

Higher in the trees lives something more deliberate.

It is smaller than the heavy basin-walker, longer-bodied, with membraned skin between its limbs and a prehensile tail bare at the underside. It glides from flattened crown to crown when the mist thickens, landing with a soft slap of pads on bark. Its face is narrow. Its eyes are poor. Along its jaw and forearms are heat-sensitive pits, and its hands are sensitive enough to read bark, moss, rainfall, the minute vibration of insects inside wood.

These gliders are the closest thing the basin has to watchers. They travel in pairs or small family clusters, seldom calling except with low reed-like pulses. They cache food in hollow trunks, arrange leaves over sleeping places, and sometimes bring beetle-lit fungus to their young. They avoid the geometric moss-fields but rest near the warm black panels. During heavy rain they gather at a distance from the three leaning posts and hang beneath branches, heads downward, listening while water climbs the invisible lines.

They have no tools, unless remembering a path is a tool. Unless placing a bitter leaf over a fungus cache to slow its growth is a tool. Unless teaching their young which stones answer underfoot is a kind of culture. They are not masters of the basin. Nothing here is. But they inherit knowledge in bodies and habits. Their routes are older than any individual. Their caution has shape.

In the pools live things that almost never enter air.

Larvae with hinged jaws. Blind shrimp like scraps of fingernail. Threadlike eels that knot around submerged roots. Soft-bodied grazers that clean mineral films from stone. Flat dark shapes that wait under leaf litter and rise only when something touches the surface above them. The central pool contains larger, older animals: pale, slow fish with reduced eyes and barbels that sweep the bottom; shelled things that seal themselves under mud when the water lowers; a black leech as long as a hand that feeds perhaps once a year and otherwise lies coiled in a crack beneath the rim.

The pool itself may be alive in a way not reducible to any creature within it. Its skin responds too coherently. Its rings sometimes travel against disturbance. Heat reflects from it wrong. The animals treat it as water, but not merely water. They breed at its edges, drink from its shallows, and avoid crossing its center. When something falls in from high leaves, the pool waits before accepting it.

The trees are inhabited by multitudes, but the trees also seem inhabited by memory.

Their hollows breathe. Not metaphorically: air moves in and out of them at different rates from the surrounding windless dark, pushed by heat, fungal metabolism, animal nesting, perhaps by deeper cavities in the stone beneath their roots. Each hollow trunk is a vertical village. Bats or bat-like things roost in the upper chambers, wrapped tight. Crabs climb the wet inner walls. Beetles tunnel through softened wood. Ferns root in pockets of old guano. Pale fungi lace the inner bark. Tree-shrews sleep in the dry side chambers. Sometimes, from the lowest dark of an old trunk, there comes a click too regular for an insect and too soft for stone.

The ancient structures are lived with, not lived in.

No animal builds upon their active surfaces. No root pierces them. Yet everything uses them. Moths rest under the arch. Crabs shelter at its base. Birds orient by it in blackness. The broad-footed basin-walker scratches itself against the side of the circular depression, but never crosses its exact center. Salamanders hunt along the straight grooves in the rock because small insects fall into them and follow them like paths. Fungi fringe the artifacts without consuming them. Life has mapped the prohibitions.

And perhaps the structures live too.

Not as animals live. Not as plants live. But they respond, conserve, adjust. The posts hum when rain begins. The black panel wakes under a beetle’s foot. Drops hesitate in certain fields. Moss grows according to gradients that imply an appetite or instruction. There is maintenance without visible maintainer. There is reaction without display. The old technologies may be dead systems discharging ancient routines, or they may be slow organisms whose bodies are stone, membrane, field, and rain.

If there is an intelligence there, it is not enthroned.

It is distributed among negotiated things. In the frog that has tuned itself to the post. In the beetle whose foot wakes a panel. In the moss that refuses a clean stone. In the glider that teaches its young not to cross a quiet patch. In the roots that wrap without breaking. In the machine that permits moss along its seam but not over its face. The basin thinks, if it thinks, by accommodation.

Nothing living there expects light. Eyes are secondary, often ornamental, often reduced. The true senses are touch, heat, vibration, chemical trace, pressure change, the taste of wet mineral, the timing of rain. Bodies are low, soft-footed, water-shedding, patient. Colors are hidden or brief. Voices do the work that brightness does elsewhere. Territory is held by sound, scent, warmth, and absence.

The inhabitants do not flourish despite the dark. They are made of it.

They carry darkness in their skins and feathers, in their lowered calls, in their cautious paths around old forces. They do not wait for dawn. Dawn is not part of their grammar. Their lives are measured by rain starting and rain easing, by pools rising and shrinking, by fungal bloom, by insect emergence, by the hum of the posts, by the long leaning of roots over stone.

What lives there is a wet, patient society without a center: moss, fungus, insect, amphibian, bird, mammal, root, pool, and machine, all keeping their distances and making use of one another. It is not peaceful. Things hunt and are eaten. Eggs fail. Larvae vanish. A careless shrew is taken by a pool-edge mouth. A frog is opened by beetles before its call has faded. But even predation feels incorporated, part of the basin’s slow agreement.

No creature there owns the place.

The place owns their habits.
