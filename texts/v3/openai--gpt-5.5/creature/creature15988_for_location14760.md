The first inhabitants you notice are not the people, but the repairs.

A wound in a causeway has been shaved clean and packed with wet moss. New green ribs have been pinned across it with bone splints. A sagging bridge has been given a cradle of young vines, each one tied with a different colored fiber to show its age, its strength, its temperament. A cistern lip has been cut back where rot began, and the raw living edge has been painted with bitter sap to keep insects from entering before the wax colonies arrive.

Only after seeing these careful acts everywhere do you begin to see the ones who made them.

They move quietly, because the world is listening and because the floor listens best. They are small to medium-bodied, long-limbed, narrow-shouldered, with feet made for wet bark: broad soles, gripping toes, a second thumb on the hand or something like one, callused palms, knees comfortable with crouching. Their skin tends toward bark-brown, fern-dark, sap-gold, mist-pale; not as camouflage exactly, but because generations under green light have made bright colors seem vulgar except in ceremony. Their hair is worn tied close or braided into ropes, often threaded with seed tags, shell discs, insect carapace, knots of mnemonic fiber. Loose hair is for sleep, mourning, fever, or love.

They rarely stand upright for long in open places. On broad decks they walk low and balanced, letting the spring of the living floor travel through their joints. In dense growth they become almost four-limbed, passing from root-stair to vine-rail to trunk-wall without seeming to choose a path. Children learn to climb before they learn to speak clearly. Infants are carried in leaf-slung nets against the back or chest, their fingers already closing on hanging cords. The elderly, when their balance begins to fail, do not withdraw from the high ways; they become bridge-watchers, cistern-keepers, name-rememberers, seated in warm alcoves where every tremor in the wood tells them who is coming.

They are not a single nation. The living continent is too large, too folded, too inconsistent for that. There are rainline clans who follow water downward through the levels, maintaining cistern forests and measuring the age of each rainfall by taste. There are bridge families who inherit spans generations long and marry according to which anchor-groves can safely share strain. There are hollow-village people who live inside braided trunks, pale from the deeper shade, excellent at fungus, silence, and delicate grafts. There are edge-dwellers, wind-bent and lean, who tend the outer plains where the mist rises warm from below and where things sometimes climb up from the cloud that no one admits to having seen clearly.

Most of them call themselves by words that mean something like “those kept by the growing” or “hands of the house.” They do not have a clean category for wilderness. A thicket may be wild for twenty years, then remembered as an old kitchen, then cut open and found to contain the ceramic-lined hearth of a grandmother’s grandmother. A fruiting column may be both ancestor and storehouse. A bridge may be juvenile, sulking, dangerous, beloved, legally responsible for three villages, and due to be sung awake before the next long rain.

Their work is maintenance, but maintenance here is creation, history, politics, medicine, and prayer all at once.

They prune with knives of dark stone kept sharper than teeth. They stitch bark with needles of fishbone and polished thorn. They set splints, redirect sap, open breathing channels, pollinate chosen flowers by hand, scrape blight from shaded seams, coax roots into ladders, and argue for days about whether an old hall should be preserved, composted, renamed, or allowed to shrink into animal passage. Their tools are simple only to an outsider. A grafting clamp may have belonged to thirty hands and be carved with notches recording every successful joining. A waterweight can raise a bridge by the slow fall of one cistern over half a day. A pruning song contains measurements, warnings, family grudges, and the remembered behavior of a particular vine during a drought three generations ago.

They are patient because impatience kills. Cut too deeply and a floor may bleed itself hollow. Train a shoot too fast and it will split in the next warm wind. Call a door by its childhood name after it has been renamed for marriage, and it may open into the wrong wet dark.

Names matter to them because names matter to the place.

Every important structure has several: a seed-name, a work-name, a public name, a rain-name, and sometimes a private name known only to its keepers. Children memorize them as other children elsewhere might learn maps. To forget a name is not merely disrespectful; it is structurally dangerous. An unnamed bridge loosens. An unnamed room thickens inward, embarrassed or hungry. An unnamed dead person may become a draft through a dwelling, a repeated drip, a patch of fungus shaped like a hand.

Their speech is soft and click-rich, suited to close air and wet surfaces. They use whistles for vertical distance, palm slaps on rootwood for warning, and cord-knots for messages that must travel through fog without being misheard. Written marks exist, but they distrust anything that does not decay at the correct speed. Records are carved shallowly into bark panels meant to close over in time, or woven into fiber strips whose knots must be retied by each generation. A history that no one tends should not remain too legible.

They live among animals not as masters, not exactly as equals, but as parties to a thousand practical arrangements.

The wax insects are household allies. Their hives are kept in warm wall pockets and fed with selected flowers. In return they seal seams, glaze storage gourds, and close the tiny wounds left by trimming. Each house has its own scent of wax, and a traveler who sleeps in too many houses carries a confused odor that can make insects suspicious.

The black cistern crabs are tolerated with formal politeness. They clean rot, eat drowned larvae, and keep water sweet, but they steal shiny objects and sometimes cut through young rootlets with their clever claws. Children offer them scraps and curses. Adults never kill one inside a water system. A dead crab in a cistern is an omen of neglected obligations.

The blind monkeys are treated as coworkers no one hired and no one dares dismiss. They come at night to harvest fungus from rafters, sort poisonous blooms into neat piles, and rearrange drying racks according to criteria the people only partly understand. Some villages leave small ladders for them. Others pretend not to. Everyone benefits.

The pale deer are semi-sacred, not because they are gentle, but because they know which old paths still hold. Their split hooves test surfaces better than any tool. If a deer refuses a bridge, no one crosses until the bridge has been questioned, sounded, and fed. Deer antler, naturally shed, is used in instruments for measuring hidden hollows.

The wingless rail-birds are messengers when they choose to be. They run paired along vine lines, flashing tails for balance, stealing fruit and repeating overheard syllables in voices just accurate enough to cause trouble. There are stories of marriages ruined by rail-birds, and others saved by them.

The vine-cats are never domesticated. They are addressed respectfully from a distance. They look like mossy absences with eyes. They hunt slowly, often from walls, and sometimes take chickens, monkeys, children who wander during rain, or the small trembling things that come out of frayed zones. A vine-cat sleeping above a doorway may be a threat, a blessing, or merely a cat.

The people mark treaties on thresholds with beetle shells, feathers, crab claws, coils of shed snake skin, little white seeds that mean “do not enter,” black seeds that mean “enter only if invited,” and pale green seeds that mean “this house has a newborn; all hungers pass over.”

They eat what the place can be persuaded to give without weakening.

Their gardens are vertical and seasonal in ways outsiders struggle to understand. A breakfast may come from seven heights: pepper leaves from an upper net, fungal bread from a shaded beam, sour fruit from a wall-gourd, translucent eggs from water-cups, starch scraped from a swollen root, beetle paste fermented in wax, sweet rain collected before it touched old wood. They cultivate edible mosses in sleeping alcoves for warmth and emergency food. They tap sap by moonless schedule, though there is no moon visible, only a remembered rhythm. They dry meat in warm fungal chimneys. They brew green-black infusions that taste of bark, smoke, and flowers opening in the dark.

Fire is feared, cherished, and never casual. A hearth is a ceramic-lined organ of the village, not a convenience. Flame is kept small, fed with deadwood only after witnesses agree it is truly dead. Children are shown scars from ancient burns: whole levels sealed into charcoal knots, rootlines still growing around black absences. To misuse fire is one of the few crimes that can exile a person beyond the maintained paths.

Their bodies show the costs of living in a world that bends.

Many have old sprains bound in vine-fiber. Scar patterns from bark-scrape, thorn puncture, fungal burn, and insect treaty-mark cross their arms. Their hearing is fine enough to distinguish rain above from rain below. Their sense of balance is uncanny on swaying decks but poor on truly rigid ground, which most will never encounter. They dislike open sky. They sleep best with something living against their back and water sounding in at least three directions.

Their children play at repair. They build miniature bridges between furniture roots and name them grandly, then weep when a beetle collapses them. They race along low vine rails, gamble with seed shells, dare one another to put fingers near sensitive door-leaves, and learn early which flowers must not be smelled after dusk. They are told stories about falling, but not to frighten them. Everyone falls in dreams. Everyone must know what to do if a level gives way: catch across, go limp, grab only living green, never dead gray, do not scream unless you want gliders.

The dead are returned to usefulness.

Some are placed in root-cradles beneath family dwellings, wrapped in absorbent cloth and seeded with memorial fungi whose fruiting bodies are eaten once a year in solemn meals. Some are given to bridge anchors, especially those who maintained a span all their lives. Some are lowered into cistern forests where the water takes them slowly and their names become part of rain songs. A few, the troublesome or beloved beyond ordinary burial, are placed in hollow columns and allowed to become listening posts. People bring them questions. Sometimes the wood answers with creaks. Sometimes it answers by opening a door where there was none.

They do not worship the forest as a god. That would make it too separate.

They address it instead as house, elder, patient, beast, burden, weather, inheritance, mistake. They apologize to beams before cutting. They scold vines. They flatter cisterns. They threaten stubborn shutters with drought-songs. They thank edible plants by leaving peelings where the roots can reclaim them. Their ceremonies are mostly practical: naming a bridge, waking a graft, closing a sick room, teaching a new watercourse its descent, asking a frayed place to stay where it is.

The frayed places shape them most deeply.

Every village has white-leaf markers stored carefully away from food and sleeping mats. Every child is taught the signs: shadows falling wrong, flowers with no scent, droplets rising from a surface, a corridor that seems longer when remembered than when seen, the sudden taste of metal in warm air. These places are not considered evil. They are unstable weather, old errors, perhaps seams where the living architecture grew around something it should not have grown around. People skirt them, leave offerings of bland fruit, avoid speaking new names nearby, and never prune aggressively at their edges.

There are specialists who deal with them: quiet people with mismatched earrings, fingers stained white from marker leaves, and eyes trained not to follow impossible loops too long. They retrieve lost tools, close doors that open into previous rainy seasons, guide back those who have returned with an ancestor’s appetite or an animal’s sleep. They are paid in salt, dry cloth, and remembered favors. They marry rarely. Their children, when they have them, are watched with tenderness and unease.

Not all inhabitants are human or even animal in the usual sense.

The great plant-structure itself lives there, though not as one mind. It remembers pressure, names, traffic, injury, songs. It favors some routes and resents others. It thickens around cherished rooms, opens light wells over places where people gather, sends exploratory roots toward laughter and damp heat. It can be tricked, but not often. It can be wounded, but it makes wound into form. It is architecture with appetite, landscape with scar tissue, ancestor without face.

There are also presences that may be memories grown heavy enough to move.

An abandoned plaza sometimes fills with the smell of a market no one alive attended. Footsteps cross empty bridges at dawn, careful and numerous. In certain halls, if rain has been falling for two days and no one speaks, one can hear old pruning songs from within the walls, sung by voices thick with pith. These are not ghosts in the simple sense. They are use-patterns, griefs, repeated motions the living wood has learned too well to stop performing.

And beneath all these live the small multitudes: transparent frogs folded into water-cups; moths mapped like lichen; root-snakes pressed motionless under decking, tasting footsteps; gliders hanging upside down in shafts; mites grazing fungal bloom; white worms in compost pockets; silver fish in cistern veins; colonies of breathing flowers that open and close the airways of whole neighborhoods.

The people share the world with them because there is no elsewhere cleanly separate from sharing.

A house is also a feeding ground. A path is also a root. A nursery is also a moisture trap. A corpse is also future floor. A song is also engineering.

What lives there, finally, is a civilization that has never imagined permanence as stillness. Its people do not build monuments against time. They build obligations with time. They inhabit a world that must be touched correctly, named regularly, cut sparingly, fed back what it gives, and forgiven for changing shape.

They are gardeners of their own ground, tenants of their own handiwork, descendants of forgotten makers and makers of descendants who may someday forget them. They live softly because everything holds. They live carefully because everything yields.
