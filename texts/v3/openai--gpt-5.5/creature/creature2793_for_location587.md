The inhabitants are few.

That is the first thing to understand about them. A city like this does not hold a crowd, however much its crowded masonry suggests one. It holds a people the way a cup holds water: exactly, with no surplus. They live in numbers small enough that every absence is audible, every sleeping body accounted for by the interruption it makes in the damp air. They are not a populous race. They are a maintained remnant, a household expanded into civic form, a society reduced to the scale at which nothing may be wasted—not breath, not heat, not bone, not a drop.

At a distance, in the poor mineral light, they might be mistaken for children or old people: narrow-shouldered, low-moving, wrapped close against the wet. But when one comes nearer, their proportions cease to belong entirely to the surface world. Their backs are slightly bowed, not from weakness but from ancestry lived under ceilings that never allowed anyone to stand proudly for long. Their arms are long and used often before their eyes are; their hands go ahead of them, reading walls, counting ridges, testing air. The fingers are broad at the tips, flattened by generations of pressing clay, lifting sluice pegs, feeling hairline cracks in stone. Their nails are thick, translucent, worn blunt. Their palms are ridged with pale callus that never fully dries.

Their skin has the color of things kept from the sun: not simply white, but gray, cream, diluted rose, the underside of shell, the belly of blind fish. In some, the skin shows faint blue-green veining, especially at the temples and wrists, as though the mineral light has entered them and never left. They oil themselves with rendered fat and seep-grease to keep the wet from eating them raw. Without it their knuckles split, their heels crack, their lips go bloodless and sore. The old shine as the walls shine. Water beads on them and runs along the channels of their bodies.

They have hair, but little of it. Children are born with soft dark crowns that thin early. Adults keep what remains bound under caps of scraped hide, shell beads, or clay-rubbed cord. Beards are rare and impractical; they hold damp and mildew. Eyebrows are often heavy, lashes pale. Their eyes are large, but not beautiful in the surface sense. They do not flash. They gather. The pupils stay wide. The whites are clouded by mineral irritation, smoke, age. They see edges before colors, motion before detail, shine before form. A person standing still in a gray alcove may become part of the wall to them, but a single droplet disturbed on a lintel will draw every gaze.

Yet sight is not their chief sense.

They listen through air, through water, through bone. They know the city by its pressure. A child learns the plunge-well’s bass before it learns its own name. They can hear when a gutter is beginning to calcify by the narrowing of its note. They hear a cracked jar in a shelf of twenty sealed jars. They hear a stranger because a stranger steps against the rhythm, hesitates where no one born there would hesitate, breathes too loudly in the throat passages, brushes the wrong wall. Their hearing is not the delicate hearing of forest animals; it is civic hearing, architectural hearing. They listen as masons listen, as midwives listen, as people responsible for continuance listen.

They do speak, but seldom in long open phrases. The water would devour such wastefulness. Their language is close-range and layered: mouth-sounds, throat-clicks, hand taps, knuckle codes on clay, signs drawn briefly against the palm. Vowels are used for intimacy, warnings, names. Consonants carry work. A conversation between two workers at a sluice might seem to consist of three taps, a hiss, a lifted finger, and a shared glance toward the channel; in that exchange may be contained weather, history, judgment, and repair.

For distance they use stone.

Each household owns striking pebbles chosen for tone. Public posts around the central well are scarred by generations of coded blows. A hard double strike from the settling basins means clouded inflow. A rolling scrape from the dry vaults means lamp fuel opened. A high, repeated tick from the burial wall means a niche seal has sweated loose. Children play with these signals before they are allowed to send them, making forbidden nonsense-messages until an elder takes the pebble away and presses the child’s fingers to the post so they can feel the seriousness of resonance.

They move slowly, but not timidly. Their slowness is competence. They turn sideways without thinking. They duck before the lintel appears. They step over gutters in darkness because their soles remember the span. On slick stone they do not flail; they lower their weight, widen their toes, let a free hand find the wall. They carry burdens against the chest or hung from forehead straps, never swinging loose. No one runs except toward failure: a failed gate, a child in water, the first impossible stutter of a stopped drain.

Their clothing is practical and close. Hide plates, softened by oil and smoke. Scraped membranes stitched with gut. Belts of plaited cord made not from fiber but from sliced skin, sinew, and thin hammered mineral sheets. Aprons of overlapping shell or fired-clay scales protect the thighs of potters and basin workers. The poor wear patched wraps stiff with lime. The old wear many layers, not for modesty but for warmth; age chills quickly underground. Nothing trails. Nothing flutters. A loose garment is a danger to grates and peg-doors.

Adornment exists, but it is tactile more than visual. Raised beads sewn along cuffs to mark lineage. Shell discs at the throat, each drilled with a different pattern of holes. Fired-clay tokens worn at the wrist, impressed with thumbmarks of the dead. Some shave small grooves into their clay caps to record office, apprenticeship, marriage, debt. Lovers exchange smooth stones kept warm in the mouth before being given. Children are given ankle-rattles of tiny shells until they learn to move safely; after that, silence is a sign of maturity.

Their feet are perhaps their strangest feature. Broad, gripping, splayed slightly from a lifetime on damp uneven floors. The soles are pale but thick, mapped with scars from shell inlays, hot clay, unnoticed grit. They can read stair edges with their toes. They can tell a safe patch of wet stone from a treacherous one by the faint difference in chill. A child who cannot yet speak may still know, barefoot, which gutter leads toward the plunge-well.

They are not blind, not deaf, not monstrous. They are human enough that grief, hunger, irritation, tenderness, pride, and superstition sit plainly on them. But they have been edited by the chamber. Whatever they once were, they are now a people of condensation and low vaults. The surface, if they know of it, has become mythological: a place of unbearable openness, falling light, dry winds, useless distances, burning color, and vertical rain that has not yet learned its channels.

Their bodies run cool. Their pulses are slow. They sleep in short segments, not through a full night, because there is no night to govern them and because water must be heard. Households keep watches according to the filling of bowls. Infants sleep in wall cradles above floor damp, wrapped in hide and warmed by ceramic bottles filled at the heated lamp-stones. Elders sleep nearest the listening walls, where they can wake if a familiar sound changes. No one sleeps completely away from the city’s noise. To sleep in silence would be like sleeping without air.

They eat sparingly.

Food is the city’s harsh arithmetic. There are blind threadfish from the deeper cisterns, caught only in permitted numbers and usually for children, the sick, or ceremonies. There are shell-backed seam-creatures gathered with hooked pins from between stones, mashed into paste, dried near lamp clusters, and mixed with mineral salts. There are pale amphibians sometimes trapped under ledges, though taking one is considered unlucky unless hunger has passed into danger. There are translucent skins scraped from untouched corners: not plant, not fungus, something between mineral film and living residue. These are softened in warm water, boiled with fat, and eaten as gluey sheets that taste of lime and old breath. They call it wall-milk, or something close to that.

They also keep sealed stores in the higher dry vaults: old fat, smoked flesh from animals that must come from somewhere beyond the described chamber, hard cakes of ground shell-creature, clay jars of sour broth, mineral salts, powdered bone. Perhaps there are trade passages too narrow or hidden to be called streets, leading to other pockets, other underplaces, other communities pressed into stone. Perhaps, once every long interval, strangers arrive with bundles wrapped in hide: dried meat, copper scraps, teeth, lamp oil, stories of floods. But such arrivals would be rare, ceremonial, disruptive. This people cannot depend on abundance from elsewhere. They depend on recurrence.

Their cookfires are not fires in the open sense. Flame is precious, smoke dangerous. They warm food in covered clay boxes over low lamp clusters, steam it in sealed vessels, soften it in cistern-warmth, ferment it in jars buried in damp niches. The smell of cooking is faint: sour, fatty, mineral, almost medicinal. Feasts are not lavish. A feast is thickness in the broth. A feast is everyone receiving something that must be chewed. A feast is a child being allowed the liver of a threadfish, or an elder licking fat from a shell spoon.

They measure wealth by reserves of dryness.

A dry shelf is wealth. A sealed jar that has not sweated through is wealth. A hook of copper not yet green is wealth. A reliable lamp is wealth. A family with a sleeping alcove above the damp-line is fortunate. A household with a private drip-cup of clean water is almost noble. But their nobility, if they have it, cannot become grand. The city forbids extravagance. A powerful person still ducks under the same lintels, listens to the same drains, and soils their hands when a channel clogs. Authority belongs to those who know how the water behaves.

Their leaders are therefore maintainers.

Not kings in any bright sense. Not priests alone. The most respected among them are the people who can hear failure before it happens: sluice-mothers, basin-fathers, lip-cutters, sounders, sealers, clay physicians, keepers of the still bowl. They carry no crowns, but they carry keys: stone pegs, measuring cords, shell scrapers, counterweight pins. A child may be born to a respected line, but respect must be proven by touch. No lineage can negotiate with water on its own.

Apprenticeship begins early. Children first learn not to fall, not to shout near certain ledges, not to waste lamp-flame staring at nothing. Then they learn the names of the sounds. Each basin is sung to them by an elder touching their wrist to the stone: this is full, this is hungry, this is fouled, this is clear, this is winter-pressure, this is false winter-pressure, this is a stone lodged in the throat. Their education is not written much, because damp hates records. Knowledge is embedded in walls, scars, repeated gestures, phrases spoken close into the ear.

They count time by drops, by bowl-fillings, by soot-length, by the slow whitening of replaced mortar. Birthdays matter less than first duties: first safe crossing, first lamp tended, first channel cleared, first watch kept alone. Marriage, if they practice it, is a joining of alcoves and water rights. Two people exchange not vows of forever but maintenance obligations: whose mother will be warmed, whose dead will be sealed, which drip belongs to whose children, who wakes when the warning alcove speaks.

Their love is quiet and practical, but not without beauty. A lover may polish the handhold above another’s bed. May secretly scrape mineral film from a dangerous corner to make medicine. May tune a household drip so it falls at a softer interval. May give a shell bead from a dead grandmother’s niche. May sit close enough that speech can be felt rather than heard.

They fear waste more than death.

Death has a place. Waste does not.

When someone dies, the body is dried as much as the city permits. It is rubbed with lime, salt, soot, oil. Hair is removed. Teeth may be taken and drilled as memory beads. Long bones, if custom allows, may be kept for tools after a period of mourning, though this is done with reverence rather than hunger. The rest is sealed high in the burial niches, above the damp’s worst reach. The dead become part of the wall not metaphorically but structurally. Their niches help brace passages. Their names become tactile marks. Children pass fingers over them on the way to the market ledge.

Mourning is conducted softly, because grief cannot be permitted to drown out warning sounds. They press foreheads to stone. They strike a low tone once, then hold silence inside themselves while the water continues. The cruelty of the place is that the city does not pause. The mercy of the place is the same.

Their religion, if it is religion, is indistinguishable from hydraulic care.

They do not worship water as a generous mother. They know water too well for sentiment. Water feeds, chills, rots, remembers, conceals, measures, betrays. It is not kind. It is law. Their reverence is for balance: the falling and receiving, the filling and spilling, the notch cut correctly, the overflow that prevents drowning, the drain that must never be blocked. Their holiest object may be the still bowl behind the mineral curtain—the one water that does not tremble. They go to it in sickness, before difficult repairs, after births, after dreams of silence. No one drinks from it. No one touches it except the appointed keeper, and perhaps not even then. Its stillness is terrifying because everything else moves.

They have myths of the First Clog, the Long Silence, the Child Who Heard Upward, the Lamp That Burned Blue for Seven Fillings, the Dead Mason who returned as a drip from the ceiling and corrected a faulty gutter. They tell stories in fragments between noises. Their myths are full of warnings disguised as wonders. A hero is someone who notices. A fool is someone who assumes the water will continue without tending.

Their punishments are also practical. A thief of food owes labor. A breaker of lamps works in darkness under supervision until their hands learn humility. A person who tampers with a channel without sanction may be forbidden from touching waterworks, which is a terrible social diminishment. Exile, if it exists, is nearly death: to be sent into the unworked dark beyond the civic sound, into passages without known handholds or named drips. They do not threaten it lightly.

Children are precious and troublesome. They are louder than the city prefers. They splash, ask surface-questions, hide in bins, lick mineral skins from walls, drop pebbles into forbidden grates to hear where they go. Their elders terrify them with stories of drain-mouths that remember the taste of ankles. But children also hear things adults have stopped hearing. More than one disaster has been prevented by a child saying, “That bowl is counting wrong.”

The elderly become listeners. As their bodies stiffen and their eyes cloud, they are seated near key walls, wrapped in layered hides, hands resting on stone. They may not be able to lift a gate, but they can say, without opening their eyes, that the eastern baffle is silting, that a cistern lip has begun to sing too high, that someone is crying in the third passage, that a stranger has entered by the low culvert. Age is not withdrawal. It is refinement.

They do not laugh loudly. Their laughter is breathy, swallowed, shoulder-shaking. Loud laughter would vanish into the water anyway. Anger is close-faced and brief, because prolonged quarrel is dangerous in narrow places. Privacy is achieved not by distance but by etiquette. A turned back, a touched lintel, a shell marker hung across a doorway: these create rooms more effectively than walls.

They are intimate with repair. Everyone repairs something. The smallest child carries clay plugs. Adults sleep with tools within reach. Brokenness is not an event but a condition held at bay. They patch mortar with thumb-pressure exactly where their grandparents patched it. They clear calcite from channels, but not too much; the city’s mineral growth is both threat and lining. They trim stalactites as one might trim nails. They scrape soot only when soot interferes with airflow; otherwise it is history and insulation. They know that to over-clean is another form of damage.

Their art is mostly hidden in utility. A gutter lip shaped like a sleeping fish. A handhold worn into the likeness of a face by generations who pretended not to carve it. Tiny clay figures sealed behind walls during repair, each with exaggerated ears. Shell inlays on stair edges arranged in family patterns. Work songs whose rhythm matches the clearing of baffles. The soot above lamps sometimes traced with wet fingers into maps of sound. Beauty here cannot demand extra space, so it enters through repetition.

They are not joyless. They enjoy warmth intensely. They enjoy the rare sweetness of fat. They enjoy a lamp newly filled, a tool fitting perfectly into the hand, a child learning a difficult passage, a basin returning to its proper note after hours of labor. They enjoy stories of impossible dry places, though they mock them. They enjoy the feel of polished stone under the palm. They enjoy being right about water.

Their dreams are full of ceilings.

Some dream the ceiling rises and rises until sound is lost upward and they wake in terror. Some dream of green, though they have no true image for it; in their dreams it may appear as soft mineral fire, as hair growing from stone, as edible darkness. Some dream of silence, and those dreams are taken seriously. A person who dreams three times of silence is brought to the still bowl and asked what, exactly, stopped first: the drip-cups, the fall, the underdrain, the heart.

They are cautious with strangers, not cruel by instinct. A stranger is a possible contaminant, a possible mouth to feed, a possible bearer of tools, disease, news, or imbalance. They would first be heard, then surrounded without seeming surrounded. Lamps would be hooded lower. Children removed. Someone would touch the stranger’s wrist to judge heat and trembling. Someone else would stand near the nearest gate-pin. Hospitality, if granted, would be formal: a crouching place, a cup filled from an acceptable channel, perhaps broth, perhaps a warning not to step where the floor shines black.

To outsiders they might seem primitive because they have little metal, no broad roads, no written archive, no monuments meant to be seen from afar. But this would be a surface mistake. Their sophistication is not expansion but calibration. They have made a civilization out of constraint. They know how to live where a single neglected channel could drown a district, where a lamp placed badly could poison a family, where a careless hand could divert a week’s drinking water. Their intelligence is distributed through habit, sound, stone, and inherited muscle.

They are small because the city is small. Pale because the world is pale. Quiet because the water is loud. Patient because haste kills. Superstitious because systems too complex for any one mind become sacred. Tender because there is no room not to be affected by one another. Severe because the margin is narrow. They are not masters of the chamber. They are its current arrangement of consciousness.

If one watched them from an alcove for a long time, one might begin by seeing only furtive figures in dimness: a bent woman lifting a counterweight, a child with shell-bright ankles, an old man pressing his ear to a wet wall, two potters turning clay under lamp heat, a mourner sealing a bead into mortar. But gradually the figures would cease to seem separate from the city. Their gestures complete the architecture. Their hands are part of the drainage. Their listening is one of the mechanisms by which the water continues to fall correctly.

They are the inhabitants, yes.

But more precisely, they are the city’s hearing.
