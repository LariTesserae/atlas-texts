The people who live there are narrow in their habits, though not in body.

They have learned to move as if the city were listening through their bones. They do not hurry unless alarms have made hurry permissible. They place their feet whole on the black paving, not heel-first, not stamping, and their insulated soles are patterned to disperse impact into a soft, granular hush. Even children learn this early: how to run without making the bridges answer, how to laugh into their sleeves, how to close a door before speaking freely.

They are mostly human, or close enough that the distinction matters only to outsiders. Mountain-born lungs are larger than lowland lungs. Their blood is dark and efficient. Their fingers are long, often chapped at the joints despite the sealed gloves, and their faces have the clear, pared look of people raised in reflected light: high color in the cheeks, pale lips, irises contracted against brightness bouncing from stone and ice. Many have small silver implants along the throat or behind the ear, not decorative, though some make them so: pressure monitors, oxygen regulators, bone-conduction communicators, tiny weather receivers that shiver before the public alarms speak.

They dress in layers that are less clothing than portable architecture. Soft inner skins wick heat and salt. Outer shells are pale gray, blue-black, bone-white, sometimes translucent at the seams where warmth is being redistributed. Their collars rise to the jaw. Their cuffs lock. Their hoods fold out into stiff crescent visors when snow begins to fall through the slit. A person crossing an upper bridge in stormlight may look almost faceless: a padded figure with a reflective mask, a line of frost along one shoulder, gloved hands held slightly away from the body so as not to freeze moisture into the joints.

Indoors, when the seals have shut and the pressure steadies, they become smaller. They peel out of the outer shells and hang them on warm rods. They uncover thin wrists, close-cropped hair, tattooed scalps, old scars from ice burns and adhesive failures. They wear soft house clothes in muted colors: rust, smoke-green, faded amber, the few warm shades permitted by dyes that do not off-gas or stain filtration membranes. Their dwellings are shallow, but they make depth with curtains, recessed sleeping shelves, fold-out tables, walls that remember a handprint and glow faintly for an hour after being touched.

They are not a loud people, but they are not solemn. Their humor is compressed, exact, often technical. A joke may consist of changing one digit on a public water tally and waiting for the district hydrologist to notice; the apology will be formal, the laughter private and intense. They make games out of restraint: who can cross the echo court without waking the upper microphones, who can identify a pump by a single change in its pitch, who can tell which ledge is thawing by the smell of the air.

They know the city by systems rather than streets.

Ask one for directions and they may say: go past the second warm conduit, take the lift where the draft turns mineral, cross under the west meltline but do not touch the rail, then descend until the wall sounds hollow. They navigate by temperature on the face, by the timing of condensation, by which gutters are singing. A child of eight can read frost. An old engineer can feel, through a palm laid against ceramic, whether water behind the panel is clean, brackish, useful, or dangerous.

Their professions are as specialized as organs.

There are catchment clerks who account for the daily descent of meltwater in measures too small for outsiders to respect. They sit in glass booths above the lower channels, watching beads gather and vanish through filament grids. They speak in fractions of drops. They can tell from mineral taste whether a roofline has cracked.

There are frost barbers, usually young and wiry, who go out before dawn on tether lines to shave excess ice from hinges, grilles, mast collars, and window lips. They carry heated combs and silent chisels. Their work is beautiful for the few seconds before it is gone: translucent curls falling into collection nets, pale dust glittering in the shaft-light.

There are structural listeners who do almost nothing visible. They sit in quiet rooms with their hands resting on plates coupled to the rock. They listen to the fault, the bridges, the anchors, the slow conversation between built things and unwilling stone. Some use instruments; the best are instruments. After a mountain-knock, everyone waits for their verdict.

There are membrane tailors who repair the transparent skins stretched across exposed walks and living fronts. They work with molecular adhesives and warm breath held behind masks, laying patches so thin the eye cannot find them once they set. Their guild marks are not signatures but interruptions in frost pattern: a repaired panel blooms differently from an untouched one.

There are gravity attendants who maintain the lift zones. They are calm people, often broad-handed, with a reputation for good marriages and slow anger. They test the ascent plates each morning by releasing weighted spheres into the altered field and watching them rise. No one steps onto a plate until an attendant has nodded.

There are doctors of pressure, doctors of cold, doctors of darkness, doctors of children born too early in thin air. Clinics glow amber behind sealed doors. In them the smell of the city changes most: antiseptic, warmed saline, human hair, nutrient broth, the faint animal fear of bodies being bodies in a place that prefers clean mineral processes.

They eat carefully. Nothing fresh arrives casually. Food is stored, cultured, condensed, printed, fermented, revived from dormancy. The markets open by schedule not because goods are plentiful but because heat, air exchange, and crowd density must be budgeted. A market hour is one of the few times the city seems crowded. People stand shoulder to shoulder under low lamps while vendors unfold trays from the wall: salt cakes, protein ribbons, cultured milk gel, dried fruit thin as stained glass, mineral broth, yeast loaves with dense gray crusts, packets of spice so precious they are sniffed before purchase.

The strongest smell in the markets is not food but longing.

Children press their masks to cases where imported green things are sometimes displayed: a bruised pear under condensation glass, six leaves of basil, a jar of preserved flower petals from below the snowline. They are not allowed to touch. They may not even breathe too closely. Green is treated with a suspicion approaching reverence. It rots, it seeds, it brings spores, it clogs filters, it proves that other worlds are careless.

Some inhabitants have never seen a forest except in flat images. Their dreams of trees are structurally wrong. They imagine trunks like support columns, leaves like small solar membranes, roots like plumbing with bad discipline. When they descend to lower altitudes as adults, many find vegetation obscene at first: uncontrolled multiplication, wet surfaces no one has measured, pollen loose in the air like civic negligence.

They do descend, but not often.

The city needs exchange. Couriers come and go through concealed approaches, through pressure galleries bored behind the upper snowfields, through aircraft docks hidden by weather screens. The residents of the crack trade water data, mountain forecasts, cryogenic storage, rare minerals, observatory time, fault-stress modeling, and technologies for living where people were not invited. In return they receive food, medicine, replacement membranes, children’s books, music files, lowland cloth, machine parts, and news they read days late in quiet rooms.

Outsiders find them courteous and difficult. They do not waste words on welcome rituals that hold doors open too long. They dislike perfume. They notice dripping hair with alarm. They flinch when visitors speak over one another. They answer questions precisely, sometimes so precisely that warmth seems absent, though this is not true. Their care is practical: a dry glove placed silently in your hand; a route changed because you looked tired; a cup warmed to the exact temperature that will not steam.

They love by maintenance.

A parent checks the seal on a child’s hood three times and says nothing tender. A spouse recalibrates the floor heat beneath the other’s sleeping shelf after noticing one cold foot in the morning. Friends meet in a sound-dead court and sit without speaking while meltwater ticks behind the wall. Mourning is done by keeping a dead person’s assigned systems immaculate for one full season. The bereaved polish intakes, audit channels, mend panels, and leave no visible shrine except reliability.

Their dead do not remain in the crack. There is no room, and decomposition is an engineering problem. Bodies are carried to the high cremation annex beyond the inhabited seam, where heat can be spent under strict accounting, or else prepared for descent to family places below. A little mineral ash is sometimes retained in sealed glass and set into a threshold, a bridge rail, a listening plate. In that way the dead become part of maintenance: not ghosts, not ancestors exactly, but tolerated inclusions in the city’s fabric.

Religion, where it exists, has taken the shape of attention.

Some believe the mountain is alive, but they do not sentimentalize it. If it is alive, it is vast, cold, and uninterested, with movements too slow to be mercy and too sudden to be judgment. Others believe only in load tolerances, weather models, and the ethics of correct measurement. Between these positions there is little conflict. Both groups bow their heads when the fault knocks. Both wait for the sensors. Both exhale only after the blue lights remain blue.

Their ceremonies are brief and timed to environmental windows.

At first sun, when angled mirrors throw hard light down the seam, children born that winter are brought onto a protected terrace and held up toward the slit. Not for blessing, the adults say, but for registration of shadow. A new person must cast a shadow in the city ledger. The child’s outline is captured for a moment on a frost plate, then stored with name, parentage, oxygen history, and first breath volume.

At midwinter, when direct sun barely finds the crack, the residents extinguish all nonessential lamps for several minutes. The city remains lit by instruments, ice glow, and the amber emergency veins behind walls. Everyone stands where they are. The water continues to move. The pumps do not stop. The ritual is called Seeing What Holds.

At the spring surge, when meltwater from the snow lip comes down in force, the lower districts gather to watch the first great run pass through transparent channels. No one cheers. The sound would be too much. Instead they touch two fingers to the nearest rail and feel the water enter the city like a pulse returning to a limb.

There are also those who live there but are not counted as citizens.

The maintenance machines have names, though officially they are asset numbers. They cling to walls on magnetic feet and move with the patient delicacy of insects. Some are no larger than a hand; others are long, jointed crawlers that fold themselves around conduits and sleep in charging niches with their tool-arms tucked under them. Children feed them forbidden crumbs of thermal paste. Engineers pretend not to know.

There are diagnostic kites that unfold in the vertical air shaft and hover on adjustments too small to see. There are snow probes like pale eels that vanish upward into drifts and return with pressure maps. There are gutter needles, vent moths, seal spiders, lens grazers. None are intelligent in the way people are, but the city has lived with them long enough to grant them personality. A stubborn de-icer may be cursed by three generations under the same nickname.

And in the drainage channels, the translucent cold-creatures persist.

The residents call them glass shrimp, although they are not shrimp and have never seen glass except as walls. They flicker against the current, flat and jointed, feeding where chemistry briefly becomes edible. They are regarded as signs of good boundary conditions: enough warmth to culture a film, not enough contamination to poison it. If the glass shrimp vanish from a channel, someone is summoned. If they multiply too thickly, someone else is summoned.

Children love them with the restrained ache reserved for things that cannot be held. They lie on their stomachs above inspection grates, faces lit blue from below, watching the tiny bodies tremble upstream. They give them names, all unused by the creatures. They are warned again and again: do not touch. Your hand is weather from another planet.

The city’s elders are recognizable by their stillness. They waste no motion. Their eyes move before their heads do. They have survived seal failures, whiteout blockages, lift hesitations, two-day pump silences, falls arrested by tether webbing, and at least one shift of the mountain large enough to rename streets. They remember when some blank rock faces were open air. They remember the first winter, or claim to. They sit in warm alcoves near public passages and correct young workers without raising their voices.

They are not nostalgic. Nostalgia requires an idea that the past was safer. Here, no one seriously believes that. The past was less sealed, less mapped, less buffered, less forgiving. The city improves or it dies.

The young, however, are restless in ways the city cannot entirely engineer away. They climb unapproved ledges. They whisper in echo chambers to hear their words come back strange. They trade contraband recordings of rain falling on leaves. They dare each other to stand beneath legal drips before the filaments catch them. Some paint invisible designs on exterior panels using compounds that reveal themselves only when frost forms: enormous birds, impossible vines, open doors drawn on blank stone.

A few leave.

A few return unable to sleep without the mountain pressing close on both sides.

Those who stay develop a particular kind of interior spaciousness. Living in a crack does not make them feel confined; it makes them distrustful of waste. A broad lowland avenue may seem to them less like freedom than failure of imagination. Why so much open air doing nothing? Why so many surfaces unmeasured? Why water allowed to fall and disappear?

They are custodians of a wound that has become habitable. They know this, though they do not say it poetically. Their city exists because stone opened by violence and had not yet closed. Everything they do is a negotiation with that fact. They build lightly, seal tightly, speak softly, count constantly. They are temporary in the way all mountain things are temporary, but they have made temporariness exact.

At night, when the membranes lower and the upper slit goes black except for moving snow, they withdraw into their lit shelves in the stone. From outside, if anyone could see into the crack, the city would look almost uninhabited: a few amber rectangles, a bridge light dimming, a maintenance machine crossing a wall like a thought.

But behind the panels people are awake.

Someone is warming broth. Someone is recalculating the west conduits. Someone is rocking a child in silence while frost thickens on the outer skin. Someone is listening to the fault. Someone is repairing a glove for another person who will need it before dawn. Someone is watching glass shrimp travel upstream through mineral dark.

They live there carefully.

They live there as if care itself were the species the mountain had permitted.
