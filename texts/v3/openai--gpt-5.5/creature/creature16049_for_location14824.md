What lives there is not a set of species so much as an argument between mineral and appetite.

The first inhabitant is the valley itself.

Not metaphorically. Not as poets say a forest is alive because birds move through it. The under-valley is a body distributed across miles: a many-rooted, many-mouthed, heat-storing organism or congress of organisms whose members have forgotten where one individual ends. Its columns are bones only when they need to bear weight, lungs when the pressure changes, teeth when they find stone, ears when sound travels through them. It grows architecture the way other things grow hair. It makes rooms, closes them, tries again. It learns by pressure, shadow, vibration, chemistry. It has no face, but it notices.

Its oldest parts are dark, polished, and hard as horn. Its youngest parts are pale and fever-warm, smelling of pepper, milkweed, hot dust, and split resin. It feeds without drinking. It grazes on basalt, salts, buried metals, ash, stale air, animal breath, lightning, heat. It has root-mouths like pale finger pads that press against stone until the stone yields molecule by molecule. Around seams of copper or iron it becomes aggressive, knotting itself into fists. Around salt it grows delicate and crystalline, sending out fans of translucent tissue that crackle when touched.

It is not one intelligence, if intelligence means a mind with a center. It is more like weather that remembers. A repeated footpath becomes easier. A repeated camp becomes enclosed. A repeated drumbeat may be answered after several days by a new hollow grown in the bark of a trunk. It mistakes persistence for request. It mistakes shadow for hunger. It does not love or hate the smaller lives within it, but it incorporates their habits into its own becoming.

Beneath everything, in the narrowest strata, live the powder-makers.

They are smaller than insects, smaller than seeds: mites, dry fungi, mineral yeasts, flake-lichens, pale bacterial crusts that never see wetness and would drown in a drop of water. They digest brittleness. They turn shed bark to dust, dust to rind, rind to aromatic cakes. Some form gray veils over old corpses and reduce them to articulated salt. Others colonize abandoned seed pods and make them hum faintly with fermentation. They are the valley’s hidden weather, raising invisible storms whenever a hoof strikes the floor.

Above them are the raspers.

These are the small continuous mouths of the place: beetles with mandibles like chisels, worms sheathed in cork, translucent grubs that eat only the inner lining of root-tunnels, ants that harvest pollen not for sweetness but for its powdered fat. Some beetles polish living bark until it shines black and reflective; the plants tolerate them because polished bark sheds spores of parasites. Others drill perfectly round holes in mineralized stems, leaving behind flutes that the valley’s breath later plays.

There are termites here, but not the damp, secret kind. These are dry-winged, amber-jointed, and hot to the touch. Their colonies rise as comb-towers under ledges, each chamber sealed with wax and ground mica. They farm brittle fungi in stacked shelves and carry flecks of metal in their jaws. When disturbed, they pour out like blown ash, making a sound like paper being torn in a thousand hands.

There are bees without flowers, or with flowers so altered that nectar would seem vulgar. Pale, eyeless bees scrape wax from leaf seams and carry resin beads in the crooks of their legs. Their combs stand freestanding, delicate and architectural, built around currents of warm air. They raise their young on powdered pollen, mineral dust, and the dried secretions of certain fruiting membranes. Their honey, if it can be called honey, is a granular amber paste that burns blue when touched with flame.

The plants use them. The bees use the plants. Neither arrangement is stable.

Many plants here are traps, but not always for meat.

Some grow jaws to catch shadows. A silver-leaved fan will clap shut around a passing glider, not to digest the animal, but to steal the heat of its panic and the mites in its fur. Thorn mazes close around herds and hold them just long enough to harvest breath, salt, dandruff, and alarm scent. Velvet nets rise from the floor only after counting a rhythm of steps; they ignore a single traveler, then seize the twentieth beast in a migration. Seed pods wait for a particular beetle bite, a particular acid from a particular mouth, and will remain sealed for decades without it.

Other plants are generous in a way that is indistinguishable from manipulation. Vines shed edible skins when grazers pass, then grow hooks in the grazers’ fur to spread their knots. Warm fruit open for larvae, feeding them protein dust until the larvae hatch with their gut already seeded with the plant’s spores. Cork chimneys sing in low tones that attract gliders, whose droppings feed the chimney roots. Curtains of thin leaves release calming oils when herds stampede, not out of mercy but because trampling breaks too much young tissue.

The great grazers are among the most visible inhabitants.

They are soft-bodied animals the size of cattle, jointed like larvae, with heavy shovel lips and ringed segments under hide the color of old parchment. They move slowly until frightened, then all their joints work at once and they surge forward with awful speed. They eat cork, rind, root-hair, dry moss, shed membrane, even the mineral pellets excreted by others. Their teeth are not teeth but stacked plates that continually crumble and regrow. Their blood is thick, saline, and golden-brown, more like warm resin than fluid.

On their backs grow little countries: parasitic mosses, mite orchards, miniature thorn scrub, wax beads, fungal fans. Smaller creatures climb them as moving hills. There are long-snouted pickers that live almost entirely on the gardens of grazer backs, and narrow-striped cats that live on the pickers. Old grazers become ecosystems before they die. Dead ones become geography within an hour.

The burrowers are quieter but more numerous.

They are pale mammals, if mammal is still the right word for something that nurses its young on dry meal expressed from chest glands. Their skin is suede-soft and pollen-yellowed, their eyes reduced to black pinholes, their faces widened into shovels. They move in herds under the root mats, lifting the surface in traveling waves. When they surface, they do it all together: hundreds of blunt heads pushing up through fibrous plates, nostrils flaring to taste dust.

Their tunnels do not last. The valley heals behind them. So the burrowers are not makers of burrows but makers of temporary permission. They know the softnesses in the floor. They feel pressure changes before convulsions and erupt upward seconds before a passage seals. People follow them when desperate, though not too closely; the burrowers flee into spaces a human body cannot survive.

There are lizards that have become bark.

They sleep vertically on trunks with all four feet spread, bodies flattened, armored in plates patterned like split bark and mica. Their mouths hang open, pink and motionless, catching drifting mites and winged larvae. When they move, they do so in sudden exact increments, vanishing into pattern after pattern. Some carry little crowns of sensory hairs along the spine; others have throat sacs that inflate and deflate to mimic the breathing pores of plants. Predators overlook them. Prey lands on them. They live by being a surface until they are a mouth.

In the upper tangle live the gliders.

They are not birds. Feathers would be wasted here. They have long fingers, stretched membranes, hot skin, cartilage masks over their faces, and large dark eyes protected by inner lids against dust. Their ribs show through their sides like the frame of a fan. They roost in hanging root nets by the hundreds, wrapped in their own wings, muttering metallic cries in sleep.

They launch from bridges and ride the valley’s breath: updrafts from cork chimneys, exhalations from warming bulbs, pressure waves before root convulsions. During ordinary times they are precise and beautiful, black commas cutting through parchment light. During upheavals they become panic made visible. They rise in sheets so dense they dim the ceiling, colliding, screaming, shedding dry scales that drift down like pepper.

Some gliders are fruit-eaters. Some take insects. Some have hooked thumbs and hunt the young of other roosts. The largest, with white masks and red throats, sometimes follow human caravans, because humans disturb hidden grubs and carry edible scraps. People consider them bad witnesses: wherever the white-masked gliders circle, something has become vulnerable.

The predators of the valley are rarely simple pursuers.

There are thin cats with shoulder membranes and skin patterned like root-shadow. They leap more than run, stretching between trunks, changing direction in midair with a twist of their ribs. Their paws are silent on cork but loud on husk, and they seem to know this, choosing surfaces with unnerving care. Their eyes are green-black. Their whiskers are stiff enough to read air currents. They kill quickly and then drag prey into folds where roots will not immediately contest them.

There are blind raptors that no longer belong to any sky. Their wings are short, clawed, and mostly used for balance. They hunt by tremor: the ticking fall of seeds, the rasp of a beetle under bark, the heart-shudder of a small mammal beneath a root plate. Their heads are featherless and armored, their beaks long and pale. They stand motionless for hours on ribbed domes, then strike the ground with enough force to crack woody flooring.

There are coil-beasts that pretend to be hanging roots. Segmented, dry-skinned, eyeless along most of their length, they suspend themselves from arches in loose loops. Their mouths are concealed until opened: circular, thorned, lined with backward hooks. They do not chase. They wait for fur, membrane, or sleeve to brush them. Then the root comes alive.

And there are predators that are not animals at all: groves, pits, walls, sensory fields. A person may be hunted by a place deciding to become narrow.

The human inhabitants live as a species of caution.

They are lean, dust-colored people with cracked lips, scarred hands, and eyes trained to look everywhere except where the valley wants attention. Their hair is often bound tight or lacquered under caps, because loose hair catches pollen, hooks, eggs, listening fibers. Children wear masks almost as soon as they can walk: little snouted things packed with aromatic felt, charcoal dust, and bitter leaf. Adults decorate theirs with cuts and beads, not for beauty alone but for identification in the noise and haze.

Their clothing is layered and practical: barkcloth stiffened with resin, suede hides from burrowers, lacquered leaf plates over the shoulders, gloves with thorn-proof palms. A well-made cloak has a slick outside to shed spores and a fibrous inside that can be torn for cordage. Nothing they wear is merely worn. Every garment is filter, armor, storage, signal, bandage, tinder, shade.

They do not think of themselves as owners of the under-valley. Ownership would be absurd. They think in terms closer to tenancy, trespass, kinship, debt, and weather. A stable column that has held three generations of platforms is called a grandmother, but no one trusts it not to split tomorrow. A dangerous grove may be addressed politely before crossing. A nursery field is never cursed aloud. Words matter less because the valley understands them than because fear makes people careless.

Their settlements are pauses around temporary agreements.

A good settlement begins with a column that has not shifted in a season, a warm vent that does not exhale poison, a nearby stand of edible rind, and at least two escape routes above trampling height. Platforms are lashed to living buttresses with fiber ropes that can be cut quickly. Huts are low and oval, woven from root-rib, patched with insect wing, barkcloth, and hide. Floors are sprung, not rigid; a house that cannot flex becomes a coffin when the valley heaves.

Nothing is built without a way to abandon it.

Their ladders are pulled up at nightless intervals of dimming. Their sleeping hammocks hang high and apart, because if one is swallowed or infested the others may survive. Tool caches are hidden in hollow knots, marked by scent rather than sight. Food is stored in baskets slung from ceiling tendrils, away from burrowers, grazers, and children. Maps are carved on chalky stone and understood to be lies that were true yesterday.

They are excellent listeners.

Every adult carries a staff, not only for walking but for hearing. They set the butt to the floor and press their jaw or brow to the shaft, reading tremors through bone. They can distinguish grazer herds from root pressure, seedfall from raptor strike, ordinary ceiling clicks from the dry stutter that precedes collapse. In loud districts they speak with hands, shoulders, and mask-tilts. Their gesture language is large enough for jokes, warnings, bargaining, grief, insults, courtship, and theology.

Children learn first not names but consequences.

Fresh white tendril: wait.

Blue-black sheen on bark: do not touch with bare skin.

Warm wind smelling of cloves: passage opening.

Bitter almond: climb.

Sudden insect silence: climb faster.

Hair-field leaning toward you: stop speaking.

Silver leaves closing overhead: cast no moving shadow.

A fruit that pulses when held is not food, or not yet, or not for humans.

They have domesticated very little. Domestication requires predictability, and predictability attracts the valley’s attention. Instead they cultivate temporary alliances. They keep wax-bees in portable comb frames and release them before migrations. They encourage certain bark lizards to sleep near food stores by feeding them mites. They follow burrower herds but never pen them. They plant cuttings that may become shelter if they are lucky and a trap if they are arrogant.

Their firekeepers are half-priests, half-engineers, half-criminals in the eyes of anyone who has seen a resin flare go wrong.

Fire is carried in mineral cups, banked in ash, fed with dense cakes of cured resin and powdered salt. The best flame is almost invisible and produces little smoke. Children are not allowed to stare into it too long, not because of superstition but because sparks follow breath, and breath follows fascination. Ash is precious. Char is medicine. Soot marks warnings that living bark cannot easily heal over.

They eat what can be eaten before it becomes something else.

Cork strips roasted over covered heat. Powdered protein from opened larvae-fruit. Salt pellets ground with bitter seeds. Grazer fat rendered into waxy cakes. Mite paste. Bark tea without water, made by heating aromatic shavings until vapors condense on cool stone, then scraping the residue. Eggs laid in husk. Fungal wafers cured dry and brittle. Their mouths are adapted, culturally if not biologically, to dust and intensity: sharp, resinous, mineral flavors; foods that crack, crumble, smoke, or sting.

They do not drink. Or if their ancestors once did, the memory has become myth.

Moisture is spoken of as a dangerous abundance, a softness that rots structure and drowns breath. In old songs, the upper world is not a paradise of rivers but a place where the sky leaks and the ground dissolves into mud. Some people believe water is a solvent spirit that would unmake the valley if it entered in sufficient quantity. Others dismiss this as children’s dread. None of them have seen rain.

Their dead are not buried. Burial belongs to soil, and there is no soil.

A corpse is wrapped, sung over quickly, and given to a chosen hunger. Some families offer their dead to bone-boring beetles so the cleaned skeleton can be hung in the rafters as an ancestor-chime. Some lay them in root clefts where the valley will take the salts and grow a memorial knot. Some burn only a hand, a mask, or a lock of hair, because whole-body burning is wasteful and dangerous. Mourning must be prompt; grief delayed too long becomes logistically difficult, as the dead will already be joining other architectures.

Their art is portable or sacrificial.

Masks, flutes, carved staffs, knot records, lacquered leaves etched with maps of vanished passages. Drums made from hollow thorn and stretched glider membrane. Tiny figures cut from obsidian and tucked into quiet pockets. Rings of stone laid in chambers where sound behaves strangely. They make music that can be heard through the feet as much as ears. Some songs are designed for humans; others are addressed to walls, seed pods, thorn gates, vents.

They tell stories about the quiet pockets.

Some say they are ears of the valley, places where the great body listens inward. Some say they are scars from ancient fires. Some say people made them long ago by teaching plants to hold still. Those who enter such chambers often leave offerings: a broken tool, a tooth, a stool, a mask with the filter removed. Not all offerings are reverent. Some are tests. Some are apologies. Some are warnings left for the next traveler: this silence is not empty.

There may be deeper inhabitants, too.

Behind translucent membranes in the walls, silhouettes move slowly during bright flares: forms too large for animals, too deliberate for roots. The people have names for them but disagree on whether they are creatures, organs, ancestors, or mistakes. One appears as a procession of knuckled limbs pressing from behind amber skin. Another as a vast headless browsing thing embedded halfway in basalt, chewing stone with hidden motions. Another as a cluster of bladders that pulse in sequence when certain drums are played.

No one hunts these beings. No one admits worshipping them. Everyone lowers their voice near their walls.

What lives in the dry under-valley, finally, is a community without outside.

Every life is surface for another life. Every shelter is also bait. Every road is a wound temporarily permitted to remain open. The inhabitants are miners, grazers, listeners, mimics, gliders, polishers, trap-builders, dust-eaters, salt-blooded children, resin-burning elders, plants that behave like weather, animals that behave like bark, people who survive by never mistaking stillness for safety.

They are not adapted to scarcity as to a hardship. They are adapted to dryness as to a law of physics. They do not yearn for rain. They do not search for rivers. Their abundance is powder, heat, mineral, breath, wax, thorn, vibration, and the endless conversion of body into passage and passage into body.

They live inside a world that is still deciding what ground means.

And they have learned, as much as any creature can, to be temporary in the presence of something that grows.
