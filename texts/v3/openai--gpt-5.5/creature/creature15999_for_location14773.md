What lives there is not a people in the usual sense, though the place has citizens.

They are citizens by pressure, by dependence, by the exact fit of one body’s habit into another body’s need. Nothing there is solitary for long. A seed lodged alone in a seam becomes a wall. A wall becomes shade. Shade becomes a sleeping hollow. A sleeping hollow gathers fur, scales, husks, eggshell, breath-salt, and the powdered remains of meals. Rootlets find it. Burrowers widen it. Pollinators pass through it. In time it becomes a room.

The first inhabitants, or the deepest ones, are the plants.

They are not tender plants. They do not seem to believe in weather. Their leaves are made like sealed vessels: thick, lacquered, ribbed internally with pale channels through which slow bitter gel moves in pulses too faint to see. They stand in ranks and clusters, but each individual is less like a shrub than like a folded cistern. Their skins shine because they must not lose anything. Even their scars close with a resinous lip, amber or green-black, which hardens almost before the wound has finished opening.

Some are fan-bladed seam plants rooted deep in the fresh cracks of the plateau. Their roots descend farther than their crowns suggest, probing the young stone for mineral vapor, old brine, trapped gases, anything that can be persuaded into life. Their leaves overlap until they form living corridors. They do not sway. They lean by degrees over days, altering passages, opening or closing alleys according to heat, pollen, grazing pressure, and the slow directives of their root-webs.

Others are cable-vines, though they are not true vines in the soft climbing sense. They grip stone by exuding a dry adhesive skin, a glue that becomes part resin and part mineral crust. They bridge gaps between towers. They braid themselves into high roads. Their swollen knots are reservoirs, greenish and translucent under a thin opaque rind. Inside them, gel folds over gel, moving reluctantly from chamber to chamber. Small animals tap these knots with specialized teeth, never piercing deeply, shaving only the condensed salts and wax blooms from the surface. A deep wound would be a civic injury; the vines have defenses against such waste.

There are also the bulb-towers at the center, the closest thing the city has to organs. They rise squat and crowded, bases pressed together like knuckles. Their outer skins are plated with old leaf-bases and mineral accretions, but inside they are chambered. Some chambers hold dormant buds. Some hold pollen. Some hold air warmed by the sun and released upward through vents to carry powders from one court to another. When the bulb-towers flower, they do not blossom so much as open carefully made faults in themselves. A hard lip splits. An oily interior shines. Dust clings to whatever warmth passes near it.

The smallest animals understand these openings better than any calendar.

There are flat grazers that resemble leaves which have learned to crawl away from the plant. They are thin, oval, and jointed at the margins, with legs tucked beneath them so that they move as sliding shadows. Their backs are veined green, amber, or bruise-blue depending on what they feed upon, and their mouths are delicate scraping pads. They do not bite into leaves. They polish them. They graze the wax bloom, the outer films, the mineral crusts and microbial skins that gather on plant surfaces. A careless grazer would die here; tearing living tissue would bring resin, bitterness, and the attention of larger mouths. So they feed as if cleaning icons.

Their eggs are laid in lines along the undersides of ledges, each egg a dry bead glued with saliva and dust. The young hatch pale and nearly transparent. For their first days they live on light-baked residues and the powder shed from adult skins. Only after their backs darken do they climb onto living leaves.

The pale burrowers belong to the stone more than to the air.

They are blunt-headed, nearly blind, with noses armored in enamel-like plates and forelimbs shaped for prying rather than digging. The salt-stone does not yield like soil; it must be split along its habits. The burrowers know those habits in their bones. They listen through their jaws to the faint ticks of crystal growth, the flex of roots, the heat strain in ledges. Then they press, wedge, rasp, and lever angular grains free.

Their tunnels are narrow, cool, and crowded with root hairs. They eat carbon lace, dead wax, fungal threads, old pollen, and the mineral-rich droppings of upper animals. What they leave behind is more valuable than what they take: loosened stone, aerated cracks, pellets of half-digested fertility packed into seams. Many plants cannot establish without them. Many sleeping chambers began as burrower galleries enlarged by generations of warm bodies.

They are communal sleepers. In the cool galleries they lie heaped flank against flank, pale backs rising and falling in slow waves. Their young are born folded and dry, with sealed eyes and transparent ears pressed flat to the skull. The mothers do not nurse with milk in the ordinary way; they exude a dense paste from abdominal pores, a concentrate of fat, salt, and microbial culture, which the young lap from the fur.

The pollinators are more visible, though no louder.

They are long-legged, glass-bodied creatures with folded wings that look like scraps of shed husk. In full light they almost vanish. Their limbs are smoky and translucent, with joints too fine to seem strong, yet they can cling for hours to a vertical salt fin, waiting for the heat to arrange itself. They fly only when flight is cheaper than climbing. Most of their lives are spent stepping, bridging, testing air with their forelegs.

Their bodies carry charge. Fine hairs along the abdomen and throat gather pollen not by stickiness but by attraction; a grain leaps to them before contact. At dusk, when the towers release their cooling breaths, the pollinators climb to ridges, spread their husk-wings, and let the faint pressure of air lift them sideways. They cross alleys like falling leaves that have changed their minds.

Their larvae live in dry flower throats, feeding not on nectar but on oil films and spent reproductive dust. They pupate in little parchment hammocks under stone lips. Empty pupal cases accumulate in the central court until the floor there crackles softly under the feet of larger animals.

Above them, in the vineways, live the warm-eyed climbers.

They are the most personlike of the animals, if personhood is judged by hands, faces, and hesitation. They are small, narrow-shouldered, with short dusty fur patterned in broken leaf-shadow: olive, tan, rust, charcoal. Their tails are prehensile and almost always wrapped around something. Their hands have long thumbs and black pads that grip resin cables without tearing them.

They move hand over hand through the upper lattice, rarely descending to the ground except at night or in the slack hours of evening. Their eyes are large, copper-brown, with horizontal pupils that narrow in glare. They feed on pollen cakes, wax shavings, scale-insects, pod rims, and the salt crusts that form around old vents. They also raid eggs when they can, though they do so carefully. A nest emptied completely is a future hunger. They seem to understand this, whether by intelligence or inherited restraint.

Their social life is quiet but intricate. They groom one another with powdered leaves. They exchange mouthfuls of mineral paste. They mark vine junctions not with urine, which would be too costly, but with cheek-wax, a dry scent rubbed from glands near the jaw. Their calls are mostly inaudible to larger ears: throat-clicks, tooth taps, the faintest squeaks blown through closed lips. At night they gather in suspended nests made from carbon lace, shed fiber, and clipped strips of dead leaf, all woven into cups under the warm sides of bulb-towers.

Their dead are not carried far. A body is wedged into a root-crotch or sealed in a hollow with husks and dust. Within a season, rootlets enter. Within several seasons, the place is greener.

The largest animals are the slow browsers of the lower ways.

They are not giants, but in that close city they feel immense. Their bodies are low, plated, and deliberate, with shoulders like rounded stones and flanks armored in overlapping shields of bark-colored keratin. Dust gathers in the seams of their plates until seedlings sometimes sprout there after rare mistless coolings, living a few days before being licked away by smaller riders.

Their heads are broad and calm. Their eyes are small, dark, and set high. The mouth is a horizontal softness beneath all that armor, with a tongue dark as wet clay though it is never truly wet. That tongue is their instrument. They extend it over shaded stone, across pollen-dusted floors, along the undersides of ledges, gathering films invisible to most creatures: mineral bloom, bacterial sheen, fallen reproductive powder, the exudates of roots.

They do browse the plants, but ritually, almost as gardeners. They take old outer leaves, softened seal-rims, spent flower lips. Their blunt side-teeth crush dry fiber into flakes. When they pass through the alleys, the plants loosen by fractions, recognizing the pressure pattern of their approach through root and stone. A browser forces open lanes that would otherwise close forever. Behind it follow smaller animals: groomers, egg thieves, parasites, dust-feeders, opportunists. On its back ride flat grazers and quick-fingered climbers picking mites from the plate seams.

Their dung is precious. It is not dropped wet, but pressed out as dense pellets encased in a mineral skin. Burrowers steal them. Roots penetrate them. Whole gardens begin where a browser has paused repeatedly in shade.

There are reptiles at the bright edges, made of stillness.

They bask among the glassy salt fins, their bodies flattened to receive heat and striped by the fins’ shadows until they become part of the mineral glare. Their scales are pale cream, green-gray, or translucent pink near the throat. They hunt by waiting at plant thresholds where small grazers and pollinators must pass. Their strikes are quick, but their lives are otherwise nearly motionless. Each has a territory no larger than a few ledges, defended not by combat but by posture, by the inflation of a dusty throat, by the slow darkening of skin patches that absorb more heat.

Their eggs are laid in old gas vents where warmth persists after sunset. The shells are chalky and thick. Hatchlings emerge already patient.

In the deepest cracks live the filament-makers.

They are neither plant nor animal in any simple way. They enter fallen leaves from within, digesting wax, resin, and the dry architecture of dead tissues. They leave behind black, porous lace that holds nutrients and traces of air. In darkness they grow as pale threads. In light they harden into soot-colored mats. They are the undertakers and the masons of fertility.

Without them, death would remain death too long. Leaves would accumulate as useless armor. Skins would lie intact. Eggshells would blow into corners and stay there. The filament-makers unlock these things slowly, translating sealed bodies into seams that roots can read.

There are also mineral lives, or things close enough to life that the distinction matters less there than elsewhere.

Crystal crusts grow around roots and bones. Salt lips extend from vents like listening ears. Silica skins form over abandoned nests, preserving their shapes after the builders are gone. These mineral structures are not alive alone, but they participate. They wick, store, shade, channel, and remember. A root entering a crystal seam changes its growth. A burrower hearing a crystal tick chooses a path. A pollinator resting on a salt fin acquires a charge that will later pull pollen from a flower’s throat.

The city’s inhabitants are thus not arranged like a food chain so much as like a set of held breaths.

The plants hold solvent.

The animals hold movement.

The stone holds heat.

The fungi hold death open long enough for life to use it.

The dust holds messages from one chamber to another.

None of them wastes. Even fear is economical. A startled climber does not scream; it freezes flat against a vine, eyes bright. A grazed leaf does not bleed; it seals. A wounded insect curls into a bead and dries. A hunted animal chooses shade before speed. Pursuit is brief, because every body knows the cost of haste.

If there is a mind there, it is distributed.

It is in the way leaf-walls tighten before midday and the way burrowers sleep deeper under them. It is in the browsers’ ancient routes, worn not into the stone by erosion but into the behavior of plants that open for them. It is in the pollinator paths shaped by warm shafts of air rising through tower throats. It is in the upper animals’ nests placed exactly where night heat lingers longest. It is in the settlement’s refusal to spill outward into the barren slopes before it can afford to go.

The residents do not possess the city. They are how the city continues.

They are dry-bodied, sealed, slow, attentive creatures; wax-skinned plants; patient mouths; silent wings; blind diggers; mineral-breathing roots; sleepers in salt-walled chambers. They live close because closeness is survival. They move little because every movement must matter. They make no songs because sound carries too far in the packed stillness and returns nothing useful.

Yet the place is not austere to them. It is abundant. It is full of edible films, charged dust, warm stone, safe hollows, hardened leaves, bitter gels, old bones becoming root-food, flowers cracking open in the exact hour of need. To an animal born there, the empty slopes beyond must seem impossible: too open, too loud with light, too poor in edges.

What lives there is a society of conservation.

Not a civilization of tools, but a civilization of fit. A green dry multitude, newly assembled and already interdependent, learning the young stone as the stone learns how to shelter life.
