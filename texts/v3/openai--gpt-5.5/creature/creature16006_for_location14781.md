What lives there is not a people, unless patience can become a people.

It is a dense, warm, blind community, descended from whatever first entered the drowned rooms and survived the narrowing of the world. Nothing in it belongs to open water anymore. Even the fish seem architectural. Even the eels have learned corners. Every body has become an answer to stone, seam, shaft, lip, grate, bell, well.

At the base of everything are the seam-films.

They are the chamber’s pasture and weather, though they do not look alive in any familiar way. They spread as thin skins along cracks where warm mineral water exhales from below: copper-black, yellow-white, violet as bruised shell, sometimes faintly luminous when disturbed. They feed on heat, salts, old metals, and the slow breath of the masonry. They do not sway. They do not branch. They thicken by fractions, grain over grain, until some grazer comes and planes them down with a mouth like a wet file.

Most of the chamber’s creatures live by learning how much of this film may be taken without starving the crack.

The smallest grazers are coin-backed things, flat and round, with translucent rims and undersides crowded with tiny combing legs. They move in rows along warm channels, each one pressing itself close to the stone as if listening through its belly. Their shells are not true armor so much as memory: mineral accretions grown from the very walls they feed on. Older ones are almost indistinguishable from fallen chips of tile until they loosen and glide a finger’s width farther along.

They do not compete openly. When a seam is crowded, they stack slightly, rim against rim, forming pale shingles over the warmth. The upper ones wait. The lower ones eat. Then, after hours or days, the arrangement reverses with a slow, courteous pressure. A dead coin-grazer is not removed. It is eaten into place by its neighbors, and its shell becomes a little terrace for larvae.

In the basins live the comma animals.

They are soft, white, folded beings, neither worm nor slug exactly, curled in the hollows that were once made for hands and vessels. Each has a blunt head with no eyes and a fringe of tasting threads that it lays over the polished ceramic like a veil. They spend most of their lives in the basin where they hatched, turning in patient circles, scraping invisible food from the bowl. When disturbed, they tighten into punctuation marks, small closures of flesh.

Once in a long while, a basin fills with them past bearing. Then several uncoil and let go. They drift out into the chamber as pale ribbons, helpless-looking but exact, catching currents with their bodies. Most are eaten. A few find another hollow. One may settle into the mouth of a carved mask and slowly die there, feeding the ranks of grazers that never enter.

The eels are the chamber’s cords.

They are thin, milky, and almost boneless in appearance, though they have fine internal needles that let them brace themselves in grilles and ceramic ribs. Their heads are narrow, their mouths small, their eyes sealed beneath skin. They move through gaps no other animal trusts, knotting themselves through old latticework, vanishing into drains, emerging from niches where no opening seemed large enough.

They are scavengers, cleaners, messengers of scent. When something dies in a high shaft or behind the stone gate, the eels know first. They gather without frenzy, each taking threadlike mouthfuls, reducing the dead thing by persistence rather than force. Their young are transparent enough that the dark line of the gut can be seen after feeding. They sleep twisted together in the old hooks on the walls, making pale ropes where lamps may once have hung.

The blind fish are more formal.

They travel in schools of seven, twelve, twenty, never quite touching. Each has long facial filaments that trail and curve, reading pressure from pillars, steps, other bodies, the breath of the central well. Their skin is the color of water over bone. Their fins are rounded, almost ceremonial, made for stopping and turning rather than speed.

They know the chamber as a pattern of nearness. A new obstruction troubles them for weeks. A shifted block changes their routes. If one fish loses a filament, the school widens around it until the thread grows back or the fish disappears. They feed on suspended motes, larvae, loosened film, the minute edible fallout of everyone else’s living. When the bells sound, they freeze in place so completely that silt settles on their backs.

Their spawning happens in the stair-treads. The females press beadlike eggs into the hollowed saddles of the steps, where warm and cool waters cross without mingling. The males fan them with motions so slight they seem like hesitation. The eggs hatch into larvae that cling upside down beneath stair lips, each one already wearing the chamber’s darkness like an inherited shell.

The white crabs of the hanging rings are the strangest to watch.

They are not large, but their legs are long and deliberate, each joint tipped with a soft pad that grips mineral-slick stone. They live almost entirely in the suspended framework above the central well, walking the rings and rods as if tending a sky no other creature may enter. Their bodies are chalky, their claws narrow and asymmetrical: one for picking, one for holding.

They eat what rises.

Bubbles from below bring traces of mineral, bacterial clots, soft debris, sometimes tiny blind larvae from deeper shafts. The crabs harvest these from the undersides of the rings and from the trembling meniscus caught in cracks. They never willingly descend to the floor. If one falls, it does not climb back immediately. It crouches where it lands, stunned by the wrongness of level stone, until eels begin to investigate. Sometimes another crab descends partway and extends a leg. Sometimes none does.

Their young are carried under the abdomen in clusters like seed pearls. When they hatch, they climb the mother’s legs and onto the ringwork. Many vanish into the upper dark, and perhaps there are higher chambers where the rings continue.

In the niches live the soft-jawed waiters.

They are the nearest things to monsters, though they are too economical to be cruel. Broad-mouthed, clay-colored, heavy-bodied, they wedge themselves into doorways, jars, low arches, and broken drain housings. Their heads are wide enough to seal a small passage. Their skin has the texture of old wet mortar. Along their lips are brushes, sieves, soft inward-pointing combs.

They do not chase. They allow the chamber to feed them.

A fish drifting too close, a bell-larva, a careless eel, a cloud of young coin-grazers dislodged by current: all may be gathered by the patient opening of that mouth. The jaw expands, the combs flare, the water enters, the edible portion remains. What is too large or too hard is released with almost tender slowness.

Some of them live for centuries, or long enough that generations of smaller animals learn the shape of their lips as part of the architecture. The oldest are furred with worm-cases and mineral lace. Their backs become ledges. Grazers feed on them carefully. Eels clean between the folds of their jaws. A niche-holder is predator, wall, shelter, and weather all at once.

There are bell-creatures too, though they are not the bells’ makers.

They are tiny, gelatinous animals shaped like inverted cups, drifting in the upper water where the stone vessels wait. They pulse only rarely. Each pulse draws water through a fringe of hair-fine cilia and releases it in a small, orderly sigh. In their translucent bells, faint organs glow and go dark. They gather near trapped air but never touch the stone rims. Perhaps those that did are no longer represented in the bloodline.

Their dead are luminous for a short time. They sink slowly, blue-white, turning as they fall. The fish rise to meet them. The crabs reach down from the rings. The chamber accepts their light as briefly as it accepts any other event.

Near the cup-marked walls live the avoiders.

No one would recognize them at first as separate creatures. They are small flattened crusts, like blisters of ivory wax, placed one to a mark but never inside it. From each blister extends a ring of almost invisible threads. They taste whatever passes near the old carved cups. Their bodies are still for months. Then, together, they shift a little lower or higher, maintaining a distance no eye can explain.

The larger animals do not graze them. The eels bend around them. Even drifting larvae seem to veer away. Whether the avoiders are poisonous, sacred by accident, electrically sensitive, or merely unpleasant in some chemical way, they have become part of the chamber’s law. The old pattern in the wall and the living ring around it now form one warning.

Below all of these, in the central well, live things that are seldom seen.

Not one beast in the abyss, not a single guardian, but a depth-population altered by warmth and pressure and long separation from the room above. Sometimes a pale appendage rises from the square shaft and rests on the lip: a many-jointed feeler, thick as a finger, furred with sensory hairs. Sometimes a mouth like a folded wound appears just below the darkness, tasting the mineral current before withdrawing. Sometimes the blind fish give the well a wider berth for a season.

The well-creatures are slow and large by the chamber’s standards. Their bodies may be ribboned, jointed, or baglike; no one has seen enough of one to know. They feed upward when the water tastes right. They release eggs or spores or larvae that drift into the main room and become food for nearly everything. In return, the dead of the chamber eventually find their way downward: fragments, shells, loosened flesh, the long rain of endings.

The well is not their home only. It is their mouth to this world.

There is also a resident intelligence, but not in any single skull.

It is in the inherited caution around the bells. It is in the way schools part before the iron-taste rises from the shaft. It is in the measured grazing of the warm seams, the refusal to enter mask-mouths, the care with which crabs step over rods thin enough to break. No creature explains these things. No creature remembers the first catastrophe. Yet the chamber is full of obedience to events older than any living body.

A bell sounds once, and everyone becomes stone.

A warm crack darkens, and the grazers thin themselves out.

A cool rain-thread descends from above, and feelers gather beneath it in a trembling congregation, tasting news from a world none can visit.

They have no names for the builders. The carved figures are not ancestors to them, except in the broadest sense: old shapes that made new shapes possible. A handhold filled with shell-growth becomes a nursery. A lamp-hook becomes an eel bed. A sacred mouth becomes a mineral spring. The inhabitants do not desecrate the chamber. They complete it according to a different grammar.

They are not primitive. They are exact.

Every body is fitted to a tolerance: the width of a drain, the warmth of a seam, the interval between bubbles, the force needed not to wake a bell. Their lives are small, but not simple. Their world is crowded with boundaries, and each species knows them with skin, whisker, pad, shell, gut, and inherited fear.

Together they make a civilization without speech or tools: a civilization of grazing, waiting, avoiding, cleaning, spawning, filtering, and yielding space. Its laws are pressure, hunger, mineral taste, darkness, and the old handmade shape of the room.

What lives there is the water learning the architecture by means of flesh.
