What lives there is easiest to miss because it has learned the basin’s first law: nothing should announce itself where water can speak instead.

It is not large. At full growth it is no longer than a forearm, though its body seems longer when it moves because it unfolds from places where no body looked possible. It lies beneath wet leaves, in the black seam between moss and stone, half sunk in the warm mud at the pool’s edge. Its skin carries the basin’s colors exactly: gray-brown like old rock, green-black like soaked moss, pale speckles like quartz scars glimpsed through lichen. When still, it becomes less like an animal than a correction in the ground, a shadow the eye accepts.

It is salamander-like, but older in its manner than salamanders seem. Its head is broad and low, with a mouth that rarely opens. Its eyes are small, dark, and wetly reflective, set high enough that it can watch from under a skim of water or leaf rot. Along the sides of its neck are soft fringes, not bright gills but dusky, rootlike folds that stir when the air is very damp. Its limbs are delicate, almost hesitant, each toe long and pale at the tip, good for feeling rather than grasping. It does not run. It presses, eases, pours itself forward.

Most of its life is spent listening through its belly.

The basin is loud to ears, but to this creature the louder world is below: seep pressure, root strain, the minute collapses of saturated soil, the shift of water through stone throats. It knows the place by vibration. A beetle landing on a fern stem, a drop striking the pool, a footstep on the rim, the first loosening of a leaf before it falls—each arrives through mud and water as a distinct event. Its body is made for such notice. It is patient not because it is dull, but because almost everything reaches it eventually.

It feeds on small things: translucent larvae in the moss, soft insects caught under leaves, pale worms in the warm muck, the slow blind life that gathers around mineral seepage. Sometimes it takes the drifting insects above the pool, rising with such care that only a dimple appears from below, as if rain has fallen upward. More often it grazes with its mouth barely open along submerged stone, drawing in films of algae, eggs, rot, and microscopic sweetness. It is both hunter and gatherer, predator and sponge.

There are few of them. Perhaps seven in the whole basin. Perhaps fewer. They do not gather unless the weather has held warm and windless for many days, and the underground water is high enough to fill the hidden cracks beneath the pool. Then, at night, they come out from separate dampnesses and lie near one another without touching. Their throats pulse. Their gill-fringes tremble. No voice carries into the air, but the pool answers with small rings. Under the water, a low clicking passes from stone to stone.

Their eggs are laid where no bird will find them: under the lip of a weeping rock, inside little stone mouths where drops fall steadily but gently. The eggs look like clear beads of sap with dark commas curled inside. Moss grows around them. Mineral film clouds them. For weeks they seem to be part of the rock’s own wetness. When the young hatch, they do not enter open water at first. They vanish into the saturated seams, into the soaked felt of liverwort and root hair, living in the thin world between pool and land.

They grow slowly. Everything here does.

A young one may spend its first year no farther than the span of two hands from the crack where it hatched. It learns the difference between falling water and moving prey, between the shadow of a bird and the harmless drift of fern-frond shade. It learns which stones stay warm after dusk. It learns the safest hollows in the moss, the places where the mud closes gently over its back without trapping it. It learns that the basin has edges, and that beyond those edges the world is too dry, too exposed, too quick.

The adults almost never leave. They do not need to. The basin is their weather, their map, their shelter, their calendar. Their bodies have given up the idea of distance. They know time instead by seep and swelling: the season when the orchids push new roots into the rock face, the season when pale fungi mark the fallen twigs, the few nights when the rim wind comes hard enough to strip water from every leaf. They know when the pool lowers by the width of a claw. They know when a new channel opens in stone.

They are not guardians in any human sense. They would not defend the place with teeth or anger. But their presence completes it. Without them the basin would still drip and green and breathe, yet something in its listening would be unanswered. They are the soft reply to the mountain’s throat-sound, the animal form of its patience.

If a person entered and stayed very still, one might eventually appear.

First there would be nothing but moss, mud, fern, the repeated punctuation of falling drops. Then a wet leaf near the pool would tilt against the direction of the wind. A patch of stone-colored shadow would separate from the stone. Two black pinpoints would open where there had only been mineral dark. It would not flee at once. It would measure the intruder through the mud, through breath, through the tremor of blood in the soles of the feet.

Then, deciding nothing useful could be made of such a large dry creature, it would sink backward into the basin.

Not vanish dramatically. Not dart. Merely become difficult to distinguish from what had always been there: wet root, dark water, old stone, moss accepting another shape.
