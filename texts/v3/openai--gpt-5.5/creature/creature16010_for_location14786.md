What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense, though there are beings among them that use tools, follow paths, keep schedules, and appear to mourn.

The lowland is inhabited by things that have learned to belong to unfinished ground.

They are damp, patient creatures, most of them. Their bodies do not hurry ahead of the land. They move as if erosion were a law of etiquette. A foot is placed only after the mud has agreed to receive it. A mouth opens only when the water has risen to the proper height. Even the hunters wait so long that predation seems less like violence than a delayed adjustment in the drainage.

The smallest residents are almost the most numerous: larvae in the black seams, translucent worms threaded through mineral crust, mites grazing on the skins of fungi, beetles that polish exposed clay grain by grain. They are the first surveyors of new land. When a shelf of silt lifts from the basin, they find it before the reeds do. They enter the cracks while the edges are still sharp and taste the new strata: salt, ash, iron, dead root, blue dust, old current. Their bodies carry the first softness into the hard places. Behind them come films of copper algae, pale root hairs, fungal milk, eggs.

The reed beds are full of small warm animals that are difficult to classify because none of them has settled on a final ancestry. They have whiskers like marsh rodents, damp skins like amphibians, small clever paws, and the habit of freezing completely when a pipe knocks. Some have fur that grows in directional bands to shed condensation into their mouths while they sleep. Some carry soft shells under the skin of their backs, plates that rise and fall with breathing. They make nests not only from reeds but from wire insulation, old tags, shed insect wings, and the thin curled skins of dried clay. Their tunnels braid through root-mats, gas pockets, and cable runs. They know which buried lines are warm. They raise their young near them.

There are crabs with soft, almost mammalian faces that live under concrete lips. They come out when the upward rain forms on bridge undersides, lifting their claws to drink from droplets hanging the wrong way. There are blind fish in the channels, pale along the belly and black along the spine, moving in files as if guided by a map laid under the mud. They surface together, not to breathe, but to read pressure changes with their backs. Eels inhabit the pipes like thoughts in an old system. They gather beneath transformer slabs, knotting and unknotting when the insulators tick. Some are scarred by current. Some glow faintly at the gills, not with light but with a remembered voltage.

The birds are the visible aristocracy of the place. Tall, folded, paper-pale, they walk through the shallows without troubling the surface. Their heads tuck and unfold with mechanical restraint. Their eyes are wide and dark and do not reflect, because reflection here is dangerous and they have given it up. They nest in conveyor frames, derrick elbows, signal housings, and the bowls of broken lamps. They build with reed stems, cable hair, bone splinters, and strips of translucent hide shed by larger things. When the bell strikes, they all lower their heads at once. Not in fear. In recognition.

The grazers are heavy, low creatures with tusks worn flat from pressing into mud. They travel in herds between reed stands, mineral licks, warm seeps, and places where the ground hums below hearing. Their hides are dark, slick, and furrowed with pale fungal cartography. Each animal carries on its flanks a changing map of where it has lain, what vents it has slept beside, what spores have found it useful. They do not graze continually. They stand for hours with their mouths sealed to the ground, teeth sunk into the wet crust, listening through their jaws to water movement and machine sleep. When one lifts its head, black mud strings from its tusks like torn roots.

Their calves are born soft and almost transparent, with blue veins visible under the hide. For the first weeks they are kept near old railbeds, where the metal below the silt holds warmth evenly. The herd circles them during pressure changes. If a false reflection rises from a pool, the adults turn their bodies to block the young from seeing it.

Behind the herds comes the tall one.

There may be more than one, but the darkness makes plurality uncertain. It has the height and narrowness of a derrick, the deliberate step of a wading bird, and the patience of abandoned machinery. Its limbs are jointed too high. Its feet are broad and careful, made for distributing weight across crusts that would swallow heavier confidence. Along its back stand spars or spines that resemble folded crane arms. In some positions it looks built; in others, starved.

It follows the grazers at a distance. It does not rush them. Sometimes it lowers its head and touches its mouthparts or tools or feelers to the hoofprints they leave. Sometimes it opens the mud with one thin forelimb and removes something small: a grub, a wire knot, a tooth, a hot stone. If it kills, it does so rarely. Old grazers leave the herd and walk toward the central facility; the tall one often accompanies them, neither driving nor guarding, simply keeping pace. Strips of its shed skin hang from pylons, where fungus enters them and slowly lights their shape from within. In that afterglow one can see that it is not a machine pretending to be an animal, nor an animal imitating a machine, but a third patience learned from both.

There are also the maintenance dwellers.

They are not often seen directly. Their presence is made of corrected tools, reclosed doors, ladders wiped clean of algae, blank calendar pages turned, valves marked with fresh scratches. They live in the raised huts, or beneath them, or in the warm crawlspaces between foundation and mud. They are human-shaped when glimpsed at the edge of a dead lamp’s amber intention, but not reliably human-sized. Some are too stooped, some too narrow, some wear masks that may be faces hardened by condensation.

They keep the old systems in partial obedience. Not repaired. Never restored. Only persuaded to continue their long, intermittent gestures. A gauge is tapped until its needle remembers trembling. A sump pump is fed a belt of woven eel-skin and copper wire. A signal lens is polished though no light passes through it. They stencil numbers on tanks that already have numbers, adding suffixes for states of being: again, under, after, not-yet. They do not speak loudly. When they count steps on walkways, they mouth the numbers without sound, because they know what listens one number behind.

Their tools are old tools altered by the marsh: wrenches with reed-wrapped handles, plumb lines weighted by teeth, voltmeters that also test blood warmth, folded maps kept in waterproof skins. They eat eggs, fungi, blind fish, and cakes made of mineral algae scraped from runoff lips. They drink from upward droplets when ordinary water is reflecting too much.

Whether they are descendants of workers, memories of workers, or organs extruded by the facility to tend itself is not clear. They behave like custodians, but also like livestock. Sometimes they leave offerings at vents: broken insulators, cleaned bones, the shed skins of tall ones, bundles of annotated tags. Sometimes the old machinery answers by turning one increment in the dark.

The central works has its own inhabitants, though perhaps they are not separate from it.

Things move inside the tanks and gantries where no animal willingly goes until age makes it curious. Damp sorting sounds come from within: soft impacts, sliding membranes, sediment poured through screens, shells arranged by size, roots combed, bones rinsed, small metal parts classified according to an order older than rust. There are chambers where landscapes may have been grown. There are belts that carry handfuls of basin from darkness to deeper darkness. There are transparent tanks full of black water in which unfinished shorelines curl and settle, each with its own reeds, its own insects, its own first hoofprints.

The beings in those inner rooms may be operators, or embryos, or the facility’s thoughts made wet enough to handle objects. They may have no stable bodies. They may inhabit gloves, pumps, valves, cattle skulls, maps, condensation trails. When something new rises from the mud—a sign, a bone, a rail switch, a species—the central works seems to notice. Not immediately. But eventually. A channel bends. A bell rings. A maintenance hut appears closer than before.

The whole lowland is inhabited, finally, by arrangements.

A ruin becomes a den. A den becomes a pump intake. A pump intake becomes an eel road. An eel road becomes a migratory calendar. A grazer’s fungal hide becomes a map. A map becomes too accurate and starts predicting seepage. A pool becomes an aperture. A reflection becomes an animal. An animal becomes part of the water system. A machine becomes weather. Weather becomes instruction.

Nothing here lives alone inside its skin.

The residents of this place are all compounds: beast and mud, tool and habit, fungus and memory, electricity and migration, worker and shrine, predator and scaffold, landscape and experiment. They are quiet because loudness belongs to finished worlds. They are slow because the land is still deciding its rules, and to move too quickly would be to choose a shape before the basin has chosen one for you.

They live by listening downward.

They drink from concrete ceilings. They nest in broken current. They follow warmth instead of light. They avoid pools that show the future unless they are ready to enter it. They accept that a path may revise itself, that a building may contain a smaller previous version of itself, that a bell without a ringer still deserves obedience.

And over all of them, under all of them, through their mouths and burrows and wires and eggs, lives the young land itself: black, warm, layered, crowded, not innocent, still rising by inches from whatever made it. It is not a backdrop to its inhabitants. It is their oldest body. It sorts them slowly into use.
