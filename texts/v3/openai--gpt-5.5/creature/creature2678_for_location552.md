What lives there is not a species so much as a covenant.

The oldest inhabitant is the plateau itself, though it did not begin as alive. It became alive slowly, by being entered and rewritten. Mineral-eating roots learned the pressures of stone; conductive vines learned the moods of the upper air; mats of black and silver tissue spread so thin across the tableland that they seemed at first like stain, then weather, then intention. Over ages those organisms grew into streets, walls, towers, nerves. The city is not their shelter. It is their body.

It thinks in tension, charge, scent, and branching.

Its thoughts are not quick in the way animal thoughts are quick. A tower may spend a century deciding to lean. A district may dream for nine seasons before changing the shape of a door. But its reflexes are immediate. It feels footsteps through root bridges. It hears wingbeats through static hairs. It knows when a herd is approaching by the minute rearrangement of dust. It can lock a passage, soften a plaza, wake a transit vine, hide a predator, betray one, remember a voice, or take the outline of a citizen’s shadow and file it inside a seed.

Its leaves are mostly not leaves. They are plates, antennae, vanes, membranes, blades of mica-fiber, black needles, hollow shells, and frilled mouths that taste salts on the wind. Its flowers are instruments. Some bloom only during electrical weather, opening copper throats to catch charge. Some are legal documents. Some are alarms. Some are graves.

The plants here do not thirst. They hunger for difference: between charged and uncharged, warm and cold, salt and salt, pressure and release, memory and forgetting. They feed by making gradients vanish. They grow by finding tension in the world and slowly spending it.

Among them move the citizens.

They are descended from people, perhaps, though the old word fits them poorly. Their bodies are narrow and carefully sealed, made less for abundance than retention. They carry their inner moisture as a miser carries gold: guarded behind membranes, reflective wrappings, vapor-lock collars, capillary skins. Their faces, when uncovered, are dry and fine-boned, with dark eyes enlarged against the dimness. Their voices are used sparingly, not from silence as virtue, but because sound is a material here and can be caught, misread, rooted, or replayed by the city.

Many of them are partly grown.

Children are grafted early with symbiotic fibers that help regulate heat and vapor. Adults wear living mantles that stiffen against wind, drink static, and whisper infrastructure reports along the spine. The very old often cease to move much. They take posts in archive groves, allowing black-needle computation to braid through their nervous systems until their memories can be flowered, copied, or sown.

They are not gardeners in the old sense. No one waters anything. They tune.

A citizen’s work may be to prune a false inference from a logic tree, to comb conductive dust from the throat of a civic organism, to persuade a bridge out of sleep, to graft a new route through a district that has become too inward, to teach a transit vine the mass-pattern of a new vehicle, to harvest antique voices from seed vaults, or to stand beneath a measuring tower and offer their shadow as calibration.

Their politics are botanical and acoustic. A law may be a scent released at dawn. A treaty may be a flowering sequence. A verdict may be a change in the vibration of a public floor. Disobedience is not always punished by force. Sometimes a doorway forgets you. Sometimes a street never hardens beneath your feet. Sometimes every moth in a plaza turns its copper-veined wings toward you at once.

They revere dryness, but not emptiness. Waste is a profanity. Spillage is catastrophe. Solvent is treated like fire. Breath is counted. Sweat is shameful unless given ceremonially to the dead districts, where white growth accepts the last damp traces of a body and mineralizes them into civic memory.

Their dead do not vanish underground. They are incorporated.

A beloved engineer may become a ribbed colonnade that strengthens during storms. A singer may be stored in a charge-blooming tree whose branches hum only when the upper atmosphere rises. A child may be kept in a seed no larger than a tooth, dormant in a cold vault until some future crisis requires the exact shape of their laughter. Ancestors are not metaphorically present. They are load-bearing.

Around these citizens lives a dry abundance of animals.

There are wall-grazers, angular and patient, with thin legs like jointed tools and mouths plated in ceramic. They scrape films from bark-stone, leaving behind pale calligraphic trails. Their stomachs are little furnaces of mineral chemistry. Their dung is powder, prized by certain civic roots.

There are static moths, some small as hands, some broad as cloaks. By day they hang folded against towers, indistinguishable from torn translucent banners. At charge-rise they open, and their wings fill with blue veins. They court in silence, writing brief equations in the air with their bodies. The city uses them as messengers when it does not trust the wind.

There are clicking arthropods in the transit joints, lacquer-black and many-eyed, custodians of contact surfaces. They clean conductive channels, eat parasitic growth, polish signal nodes with their mouthparts, and warm themselves against pulses meant for transport. They are tolerated, licensed, and occasionally elected by accident when they infest a voting branch.

There are antlered browsers, tall and pale, moving with the caution of things too large to hide but wise enough not to hurry. Their antlers are not bone alone; they are mineral aerials, grown in branching lattices that sense charge and distant movement. They feed on high silver foliage and shed their crowns in designated plazas, where citizens collect the antlers for weather instruments.

There are ribbon-hunters, almost flat when they wish to be, slipping through elevated rootways without sound. Their bodies are long bands of muscle, dark on one side and mirror-pale on the other, so that they become either shadow or glare according to need. They do not pant. They do not bleed red. When injured they give off resin and a bitter electric smell. They hunt by reading the city’s tremors, unless the city chooses to lie.

There are blind under-stone animals with skulls like wedges and limbs like folded springs. They move through cracks that are not quite tunnels, following stress, compression, and the minute argument between layers of ancient salt. They emerge before quakes, before tower-shears, before certain civic decisions. Their appearance in a plaza is considered an omen, though whether of danger or governance depends on which way their heads are turned.

There are smaller lives beyond counting: glass-voiced insects, dust nematodes, pollen symbionts, mites that live only inside signal flowers, burrowing mathematic colonies that chew branching diagrams into dead bark, silver fleas on the hides of browsers, tiny winged scavengers that descend after a kill and rise again glittering with salts.

Predators here are clean. Scavengers are sacred. Nothing soft remains soft for long.

But the most powerful inhabitant may be the city’s distributed mind: the old vegetal intelligence that is neither servant nor ruler, neither wild nor tame. It contains the citizens as organs contain blood, as forests contain paths, as memory contains names. It has agreements with herds, grudges against certain wind directions, inherited preferences about tower spacing, and something like affection for the voices it has stored.

It does not love quickly. It does not forgive quickly either.

When storms come and the upper air thickens with charge, all inhabitants enter their proper roles. Citizens close their skins and gather under civic crowns. Moths wake. Browsers kneel or stand perfectly still. Arthropods retreat into warm joints. Predators flatten along rootways. The plants lift their spines by the kilometer. The whole plateau becomes attentive.

Then the city sings.

In that singing, every life there is briefly part of one instrument: plant, animal, citizen, ancestor, machine, law, stone. Spores and engineered motes rise into the charged dark. Towers answer towers. Roots tighten. Bones in colonnades vibrate with remembered bodies. Seeds choose their routes through the air.

What lives there has learned not to flow, but to conduct.

Not to drink, but to listen.

Not to rot, but to be rewritten.

It is a dry civilization of sealed descendants, mineral plants, electric animals, incorporated dead, and one vast patient organism that has made architecture out of hunger and memory out of dust.
