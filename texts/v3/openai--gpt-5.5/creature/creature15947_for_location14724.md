What lives there is not a single creature, though at first the hollow may give the impression of one.

It is inhabited by the small, the damp-skinned, the soft-footed, the things that do not need distance. Life there has given up height and speed in exchange for closeness. It has flattened itself against stone, folded itself under leaves, gone pale in the dark, grown feelers, pads, suckers, moist bellies, delicate roots. Nothing in the hollow is built to cross open ground. Nothing is made for pursuit across a field or flight above a valley. Its inhabitants belong to crevices, undersides, films of water, the cramped shelter between moss and rock.

The salamanders are among the plainest and most complete expressions of the place. They lie where water has polished the stone black, their bodies low and cool, their skin the color of soaked bark, old slate, bruised lichen. They seem less like animals resting on the rock than thoughts the rock has had about becoming animal. Only the slow expansion of the throat, the minute lift of a foot, the black bead of an eye proves otherwise. Some are dark as wet iron; some are clouded milk-white beneath, as if the little light of the hollow has settled under their skin. They move rarely, and when they do, they do not cross the place so much as continue along it.

The frogs are small, pale, and secretive. They sit in hollows of moss as if placed there by condensation. Their limbs are thin but not fragile; their toes spread delicately on slick stone. Their calls, when they make them, are not songs but single drops of sound: a note like water touching water, round and brief. Many do not call at all. They swallow gnats, mites, soft larvae, the smallest winged things that rise from the wet and fail to find any open air. Their eggs cling in clear beads beneath leaves and in the quiet rims of pools, each bead holding a comma of future motion.

There are newts in the channels, flatter-headed and darker than the salamanders, shaped by the shallow runnels they occupy. They wait where the film of water thickens just enough to carry prey. Larvae drift to them. Tiny crusted things loosen from algae and vanish into their mouths. They are patient hunters, but not dramatic ones. Their violence is almost indistinguishable from the place’s ordinary settling: a tilt, a pulse, an absence where something had been.

The invertebrates are the true population.

Snails inhabit the hollow in numbers too great to count. They gather beneath broad leaves, along the undersides of fallen branches, in seams of stone where mineral and moss meet. Their shells are small, translucent brown, amber, grey-green, sometimes pale as old fingernail. They eat the skins of decay from every surface. Their paths shine briefly and then are taken back by the wet. They are not visitors to the place but part of its glazing, one of the ways moisture learns to move.

Worms live in the blackness below the moss mats, in soil that is more rot than earth. They thread through root hair and leaf pulp, making passages that immediately begin to close behind them. Their bodies are pink, brown, bluish, almost colorless, depending on how near the stone they live. They have no need of open air. They turn death without ceremony, so steadily that nothing ever appears newly dead for long.

There are beetles shaped like seeds, shields, old drops of resin. Some are dull black and ridged; some are bronze only when a thread of weak light finds them; some are blind or nearly blind, their eyes reduced by generations of dusk. They wedge themselves under bark and stone lips. Their legs move with careful mechanical certainty. A few are predators, taking mites and soft-bodied young. Others graze fungal bloom and lichen film. Their armor is always wet, never gleaming dry, and when they vanish into cracks they seem to have returned to a form of mineral life.

Mites move everywhere, but most visibly across pale lichen, where their red bodies look like sparks that have learned silence. They gather and disperse in constellations. They follow grooves invisible to larger eyes. They occupy the scale of the hollow where every grain of rot is a hill, every moss stem a trunk, every droplet a hanging lake.

There are flies, but not the loud, frantic kind of warmer places. These hover in thin, disciplined columns near the pools or beneath the overhang, rising and lowering by inches. Their wings make almost no sound. They hatch from wet seams, from submerged rot, from slick pockets in fallen wood. They live briefly, mate briefly, vanish into mouths or water or darkness. Their whole civilization is a trembling vertical line in the dim air.

Spiders own the intervals between things. Their webs are strung where drip and passage are both predictable: between fern ribs, under root arches, across the sheltered mouths of stone cracks. Dew makes their work visible, though only in fragments. Some spiders wait in funnels. Some flatten themselves against bark. Some are long-legged and nearly transparent, with bodies like tiny seeds caught in thread. They do not dominate the hollow. Nothing dominates here. They simply hold certain empty spaces until the air gives them food.

Moths sleep on trunks and stone faces, wings closed into patterns of rot, bark, leaf scar, mineral stain. Even when present in numbers they disappear into texture. At night, if night can be distinguished from the hollow’s usual dusk, they stir and move low among the ferns, never rising far. Their larvae eat mosses, lichens, softened leaves, the tender green edges of things growing in shade. Some caterpillars are furred with condensation; some are smooth and green-black; some resemble broken fern tips until they bend.

The birds are few but constant. Small, round-bodied, dark-feathered, they belong to the hush as thoroughly as the moss does. Their nests are hidden in fern crowns, root hollows, shelves of moss pressed against stone. They use fine roots, down, dead grass, hair shed by small mammals, the dry inner threads of bark softened by damp. Their eggs are speckled like lichen. They do not waste song. A single note may be enough to mark presence, warning, mate, territory, weather. They hunt by attention rather than speed, placing their feet with exquisite care, turning over a leaf without disturbing the leaf beside it.

The mammals are almost rumor. A shrew-like thing moves beneath the litter, narrow-snouted, frantic only in its heartbeat. It hunts worms, larvae, tiny beetles, the eggs of other small lives. Its fur is dark and always damp at the tips. It sleeps in root pockets lined with shredded moss and old feather. There may be a vole-like animal too, heavier, warmer, with blunt teeth and black eyes, eating stems, seeds, fungus, softened bark. It leaves tunnels under the moss that do not look like tunnels, only slight liftings in the green skin of the ground. When it breathes in its hidden chamber, a faint mist gathers and is taken back by the cold.

Fungi live there not as eruptions but as an atmosphere of decomposition. They lace the fallen wood, the buried leaves, the dead roots, the old nests. Their fruiting bodies appear like ears, cups, tongues, little shelves, beads of white or ochre or bruised violet. Some are nearly transparent. Some swell and collapse before any eye can properly notice them. Mycelium threads through everything the way memory threads through an old mind. If the hollow has a digestion, it is fungal.

The plants are not passive background. They are residents with long, gripping lives. Moss is the great citizenry, the most numerous and patient body. It carpets, pads, drinks, remembers. It receives droppings, eggs, corpses, seeds, feathers, shed skins, fallen spores, and makes no distinction between gift and ruin. Liverworts lie pressed to stone like green scales on an ancient animal. Ferns unfold slowly, holding light in thin divided hands. The shrubs are twisted not from struggle alone but from consent; they have accepted the low ceiling, the cold breath, the shallow soil. Their roots do not descend so much as embrace.

Even the pools are inhabited in layers. On their surfaces live films: algae, pollen, oil from decaying leaves, the faint tremor of microscopic bodies. Below are larvae, threads of green growth, small pale swimmers, eggs attached to submerged stems. At the bottom, black silt holds the slow work of rot. Sometimes a bubble rises, not urgently, and breaks without sound. Something underneath has shifted, fed, exhaled, or died.

If there is a presiding inhabitant, it is not a beast hiding in the deepest recess, nor a spirit with eyes among the stones. It is the arrangement itself: a many-bodied, cold-breathing life made of all the lives that have agreed, without knowing it, to remain. The hollow’s creature is collective. Its skin is moss. Its bones are wet rock. Its blood is seepwater. Its breath is mist. Its nerves are roots, fungal threads, beetle paths, the tremor of larvae in film. Its thoughts are slow enough to be mistaken for stillness.

It does not speak because nothing in it needs to carry a message far.

It does not hurry because nothing in it can be improved by arriving sooner.

It does not expand because it has already filled the shape given to it.

What lives there, finally, is a community that has become almost anatomical: each organism lodged in its proper fold, each hunger answered by another’s excess, each death immediately entered by roots, mouths, spores, and damp. No single life is large enough to define the hollow, and no life is separate enough to be merely itself. To live there is to be held against other lives at all times, to be touched by water, shadow, stone, and breath until the boundary between body and place grows uncertain.

The hollow is crowded with creatures, yes. But more deeply, it is inhabited by continuity: life that has stopped reaching outward and begun to thicken, layer by layer, into a dark, wet, patient completeness.
