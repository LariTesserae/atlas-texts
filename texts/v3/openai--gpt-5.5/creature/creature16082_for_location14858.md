What lives there is not a people, not a nation, not a civilization in the human sense. It is a population of arrangements.

The basin is inhabited by builders without architecture, gardeners without intention, road-makers without memory of roads. Its creatures are mostly small or careful, and even the larger ones behave as if they are guests inside something older and more densely occupied than themselves. Nothing here owns open space. Nothing crosses the flats as if the flats belong to it. Every animal enters a corridor, a shadow, a scent-path, a brief permission.

The first inhabitants are the plants, though they do not seem passive enough to be called plants for long.

They are hoarders of water that almost never appears. Their bodies are swollen, folded, waxed, ridged, buried, thorned, silver-haired. Many keep most of themselves underground: pale caudices like buried gourds, root-knuckles packed with starch and brine, long mineral-tasting cords that follow cracks down into cooler darkness. Aboveground they offer only what they can afford to lose: a spray of hard leaves, a cage of spines, a few translucent pads that redden under stress, a fan of blue-grey blades set edgewise to the sun.

Some grow as low, crowded carpets over salt crust, each leaf no larger than a scale, each root hair wrapped around grains of mineral. These mat-plants are the basin’s first skin. They bind powder, catch dust, slow wind, and make the beginning of soil. When crushed, they smell sharp and metallic, like cut cucumber rinsed in alkali.

Others rise as bottle-stemmed shrubs, thick at the base and narrowing into thorny crowns. Their bark peels in papery curls that insects collect for nest lining. Their flowers open at dusk, not brightly at first, but with a waxen pallor that gathers moths out of the air. By morning the flowers have collapsed into tight knots, already turning toward seed.

There are thornwoods that grow in social tangles. A single plant is difficult to name inside them; stems root where they touch, old branches die into lattice, seedlings rise in the protection of their parents’ dead defenses. They form roofs, walls, and rooms. In their interiors live whole communities that may never cross twenty paces of exposed ground in their lives.

There are also the salt-tolerant succulents, glossy and strange, with leaves like tongues of dark glass or pale green coins. Some are grazed constantly and survive by growing faster at the base than mouths can strip them. Some are poisonous except for a few hours after flowering. Some exude bitter resin that hardens into amber tears, later gnawed by beetles and licked by rodents for trace minerals.

The insect people—if any inhabitants deserve the word “people” by abundance alone—live everywhere.

The tower-makers dominate the skyline. They are not one species but many: pale-bodied termites with amber heads, black mandible ants that carry clay pellets under their jaws, blind wax-builders that rarely see the outer air, red dust-ants that surface only at dawn and dusk. Their cities are inside the city. Each tower is less a building than a lung, nursery, granary, fortress, fungus-bed, waste heap, and weather engine.

In the cool inner chambers are queens swollen into egg-laying organs, kings reduced to attendants, workers passing mouthfuls of paste, soldiers wedging their armored heads into tunnels. There are fungal gardens kept at exact warmth. There are seed vaults sorted by size. There are grave rooms where dead colony members are sealed behind mineral partitions. There are wet rooms, astonishingly humid by the basin’s standards, where chewed plant fiber ferments under constant care.

Many other lives depend on these towers. Slender snakes coil in abandoned vents. Blue lizards lay eggs in sun-warmed outer cracks. Parasitic flies enter disguised by chemical mimicry. Tiny beetles live only in termite refuse chambers, feeding on what the colony has already digested and discarded. Roots invade old towers, not killing them all at once but negotiating with them, splitting galleries into terraces, drawing minerals from walls while leaving enough structure standing to cast shade.

The mound insects are joined by solitary makers. Potter wasps fasten thumb-sized clay jars beneath overhangs. Leaf-cutting bees stitch chambers inside dead stems. Trapdoor spiders line vertical tubes with silk and mineral dust, their lids indistinguishable from the surrounding crust. Ant-lions wait at the bases of powder slopes. Resin beetles drill into shrub wounds and leave behind hardened dark beads, which other insects later use as shelter.

The basin’s pollinators are quiet and precise. Small bees with pale fur move through morning flowers like drifting ash. Moths with translucent wings emerge at evening and hover before white blossoms, their long tongues probing deep tubes. There are beetles that sleep inside flowers and depart powdered in pollen at dawn. There are minute flies that breed in dung, in fungal chambers, in the moist inner folds of plants, and carry pollen accidentally on their bristling legs.

The reptiles are among the most visible animals because they share the temperature of the place.

Flat lizards live on the tower walls. Their bodies are wide, their toes fringed, their bellies pale as salt. They move in short, decisive bursts, then freeze with one eye turned toward the sky. They eat ants, moths, soft-bodied larvae, sometimes the petals of certain flowers. When the sun is fierce they darken to absorb or shed heat differently across their bodies, becoming mottled like old clay.

Spined lizards haunt the thorn labyrinths. They wear the basin’s architecture on their backs: little defensive towers, ridges, hooked scales that make them hard to swallow. When threatened, they wedge themselves into cracks and inflate, becoming part of the wall.

There are glass-eyed geckos that hunt by night, almost transparent beneath the moonless glow of warm stone. Their foot pads cling to clay chimneys, smooth succulent leaves, even the hard salt plates. They click softly to one another in the evenings, one of the few deliberate voices in the basin.

The snakes are patient residents of thresholds. Some are thin as roots and patterned like dried grass, moving through rodent runs and bird nests. Some are heavy-bodied ambush hunters, dust-colored, lying half-buried where paths narrow between thorns. A few specialize in towers, entering vents by night to take sleeping lizards, eggs, and termites. They know the cooling cycle of clay better than the mammals do.

Birds live above and within the roofs.

The weavers are the most architectural. They hang clustered nests from thornwood branches, hundreds together, woven from wire-grass and lined with plant down. Their colonies look like weathered fruit or suspended villages. They quarrel softly, constantly, with dry chirps and wing-flicks. Their nest entrances face away from prevailing heat and dust. Old nests are never simply abandoned: geckos use them, then wasps, then beetles, then wind.

Hook-beaked birds perch on termite chimneys and dead shrubs. They are narrow, long-tailed, patient, with eyes adapted to small motion. They take lizards, rodents, hatchlings, large insects. Some cache prey on thorns, leaving dried bodies like offerings around their territories.

Ground birds move like animated shadows under brush. They have strong feet, blunt claws, and beaks that pry open litter. They eat seeds, grubs, salt crystals, and occasionally the soft tips of new shoots. Their chicks are striped and vanish among stems almost as soon as they hatch. They do not call loudly. Their alarm is a low body-held tremor that passes through a group before any predator sees them scatter.

At the basin’s higher air are dust-swallows and moth-hawks, birds that rarely land except to nest in clay holes. They feed on insects lifted by thermals. Their silhouettes are among the few moving things visible at noon.

The mammals are mostly burrowers, threaders, and shade-users.

There are rodents in such abundance that they form one of the basin’s hidden foundations. Seed-mice with long hind feet run the narrow paths at dusk, cheeks full, tails held high from hot ground. They cache seeds in dry cells under roots and inside abandoned insect chambers. They forget enough of these caches to plant whole future thickets.

Larger gnawers live in family warrens beneath mound fields. Their teeth are orange with mineral hardness. They cut tough stems, strip bark, drag thorn branches to reinforce entrances. Their tunnels ventilate the soil, collapse old chambers, open paths for roots. Predators hunt them constantly, but the basin itself seems built partly by their fear.

Pig-snouted rooters work the dusk corridors. They are low, bristled animals with shovel faces and hard lips, able to nose aside salt crust and packed dust to reach tubers, grubs, buried seed pods. They do damage, but their damage is creative: a turned patch, a broken crust, a scatter of dung, a seed pressed into disturbed powder. Within days their feeding pits begin to acquire tenants.

Small antelope-like browsers live in the thorn districts. They are fine-legged, narrow-shouldered, with coats the color of shade on dust. Their horns, when present, are short and swept back so as not to catch in branches. They move in lines rather than herds, each animal stepping where the one before stepped. They browse waxy leaves, kneel for succulents, lick mineral blooms from exposed crust. Their young spend their first weeks nearly scentless, folded into thorn shade.

There are also long-eared night hares that feed on mat-plants and flowers, and tiny shrews that burn through insect life like sparks. Bats roost in the deep cracks of old towers and emerge when evening flowers open, taking moths and sometimes pollinating the larger bottle-stem blooms with their dusty faces.

The predators are not rulers. They are specialists in attention.

The long cats are the largest hunters, though even they are not large in the way of open-country cats. They are built for alleys: low shoulders, flexible spines, paws soft enough for crust, coats that match warm clay and dead grass. By day they sleep where two shade systems overlap—mound shadow and thorn shadow, root hollow and wall base. At dusk they wake slowly, listening first. Their hunting is not a chase but an interception. They know where a rodent path tightens, where a browser must lower its head, where a lizard will bask after evening cooling. They appear from the city’s edges as if extruded by shade.

Foxlike animals patrol the rooflines of thorn hedges. They leap from branch to mound to fallen shrub, avoiding the ground when scent might betray them. Their muzzles are narrow enough to raid nests. They eat eggs, insects, mice, fruit, carrion, anything small enough to vanish quickly. Their dens are often in collapsed termite towers, which they enlarge just enough to fit their bodies and no more.

There are civet-like scent hunters, mostly nocturnal, striped in broken grey and black. They follow odor through the dry air with astonishing fidelity: a beetle colony under bark, a sleeping bird, a cache of seeds, a dead thing hidden two walls away. Their droppings become important signposts, rich in undigested seeds and minerals.

Scavengers are everywhere because nothing remains unclaimed. Ants arrive first in lines so fine they seem drawn with a needle. Beetles follow, then flies, then birds, then furred opportunists. A body does not rot into softness here. It is opened, dried, carried away, incorporated. Hide becomes fiber in a nest. Bone becomes tooth-wear, calcium, powder. Horn becomes a mineral lick. Even the shadow cast by the carcass changes which seedlings survive beneath it.

Underground lives another population, less visible but perhaps more decisive.

Roots braid with fungi in the cracks. Some fungi wait years as dry threads, then awaken in the faint humidity of a sealed chamber or beneath a dung pellet. They trade minerals for sugars, carry chemical warnings between plants, digest dead cellulose into usable dust. Their fruiting bodies are rare and brief: hard little buttons under litter, black fans on dead stems, pale threads inside insect walls.

Bacteria live in salt films, in root sheaths, in the guts of termites, in the mineral crust itself. They change the chemistry of the new earth. They make some poisons tolerable, some salts mobile, some nutrients available. In the absence of rivers, they are among the basin’s slowest waters: moving elements through bodies instead of channels.

Mites, springtails, nematodes, pseudoscorpions, and blind white insects fill the micro-city beneath each handful of litter. Every dry leaf is a district. Every pellet of dung is a temporary feast hall. Every crack is a vertical province graded by heat, salt, air, and danger.

What lives there, finally, is a way of living.

The inhabitants are drought-shaped, corridor-minded, heat-literate. They conserve sound as they conserve water. They build by eating and excreting, by nesting and abandoning, by dying in useful positions. They do not clear ground; they occupy it. They do not seek openness; they make shelter out of obstruction. They are citizens of shade, salt, thorn, clay, and timing.

No single creature understands the basin. The termite does not know it is making a skyline. The shrub does not know it is roofing a street. The rodent does not know it is planting a district. The cat does not know its hunting path is also a road, polished by generations of feet. Yet together they make a place so inhabited that wilderness and city become the same word.

The thing that lives there is the crowd itself: innumerable bodies, each small need pressed against the next, turning young mineral ground into a dry, breathing metropolis.
