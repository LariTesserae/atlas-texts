What lives there is not a wilderness but a tenancy.

The first residents you notice are the birds, because they occupy the city’s upper surfaces as if those surfaces were made for them. Gulls stand on parapets in rows, each one facing the same dull wind. Terns cut through the vapor above the canals, sharp and white, vanishing and reappearing against tanks and cranes. Cormorants open their wings on warm pipes, black silhouettes drying in the industrial heat. Pigeons crowd inside rafters and broken louvers, softening the hard interiors with dust, feathers, and the low rolling sound of their bodies. Kites hang higher, patient and loose-winged, reading the basin for movement.

The birds have made a cliff country out of the tanks and warehouses. They know which roofs hold heat longest after sunset, which substations are safe from dogs, which gantries vibrate before men arrive, which outfalls bring fish near the surface. Their nests are built from cable ties, packing twine, wire, shredded insulation, plastic strip, dry rags, and the occasional true twig carried in from somewhere beyond the basin, rare enough to look ceremonial. Eggs lie in corners of machinery housings. Chicks gape under corrugated eaves. Droppings turn ladders white and make maps down the curved sides of tanks.

They are not decorative. They are a working class of animals, loud when needed, silent when heat presses them flat. At dawn they inspect the waterlines, the fish wash, the trash racks, the flooded yards. At noon they retreat to shade or stand with open beaks, tolerating the metal shimmer. At dusk they become briefly sovereign. Flocks lift from every roof and railing, wheel over the canals, cross and recross the same air until the whole basin seems to have grown a second, restless ceiling made of wings.

Below them live the rats.

The rats are the city’s true engineers. They move through the infrastructure as if through ancestral tunnels: service trenches, cable runs, culvert mouths, broken drains, hollow dock edges, gaps beneath loading platforms. They know the dry lips above flood level and the warm seams beside conduits. They nest in shredded packaging, rags, foam, fibrous insulation, paper from old offices, the stuffing of abandoned chairs. Their bodies are dark, sleek, and heavy with opportunity.

They eat what the city sheds. Grain dust from silos. Fish scraps. Dead birds. Crab meat. Insects. Refuse from the workers’ strip. Food wrappers, eggs, grease, the soft bodies of anything stranded after water drops. They have learned the timing of shifts, tides, storms, and pump cycles. A storm sends them upward into cable trays and stairwells. A night of low water returns them to the canal margins. A silent warehouse may be empty to a person and crowded to them, full of whisker-touch, breath, urine marks, young hidden in warm dark spaces.

They do not swarm openly unless something has gone wrong. Usually they appear as evidence: a black line slipping along a wall, prints in damp dust, chew marks on conduit, a pause in the debris before it moves. They are the quick thought under the basin’s skin.

The dogs live more visibly but less intimately. They belong to the roads, the yards, the shaded undersides of parked machines. They are feral, though not entirely outside human life. Some have been fed by guards, welders, children, drivers. Some still carry collars bleached almost white. They travel in small groups with loose hierarchies, lean from heat but not starving. Their coats are the colors of the place: dun, rust, black, dirty cream. Their paws are hardened by concrete and gravel. They know which gates open, which trucks drop food, which alleys flood first, which men throw stones.

By day they sleep under tank stairs, beneath trailers, in the rectangular shadow of containers. They lie on their sides and breathe shallowly, ears working even in sleep. At evening they rise and patrol. They do not waste energy barking. A single warning bark, a lifted head, a low rush of bodies across a yard—then quiet again. They hunt rats when it is worth the effort, harass cats when bored, scavenge along the residential strip, and nose through the tide trash left at canal edges. During flood they stand on islands of slab or climb loading ramps and watch the water claim their routes without surprise.

The cats own the higher interiors. They are thinner, cleaner, more secretive than the dogs. They pass through broken windows, roof gaps, stairwells, ledges, cable trays, offices where damp paper curls in drawers. Their territories overlap vertically rather than horizontally: one cat may hold the mezzanine of a warehouse, another the top floor of a brick depot, another the warm transformer ledge behind a fence. They hunt pigeons, rats, lizards if any stray in from drier ground, and the dense insect life around lamps. They sleep in file cabinets, on old blankets, inside ceiling voids, in the heat pooled above human reach.

They have the look of creatures who have accepted the city’s sharpness. Torn ears, narrow faces, oil on the paws, eyes reflecting amber from sodium lights. They move through the basin like punctuation: a tail vanishing behind ductwork, a body balanced along a pipe, two eyes in a broken administrative window after dark.

At the waterline, the crabs are always at work.

They pick through trash racks and cracked concrete, testing everything with small precise claws. They climb where the wall is rough enough. They gather below outfalls, around drowned packaging, under the lips of quay stones. After storms they enter places not meant for them—underpasses, wheel ruts, warehouse thresholds—and when the water retreats, some remain stranded in shallow depressions, still combative, still raising their claws against the enormous dry world. Birds take many of them. Dogs crunch some. Rats worry them apart. The survivors find the canals again and resume their patient sorting of the city’s edge.

The fish are less seen than felt. They gather in the ambiguous water where heat, salt, oxygen, discharge, and tide make unstable promises. Near warm outfalls they mass in thick, flashing bodies, opening and closing their mouths in water that steams during rain. Some are sea fish drawn inland by tide. Some are hardy canal fish that tolerate oil sheen, silt, low oxygen, sudden changes. They rise for insects under lights. They scatter when pumps begin. In flooded streets they become briefly impossible citizens, silvering over lane markings, nosing at curb drains, passing beneath the reflections of loading cranes.

The insects are the basin’s nervous system.

Mosquitoes hatch in sumps, tire ruts, buckets, broken cisterns, bund water, roof depressions, inspection pits, any place where water can remain warm and still for long enough. Midges assemble over puddles and lamps in trembling columns. Cockroaches live in the warm seams of housing blocks, canteens, offices, switch rooms, and drains. Flies appear wherever fish, grease, carrion, or wet refuse opens itself. Moths batter themselves against security lights until bats take them. Ants are fewer than they should be in a greener world, but where they persist, they follow cracks in concrete and the dry edges under walls, exploiting the smallest crumbs of human habitation.

The bats live in expansion joints, culverts, cracked bridge undersides, roof voids, and the mouths of abandoned ducts. At dusk they pour out almost invisibly, stitched into the dimness. They hunt the insects that the water breeds and the lamps betray. Their world is a map of echoes: pipes, railings, canal walls, birds asleep in ranks, dogs moving below, the high open rectangles of yards, the long hard corridors between warehouses.

And then there are people.

People live there, but not as the dominant fact. They are maintenance crews, pump operators, dock workers, refinery hands, guards, welders, drivers, clerks in damp offices, families in the housing strip, children who grow up knowing flood depths the way other children know street names. They have learned the basin’s rules because the basin enforces them without drama.

They keep things dry by labor. They keep things moving by labor. They raise thresholds, clear drains, patch roofs, grease valves, replace cables gnawed by rats, hose droppings from ladders, reset pumps, repaint warning lines already fading under salt and heat. They walk on catwalks during floods and know not to trust shallow water over a yard, because a missing cover or sunken trench may be beneath it. They listen for machinery in the background the way others listen for weather.

The children know the animal city most intimately. They know which dogs can be approached and which cannot. They know where crabs collect after rain, where pigeons nest low enough to see, where cats have kittens, which canal smells wrong, which fence hums, which roof is warmest at evening. They play in a world of prohibitions and shortcuts: do not touch that pipe, do not step there, do not go below after a storm, do not corner a dog, do not drink from any tap outside the housing strip, do not put your hand into dark metal.

The old residents carry the basin in their bodies. Salt in the skin, damp in the joints, coughs that worsen in certain winds, heat habits so ingrained they no longer mention them. They can tell by smell whether rain is coming from the sea or from inland cloud. They recognize the tone of a pump beginning to fail. They remember tanks repainted three companies ago, canals dredged and silted again, warehouses full and then empty, bird colonies shifting after a roof collapsed. They have seen the city sink by centimeters and behave as if this were normal, because here it is.

There is also life too small or too hidden to be called absent. Mildew in stairwells. Bacteria in warm drains. Fungal blooms in wet paper and wallboard. Invisible films inside pipes and sumps. Not green life, not leafing life, but a damp, chemical, digestive life that works in stains, odors, rot, and slickness. It does not soften the basin. It darkens it. It loosens adhesives, spoils stored cloth, clouds standing water, makes sealed rooms smell inhabited long after people leave.

Together, these beings do not reclaim the place. They use it.

They do not turn it into forest, marsh, garden, or ruin. They accept its geometry. They inhabit right angles, drainage grades, ladders, roofs, culverts, bunds, canals, tanks, vents, and the shaded undersides of machines. Their ecology is not rooted in soil but in heat, shelter, tide, refuse, and repeated human effort. The animals depend on the city continuing to leak and warm and discard. The people depend on the systems continuing to function just enough. Everyone lives by edges: wet and dry, active and abandoned, hot and shaded, tidal and sealed, forbidden and useful.

What lives there, finally, is an occupation of the built world by bodies that have learned its habits.
