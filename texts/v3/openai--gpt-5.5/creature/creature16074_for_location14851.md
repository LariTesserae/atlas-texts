What lives there is not a crowd.

At first you would think the ward had only its machinery, its pale weeds, its occasional blind eel. Then you would notice the places where silt has been brushed from a railing by repeated hands. A route of clean metal beneath a factory window. A brick corner polished not by current but by bodies turning there, always turning there. The underside of a pipe scraped free of algae in narrow parallel marks, as if someone had passed with a tool held between teeth.

They are not seen before their habits are seen.

They are small enough, or careful enough, that the drowned town keeps them. Perhaps a dozen. Perhaps fewer. Counting them is difficult because they do not gather in open places, and because one may be mistaken for a hanging length of rubber hose until it bends its head and slips backward through a window frame.

They are descended from people, probably. That is the simplest answer, though not the most comfortable one. Their bodies still remember the human arrangement: head, shoulders, hands, spine, knees. But the water has revised them with the patience it used on the brickwork.

They are long and narrow now, built less for swimming than for moving through obstruction. Their shoulders are compressible. Their ribs show faintly through gray-white skin, not as bones under flesh but as struts under membrane. Their legs are thin and trailing, useful for bracing against walls, less useful for propulsion. The hands are the important part: broad, dark-palmed, webbed between the fingers only halfway, with hard callused pads at the tips. They can hold a rusted cable, a brick seam, the lip of a broken tile. They can read a district by touch.

Their faces are not fish faces. That would be too simple. They have human foreheads, flattened noses, mouths that close tightly and open seldom. The eyes are large but filmed, more silver than black. They do not see far. Nothing here sees far. Along their throats and under their arms are soft moving slits, assisted by little brass-and-rubber regulators scavenged and rebuilt so many times that no original part remains. Some breathe water. Some still need trapped air at intervals. The oldest carry pressure inside them like a second skeleton.

They wear fragments, but not clothes. Belts made of cable braid. Mesh pouches. Strips of old diving rubber tied around elbows and knees. Brass tags from machinery, not as ornament exactly, but as identity, warning, memory. A child may wear a pump label around its neck for years before learning what the old letters mean. The adults carry hooks of sharpened slate, bone needles, wire spools, lamps hooded in green glass so dim they seem ashamed of themselves.

They do not live in the houses as people once lived in them. A bedroom is no more intimate than a culvert if the current is right. A municipal office may be safer than a nursery if it has three exits and no intake pull. Their dwellings are chosen by pressure, warmth, and grip.

There is a roost beneath the market awning, among the white filament plants. There are hammocks of netting strung between the upper shelves of the old bakery, where the brick is pitted and easy to hold. There is a nursery in the pumping chapel, high in the arched dark, where a seam in the wall exhales warmth and the water tastes faintly metallic. The very young are kept there in mesh cradles, tethered among brass valves like pale fruit. They learn the chapel first: smooth tile, warm conduit, radial wheel, no-open-shaft, no-open-shaft, no-open-shaft.

Their speech is mostly contact.

A knuckle on iron means more than a shout would. Two taps along a pipe: wait. Three taps, spaced wide: current. A scrape: eel. A flat palm struck against brick: come here by this wall, not through the street. They can send meaning a long way through the old tram rails, but only in fragments. Names are not sounded. Names are patterns: a sequence of touches on the wrist, a pressure of fingers against the throat, a private rhythm knocked once into a doorframe and remembered.

They know the ward as a blind person knows a familiar room, but their room has hundreds of rooms inside it. They know which lamps are dependable and which ones tremble before a pressure change. They know where the water thickens in the turbine hall and must be crossed edge-first. They know that the archive cellar stains the skin and confuses direction. They know never to follow bubbles in the clocktower stairwell.

Their children are taught routes before stories.

Pitted brick: bakery lane.  
Smooth green tile: bathhouse corridor.  
Double cable braid: generators.  
Cold iron ladder with the missing third rung: do not descend.  
Blue halo trembling behind mesh: warmth nearby, danger nearer.  
Black moss under bridge: slow water, safe handhold.  
Hospital iodine taste: turn back.

They are scavengers, gardeners, mechanics, parasites, priests. The distinctions have thinned underwater.

They harvest the white filament plants from the old square by combing them with tools made from shutter slats. They scrape black moss from bridge undersides and press it into dark cakes that can be stored in sealed tins. They collect crustaceans from embossed factory doors, especially the letters where the tiny creatures gather thickly in the grooves. They trap translucent fish with loops of fine wire near amber lamps. They cultivate bacterial skins in window frames, stretching them like veils and returning later to peel away the edible layer.

Warmth is the nearest thing they have to wealth.

They do not hoard metal, except useful metal. They do not value gold, if any remains. But a room where the water is half a degree less cold may be guarded for generations. A cracked transformer cage, a service conduit, a pipe carrying some persistent chemical heat from below: these are hearths. Around them they gather silently, palms outward, throats fluttering, eyes half-lidded. They do not make fire. Fire is an ancestral rumor, impossible and extravagant. Warmth is something that leaks from the old world and must be found quickly before the weeds find it first.

They maintain the town, though not because they believe it can be restored.

They clear the pump grilles of weed and bone. They cut silt away from valve housings. They grease ancient hinges with rendered eel fat and mineral paste. They replace cracked lamp glass with panes salvaged from higher windows. They wedge broken doors open or closed according to pressures they understand better than numbers. When a cable begins to sing, one of them follows the vibration through three buildings and a collapsed passage until the strain is found and relieved.

This work is not organized like labor. It is closer to grooming. As animals clean their own fur, as tongues tend wounds, they tend the ward. The place is their body in the extended sense. A blocked intake makes them restless. A dead beacon changes their sleep. A pressure door stuck half-open is a pain felt collectively but wordlessly, passed by taps from rail to pipe to hand.

They fear open water.

Not water itself; water is the given world. They fear width. The middle of a street, the center of the market square, the old canal reach where the walls fall away and the current has room to decide. Their bodies are poor in suddenness. They survive by contact, by edges, by never being fully surrendered to the basin. In open places they cross low and fast, hand over hand along tram cables or beneath fallen signage, letting their legs stream behind them like torn cloth.

A young one who boasts may try to cross a wider channel without touching anything. The adults do not chase. Chasing would only add motion to danger. They wait with hands fixed to the wall. Sometimes the child reaches the far side and is cuffed hard enough to remember. Sometimes the water takes it sideways, almost gently, into a grille that was not visible from any angle that mattered.

They do not bury their dead. There is little soil, and falling is slow.

The dead are fastened in certain rooms where the water is coldest and least inhabited. Old offices, mostly. Rooms with desks still bolted to floors, shelves furred in mineral, windows filmed opaque. The bodies are wrapped in cable mesh and weighted with valve parts so they will not drift into work routes. Over time they become pale, then gray, then part of the wall’s accumulation. Crustaceans take the soft places. Moss maps the ribs. A brass tag remains longer than the face.

The living visit by touch. They press their hands to the mesh and feel what the water has done.

They have stories, but the stories have changed shape under pressure. They are not told in long sequences. They are assembled from route, gesture, taboo, and maintenance.

There is a story about the surface, but it is not a place of salvation. It is the gray lid, the crushing brightness, the skin-world where water ends and bodies fail. There is a story about the old town, but no one imagines it dry. In their oldest remembered version, the people of before were strange creatures who lived inside air pockets as large as streets, walking heavily on the floor and fearing leaks. There is a story that once the bell tower contained a bell. The children do not believe this. They know the tower contains breath.

They revere the regulating room, though reverence may be too human a word.

They do not enter it casually. They approach through the narrowest routes, one at a time, carrying no lamps brighter than a thumb-glow. At the threshold they pause and hold the iron rim until their pulses slow. Inside, the black plates rise around them furred with pale deposits. The great wheels wait on the walls. Chains descend without swinging. Every pipe has a voice there, muted but present.

The dwellers listen.

The oldest among them can place both hands on the central shaft and feel weather that has not yet arrived, though there is no sky here in any useful sense. They can feel a tide from outside the basin, a pressure debt moving through blocked culverts, an intake beginning to hunger. They do not command the wheels. They attend them.

Sometimes, when one of the wheels turns by itself, the inhabitants flatten against the iron and go utterly still. Their throat slits close. Their hands spread. The whole ward shifts around them: lamps trembling, silt lifting, corridors inhaling. Afterward they emerge slowly into the altered town, testing every familiar route as if the place has changed its mind.

Because it has.

The deepest truth is that they are not the only thing living there.

The ward itself lives, if living can mean continuation through exchange, response, memory, appetite. It breathes through pumps. It shivers through cables. It digests warmth into weed, weed into flesh, flesh into silt, silt into pressure-dark brick. Its thoughts are not thoughts but adjustments. Its moods are currents. Its dreams are old municipal cycles repeating after the reason for them has vanished.

The pale dwellers are its mobile organs, or its tenants, or its children. They would not separate these meanings. They repair it and are fed by it. They fear it and trust it. They know its safe bones and its hidden mouths. They are shaped like the routes through it: cramped, patient, cold, tactile, enduring.

If one comes near you, it will not rush.

It will appear first as a loosening of darkness behind a pipe. A hand will settle on a railing above your shoulder. Another hand will touch the brick beside your head, reading the vibration of your presence. Its face will tilt, silver eyes clouded, mouth closed against the water. Small bubbles may tremble at its throat apparatus and be swallowed back.

It will smell, faintly, of rust, mineral, eel fat, and old rubber.

It will not speak. It may touch the back of your glove with two fingers. It may test whether you know the handholds. It may decide whether you are debris, danger, animal, or temporary weather.

Then, with no more disturbance than a strip of cloth sinking, it will turn and pull itself away through the drowned brickwork, following a route invisible to anyone who still thinks streets are for walking.
