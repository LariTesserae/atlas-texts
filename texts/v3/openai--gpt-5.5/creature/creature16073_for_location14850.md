What lives there is not one species, and not quite a community in the surface sense. It is a damp arrangement of lives that have accepted the same terms.

The first inhabitant is the water.

It is older here than any family and more socially important than any official. It has addresses, habits, moods. It comes thinly down the walls, gathers at curbs, waits under grates, thickens in basins, moves through pipes no hand has opened in living memory. The people speak of it as if it were both weather and neighbor. Not “the canal is rising,” but “the east water is coming back.” Not “there is a leak,” but “the wall has started remembering.”

Some water is trusted. Some is worked around. Some is avoided with a politeness that resembles fear. Children learn early to read its surfaces: the skin of oil that means an old machine is bleeding, the trembling that means pressure behind the stones, the black unreflecting patch that means something below is drinking light. They learn which puddles can be stepped through and which must be greeted from the edge. They learn that a dry place in a wet street is not safety, but an event waiting to be understood.

The second inhabitant is the stone.

The limestone is not dead structure. It sweats, softens, blooms, refuses, permits. It closes around old bolts and releases new cracks. It tastes the pressure behind itself for years before giving way. It accepts roots as tenants and pipes as bones. The settlement has been built inside it, but not conquered from it. Every room is leased from stone under conditions that may change.

The inhabitants know this. They do not renovate in the surface manner. They patch, brace, persuade. They lay cork where the walls exhale too cold. They hang tar paper where mineral rain stains the beds. They do not knock down walls unless the wall has already begun to leave. When a new passage opens, no one says it was made. They say it arrived.

The machines live there too.

They are not alive in the way animals are, but they are not objects either. They have slept too long in the wet and been listened to too carefully. Their casings hold warmth after no fire has burned. Their gauges fog with private weather. Their wheels seize, then turn once in the night. The old pumping engine is treated with the regard given to an enormous infirm beast: dangerous, necessary, mostly dreaming, capable of changing the level of everyone’s floor with a single slow motion.

There are people assigned—though no one assigned them—to its moods. They clean the teeth of its gears with oil-black brushes. They clear pale growth from the piston rods. They place their palms against housings and listen through bone. They do not expect it to obey. They hope to understand what it is already doing.

The furnaces, though cold, are watched with particular caution. Their red pulses are not spoken of during meals. Some say the glow is coal remembering fire. Some say something below the settlement breathes against the grates. Some say all old industry leaves a heart behind, and that this one was too large to remove.

The animals are the most visible citizens.

White newts own the pipe-shadows. They flatten themselves behind iron brackets and blink through skin so pale it seems lit from underneath. Transparent shrimp keep the gutters clean of what the people cannot name. Black beetles travel in lines so orderly they look employed. Moths sleep in dead gauges, wing to glass, as if dreaming of pressure. Blind swallows pass through the rafters in clicking arcs, their nests made of lint, root hair, and labels peeled from old crates.

The eels in the canals are respected. They are not pets, not pests, not food except in bad years and with apology. They surface beside dock pilings and show backs patterned in brown, brass, and green, like old measuring instruments. Some are known individually by scars or by the way they coil beneath particular bridges. A child who sees the same eel three times before winter is considered likely to become a listener, pipe-worker, or map-reader.

The pale rats are clever, ragged, and ambitious. They fish badly but with conviction. They lower their tails into the canals as lures. They steal filter-charcoal, sleep in glove bins, and gnaw only the inessential parts of ledgers, as if they can read civic importance. The people tolerate them unless they enter shrines or nurseries. Rats that survive long enough become almost white, then almost blue, then vanish into higher pipes and are never trapped again.

The valve crabs are not tolerated so much as consulted.

They gather at pipe junctions where pressures meet and quarrel. Their shells are mottled like oxidized brass. Their legs are jointed too delicately for the weight of their bodies, yet they cling to wet metal with absolute authority. They tap. They answer one another through the pipes. Their codes predate most living repair-workers, though some elderly inhabitants claim to understand the simpler sequences: blockage, heat, bad air, rain coming, open nothing, run.

A valve crab moved from its junction will return unless prevented. If prevented, the pipe usually fails.

Bell frogs occupy telephones, switch boxes, speaking tubes, and once, famously, the empty chest cavity of a bronze statue in the Low Yard. Their calls are soft and internal. A receiver lifted at the wrong hour may contain only breath, or the wet click of a throat, or a distant chorus from instruments no longer connected to any exchange. Children dare each other to ask questions. The frogs sometimes answer, but never in language and never immediately. Their replies may arrive days later as ringing from a wall where there is no bell.

There is also the ceiling-walker.

No one describes it fully, which is partly custom and partly mercy. It moves at dusk above one avenue, between the lamp cages and the root curtains, placing many fine feet on pipes, stone ribs, conduit, rusted brackets. Its passage makes no sound except the faintest rearrangement of droplets. It is long enough that those who forget and look up cannot agree where it begins. It may be an animal. It may be a colony. It may be something that came down through a ventilation shaft and learned restraint.

The rule is simple: do not look up when it passes.

The people obey this with the calm seriousness they give all useful laws. They lower their eyes, adjust their bundles, continue along the raised walks. Dogs—where dogs are kept—are trained to look at their owners’ boots. Lamps gutter green for a moment. The ceiling-walker goes on.

As for the human inhabitants, they are not numerous, but they are deeply placed.

They are narrow-shouldered more often than not, pale in the way of things grown under cover, with hair that curls or hangs according to the damp. Their clothes are layered, patched, and waxed at the edges: canvas, wool, rubberized aprons, old uniform coats, knitted sleeves, boots repaired beyond original design. Many wear small tools on cords at the waist: valve keys, scraper hooks, chalk, folded mesh, listening rods, cork plugs, bells that are never rung unless dropped.

Their hands are distinctive. The nails are kept short. The fingertips are callused not from rough labor alone, but from testing surfaces: heat, vibration, slime, grit, the pulse in a pipe, the give of mortar before it fails. Their palms know the difference between condensation and warning. Children are praised less for strength than for noticing.

They live in converted industrial rooms, but conversion here does not erase the old purpose of a place. It layers over it.

A family in an office may sleep beneath filing shelves now filled with dried fungus, oilcloth, toys, and jars of clean screws. A former boiler room may hold three generations behind curtains hung from belt rails, the furnace mouth tiled over and used as a winter shrine. Signal boxes become watch-houses, bedrooms, clinics. Storage cages are prized because they already have doors and overhead hooks. Chemical lockers make good kitchens once lined with ceramic and prayer. In many homes there is one wall no one touches because gauges remain there, even if the needles have not moved in decades.

They do not have much furniture. Too much furniture traps damp. Beds are raised on brick piers or suspended from chains. Tables fold down from walls. Chairs are metal, cork-seated, or assembled from crate wood that must be smoked monthly against mold. Household wealth is counted in dry shelves, intact glass, good gaskets, lamp oil, old maps, warm blankets, uncontaminated salt, and tools inherited with their uses remembered.

They cook carefully.

There are stews of cultivated mold, canal shrimp, root flour, pale beans from surface trade, eel on feast days, beetle meal in lean seasons, bitter greens grown under blue lamps, and cakes steamed over reliable pipes. Tea is made from leaves caught in autumn filters and dried on wire racks above transformers. Mineral water is boiled twice unless taken from one of the known cold spouts. Children are given broth with rust-iron skimmed from the basin when their blood is thought too thin.

Meals are quiet but not joyless. Voices are low because the walls keep confidences poorly. Laughter happens in short bursts, as if released from pressure. Songs exist, but many have no melody a surface person would recognize; they are timing songs for turning valves, clearing grates, hauling chain, counting breaths in sealed passages. A lullaby may also be an evacuation procedure. A wedding chant may list safe routes through the western drains.

They are practical people, but not plain.

They decorate what dryness they can protect. Door lintels are painted with enamel fish, wheel-shapes, winged gauges, red hands, black canals, seven-spouted basins. Children press beetle shells into soft mortar to make stars. Curtains are embroidered with pipe diagrams and family marks. Shrines glow behind cracked glass in meter panels: little arrangements of wick-light, brass washers, saints no surface church remembers, crab claws, root threads, photographs silvered by damp, and pressure needles set to impossible values.

Their ceremonies are maintenance and their maintenance is ceremony.

When a child first crosses the Low Yard alone, an elder stains the child’s boots with basin water from three spouts: cold for caution, orange for endurance, violet for secrecy. When someone dies, the family does not bury them in the stone unless the stone has asked. Most are wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with clean iron, and lowered into a particular still reach of canal where blind fish gather. Others are cremated in the small legal furnace behind the old laundry, if fuel can be spared. The ashes are mixed with lime and used to seal cracks in thresholds. Thus the dead keep water out for the living.

The people have trades, though many trades overlap.

There are skim-workers, who gather oils from canal surfaces with wide black cloths and sell the separated fractions to lamp-keepers and machinists.

There are cork-liners, who make rooms habitable one panel at a time.

There are mold-tenders, who know which porcelain trays produce food, medicine, dye, poison, or dreams.

There are lamp-tenders, whose authority is greater than it appears. To decide where light remains is to decide which passages stay part of the settlement.

There are eel-watchers, grate-clearers, rail-sounders, root-cutters, rust-doctors, bell-refusers, cart-followers, map-copyists, and those who listen at sealed doors after rain.

The listeners are treated with a mixture of respect and pity. They kneel with one ear to pipes, walls, floors, old housings, canal stones. They hear movements others miss: pressure building behind a forgotten valve, a pump miscounting its stroke, a new hollow forming in limestone, the scratch of roots entering a fuse box, machinery waking behind a wall. Many listeners become eccentric. Some begin answering sounds that no one else hears. But their warnings save streets, and so they are fed well.

There are children, though fewer than one expects in a city.

They are quick, solemn in groups, wild when alone. They know how to balance on railings slick with condensation. They keep shrimp in jars until scolded. They trade dry matches, moth wings, colored wire, and stories about upward tunnels. They are forbidden the black canals, the giant doors of the pumping hall, and any staircase that smells of violets. Naturally, they investigate all three.

Their games imitate civic danger. They play flood, collapse, lamp-out, wrong bell, thirteen breaths. In one game, a child lies very still and pretends to be a dry stone while the others must decide whether to step on them. In another, they draw maps of streets that do not exist, then wait to see if the walls sweat.

Old people sit near thresholds where they can feel drafts and judge changes in weather days before the surface knows it has rained. Their memories are less chronological than hydrological. They say things like, “That was the year the west canal tasted of plaster,” or “Your mother was born during the long lowering,” or “He vanished before the violet spout changed its mind.” They remember names for machines no one can operate and routes through tunnels that are now iron-mouthed and locked.

They also remember which town is above, but not consistently.

This is not considered lying. The settlement belongs below several towns, or below the idea of town, or below whichever town has most recently leaked into it. Surface visitors care about geography. The people below care about flow. If rainwater from one district reaches the south filters, if confetti from another festival clogs the intake, if plaster dust from a demolition sifts down through the eastern vents, then all those places are above in the only way that matters.

There are visitors from the surface sometimes.

They arrive with dry boots, loud voices, and the instinct to look up. The settlement quiets around them. Animals withdraw. Lamps seem to show less. The visitors ask who governs the place, who owns the pumps, why the machines are maintained, whether the tunnels can be opened, whether there is anything valuable in the old sheds.

The inhabitants answer politely and incompletely.

Governance is done by threshold, pressure, and memory. Ownership is a dry concept. The pumps are maintained because otherwise the street where your grandmother sleeps becomes water. The tunnels open when they open. There is value everywhere, but very little that can survive being taken away.

Some visitors leave quickly, offended by damp and ambiguity. Some stay too long and begin to hear bells. A few are adopted by work: a broken ankle, a lamp to tend, a mold tray that responds to their hands, a child who follows them, a room that remains dry only when they sleep in it. After a few years their surface town becomes uncertain. After a decade they can no longer drink bright water without tasting absence.

The map in the pumping hall may be the oldest inhabitant after water and stone.

Animals gather before it because they know, or because the wall behind it gives off a warmth perceptible only to nonhuman bodies, or because the enamel lines hum at a frequency below hearing. The human inhabitants visit too, but less often than one might think. It is dangerous to consult prophecy too frequently, especially when prophecy is architectural.

The map shows the settlement and not the settlement. It remembers streets gone under, predicts canals not yet wet, names places before they open. New paint appears without brush marks. A glossy black passage may bloom on the wall years before the corresponding limestone begins to bead. Once it does, families watch the sweating place. Children are kept away. Tools are sharpened but not used. Lamps are brought. Offerings are sometimes left: a clean washer, a cup of boiled water, a dead moth, a strip of dry cloth.

Then, eventually, the wall opens.

Not dramatically. No thunder. No collapse unless something has been foolish. The stone darkens, softens, loosens its own certainty. Mortar exhales. A seam becomes a crack. A crack becomes a mouth. Water speaks first. Air follows. Sometimes what lies beyond is a small storage room with shelves intact and labels readable. Sometimes a stair descends into blackness colder than law. Sometimes there is a brick passage with rail tracks already polished. Sometimes the new space contains a chair, a lamp, and wet footprints leading out.

No one digs. Digging wounds. Waiting harvests.

What lives there, then, is a population trained by seepage.

People, certainly. Animals, abundantly. Fungi, roots, machines, maps, mineral blooms, inherited rules, and waters of varying honesty. But more than any one body, what lives there is maintenance: maintenance as culture, as religion, as ecology, as treaty.

The settlement survives because nothing in it is allowed to be merely itself. A pipe is plumbing, weather, instrument, perch, warning system, shrine support, and possible intestine of a sleeping machine. A canal is drainage, road, pantry, grave, border, mirror, and mouth. A lamp is light, law, memory, and claim. A wall is shelter until it becomes message. A leak is nuisance until it becomes calendar. A creature is vermin until it proves to be infrastructure.

Even the people are partly custodians and partly symptoms. They have been shaped by the place until their caution, their quiet voices, their bent shoulders, their clever hands, their refusal to hurry, all resemble forms of damp stone. They do not master the settlement. They participate in its not-falling-apart.

And the city, in return, allows them to remain.

It gives them cool air, edible darkness, warnings through crabs and pipes, lamps reflected twice, rooms that can be made almost dry, machines that still remember usefulness, and passages that ripen when the time is right. It gives them danger slowly enough to answer. It gives them history softened into habitation.

What lives below the town is a civic organism made of many citizens, only some of them human.

It breathes through drains.

It dreams in engines.

It grows by sweating through stone.
