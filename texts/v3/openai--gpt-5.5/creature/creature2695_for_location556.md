The basin is not inhabited by a single people so much as by a young arrangement of lives that have not finished deciding where creature ends and weather begins.

At first, the obvious residents are the small wet things: the green-throated frogs on the new stone, the blind mud-salamanders, the glass-winged moths, the reed-climbing crustaceans, the seedlike hoppers with sudden mouths. They are quick, abundant, opportunistic, and bright in the way of animals that have found a place before the place has made up its mind. They do not live in the basin as animals live in an old forest, by fitting themselves into inherited niches. They live there like sparks on cooling metal. They test every surface. They answer every new rule with a new habit.

But they are not the only inhabitants.

There are keepers.

They are rarely seen all at once, and never at first glance. A visitor might mistake one for a lump of pale root, a doubled reed stem, a wet length of discarded membrane caught beside a stone. They are small, usually no taller than a crouching child, though height means little for them because they seldom stand fully upright. Their bodies are narrow and folded, made for lowering themselves close to mud, for listening through their palms and bellies. Their skin is the color of soaked ash, faintly translucent over a slow inner amber. In certain light one can see mineral veins inside them, not bones exactly, but pale branching supports like the unfinished ribs of the basin itself.

They have four limbs that can serve as hands or feet depending on the slope, the weather, and the task. Each digit ends in a soft pad ringed with tiny hooks, good for climbing the smooth new rock without scratching it. Along their backs grow short filaments like fungal threads or wet fur. These filaments drink mist, taste mineral change, and tremble before the deep ground-tone passes through the basin. Their heads are long and blunt, without visible ears. Their eyes, when open, are not placed forward like a hunter’s; they sit low and wide and shine dimly blue-black, useful less for seeing objects than for noticing warmth, pressure, and reflections that move too early.

Their mouths are delicate. They do not bite much. They sip, press, hum, and name.

Naming is one of their chief labors.

A keeper will spend an hour beside a stone newly swollen from the ground, touching it with two fingers and making a sound too soft to carry. The sound is not a word in any language meant for air. It is a contact-name, a vibration placed into the stone’s damp surface. Afterward, condensation gathers there first. Moths find it. Frogs visit. Copper moss begins a slow claim on its shaded side. In this way the keepers do not plant gardens exactly, but invite them.

They are not masters of the basin. They are more like midwives to its local physics.

They tend the black posts at the center with great caution. They never enter the ring in pairs. One will approach alone, usually after the deep tone, carrying a shallow cup made of softened shell or a folded piece of bark-white ceramic peeled from some older structure. It will touch each post in turn, not with reverence exactly, but with the care one gives to the breathing place of an infant. If a hovering droplet has grown too large, the keeper leans close and lets it fall onto its tongue. If the pitch between posts has drifted, it answers with a throat-hum until the air tightens and the insects’ wingbeats settle into a new interval.

Their throats do not glow like the frogs’, but something moves there when they sing: a low green-brown flicker, as if light were being filtered through deep water and mud.

They live in hollows sung into the rock by frogs.

This is not metaphor. The frogs are not their pets, but they are their oldest collaborators. Each season, when the stone is most willing, the keepers gather below the pale ribs and coax the frogs into choruses. They do this by wetting certain surfaces, by tapping reed stems, by arranging beetle shells into tiny resonant piles. The frogs answer. Their green throats pulse. The rock receives the song and, over nights or weeks, softens inward. A hollow forms: not cut, not dug, but persuaded. Some are no larger than a fist and used for storing mineral needles, dead moth wings, or droplets caught from other days. Others are big enough for a keeper to curl inside during the heaviest upward rain.

The interiors of these dwellings are plain. A mat of copper moss. A few polished stones arranged by tone rather than appearance. Strips of translucent shed skin drying against the wall. Bowls that do not always contain what they seem to contain. No fire. Almost no tools in the ordinary sense. Their tools are intervals, names, temperatures, remembered rainfall, and the cooperation of animals too small to understand they are cooperating.

They eat lightly.

They drink mineral seepage, lick living films from pools, and feed on a paste made by mixing mud, fungal thread, and the transparent eggs of certain insects. They do not farm, but they encourage. A keeper may spend days guiding blind salamanders through mud beneath a reed bed, because their tunnels aerate the paste where a particular fungus thickens. They carry beetles from one warm crack to another so the beetles’ patterned shells will shed flakes of conductive mineral into the soil. They leave softened bones near moss mats, not as offerings but as amendments.

Their young begin in water, though not in ordinary pools.

When the bowls beneath the overhang have collected enough borrowed rainfall, the keepers pour those gathered moments into a shallow depression near the ring of posts. The water does not splash correctly. It arrives in layers: old rain, future mist, a drop from a storm that has not yet reached the mountain, a silver bead from a morning that occurred before the basin had an edge. Into this unstable wetness they place clusters of eggs like clear seeds, each with a dark comma slowly turning inside.

The larvae hatch almost invisible. For a time they live only as disturbances in reflection. You see them in pools before you see them in the air: warmer little shapes darting ahead of themselves. Later they acquire bodies from what the basin offers — mineral, fungus, warmth, sound. A brood raised after many ground-tones may grow heavier, paler, more stone-veined. A brood raised during long mist may become slight and almost membrane-thin. No generation is exactly like the last because the place is not exactly like itself from one season to another.

The keepers accept this. To them, identity is not a fixed shape. A living thing is a rule with appetite.

They speak seldom in open air. Most of their language travels through contact: knuckles against stone, throat pressed to mud, fingertips on the black posts, filaments touching filaments. When they must communicate across distance, they use the frogs. A sequence of coaxed calls can mean warmth leaking from the eastern structure, shadow-drift too sharp, pool reversal imminent, fresh rock ready to be named. The whole basin can become their message-board, loud with animals that do not know the sentences they are helping to carry.

They have no kings, no houses of authority, no evident ceremonies of power. But they do have specialists, if that word can be used for tendencies that gradually become bodies.

Some are post-listeners, thin and dark-palmed, with filaments that stand erect before changes in pressure. They spend much of their lives near the central ring and become strange from it. Sounds reach them out of order. They flinch before things happen. Older post-listeners sometimes answer questions a breath too soon, or mourn an injury before the wounded creature arrives.

Some are rock-singers, broad-throated and patient. Their jaws are heavier, their chest cavities resonant. They work with frog choruses and ceramic pops, shaping hollows, sealing cracks, making ledges safer for moss and eggs. When they die, their bodies are placed against new stone, and the stone usually takes them in within a few days, preserving their pale ribs as cloudy arcs.

Some are rain-keepers. They tend the bowls, the named stones, and the places where water prefers to begin. Their fingers are always beaded with condensation. They remember rainfall not by date, but by flavor: iron-sweet, cold-before-thunder, moth-heavy, blue with distant lightning, bitter from the cloud-edge.

Some are edge-watchers.

The edge-watchers are the strangest of them. They live near the fourth side, where the basin falls into dim cloud. They are taller, or seem taller because they stand more often upright. Mineral needles grow along their shoulders, all aligned toward the drop. They watch the place where the rules stop. At night, when something larger moves beyond the cloud-edge with slow even steps, the edge-watchers answer it by pressing their palms into the mud and making no sound at all.

It may be that the larger thing is one of them.

Not an adult, exactly. Not a god. Perhaps an exterior form, a life-stage that only develops outside the basin, where the mountain has not agreed to be gentle. The keepers do not fear it, but they become very still when it passes. The frogs go dark. The ribbon-snakes emerge. The black posts hold their droplets tight. Then the steps continue along the unseen slope and vanish into cloud, and the basin exhales all its small voices again.

The buried white structure at the eastern rim is not their home, though it is connected to their lives more deeply than any dwelling.

They call it, if the translation can be trusted, the cold seed.

They do not enter it. They do not appear to know whether it is alive, built, fallen, planted, or remembered into place by the mountain. They tend its warmth-leaks, scrape mineral crust from its breathing plates, and bring injured creatures to the mud around its base. Sick frogs recover there. Moth eggs hatch early. Cracked beetle shells reseal with gold-colored seams. Sometimes a dying keeper crawls near the white structure and lies there while its plates open and close through a long dim day. If the structure accepts the body, the mud beneath warms, and a few days later new rock swells somewhere else in the basin, veined faintly with the same amber that once moved under the keeper’s skin.

They are not immortal. But death here is not treated as disappearance. It is a change in assignment.

The keepers do not bury their dead. They place them where they will be most useful: against rock that needs memory, near pools that need warmth, under reed stems that have begun to dry, beside posts whose pitch has thinned. Small mouths take what they need. Mist takes what it can. The mud investigates the rest. Bones curl and soften. Skin becomes film. Filaments root briefly, fruiting into tiny pale knobs that the young eat during their first days in air.

Because nothing decays quickly, grief is also slow. A dead keeper remains present for a long time: visible as a cloudy spiral in stone, as a warm reflection in a pool, as a patch of moss that brightens orange when touched by rain.

Their relationship with the other animals is intimate but not sentimental. They are bitten, parasitized, robbed, followed, and occasionally eaten. Ribbon-snakes take their larvae when they can. The upside-down pool-hunting bird has been known to spear a young keeper through the back and carry it to a ledge. The seed-mouthed hoppers chew their shed skin before it is ready. None of this makes the keepers enemies of those creatures. Predation is simply one of the basin’s quicker methods of rearrangement.

Still, they are careful. They know safe intervals. They know which cracks hold snakes during the warm part of the cycle. They know when the pools will reverse and send rain upward. They know to flatten themselves when shadows lean too sharply toward the ring. They teach this knowledge by touch, by guided walking, by allowing the young to make small survivable mistakes.

A young keeper must learn the basin as a sequence of permissions.

This mud may hold you if you hum first.

This pool shows the next breath, but not the one after.

This stone has no name yet; do not sleep against it.

This ladder is not for climbing unless thunder has remembered it.

This shadow is only hungry for sound.

Their technology is difficult to separate from their manners. A human visitor might ask whether the keepers made the black posts, the ceramic hut, the transparent ladder. The better answer is that they maintain relationships with them. They inherit devices the way other peoples inherit weather, and they modify them the way moss modifies stone: slowly, chemically, by persistence and presence.

They polish the stones arranged along no obvious path because the path is not spatial. It is temporal. Step beside the first stone during mist, beside the second after a frog-call, beside the third before the ground-tone reaches the teeth, and one arrives at the eastern rim a few breaths earlier than one should. The keepers use this path when a pool changes too quickly or a young one wanders near the edge.

The transparent ladder is used rarely. During thunder, when it becomes solid, the keepers climb it one at a time into air that has briefly acquired rungs. They harvest something from the underside of the vapor ceiling: not fruit, not water, but dark soft nodules of condensed sound. These are brought down and pressed into cracks where new stone has grown confused. By morning the crack has chosen a direction.

For all their strangeness, they are not solemn creatures.

They play.

They race reflections in the pools, trying to catch the warmer image before it moves ahead. They arrange mineral needles on sleeping companions so that, at waking, a whole body shivers toward the cloud-edge. They teach beetles to walk across the black posts and laugh — a breathy clicking, almost inaudible under the water noise — when the insects’ wingbeats come out an octave wrong. Young keepers leap from reed clump to reed clump during upward rain, trying not to be carried into the cloud ceiling. Sometimes they fail and return hours later, wet with weather from somewhere else.

They are curious about visitors, but slowly. They do not surround. They do not announce. They watch from rock hollows and beneath exposed roots. If a stranger speaks aloud to a stone and water gathers there, the keepers notice. If the stranger steps into the ring of posts and panics when sounds arrive out of order, they retreat politely. If the stranger leaves metal overnight, the keepers inspect the mineral fur by dawn and may rearrange the needles into a question.

Their questions are hard to recognize.

A line of condensation on a boot.

A frog-call repeated from the wrong direction.

A bowl placed where no bowl had been.

A small stone, newly named, set beside the visitor’s sleeping place and wet only on the side facing home.

What they want, if wanting is the right word, is continuation without hardening. They live in a place too young for tradition but already full of memory. Their work is to keep the basin from becoming merely rock, merely swamp, merely ruin, merely machine. They preserve its uncertainty. They tune it so that water may still try upward, shadows may still drift, frogs may still carve rooms with their throats, and the mountain may continue becoming itself without crushing the fragile abundance gathered in its palm.

They are the basin’s first citizens, though they would not understand citizenship.

They are its listeners.

Its damp-handed editors.

Its children and caretakers.

And perhaps, when enough ages have passed and the mountain finally decides what kind of stone it is, the keepers will vanish into it completely. Their bodies will become pale arcs in the ribs. Their songs will remain as hollows. Their names will call condensation from the air. The frogs will continue shaping ledges by instinct. The posts will hum in twelve tones. The white structure will breathe its impossible stars.

And anyone standing there in the warm dim basin, hearing water move in every direction at once, will feel watched — not by eyes, exactly, but by a patient local intelligence distributed through mud, mist, animal throat, and new stone.

That is what lives there: not a species alone, but a young ecology learning to think.
