The people who live there are a wet, loud, practical kind of people.

At first glance they seem merely poor or weather-beaten, because everything about them is patched, darkened, rubbed smooth by use. Their clothes cling. Their hair is tied up, shaved short, braided with cord, or greased flat against the skull to keep it out of the face. Their skin shines almost constantly, not with leisure or ornament but with salt water, sweat, fish oil, rain, and the pale lime dust that settles into every crease. They are not clean in the dry-land sense, but they are not careless. They scrub what must be scrubbed. They scrape fungus from belts and sandals. They wash cuts immediately. They know rot as an enemy with habits.

They are compact people, mostly. Not dwarfish, not deformed, but built low and solid in a way that suits the city: wide feet, strong calves, thick hands, shoulders accustomed to carrying jars and baskets rather than swinging axes or drawing long bows. Their toes spread when they walk. The soles of their feet are callused into a kind of living leather, pale at the edges, dark and polished beneath. Children learn early to step without splashing when silence is useful, and to splash hard when they want crabs or rats to scatter before them.

Their movement is distinctive. Outsiders walk with the anxious lift-and-place care of people afraid of mud. The inhabitants do not. They flow over the city’s broken wet surfaces with a shallow, precise rhythm: heel only where the shell paving grips, toe on the higher stone, one hand brushing a wall, a knee turning sideways between baskets, a shoulder dipping under ropes. They can carry a sleeping infant, a fish basket, and a jar of limewater through a flooded lane while arguing with someone two courtyards away.

They speak loudly because they must, but they do not think of themselves as loud. To them a dry inland voice sounds secretive, weak, almost sick. Their own language is made for carrying through water-noise. It has hard front sounds, abrupt calls, repeated syllables, whistles, clucks of the tongue, and a whole grammar of gestures used when the roar swallows speech. A raised thumb toward the ear means listen to the drain. Two fingers cut downward means the water is dropping. A flat palm held low means wait, the mud will take you. Children shout before they can form proper words.

They do not value silence. Silence is not peace to them. Silence means a blocked channel, a bird flock startled away, an animal dead in its pen, a cistern gone dry, a tide arriving wrongly, a room filling where it should not. Their elders are admired for their hearing. Old men and old women sit on raised stone thresholds with clouded eyes and heads tilted, apparently doing nothing, while actually reading the city by sound. They can tell whether a grate is clogged three streets away. They hear the hollow knock of water beneath a paving slab before it fails. They know the slap of fish driven into a market trap from the slap of loosened masonry in a culvert.

They are maintenance people before they are anything else.

Their heroes are not conquerors. Their stories are about the woman who heard the western wall breathe before the night flood came, the brothers who packed a split embankment with hair mat and clay while water climbed their chests, the child who found a seep under the lime kilns, the old mason whose flood doors still close true after forty years. A person earns respect by noticing small failures early. Laziness is less hated than negligence. A fool may be forgiven; someone who leaves shells in a drain is remembered.

Their bodies carry the marks of their city. The backs of their hands are scored with shell cuts. Ankles are scarred by crab claws, rope burns, fish spines, and infected scratches survived in childhood. Many have pale patches where salt sores healed badly. Their eyes are narrowed from living in constant reflected gray brightness. Their teeth are worn by grit from shell-ground meal and fish dried in mineral dust. Their fingernails are blunt and often rimmed with lime. Babies smell of milk, smoke, fish oil, and damp cloth almost from birth.

They dress for wetness, not modesty or display. Most daily clothing is wrapped, tied, and easily lifted: short skirts of cured hide or woven hair-fiber, belts hung with shell knives, bone needles, keys, charms, drain hooks, fish spikes. In cooler rain they wear capes of oiled skin or felted animal hair, heavy with water at the hem. Fine clothing exists, but it is not delicate. Status shows in waterproofing, in well-cured leather, in copper fasteners that do not stain too green, in shell beads matched by color and size, in lime-white ceremonial wraps kept raised in sealed chests.

They ornament themselves with the city’s own materials. Shell discs stitched into collars. Fish vertebrae strung as counting beads. Copper rings in ears and lips, kept small because large ones catch on nets. Black clay painted across the brow to cut glare. White lime marks on the throat and wrists during rites. Children wear little charms shaped like flood doors, crabs, waterbirds, and paired feet on stepping stones. A charm shaped like a dry tree would mean nothing to most of them except foreignness.

They eat what the wet world gives them. Fish in countless forms: fresh, fermented, dried, smoked over dung fuel, pounded into paste, stewed with imported grain, wrapped in gut, cured in brine. Crabs cracked with stone hammers. Mollusks roasted in heaps and pried out with bone picks. Turtle eggs when allowed, forbidden in some districts, treasured in others. Eels, pale cistern fish, bird eggs, offal, blood cakes, rendered fat. Grain is precious but ordinary enough to be known: porridge, flat cakes, sour mash. Plant foods arrive already dead, in sacks and bricks, and are treated with the care other peoples give to fruit on the tree. A moldy grain store can ruin a household.

They keep animals not as companions in the sentimental sense, but as neighbors, workers, dangers, food, wealth, smell, warmth, and obligation.

Dogs belong everywhere and nowhere. They are scarred, narrow-eyed, damp-backed, tolerated because they kill rats and warn of shifting mud. Children sleep beside them on raised benches. A good drain dog, one that can smell a drowned rat or a cracked cistern wall, may be valued more than a copper knife.

The mud-hooved pack beasts are treated with gruff tenderness. Their keepers know every split hoof, every sore strap mark, every temper. The beasts haul shell, lime, fish jars, wet brick, and sleeping old people during floods. They are ugly animals by dry-country standards: low, wide, nostrils always running, eyes half-veiled, hides caked with pale mud. But the city could not keep its shape without them.

The tusked swine are feared and respected. They eat what would otherwise rot. They can also break a child’s leg or chew through a careless hand. Their pens are double-lipped and floored with stone. The people curse them constantly and decorate their gateposts with little swine figures to keep them healthy.

The great dark water-beasts, buffalo-like, are almost sacred in some quarters. They stand steaming in the warm rain, patient as stones, while men and women hitch sledges to them. Their horns are polished with fish oil. Their dung is too useful to waste. Their deaths are events of civic significance: meat, hide, horn, bone, sinew, fuel, grief, argument, ritual.

But the human beings are not the only inhabitants. The city is shared more completely than they like to admit.

Crabs live in the drains like a second citizenry. They know the underside of the city better than any mason. They pour out at dusk in pale, clicking companies, lifting scraps in their claws, vanishing sideways under walls. Children trap them in wicker cones. Priests observe them for omens. A sudden absence of crabs from a channel is alarming.

Birds own the upper edges. Long-necked white and gray fish-stabbers stand like thin old judges on parapets. Gulls scream from racks. Black-backed scavengers watch meat stalls with a patience that looks almost human. Terns wheel low in the damp air, their wings cutting the mist. People hate them, feed them, read the weather in them, imitate them in children’s games, and carve them on household charms.

The amphibious things under the steps are older in feeling than the city, though perhaps they came after it. Heavy-headed, slick, legged eels or salamanders, they lie in warm shallows with their lidless eyes just above the water. They eat fish scraps and rats. Some households tolerate one beneath the entrance because it keeps smaller vermin away. Others kill them on sight. Their skins are used in medicine, their teeth in love charms, their eggs in secret dares among children.

Mold lives there too, and is treated almost as weather. Pale fur on leather. Blue-black bloom on stored meal. Pink films in basins. White salt whiskers on walls. Greenish slickness in pans where no true plant grows. The people speak of these growths with precise names, distinguishing harmless wall-sweat from the kind that ruins lungs, edible rind from poison fuzz, sacred bloom from household shame. Scraping mold is a daily act, as common as washing hands.

The city’s society is divided less by noble blood than by responsibility to water.

There are drain families, who know the buried channels and marry among themselves because their knowledge is inherited through touch, smell, and repetition. They carry hooked poles, shell scoops, and short mallets. Their arms are strong from lifting grates. They are respected and avoided, because they handle the city’s filth and its survival.

There are wall-packers and embankment people, clay-handed, slow-speaking, trusted in emergencies. They judge mud by squeezing it between thumb and palm. They know which shell rubble bites and which rolls, which ash layer will sour, which hair mat will hold. Their children make toy walls before they make dolls.

There are fish houses, loud and wealthy in perishable wealth. They own traps, boats, racks, knives, salting jars, rights to certain channels at certain hours. Their courtyards glitter with scales. Their women and men both have forearms nicked from fins and blades. They sleep lightly because fish spoils fast and thieves know it.

There are lime burners, half-blind and coughing, whitened like ghosts by their work. Their kilns are hot islands in the damp city. They turn shell into the substance that keeps walls sealed, floors hard, bodies purified, tombs closed, and sickness at bay. People fear their tempers and court their favor. A lime burner’s curse is taken seriously.

There are animal wards, boat handlers, cistern keepers, shell counters, market callers, roof patchers, salt-field walkers, potters, bone carvers, net menders, and those who do nothing but carry: jars on hips, baskets on backs, cords across their foreheads, sledges behind them through ankle-deep water.

Their rulers, if they have rulers, are probably not kings in a glittering hall. Power belongs to whoever can command labor before failure. A council of threshold elders. A master of gates. Lineages that own the highest platforms. Priests who keep the tide marks. Storehouse families who control imported grain. People who know when to close flood doors and how much food sits above waterline. Authority here must be practical or it becomes ridiculous. No one can afford a lord who does not understand drains.

Their religion is not lofty. It is low, damp, and exact.

They honor boundaries: wet and solid, safe and sinking, sealed and leaking, living flesh and useful bone, shell and lime, tide and basin, breath and drowning. Their shrines are raised only a little higher than ordinary rooms, but meticulously maintained. Offerings are fish oil, first shells, polished crab claws, shaped lumps of white lime, little clay flood doors, animal teeth, jars of clean water. They do not pray for rain to come or go. They pray for balance. Not too high. Not too low. Not stagnant. Not rushing. Hold.

Their dead are not buried deep, because deep belongs to water. Some are sealed into raised wall tombs with lime. Some are placed in stone boxes above flood marks. Some bones are cleaned and kept in family niches, whitened and handled during repairs, as if ancestors help hold the house together. The worst fate is not death but being washed loose, becoming unplaced, a bone in a drain, a name without a threshold.

They do not think of their land as unfinished in the way outsiders do. To them, all land is something maintained. Dry-country people seem naive, trusting hills merely because they have stood a long time. These basin people know better: everything fails unless tended. Stone cracks. Mud moves. Rope rots. Animals push. Water searches. The world is not stable; it is negotiated.

Their children are fearless in ways that terrify visitors. They run along wall lips above black water. They reach into shallow channels and pull out crabs by the back shell. They can tell edible mollusks from fever ones by smell. They know which dogs bite, which swine charge, which old women carry sweets, which paving stones rock underfoot. They play at flood: one group builds a clay wall, another attacks it with cups of water, a third judges whether the gate held. They sing counting songs based on tide stains.

Their old people shrink upward. As their legs weaken, they live more and more on thresholds, benches, platforms, roof shelves, and memory. They become listeners, sorters, charm-tiers, net inspectors, baby watchers, keepers of safe routes. An elder may no longer cross the market unaided but may still save a district by saying, “That drip is new.”

Outsiders stand out immediately. They are too dry, too quiet, too tall in their posture, too trusting of open ground. They bring wood, grain, copper, stories of trees, stories of hills, cloth that mildews, tools that rust, diseases that behave strangely in the damp. The city needs them but does not envy them entirely. A person from the high dry world seems to live far from the truth. They do not wake to water under the floor. They do not know what holds them up.

And beneath all the human arrangements, there is another kind of inhabitant: the city itself, or something very close to it.

Not a mind, exactly. Not a god with a face. But a collective creature made of water levels, shell heaps, animal heat, lime-sealed rooms, drains, habits, rot, repair, births, market cries, and the same hand motions repeated for generations. It breathes through culverts. It sweats through walls. It digests fish, shell, dung, ash, and imported grain. It grows by embankment and silt. It sheds bricks into water and replaces them. It trains its people as much as they build it.

The people who live there are therefore not masters of the place. They are its current skin. Its moving hands. Its voices.

They are basin folk: wet-footed, sharp-eared, salt-skinned, stubborn, communal, suspicious of dryness, rich in techniques, poor in leisure, intimate with animals, and bound by maintenance. They live where the world has not hardened, and instead of waiting for it to become firm, they have learned the art of staying afloat without ever quite floating away.
