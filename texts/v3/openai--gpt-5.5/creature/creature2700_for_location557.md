What lives there is almost entirely plant, fungus, and people.

The people are quiet because the place has taught them to be. Not timid, not secretive, but economical with motion. They walk as if every surface has a memory of being liquid. Their feet know stone lips, slick moss seams, hollow-sounding planks, the false firmness of soaked soil. Children learn balance before they learn names: how to cross a sweating ledge with a basket against one hip, how to test a reed mat with the toes, how to hear the difference between water moving under a path and water undermining it.

They are a damp-weather people, broad-handed and sure-footed, with clothes that never quite become light. They wear woven reed, bark cloth, beaten fiber, layered plant felt, and wrapped skirts or tunics that can be lifted above the knees in flooded yards. Nothing about them is made from hide, wool, horn, feather, or sinew. Their sandals are plaited from reed and root fiber; their belts are braided cord; their hats are broad, stiff, and vegetable. In dry places such clothing would seem coarse. Here it belongs: swelling, darkening, taking the shape of the air.

Their tools are quiet tools. Stone blades set into wooden handles. Ceramic scoops. Clay-lined baskets. Polished digging sticks hardened by fire. Split-stone wedges for prying slabs from the fresh cliffs. Reed knives with replaceable edges. Long-handled skimmers for clearing leaves from channels. They do not have the quick metallic clatter of a town with forges, hooves, wheels, bells, and harness. Their work sounds like pressure: scraping, lifting, pressing, draining, cutting stems, setting stones into wet clay.

They live by tending gradients.

A person here is not first a hunter, herder, rider, fisher, or merchant. A person is someone who understands where water wishes to go and where it must be persuaded to wait. The most respected among them are not warriors or wealthy traders but level-readers, sluice keepers, terrace builders, reed stewards, cistern cleaners, seed carriers, and those old masons who can look at a pale stone shelf and know whether it will hold a house for three generations or breathe itself apart by next rain-season.

Their speech has many words for wetness. Not simply rain, pool, mud, stream, flood, and mist, but the first sweating of a wall before a seep opens; the black softness beneath a root bed when it is almost too rich; the clear water fit for storage; the tired water sent through reeds; the thin moving skin over stone after a warm shower; the dangerous shine that means a slab is loosening from below. They name water by behavior, not appearance.

They do not hurry unless a wall has begun to fail.

Their days begin without animal sound. No rooster, no dogs, no insects waking in thatch. The settlement comes awake through human maintenance: shutters opened, jars uncovered, feet on stone, someone emptying a roof basin, someone lifting the woven lid from a cistern to inspect the surface. Voices stay low because there is nothing to compete with. A shout would travel strangely, flattening against damp air and returning from walls without life in it.

Children are carried on hips or in reed slings, then set down early to crawl among mats and stone thresholds. Their games imitate waterwork. They build miniature channels in mud, float bits of bark from one basin to another, dam and release puddles, argue over whose little field has drowned and whose has dried. They do not chase animals because there are none to chase. Their stories do not begin with foxes, ravens, wolves, horses, beetles, serpents, or fish. Their imagined beings are stones that wake, reeds that remember hands, pools that keep faces, terraces that grow jealous if neglected, clouds that forget how to leave.

The elders are keepers of slopes. They remember which wall settled in the year of three warm fogs, which basin went sour when reeds were cut too close, which family once paved over a breathing seam and lost the floor of their house. Age here is associated with patience and seep knowledge. An old person sitting beside a channel is not idle; they may be listening for the beginning of a change no younger ear would notice.

Food is grown close, wet, and deliberately.

They eat roots from raised ridges: pale tubers, red-fleshed wetland roots, starchy corms lifted with forked sticks and washed in stone troughs. They grow leaf crops in bordered beds, cutting them again and again rather than pulling them whole. They cultivate reeds as another people might cultivate wheat. There are fruiting trees on mounds, carefully drained, their blossoms visited by human hands. Pollination is labor here. Without insects, flowers are not left to chance. At the right time, people move through groves with brushes of fiber, soft reed tips, or fingertips dusted with pollen, transferring future harvests one bloom at a time.

Seeds do not travel far unless carried. So carrying seeds is a social act, almost a moral one. Families keep packets of dry seed in raised granaries, sealed in ceramic jars with ash and charcoal. Some seeds are owed, some gifted, some never taken beyond a lineage’s beds without permission. To plant is not merely to use soil but to decide where a species will be allowed to move.

Fungi are also residents, though not spoken of as plants. They live in shaded chambers, under damp reed covers, in stacked beds of rotted stems and leaf-mass. Some are food; some are medicine; some are feared because they soften beams, sour stored roots, or creep into walls. The people know them by smell and touch. In a world without grazing mouths and burrowing bodies, fungus is one of the great transformers. It is rot, digestion, memory, and warning.

Their houses are low because warmth and damp collect differently under high roofs, and because heavy weather asks humility of buildings. Doorways make visitors bow. Interiors are dim, reed-screened, smelling of wet stone, stored roots, smoke when the kiln has been used, and the green mineral scent of mats drying badly. Floors are raised above the breathing ground. Sleeping platforms stand on stone feet. Nothing valuable rests directly on earth.

They do not own much that cannot survive moisture.

A household’s wealth is visible in raised storage, good mats, clean channels, roof reeds neatly layered, a cistern with clear water, a grove that fruits reliably, dry seed, sound stonework, and many usable baskets. Reeds are wealth because reeds become almost everything: roof, wall, screen, cord, sleeping mat, carrying frame, filter, writing strip, child’s toy, funeral wrapping, drainage bed. A careless reed cutter is treated as someone who harms the future.

Because there are no animals, their loneliness is of a kind other peoples might not understand.

There are no companion creatures under tables, no pests in grain, no moths in cloth, no mice in walls, no birds stealing seed, no fish breaking the skin of pools. Nothing looks back at them from the marsh except other people. This gives their society an intense human inwardness. Faces matter. Footsteps matter. The absence of other moving life makes each human movement larger, more consequential. A person crossing a plaza at dusk can seem like the only animated thing in the world.

Their rituals gather around water levels.

Birth water is poured through clean gravel before being used to wash a newborn. Marriage may involve joining two household channels, or exchanging jars of cistern water, proving that one dwelling’s carefulness can be trusted by another. Death is not given to animals, fire alone, or deep dry earth. The dead are wrapped in reed and fiber, placed in stone-lined chambers or wet clay tombs above flood height, returned slowly to mineral and fungus, not hurried. Names may be carved into channel stones where hands will pass over them for years while clearing leaves.

They mark time by maintenance. The season when roof reeds must be turned. The weeks when pollen must be carried. The interval between cistern cleanings. The fog period when stored roots are most likely to soften. The bright days rare enough to dry mats properly. The year a new ledge was cut. The generation a terrace has held.

Their politics are probably basin politics. Each neighborhood has its shared water: its cistern court, reed bed, overflow path, waste channel, garden mounds. To neglect one’s section is to endanger others downhill. Authority belongs to councils of those trusted to judge saturation. Disputes concern flow, shade, seed, labor, and maintenance: who failed to clear a channel, who took too much dry stone, whose garden wall slowed water into another family’s house, who may plant trees on a mound that changes the drainage of a lower plot.

They are not warlike in the usual sense. Their landscape has no room for charging, no pasture to seize, no herds to raid, no roads fit for armies. But they are not innocent. They know sabotage: a blocked channel, a loosened retaining stone, a contaminated cistern, seed withheld, a grove neglected out of spite. Their laws are severe around water because water is the common body of the town.

Their art is restrained. Not because they lack imagination, but because surfaces are always under attack from damp. Paint must be mineral and simple. Carving works better than cloth display. Patterns are incised into lintels, cistern rims, paving slabs, storage jars: stepped lines, basin shapes, reed clusters, terrace marks, falling-water combs. Their beauty is often functional first. A well-made channel mouth is admired. A roof edge that drops cleanly into a basin is beautiful. A causeway that stays level through many wet years is praised like a poem.

Music, if they make it, is soft and percussive: hollow reeds, struck stone, ceramic bowls with water in them, voices braided close together rather than projected outward. In a place with no birdsong, human song would be powerful, perhaps used sparingly. A group singing across the central basin at evening might feel less like entertainment than like introducing living breath into a world otherwise held in suspension.

At night they do not fear predators. They fear slipping, fever, bad water, softened walls, unseen edges, rot in storage, children wandering onto wet stone, the quiet failure of a terrace after too many years of seeming stable. Darkness here has no eyes in it but human ones. That may be comforting. It may also be worse.

The other inhabitants endure around them with a still persistence.

Moss lives like a low animal substitute, thickening in seams, swallowing the sharpness of shaded stone by fractions. Reeds stand in cultivated ranks and wild bands, the closest thing the town has to crowds. Vines climb walls but do not swarm unless permitted. Root crops fatten in black ridges. Trees stand isolated on their mounds like honored guests who must be kept from drowning. Algae skin the slower pools. Fungi thread through rot and wall-shadow. Bacteria and invisible life do what teeth and stomachs do elsewhere: reducing, souring, transforming.

But the people are the only walkers.

They are the pollinators, dispersers, dredgers, pruners, cutters, carriers, builders, mourners, and witnesses. Without them the plateau would still be wet and green, but more closed, more choked, more circular. With them it has lines: causeways, beds, channels, roof edges, cistern rims, stone thresholds. They do not dominate the place so much as translate it into habit.

They seem ancient not because their buildings are old, but because their lives are repetitive in the way water is repetitive. Every generation receives the same instruction: keep above the water, but do not insult it. Leave it paths. Give it beds. Drink only what has been clarified. Do not build on breathing ground. Do not waste reeds. Carry seed. Clear leaves. Listen to stone.

They are a people made by dampness, height, and silence: careful, communal, observant, slow to anger but unforgiving of negligence. They live without animals and therefore without much of the world’s usual restlessness. In their place, motion has narrowed into human hands and falling water.

And so what lives there is not a bustling city population, but a patient one: reed-clad, stone-footed, water-literate people moving through warm mist among plants that depend on them to travel, bloom, and persist. They are the mobile part of the marsh, the memory of its slopes, the hands by which a young mountain learns to hold still.
