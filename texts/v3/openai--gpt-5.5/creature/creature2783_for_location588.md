What lives there is not one creature, but a whole sealed economy of thirstless things.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They are the pasture, the mold of the mineral world, though they are not mold. They are mats of slow-bodied chemistry that cling to stone where heat and fumes meet: gray where the salts are old, amber where sulfur leaks, black-purple where the basalt sweats trace metals. They have no leaves, no stems, no softness. They thicken by fractions, harden, flake, and heal. In the dimness they look like bruises on rock, but under the heat-pulse they wake slightly, drawing brightness from the seams and turning it into bitter nourishment. They are the basin’s grass, its reef, its skin.

Most of the animals are built to scrape them.

The commonest are little plated grazers, no larger than fingernails, though some grow to the length of a hand. They move in tight carpets over the salt plates, each body made of overlapping shields the color of ash, bone, or tarnished metal. Their undersides are crowded with tiny legs; their fronts are blunt wedges fitted with rasping mouths. When still, they are almost indistinguishable from chips of broken crust. When the heat rises, they flow out by the thousands and polish the living films down to a dull shine.

Among them live smaller things: bead mites, hinge-backed flecks, needle-legged specks that could hide in the print of a thumb. They inhabit the spaces between salt crystals as if those gaps were caverns. Some feed on mineral skin. Some feed on eggs. Some ride larger animals, sealed beneath the margins of their plates, drinking vapor from the breath-slits and eating the salt that gathers there.

There are blind climbers on the walls.

They cling to the inward-leaning basalt in layered clusters, pale and jointed, with folded limbs like dry hooks. Their bodies are narrow and wax-coated, their heads little more than mouth and feelers. They descend during the warm breaths to graze under ledges where the richest films grow. When danger passes through the air, a chemical alarm rather than a sound, they freeze in place so completely that they become part of the stone’s texture. Whole colonies can vanish by becoming motionless.

The hunters are flatter.

Some lie on the floor like flakes of black glass. They are wide, thin animals with hinged edges and mouths that open downward. A grazer crossing one is simply gone: the plate flexes, seals, and settles again. Others wait above, needle-mouthed and patient, suspended under slabs with their limbs folded close. They do not chase. They measure heat, vibration, salt-trace, the faint electric trembling of bodies moving over warmed stone. Then one limb unfolds, precise as a tool, and pins.

The larger predators are rare but unavoidable.

They live in wall-gaps and broken rings of old stonework, person-sized only when fully extended. At rest they resemble collapsed shields or slabs of fused clinker. Their skins are black, gray, and violet-brown, polished by abrasion; along their sides are narrow breathing slits that open and close in a slow, dry bellows motion. They have no visible eyes. Instead, their heads carry fans of mineral-stiff filaments, each one sensitive to heat, pressure, and the chemical signatures of fear, molt, blood, and exposed moisture.

They are not fast in the usual sense. They are sudden after long waiting. A whole day of stillness may end in one movement: a sliding lunge, a folding of plates, a whisper of grit. Then they become stone again, digesting without display.

There are winged things too, though they seem less like fliers than flakes lifted by heat.

They hang in the roof-shadows when the basin cools, their vanes folded tight along brittle bodies. When the floor warms and the air begins to rise, they loosen and drift upward on the convection. Their wings are translucent, smoked at the edges, veined with salt-white lines. They do not sing or swarm noisily. They tilt, settle, lift, and descend in careful silence, feeding on spores, dust, shed particles of living film, and the smaller mites that ride the warm currents. Their larvae are not winged at all; they are armored threads that bore shallow grooves under mineral skins and emerge only when their bodies have hardened enough not to lose themselves to the air.

Eggs are everywhere, though seldom visible.

They are glued beneath salt lips, packed inside cracks, sealed under the discarded plates of dead grazers, hidden in the old fitted rings of stone. Many are hard as grit. Some wait through several heat cycles before opening. Some only hatch when exposed to the bitter vapor that rises from the deep seams. Most young are born miniature and complete: armored, dry, ready to hide. There is no helpless softness here. Infancy is brief because anything soft is a public feast.

Scavengers form the basin’s second tide.

When the heat falls and the main grazers withdraw, white threadlike crawlers emerge from seams. Bead-bodied mites gather around cracked shells. Blunt-headed scavengers with shovel mouths enter the remains of kills and moltings, reducing them to clean fragments. Nothing rots here. It is stripped, powdered, salted, and returned. Death becomes crust, film food, mineral trace, warning mark.

The old stone structures have their own residents.

In the low rings of fitted rock live sheltering colonies of broad-backed animals that wedge themselves into gaps and seal the openings with their bodies. They seem almost architectural. Their young stack broken flakes around favored cracks. Adults drag chips of saltstone and basalt into partial walls, not as craft exactly, but as inherited behavior: arrange, narrow, shade, protect. Over many generations their habits have made cairns, collars, little chambers, and false ruins. What looks like the memory of builders may be the slow expression of burrowers.

Or perhaps they inherited the ruins from something else. If so, whatever made the first rings is gone, absorbed into the basin’s mineral patience, leaving only habits that other bodies have learned to use.

There is no society in the soft sense, but there are agreements enforced by the place.

Do not waste motion.  
Do not expose moisture.  
Do not cross a warm plate without tasting it.  
Do not molt in the open.  
Do not ignore a fresh crack in the salt.  
Do not mistake darkness for safety.  
Do not mistake stillness for absence.

Many creatures communicate by leaving traces: alkaline smears, bitter droplets that dry instantly into crystals, scraped marks, heat-darkened patches on stone. A predator’s passage can empty a ledge without a sound. A fertile film-bloom can draw grazers from every crack. A death can summon scavengers before the body stops twitching.

The basin’s inhabitants are not lush, not generous, not expressive. They are exact. Their beauty is in their fit: every sealed joint, every folded limb, every waxed membrane and salt-hard egg answering the same ancient pressure. They have become more like the mountain than like animals elsewhere. Basalt-colored, salt-edged, fume-fed, almost geological in patience.

Yet they are alive with great intensity.

The hollow is crowded with hungers small enough to live between crystals and old enough to know the mountain’s breathing. It is a city without voices, a reef without water, a pasture without green, a furnace-organ packed with armored lives. Its creatures do not conquer the dryness; they have accepted it so completely that thirst has disappeared from their design.
