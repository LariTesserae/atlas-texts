What lives there is not one thing, but a tenement of the wet and patient.

At first it seems empty because nothing announces itself properly. No animal owns the chamber in the way a bird owns a branch or a fox owns a den. The residents are arranged by pressure, darkness, and surface. They live in layers, each keeping to the part of the drowned hall that can still be used.

Closest to the walls are the slow citizens.

They are mussels, oysters, limp gray shells, barnacle-like crusts, tube worms, snails, and bacterial skins. They make no territory except by attaching. They have turned the hall’s hardware into reef. Valve stems have beards of shell. Ladders wear colonies. Cable trays sag under living weight. The old iron brackets, once meant to hold gauges and conduits, now hold clustered mouths filtering whatever the water brings.

These creatures do not seem to move, but they are the chamber’s true permanence. They outlast pump cycles, floods, darkness, and the panic of fish. They open when the current is favorable. They close when vibration passes through the metal. Their lives are a pulse too small to notice unless you wait for it: shell lips parting, cilia working, soft bodies tasting rust, oil, algae, rot, mineral, rain.

Over them graze the snails.

They move as if time has thickened around them. Their paths cross warning signs, cracked tile, cloudy glass, enamel plates with words no animal needs. They leave pale trails through the brown-green film. A snail may spend half a day traversing the curve of a lamp housing, feeding from the light’s weak garden. When the pumps start, it tightens itself against the surface and becomes a small sealed fact.

In the silt are the hidden, numerous lives.

Worms withdraw into tubes at the first tremor. Larvae bend and uncurl. Amphipods flick sideways between rust flakes. Tiny translucent animals drift in the water column and vanish when a fish turns. They are the chamber’s nervous dust, everywhere and almost unseen. Much of the hall’s life passes through them: bat droppings, rat leavings, dead insects, algae, scraps from above, the softened remains of other bodies. They make the old service room edible.

The fish are the visible inhabitants, though even they are seen in fragments.

Small ones keep to the middle shadows and pipe edges, silver when they cross a lamp beam, brown when they stop. They travel in loose agreements rather than schools, scattering at vibration and gathering again near food. Their bodies are plain, practical, almost industrial in their suitability: blunt heads, quick fins, muted flanks, eyes adjusted to a world of brief light and long murk.

They know the chamber better than any map could. They know the dead water behind a pillar where a current cannot reach them. They know which pipe mouth exhales food after a pump coughs. They know the amber lamp’s algae patch and the safe underside of the bowed grating. They know when to rise and when to flatten themselves into darkness.

The larger fish live lower.

They are not many. They do not need to be. Thick, scarred, dark-backed, they wait in the central trench where the cold lies deepest and the water moves with purpose. They face into the flow with the stillness of tools. Their mouths open and close, not in expression but in function. When the pumps stir the silt and small creatures lose their grip, these fish ascend without hurry until the last instant. Then there is a pale flash, a collapse of distance, and something is gone.

They are old in the way urban animals become old: not majestic, but efficient. Their fins are nicked. Their eyes are filmed. Their bodies remember collisions with grates, pipes, flood debris, each other. They have survived chemical changes, oxygen failures, dry seasons, storm surges, and the sudden violence of automatic machinery. They are the trench’s quiet authorities.

In the pipe mouths live the eel-like ones.

Sometimes only a head shows: dark, slick, blunt-snouted, jaw working in the current. Sometimes a length of body loops out and withdraws again, muscular and undecorated. They use the city’s own plumbing as burrow and road. One may vanish into a black circular opening and reappear hours later from a cracked conduit across the room, as if the wall itself has passed it through.

They are neither common nor rare. They are simply present often enough that every hole seems potentially occupied. Their dens are rimmed with disturbed silt, fish scales, shell fragments, and the fine trembling attention of smaller creatures avoiding them.

Above the waterline, in the narrow stale air, live the warm-blooded opportunists.

The rats are the most established. They have made a country out of cable trays, pipe runs, dry brackets, and the backs of rusted cabinets. They are sleek and dark, with wet fur and bright black eyes. They move with a confidence that seems almost insulting in such a precarious place. They can cross a pipe slick with condensation, vanish behind a valve cluster, drop into the water, swim hard against a weak current, and climb out under a ledge no human would notice.

Their nests are built from insulation, shredded labels, plastic strands, dead leaves, and old cloth carried in by floods. They smell of musk, damp fur, and metal. They feed on whatever the hall offers: stranded fish after a water drop, insect hatches, eggs, soft-bodied things, scraps washed down from the city, one another if necessary. During pump surges they retreat upward, whiskers trembling, bodies pressed flat to concrete ribs while the water below becomes opaque and full of force.

They are not kings of the place. Nothing is. But they are among the few creatures here that seem to decide.

The bats are more occasional, more delicate. They enter through vents and forgotten shafts when the season allows. A few hang in the high arch where the air pocket is widest, folded like damp scraps of leather against the concrete. Their droppings fall into the water and become part of the food chain almost at once. When insects hatch thickly around a warm lamp, the bats wake into brief, precise flight, turning in the low air with impossible care, never touching the water though every wingbeat seems too close.

There are insects in every temporary form.

Midges tremble above the surface in little columns when the air is calm. Gnats gather near lamps. Moths powder themselves against yellowed glass. Mosquitoes test the quieter alcoves. Their larvae hang in stagnant pockets, breathing from the surface until a ripple breaks their posture. Some generations thrive; others are wiped out by a single rise in water or a change in chemistry. They live by timing, and many time it badly.

Where the chamber nearly touches the city above, there may be pale lizards, geckos, spiders, things that arrived by accident and found the warmth of a lamp or the dryness of a conduit enough. They are marginal residents, dependent on small chances: a crack that remains open, a ledge that stays dry, insects enough to justify staying. Their presence makes the hall feel stranger not because they are strange, but because they are ordinary creatures in an unreasonable room.

And everywhere, more than any visible animal, live the films.

Bacteria, algae, fungi, mineral mats, slick black colonies on rubber, green veils under light, orange blooms where iron feeds them, gray skins in still corners. They are the hall’s first and last inhabitants. They soften the built surfaces into food. They turn corrosion into habitat. They make the tiles slippery, the pipes furred, the water clouded, the metal alive-looking. Without them the room would be only flooded infrastructure. With them, it digests.

The residents do not form a peaceful world. They eat constantly, hide constantly, wait constantly. Eggs are eaten. Larvae are sifted from silt. Shellfish filter the remains of others. Rats raid what they can reach. Fish take insects from the surface and smaller fish from the shadows. Eels take fish. Old fish take anything careless enough to cross the trench at the wrong time.

But the violence is subdued by the water. Nothing here is theatrical. Predation is a flick, a suction, a disappearance. Death becomes particles, then film, then flesh again.

All of them live by the machinery’s weather.

A pump starting is sunrise, storm, alarm, and dinner bell at once. The mussels close. The fish turn into the current. The worms vanish. The rats climb. The silt rises and makes the whole chamber blind. For a while the hall belongs to pressure. Then the surge passes, and life unfolds again from seams and brackets and pipe mouths.

The creatures here are not deformed wonders or relics of some secret world. They are city animals, water animals, adaptable animals. They are the kinds of lives that accept bad light, hard surfaces, chemical water, and sudden change. Their strangeness comes from their agreement with the place. They have taken the old commands of the hall—flow here, drain there, withstand pressure, endure vibration—and made them biological.

What lives there is a drowned neighborhood.

Its houses are pipes. Its trees are ladders. Its cliffs are tiled walls. Its caves are conduits. Its seasons are rain, voltage, pump failure, and maintenance no one performs. Its citizens are silver fish, old trench fish, eel-shadows, shell clusters, snails, worms, amphipods, rats, bats, insects, algae, and the invisible laboring multitudes in the film.

They do not know the hall is abandoned.

To them it is complete.
