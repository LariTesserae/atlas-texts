At first, it seems that no one lives there.

Then it becomes clear that this is only because the inhabitants do not distinguish themselves sharply from their work.

The largest resident is the town itself: not as a single mind, exactly, and not as a mere collection of plants, but as a maintained body of habits. Its roots remember stresses in walls. Its vines know the routes of belts and cables. Its mosses thicken where winter drafts have found cracks. Its cistern-leaves unfold before rain because older leaves, older membranes, older human hands once taught them the hinge and angle. It does not think in words, but it has preferences. It dislikes exposed metal. It hoards water. It tolerates brick. It loves pressure, shade, slow circulation, repeated paths.

Under everything is the rootwork: a dark, fibrous citizenry threading through clinker, clay, buried pipe, bone-dry cable sheaths, and the foundations of former warehouses. It senses weight and damp, mineral and rot, vibration and salt. When a cart passes, the mat beneath the lane compresses and slowly rebounds. When a tank leaks, rootlets arrive before anyone has called it a leak. When a wall begins to lean, willow ribs thicken on the sheltered side and draw it back over seasons. This underground life is not wild. It is organized into districts by moisture and function: drinking roots around cisterns, anchoring roots beneath towers, fine sensing roots under workshops, pale fungal mats around fermentation chambers, tougher cable roots along the old tramline.

The visible plants are the town’s working limbs. Ivy seals. Lichen claims and preserves. Moss cushions and insulates. Willow frames doorways, rafters, braces, and gantries. Pale leaf-membranes serve as windows, shutters, valves, and rain-catchers. Ferns keep damp in the courtyards. Vines pull belts. Espaliered trees grow handles, teeth, hooks, slats, and curved ribs according to molds they have occupied since seedlinghood. Some plants are almost tools; others are almost clerks. A row of wax-leaved shrubs outside the oil house records humidity by the angle of its leaves. A slow-budding creeper near the repair shed flowers only when a certain fermentation tank has reached the right acidity. The town reads itself through growth.

There are animals, but they are peripheral, tolerated rather than invited. The snails are inspectors of undersides. They move along tanks, pipes, and window-veils, leaving silver measurements no one fully ignores. The moths live in the press-house rafters and hatch in the warmth above the pulp vats. Their dust is faintly alkaline and useful in paper-making, so small trays are left beneath their perches. The birds are visitors from the pale fields beyond the drainage ring. They come for insects, moss seed, a night’s shelter in the chimney trees. They do not nest low. Nothing nests low unless it has permission from the roots.

And there are people.

Not many. Fewer than the town could once hold, but more than a passerby would guess. They live in the dry upper rooms, the warm spaces behind workshops, the long side chambers of the municipal factory, and in small houses whose walls are so thick with trained growth that from outside they resemble storage sheds or hedged machines.

They are quiet because quiet is practical here. Loudness startles nothing in particular, but vibration travels. A slammed door can jar a valve. A careless boot can bruise a young brace-root growing beneath a path. They walk heel-light and side-footed, with a habit of pausing before thresholds, not from superstition but courtesy: a door may be tightening, a leaf-screen may be exhaling, a latch-vine may need to be loosened from the correct side.

Their clothing is made in the town’s own manner: felted fiber, waxed leaf-cloth, dark woven vine-thread, patched wool from outside trade, aprons stiff with resin. They wear high soft boots that shed spores and do not cut into moss. In rain they put on hooded capes with seams that channel water into little hip-flasks, because even the water that falls on a person belongs first to the town. The older ones often have green or brown staining at the fingertips from sap, tannin, algae gel, lichen dyes. Many carry pruning knives, moisture keys, small ceramic gauges, twine, and brass tags stamped with dates.

Their bodies have adapted by habit rather than by miracle. They are pale from overcast light, strong in the hands and back, patient in the knees. They know how to stand still for long periods and listen to a mechanism that is mostly plant. They can tell the difference between healthy dripping and wasteful dripping, between a settling creak and a warning creak, between fermentation heat and fever heat. They sleep lightly when rain begins. A whole household may rise at three in the morning to open gutters, pull membranes into place, or clear leaves from a cistern mouth by lamplight.

They do not command the town. They tend it, bargain with it, anticipate it, sometimes trick it.

A child learns early that a street is not a fixed thing. It is an agreement kept open by feet, pruning, drainage, and need. If no one uses a lane, the lane will narrow politely, then decisively. If a storehouse door is not opened for a month, the frame will begin to seal itself with soft green lips. If a tool is left on a bench too long, a filament will find it, taste its handle, and decide whether it is useful, harmful, or simply available. Children are taught to hang everything up. They are taught to thank hinges by oiling them and to thank vines by cutting them cleanly.

Their schools, if the word still applies, are workshops. They learn letters from enamel signs: FIBER, OIL, DRY GOODS, SEED, REPAIR. They learn arithmetic from cistern levels. They learn history from molds scratched with dates and from the old iron rails disappearing into green. They learn weather by the behavior of membranes. They learn patience from seed-machinery, where the part you need next winter must be started now.

The adults have trades, though no trade is purely human.

There are rain-setters, who maintain the catchment leaves and gutters. They know every roofline and every bad slope. They carry ladders made of root-grown rungs and climb during drizzle, adjusting the angle of membranes while water runs down their sleeves.

There are pressure-keepers, who listen to tanks, valves, sap-lines, and fermentation chambers. They are part engineer, part brewer, part physician. A pressure-keeper can place an ear against a pipe and hear whether a distant press will complete its stroke.

There are molders, who raise replacement parts. They clamp seedlings into iron forms, rotate them toward or away from light, feed them mineral broths, scar them where thickening is needed, and prune away ambition. They may spend years growing a single gear.

There are sealers, who work with resin, moss, and lichen to preserve the dead materials the town still needs. They speak of iron as though it were an old animal requiring constant salve. They distrust shine. A shiny surface is a surface not yet properly inhabited.

There are dry-store wardens, severe and almost ceremonious, because dryness is rare and precious. They keep paper, seed, salt, powdered medicine, spare brass, and written records behind double curtains of treated bark. They are the only people in town who routinely scold children.

There are tram-watchers for the little service carts. They clear the trellis, check the buried cables, grease what still requires grease, and coax the compact engines out on scheduled days. The carts move slowly enough that one may walk beside them, and often someone does, hand resting on the railing as if accompanying an elderly relative.

Their houses are austere but not miserable. Inside, there is warmth from compost walls and fermentation flues. Sleeping platforms are grown from thickened rootwood and covered in mats of dried fiber. Cupboards are hollowed living trunks with doors that must be rubbed with bitter paste to prevent them from healing shut. Windows are leaf-screens, replaced seasonally. Tables are smooth, dark, and slightly warm. Cups are ceramic, metal, or pressed pulp sealed with resin. Nothing is casually decorative, but many useful things are beautiful from long handling: a gauge polished by thumbs, a pruning knife with a root-grown grip, a water measure etched with generations of marks.

They eat what the town and its border fields allow: roots, cool-weather greens, fungi, pale beans, pressed seed cakes, fermented leaf pastes, snails when there are too many, eggs when birds leave them where they should not, traded grain from beyond the embankments, sharp preserves stored in dark crocks. They drink infusions, weak beers, mineral water, sometimes a clear distillate kept for cold nights and difficult repairs. Meals are quiet, but not joyless. Laughter happens indoors, softened by moss walls.

Their ceremonies are maintenance enlarged until it becomes communal memory.

The first rain after a long dry interval is not celebrated with shouting. People come out and stand under eaves, watching the leaves adjust. Children are allowed to open the small cistern mouths in the square. The pressure-keepers listen for the first sequence of valves. When the central engine completes its first full wet-cycle, someone rings a low bell made from an old compressed gas cylinder, and the sound rolls through the root-matted streets like distant thunder.

When a building is sealed for disuse, no one calls it abandonment. Its useful contents are removed. Its doors are tagged. Its gutters are redirected. Then it is given over to inhabitants that do not walk: moss, lichen, bindweed, fungus, fern. After some years it may become insulation for an adjacent workshop, a nursery wall, a cistern buttress, or simply a quiet mass of held material. To leave something empty would be indecent.

Their dead are also not left empty. Bodies are wrapped in fiber cloth, marked with brass or ceramic name tags, and placed in warm compost vaults beneath the chimney towers. The heat rises through the old flues. The trees at the chimney crowns feed on them slowly. In morning vapor, when white scarves drift from the brick mouths, the living sometimes look up and note who has joined the town’s weather.

They are not sentimental about this, but they are not indifferent.

The people speak softly even when alone. Their language has many verbs for moisture: water newly fallen, water stored, water fit for roots, water fouled by metal, water held in moss, water traveling invisibly, water promised by the air but not yet given. They also have many words for degrees of occupation: inhabited by use, inhabited by growth, inhabited by memory, inhabited by danger, inhabited by rot, inhabited properly. To call something bare is close to calling it at risk.

They have little interest in conquest or expansion. Beyond the ring lie fields, scrub, weather, other settlements perhaps, roads perhaps, but the town’s attention turns inward. Its ambition is continuity. Keep the belts moving. Keep the cisterns sound. Keep the root-mats from choking the cellars. Keep the old signs legible. Grow what will be needed before the need becomes urgent. Do not waste water. Do not leave dead matter unheld. Do not confuse speed with repair.

If they seem secretive, it is mostly because they are slow. A stranger expects greeting, explanation, decision. The town does not work at that pace. A stranger’s boots must be cleaned. Their breath must be accounted for in the warm rooms. Their tools must be inspected for bare metal or invasive seed. Their questions must be answered in ways that will not make them interfere. Hospitality exists, but it has procedures.

A guest may be given a cup of pale tea, a dry stool, a place to sleep under a leaf-window. They may be warned not to touch the hanging vines in the press-house, not to step on the darker moss by the cisterns, not to force any door that resists. If they stay long enough, they will be handed a broom, a scraper, a coil of fiber, a tray of seedlings to rotate toward the light. No one lives here without becoming part of maintenance.

The true inhabitants, then, are not only the people, nor only the plants, nor only the machines that still sigh and turn in their green housings.

They are the arrangements between them.

A vine that pulls a belt because a human child guided it years ago.  
A pump that runs because rainwater, sap pressure, and old brass still agree.  
A family that sleeps above a fermentation chamber and wakes when its breathing changes.  
A chimney tree rooted in coal soot and human ash.  
A doorway that opens when asked correctly.  
A street kept passable by feet, pruning, and mutual restraint.

What lives there is a civic organism with human hands, vegetal patience, fungal memory, and industrial bones. It does not flourish wildly. It persists. It makes. It repairs. It drinks the rain when it comes and remembers every drop.
