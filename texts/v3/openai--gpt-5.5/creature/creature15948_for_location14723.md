What lives there is not one species, though from a distance it may seem so.

The chief inhabitant is the city itself: not a forest, not a machine, not an administration, but a slow composite organism that has learned to impersonate all three. It lives in districts and conduits, in municipal rings and vascular pressure, in bylaws scarred into bark and in pumps that still remember the hands of extinct engineers. It does not sleep. It idles. It adjusts.

Its roots are its streets, but also its nerves. A footstep travels farther than sound through the old rootwood. A crowd is felt as warmth and pressure. A disused quarter becomes numb, then thoughtful, then begins to fold inward and digest the idea of having been inhabited. The city grows conservatively. It prefers repair to expansion, thickening an arch, narrowing a stair, healing a window until only greenish light can pass through. When it builds something new, it does so with the grave patience of an institution issuing a permit to itself.

It feeds on weather, minerals, work, and memory.

The factories are among its organs. They breathe for it, refine it, wound it, and are repaired by it. Their resin presses are not separate from the groves that supply them; their belts are lined with cambium; their electric hum runs into leaf and root. Some machinery remains stubbornly metallic, half-swallowed but still functional, like old bones retained because they are useful. Gears turn in wooden housings. Copper veins run beside sap veins. Fungal furnaces burn without flame beneath vats of luminous resin. The city has not rejected industry. It has metabolized it.

There are people living there, but they are not masters of the place. They are custodians, interpreters, tenants, parasites, priests, mechanics, and sometimes food for rumor.

They are thin from altitude and weather, broad-shouldered from climbing, with faces wind-reddened above respirator marks. Their clothes are practical and layered: waxed canvas, rubber, wool, old factory leather, gloves stiffened by sap, hoods with pollen filters, harnesses hung with pruning hooks and diagnostic rods. Almost everyone carries a small mallet or listening pin for tapping walls. Many have a city-map tattooed or scarred somewhere on the body, though no map remains accurate for long.

They walk carefully. Nobody who lives there hurries unless machinery is failing or a district is shifting. They know which plazas lengthen in fog, which staircases become ambitious after rain, which doors should never be opened while grieving. Children learn routes the way other children learn prayers. They are taught to greet intersections, not out of superstition exactly, but because intersections remember discourtesy.

Their homes are warm pockets inside larger living masses. A family may occupy three rooms in what was once an apartment block and is now the fruiting shoulder of a municipal vine. Their walls thicken in winter. Their ceilings drip during pressure changes. Their windows are translucent membranes that must be rubbed with oil to keep them from clouding over. Furniture is tied down or grown in place. Beds rest on platforms that lift slightly before storms. Cupboards sometimes seal themselves if food spoils inside them, and sometimes if secrets are spoken too often nearby.

They eat what the city permits and what they can cultivate without provoking it. Root-starch. Sour leaf-bread. Pale fungi raised in warm ducts. Resin-sweets chewed in cold weather. Thin milk from the nearly invisible hoofed animals that graze on the oxidized moss-squares at dusk. Bitter teas made from corrective herbs. Factory workers drink broth blackened with iron and pollen to keep their lungs from closing during the yellow months.

They are not numerous. There are perhaps enough people to maintain a quarter of the city, and so the city has learned to maintain the rest of itself. Many occupations are less about making things than negotiating with ongoing growth.

There are pruners of public ways, who cut back impatient walls and train bridges not to sag into canals that no longer exist. There are pressure-listeners, who descend into the vascular galleries with brass gauges, ear trumpets, and long knives in case a sap-main spasms. There are bark-readers in the civic grove, educated in law, botany, weather, and the grammar of healing scars. There are rot-callers, fungal engineers, cistern midwives, rail-root conductors, membrane glaziers, pollen wardens, shadow surveyors, and clerks whose only duty is to record which streets were present that morning.

The bark-readers are the nearest thing to officials. They do not govern so much as translate. The civic grove issues decrees by thickening, splitting, discoloring, flowering, or healing over previous law. A decree may read: reduce resin extraction in the eastern wards. It may read: no bells after second fog. It may read: children are not to approach the pale curb in Saint Orra’s Square until the year has finished repeating. Sometimes it produces maps of neighborhoods not yet grown, and then surveyors go there with string and poles, waiting for the streets to arrive.

The residents argue with the grove. They file petitions by cutting shallow marks into designated bark and filling them with soot, salt, or blood depending on urgency. The grove does not answer quickly. Sometimes it answers by growing a bench. Sometimes by sealing a stair. Sometimes by dropping a seedpod containing, folded in its wet interior, a perfectly formed brass key to a door nobody remembers.

The people are affectionate toward the city, but their affection is wary. They know it has saved them from cold, famine, erosion, and the collapse of old infrastructures. They also know it has absorbed cemeteries, schools, warehouses, and entire families who stayed too long in rooms that were becoming something else.

Their dead are not buried in soil. There is almost no ordinary soil left. The dead are given to remembrance beds in the civic groves or to private wall-niches lined with root hair. Over years, the names of the dead appear as grain patterns, leaf discolorations, odd blooms, or brief voices in the pipes during rain. Ancestors are therefore not gone. They become inconveniently municipal.

There are children, though fewer than there should be. They are loud in the covered walks, bright-coated, sure-footed, and unafraid of heights. They play at being lost, which adults hate. They dare one another to step on seams between root-plates, to knock on sealed blossoms, to whisper insults into old chimneys and wait for steam to answer. They keep moths in jars and trade immature seed-capsules like toys. They know small shortcuts that adults deny exist. Some children are born with a sensitivity to the city’s pressure changes and cry before streets shift; these are apprenticed early, treated gently, and watched with unease.

Some adults are changed by long residence.

Their skin may develop faint greenish undertones at the joints, not from plant blood but from years of pollen exposure and medicinal resin. Their hair catches spores. Their lungs become trained to filters. The oldest maintenance workers can sleep through factory thunder but wake instantly when a root-pump misses half a beat. A few people grow what are politely called adhesions: patches where callused skin bonds too eagerly with bark or cane. Such people wear waxed wrappings and avoid leaning against walls. They are pitied, honored, and quietly feared, because they often begin to know things before the bark-readers do.

There are also those who have given themselves further.

They live in maintenance shrines deep under the civic grove, in warm chambers where conduits pulse like throats. Their bodies are supported by harnesses and root-cables. They speak rarely with their own mouths. Instead, messages arrive from them as knocks in pipes, as changes in lamp-veins, as warning blossoms opening above dangerous bridges. They are called anchors, though whether they anchor the city to the present or the present to the city is a matter of doctrine. Some entered the role willingly. Some were rescued from accidents and could not be fully separated from the infrastructure afterward. They are consulted during temporal irregularities, flood routing, and elections.

There are animals, but they live as if under treaty.

The pale moths are drawn to lamp-veins and electrical flowers. Their wings are almost transparent, marked with patterns like circuit diagrams or leaf skeletons. They appear before machinery failure. A cloud of them around a pump-house means evacuation; a single moth in a bedroom means someone has left a light on inside the wall.

The small wingless birds nest in hollow signs and station markers. They are not truly flightless; they move through air by falling sideways from one surface to another, vanishing into cracks with triangular flicks of shadow. They eat pollen clots and tiny mineral flakes. Their calls resemble distant telegraph keys. Children imitate them. Engineers listen to them, because they stop calling before streets buckle.

The thin hoofed things are seen mostly at dusk. They are tall as deer but narrow as reeds, with translucent ears and black hooves split into delicate rootlike toes. They graze on copper moss and lick condensed minerals from rail lines. No one hunts them. When one dies, its bones are found threaded with green filaments, already being archived by the city. Some say they are native to the old mountain before the city. Others say they are escaped livestock that the vegetation redesigned for steepness and silence.

There are insects in hidden places, but never enough. Their scarcity is part of the city’s strangeness. Pollination is handled by wind, pressure shifts, mechanical vibration, and perhaps by memory. Flowers open when certain names are spoken. Seedpods mature beside busy stairways. Spores release during factory sirens. The city does not need the usual messengers of life. It has replaced them with procedure.

Fungi live there in great number, though mostly unseen. They occupy furnace pits, sealed apartments, damp undersides of bridges, and the cavities around swallowed brick. They are not decay alone; they are accountants of decay. They decide what becomes heat, what becomes medicine, what becomes warning color, what becomes structural reinforcement. Factory mycologists court them with ash, warmth, and old paper records. Some fungal colonies glow faintly in the dark under root-streets, forming maps of moisture and stress. Others produce narcotic dust used only in surgery, prophecy, and certain forms of civil arbitration.

There are also older inhabitants that may not be alive in any standard sense.

Time lives there badly.

It inhabits worn curbs, pale walls, upper staircases, puddles, unused platforms, and places where shadows disagree. It behaves like a scavenger and like weather. In some districts it gnaws things down to smoothness. In others it preserves a fallen leaf for months, or returns a tool as the sound of labor rather than the tool itself. The residents treat temporal thinness like rot, avalanche risk, or contagious illness. They mark it with white fiber ropes. They test it with sacrificial nails. They do not let children play near it, which of course ensures that children will try.

Memories also live there, but not as ghosts exactly.

A railway platform may remember crowds so strongly that on cold mornings the air fills with body warmth and the smell of wool, though no one is visible. A factory stair may repeat the bootfalls of a worker who died before anyone now alive was born. Certain apartments continue to set aside alcoves for furniture long removed. Doorways retain expectations. If enough people believe a passage should lead to the cistern market, it may do so, at least while the belief remains fresh.

This makes the city partly democratic and partly haunted. Consensus alters circulation. Habit shortens roads. Grief deranges entrances. Public memory is infrastructure.

The loudspeakers in the seedpods have voices of their own. Some are official: weather reports, quotas, pollen advisories, warnings of district contraction. Others are old recordings tangled in the vascular network. They announce trains that no longer run, festivals last held centuries ago, evacuation orders from disasters since absorbed into civic myth. Occasionally a loudspeaker will whisper a private name into an empty plaza. When this happens, the named person is expected to report to the nearest bark-reader, not because punishment is certain, but because the city has noticed them.

The city’s plant matter is not a single intelligence in the human sense. It is more like an enormous habit that has become aware of itself through repetition. It knows flow, pressure, load, heat, moisture, absence. It knows where people walk and where they have stopped walking. It knows which rooms are loved. It knows which machines are essential because they hurt when they fail. It does not understand loneliness, but it produces empty plazas. It does not understand law, but it grows decrees. It does not understand death, but it keeps names.

If asked who rules there, the residents might laugh.

The grove rules. The wind rules. The factories rule. The maintenance guilds rule. The old rails rule wherever they still pull themselves straight. The cistern-flowers rule during drought. The pollen calendar rules all lungs. The mountain rules by continuing, slowly, to wear away beneath everything.

And beneath even that, perhaps, lives the original city: the one of stone and copper and scaffolds, swallowed but not erased. It persists as buried arches, rusted girders, brick corridors under roots, a street grid the plants still obey without knowing why. Its dead plans are trellises for living matter. Its utilities are ancestral veins. Its civic intentions have become botanical instincts.

So what lives there is a population of survivors inside a survivor.

Humans in coats and respirators. Moths like failed sparks. Hoofed silhouettes. Fungi with municipal duties. Machines with wooden lungs. Buildings that heal. Roads that remember. Laws that bud and scar. An old mountain still eroding upward into sky. And above all, a vast green civic body, neither wild nor civilized, continuing its work in cold air: breathing, manufacturing, recording, warning, absorbing, and slowly deciding what a city is after stone has gone.
