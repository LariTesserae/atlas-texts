The ones who live there are not merely townspeople. They are keepers of a pause in the mountain, and they have learned to behave as though every wall, root, animal path, dripline, and breath of vapor has equal claim to the place.

They are a small, quiet-bodied people, narrow-shouldered from climbing and strong in the hands. Their feet know stone better than their eyes do. They move with the soft, testing gait of those who have grown up above drops, on wet ledges, among surfaces that may not remain surfaces tomorrow. Even the children place their weight carefully. Before they can count, they are taught to tap rock with a staff, to feel for hollowness under paving, to notice when moss has grown too thick over a crack, to distrust new smoothness in stone.

Their faces tend to be still, but not blank. Expression lives in their eyes, fingers, shoulders, the tilt of the head. They have many small gestures for things that elsewhere would be shouted: fog rising wrong, bird fear, loose shale, a sleeping snake, a child too near an animal corridor, a cistern crab under a basket. Their speech is low and close to the mouth. Outsiders think them secretive until they understand that the settlement itself punishes loud certainty. A raised voice seems to go nowhere; it is swallowed by moss, broken by leaves, given back as unease.

They dress as if every day is half-rain. Bark cloth softened by beating. Felted fiber under oil-dark cloaks. Short capes that shed mist from the shoulders. Sleeves tied tight for climbing. Ankles wrapped against leeches, spores, and cold stone. Children wear little bells only until they learn balance; afterward the bells are taken away and hung on the silent shrines, where they rust without ringing. Adults carry knives rarely, and when they do the blades are small, treasured things wrapped in dry leaf and bone.

Their hair is often bound close, braided with polished seeds, tooth charms, shells from trade, or tiny beads of hammered copper. Copper is not wealth exactly. It is weather captured in metal. It flashes too brightly for everyday use, so it appears at thresholds, on ritual tools, at the throats of elders, on the handles of instruments used near the well. Most ordinary things are made of what the settlement can persuade into usefulness: horn spoons, shell scrapers, stone awls, bone needles, vine rope, baskets sealed with wax and resin, hinges of leather gone dark from handling.

They are builders, but not conquerors. Their houses show the shape of their thinking. No wall is made without considering what must pass beside it, beneath it, through it, or over it. A good house does not merely keep people safe; it avoids offending the routes of frogs, birds, crabs, pollinating moths, roof-deer, and the unnamed small things that travel at night. Children are praised not for building high, but for leaving the correct gaps.

Among them are water-callers, though they would dislike that name. They do not summon water. They arrange themselves to receive it. At dawn they go out to the upper terraces and wring the night from nets of hair and plant fiber. They scrape pearls of condensation from stone lips into jars. They adjust broad leaves so droplets run into channels. They know which roof-shingle sweats first, which cliff seam gives bitter water, which lichen means a jar has gone bad, which morning mist will feed the gardens and which will only wet the skin.

There are gardeners of bowls and crevices. They plant in small, deliberate pockets: tubers in waxed leaf-liners, pale greens beneath slatted shade, vine fruits along bone-colored poles, herbs in old cracks where roof warmth lingers. They do not think of soil as ground. Soil is a substance one carries, protects, divides, inherits, mourns. Some families own almost no stone but possess deep baskets of black earth tended for generations. To spill such soil is a sorrow. To steal it is worse than theft.

There are also death-gardeners, who tend the high crevices where the wrapped dead are placed. They climb in pairs, never speaking above a whisper, carrying moss, bark cloth, and root cuttings. They trim the living mats that enter the shrouds. They know which ancestors feed fern, which feed bitter fruit, which have become tangles of white root and flowering vine. They do not regard this as horror. It is how the town remembers with leaves. During famine or festival, fruit from these gardens is eaten slowly, with names spoken between bites.

Their elders are less rulers than interpreters of balance. They sit in the meeting house under the folded-leaf roof, listening to reports: a bridge-vine sagging too much, birds refusing a roofline, too many blind fish drawn from the well, the Patient One stripping bark higher than usual, new frost on an inner wall, children dreaming the same green chamber. Decisions are made carefully, not because the people are indecisive, but because every action touches too many lives. A wall moved one pace may change the path of crabs, which changes the algae, which changes the cisterns, which changes the frogs, which changes the striped predators, which changes where children may sleep.

They have hunters, but hunting here is closer to petition than pursuit. Eggs are taken from cliff nests by strict memory: one marked nest, one visit, never greed. Deer are not driven into traps unless the winter has been cruel. Birds are almost never killed. Frogs may be gathered after certain rains, but white roof-frogs are left alone if they have nested in supports, for a house with frogs is considered watched. Fish from the dry well are not eaten immediately. They are placed first in black bowls. If they live until morning, they are returned. If they die, they may be cooked with moon fern and shared only among those who dreamed the previous night.

Their children grow up among too many eyes. Infants sleep in hanging nets where crabs cannot reach and where snakes must make themselves visible. Toddlers are tied to long waist cords when fog thickens, not because they run away, but because paths sometimes do. Children learn the language of silent birds before they learn all adult speech. A raised crest means wait. A flock folding itself downward means cover the jars. Tail-left, tail-right, stillness, ripple, vanish: these are lessons as important as names.

They play at building miniature settlements from slate chips and moss, but the best child builders leave animal corridors through their toys. They drop lost teeth into the well and listen for the impossible landing. They dare one another to sleep near the plaza rim and wake with images. The brave claim to have seen the upside-down city beneath the mountain. The wiser ones say nothing for several days.

Dreamers hold an uneasy place among them. Everyone dreams, but not everyone gathers weather from the well. Those who do wake with certain images lodged behind their teeth: antlered fish, black stones sweating stars, rooms full of roots breathing like lungs, a second town hanging below this one with its people walking on the underside of slate. The dreamers are not treated as prophets. Prophets are dangerous. Instead they are treated like people who have stood too long in rain. They are warmed, fed salt, asked what clung to them, and told which parts to let dry away.

There are shrine-keepers, though their shrines are modest: cliff cuts, niches for snakes, masks hung where paths divide, stones stacked at places where the ground once breathed. They do not worship a single god of the mountain. Their reverence is more practical and more intimate. They honor agreements. The agreement with falling stone. The agreement with roots. The agreement with the well. The agreement with the Patient One. The agreement with the dead, who are still feeding the town. The agreement with silence, which must not be mistaken for peace.

For the people are not alone there.

The settlement is also inhabited by moths like folded scraps of old wall, waking at first mist to gather around warmth. They climb the air in pale columns and dust roof beams with powder fine as ash. The townspeople read their density as others read smoke. Too many moths at one chimney means fever inside. No moths at all means something colder than weather has passed nearby.

Pale crabs live in the cisterns, patient and mineral-colored. Their claws are delicate but persistent. They graze algae, clean stone, dismantle baskets for salt, and appear after rain as if the town’s water has grown legs. Children are taught to step around them. Adults curse them softly and repair what they ruin. No one kills them without reason, because cisterns that lose their crabs sour quickly.

There are roof-frogs, white as knucklebone, blinking from beam hollows and cistern mouths. They emerge at dusk, soft-throated and nearly soundless, eating the insects that would otherwise spoil stored fruit. A family with many frogs in its roof is considered fortunate, though also damp.

There are translucent-throated lizards under the vine bridges, their bodies flat against green shade, pulsing with swallowed light. There are beetles that cross thresholds in solemn processions and are stepped over with the same courtesy given to cracks in sacred stone. There are waist-high deer with blackened lips, moving through courtyards like shy thoughts. They belong to no one, yet some return to the same sleeping walls for years and are recognized by scar, ear notch, or the pattern of fern dust on their muzzles.

There are the silent birds, perhaps the true citizens of the upper town. They gather in impossible numbers without song. Their communication is fan, crest, throat, wing, angle. They fill rafters, cliffs, rooflines, and plaza edges with judgmental stillness. They watch the well at midnight in rows. They know before anyone when stone will fall. They abandon roofs before sickness appears. They gather over births, deaths, and certain arguments, not interfering, only marking that the event has entered the larger attention of the place.

There are arboreal long-tailed creatures in the beams and upper limbs, clever-handed and solemn, passing their infants hand to hand above doorways. They steal fruit but also warn of snakes. They untie knots if bored. Some families leave them puzzle-bundles of vine and seed to keep them from dismantling roof lashings. When one dies, its troop may carry the body through the high paths for a day before leaving it in a death-garden not meant for humans. The gardeners do not remove it.

There are striped predators that descend near dawn along vine bridges, thin and offended-looking, hunting frogs and careless small animals. They are not evil. They are hunger shaped for silence. If one enters a sleeping room, bitter-root powder sends it away, insulted but obedient to the old boundary.

And below, in the ravine’s wet blue throat, lives the Patient One.

No one agrees on its shape. Some say it is bear-like, but with too long a face. Some say it is a sloth of the first forest, not finished waking. Some say it is not one creature but the ravine itself growing limbs when fog is thick enough. It climbs without sound. It leaves long wet hairs on branches and frost on stones it breathes against. It eats lower gardens to the soil, never breaking the baskets or stakes, as if it understands ownership but not restraint. The people feed it extra tubers, not as tribute exactly, but as maintenance. An unfed hunger becomes architecture’s problem.

Other things live there too, less named.

Handprints appear in mud around the pools, too delicate to belong to any seen animal. Something drinks droplets from the undersides of leaves without disturbing the leaves themselves. Something opens passages through fern walls and closes them before morning. Blind fish arrive from a well with no water. New cracks exhale air that smells of caves no one has entered. The people do not hurry to classify these presences. Naming too quickly is considered a kind of rudeness.

The inhabitants, human and otherwise, form a society without a single center, though the plaza pretends to be one. The true life of the town is distributed through routes: water routes, animal routes, root routes, dream routes, death routes, fog routes, paths taken only by children, paths taken only by crabs, paths no person uses after dusk. Every creature is part citizen, part trespasser. Every house is both shelter and interruption. Every night the settlement renegotiates its right to remain.

The people know they are outnumbered. This does not humiliate them. It instructs them.

They are not timid, but they are careful. They are not primitive in mind, only spare in material. Their intelligence is in restraint: the unblocked hole, the slanted stone, the quiet hinge, the roof that feeds a jar, the garden planted for a creature that might otherwise climb too high. They have survived by understanding that dominance would fail here. The mountain is too young. The vegetation too eager. The animals too numerous. The well too deep in directions other than down.

So they live as negotiators in a wet, unfinished world.

At dusk they close doors imperfectly. They leave offerings where offerings are useful. They lift children into nets. They cover bowls against crabs. They watch birds, moths, mist, bridges, roofs, and the behavior of darkness. They listen not for noise, but for imbalance.

And all around them, the town fills with its other residents: fur over beams, claws in cisterns, wings under eaves, roots in the dead, fish in the airless well, unseen weight on the lower paths, and the cold breath of the mountain moving through every carefully left gap.
