What lives there has learned not to rise.

The chief inhabitants are low-bodied, wet-skinned things, neither wholly of water nor wholly of land, shaped by the same patience that has softened the stones. They keep close to the ground, bellies almost touching mud, their limbs spread wide and jointed for purchase on slick rock, silt, and flooded grass. From a distance they are difficult to separate from the country itself: a dark stone back, a mound of sedge, a log softened by rot, a patch of shadow at the edge of a pool.

Only when they move does the eye understand them.

They move slowly most of the time, with a careful, spreading gait, placing broad webbed feet as if testing the memory of the earth. Their toes are long and flexible, able to grip stone seams, stir mud, or float them through water only a hand deep. They do not leap like frogs or run like deer. Sudden height is not in them. Their escape is downward, sideways, into glare, into reflection, into the confusion between wet rock and wet hide.

Their bodies are broad and flattened, with strong shoulders and hips hidden under thick folds of skin. Along the back lies a series of low plates, not hard armor exactly, but dense, leathery shields mottled blue-gray, peat-brown, copper, and algae-green. The oldest of them carry stains of the plain upon themselves: mineral pale at the ridges, blackened in the hollows, sometimes filmed with living green. Tiny snails rasp at them without alarm. Insects land on them. Their stillness invites small life.

Their heads are wide and blunt, set low between the forelimbs. The eyes sit high, amber or dark gold, with a horizontal calm to them. When the animal rests submerged, only those eyes and the soft valves of the nostrils show above the pool, making three small interruptions in the reflected sky. They can remain that way for a very long time. Breath comes rarely. A bubble may tremble loose from the mud near the mouth and rise, and that is all.

Their skin must stay wet. It is not slimy so much as continually damp, able to drink from the air, from mud, from stone sweat. In dry heat they retreat to shaded seams and black hollows, pressing themselves into clay until the clay receives their shape. During long rain they emerge everywhere, as though the plain has remembered them and brought them back to its surface.

They feed without ceremony. Their mouths are soft at the edges but lined within by rough plates and small backward teeth. They graze algae from stone. They nose among sedge roots for worms. They suck snails from their shells, crush crablike burrowers, swallow larvae, fish, eggs, carrion softened by warmth. They are not hunters in the swift sense. They harvest what seepage gathers. A dying fish in a shrinking pan, a drowned nest of insects, a trail of eggs under fernlike leaves — these are enough. Their lives are made from small things collected patiently.

At dusk they call.

The sound is deeper than the frogs, less frantic, and seems at first to come from the mud itself: a hollow wooden gulp, then a tremor, then a long vibrating note held in the throat pouch until the nearby water rings. One begins in a hollow. Another answers beyond a rib of stone. A third replies from so far off it is almost horizon. Their calls travel through air, water, and saturated ground, passing beneath the shrill insect weather and the harsh voices of birds.

They do not gather in crowds except after rain. Then, when old pools join and channels wake, they follow the taste of fresh water over stone. They come from cracks, pans, reed shadows, and shallow burrows, appearing one by one across the shining plain. Males settle in low basins and call until their throats swell pale and translucent. Females move between them with deliberate care, choosing not the loudest voice but the safest water: warm, mineral-rich, neither too open to birds nor too quick to vanish.

Their eggs are laid in strings and soft clusters, fastened under stones, among sedge roots, or in the flooded cracks where silt has collected. The eggs are clear brown beads, each with a dark comma of life curled inside. Many are eaten. Wading birds find them. Fish find them. Dragonfly larvae and crabs find them. But enough remain.

The young hatch as small, transparent swimmers with feathery gills and black pin eyes. At first they are no more than flickers in amber water, feeding on films and motes. Their bodies grow heavier as the pools warm. Limbs bud, broaden, and strengthen. Their gills shrink. They begin to breathe the thick air. When their pool dries too quickly, many die and return at once to mud. When it lasts, they crawl out one wet morning as thumb-length versions of the elders, already mottled like stone.

They grow slowly. Some live many seasons. The oldest are large enough that a heron will step around them rather than risk the snap of their jaws. Raptors take the small ones. Snakes, fish, and sharp-billed birds take the young. The great adults have fewer enemies, not because they are fierce, but because they are bitter, heavy, and difficult to kill. Their skin carries a mineral, sour taste drawn from the old wet ground. A predator that bites one often remembers.

They are social, but not possessive. They know one another by throat sound, scent, and the pressure of movement through water. Two meeting at a pool may touch nostrils, push flank to flank, or rest together half-submerged for an afternoon without further exchange. They do not hold territories with violence. The plain itself makes territory uncertain. A hollow is good one week and gone the next. A channel belongs to whoever can breathe there when the water lies in it.

Their memory is not of landmarks in the human sense. It is a memory of dampness.

They remember which stone sweats longest after rain. Which dark basin keeps tadpoles alive into the hot months. Which clay seals well around the body during drought. Which rib of rock warms first at morning. Which sedge roots shelter eggs from probing beaks. They carry maps made of taste, temperature, pressure, and season. An old female may cross miles of open wet stone to return to a pan she used years before, not because it looks the same, but because the minerals in the water say the same thing to her tongue.

If they have wisdom, it is not a wisdom that makes tools or walls. It is a wisdom of waiting correctly.

They do not build. They do not stack, carve, or clear. Their shelters are found, pressed, widened by use, and abandoned when water changes its mind. A resting hollow shaped by one generation may hold another, but no one made it with intention sharp enough to be called construction. Their bodies leave impressions that rain smooths. Their trails are low, vague darkenings between pools. Their whole civilization, if it can be called that, dissolves behind them.

Yet they are not simple.

They listen constantly. A footfall far off travels through wet stone. Wing-shadow changes the light. The splash of a fish, the bubble of gas, the thin alarm of shorebirds, the silence after a frog stops calling — all of it enters them. They live in a world where distance is open but concealment is everywhere, where danger can be seen coming from far away and still arrive as a beak from above. Their stillness is attention, not emptiness.

In the hottest part of the day they disappear. The plain seems to belong then to insects, birds, and mirrors of sky. But beneath the reflected clouds, under ledges of softened rock, in black mud and root shade, the low ones wait with slow hearts. Their backs darken. Their eyes film over. Water passes across their skin molecule by molecule. They endure the afternoon as the stones endure it.

Evening returns them.

First one head rises in a pool gone bronze with sunset. Then another shape detaches itself from a slick rock plate. A small one crosses open mud, leaving a chain of handlike prints that fill at once with water. An old male lifts his throat and begins the first deep note. Frogs answer above him. Insects thicken the air. Birds make their last harsh calls. The plain’s many voices braid together, and the low ones become part of the under-singing, the oldest-sounding part, as if the land itself has learned to breathe through them.

They are creatures of the threshold: between fish and walker, stone and mud, silence and call, patience and appetite. They do not own the low country. They are one of the ways the low country continues. They rise from its wet hollows, wear its colors, speak in its damp dusk, lay their young in its temporary waters, and sink back whenever sun, drought, or danger asks them to vanish.

To see one clearly is to feel that it has not adapted to the plain so much as been slowly persuaded into existence by it.

A body flattened by ages.

A voice made for distance.

A skin that remembers rain.

A life that does not resist seepage, but belongs to it.
