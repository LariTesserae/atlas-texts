What lives there has learned not to interrupt the water.

The first inhabitants are the builders, though they do not know themselves as such. They are the plateau-plants: black-green, slow-veined, saltless in taste but full of minerals, rooted into their own dead centuries. They are not trees, not kelp, not coral, though they borrow from all three. Their trunks braid, harden, hollow, fuse, and roof over. Their roots do not simply hold the ground; they are the ground. Each living surface is laid over an older one, and beneath that another, and beneath that another, until the mountain is only a rumor under plant memory.

They grow with a kind of enormous courtesy. No stem expands without leaving a throat for current. No wall closes without keeping pores. If a young mass swells too thick, its inner tissues starve, and transparent borers arrive to lace it with tunnels. If it survives, it survives as architecture: ribbed, breathable, inhabited. The plateau-plants flower rarely, and not beautifully. Their blossoms are blind knots that open in crevices, slick with taste and faint charge. Passing mouths pollinate them without knowing. Some seeds are released into the current and go over the rim. Others are kept inside the walls for ages, dark and hard as buried eyes.

Their living parts pulse slowly beneath pale mineral bark. A wound made by a grazer may remain visible longer than a human civilization would endure, closing by fractions, ringed in bruised green light. Their oldest columns are awake only in strips, with dead white buttresses around a central thread of life. They do not move except by growth, but growth, here, is enough to count as motion.

Among them live the grazers.

They are long, low animals, armored in overlapping plates the color of old ivory and wet slate. Their bodies are shaped to the terraces and roads: flat below, arched above, with many short limbs hidden under the shell-rim, each limb ending in soft gripping pads. They move in processions too slow to seem deliberate, following routes etched into their species by hunger and inheritance. Their heads are blunt. Their mouths are circular rasping discs, ringed with stone-hard teeth that renew from within like root growth.

They feed on the living skin of the plateau. They do not destroy it. They polish it.

Where they pass, they leave oval pale scars, smooth and clean, each one a bite taken from the surface of the world. The scars dim green at the edges and begin the long work of closing. Small animals come after to lick the exposed sap. Veils of pale fungus settle there. Tiny bell-creatures attach to the rims. In this way a grazer’s meal becomes a neighborhood.

The oldest grazers carry gardens on their backs. Their shells are grooved and pitted, and in those grooves grow miniature versions of the plateau itself: thread-plants, bead-fronds, little hooked roots, translucent snails, colonies of trembling cups. A great grazer crossing an empty plain may seem, from above, like a moving island with its own forest and weather.

There are fish, though many are barely fish anymore.

The common ones are pale and processional, with long fins like torn cloth and eyes reduced to cloudy pearls. They feel the world with their skin. Their flanks are lined with pressure organs so sensitive they can read the difference between a root-arch, a moving animal, and a distant collapse by the shape of the water’s touch. They travel along terrace edges and through colonnades in loose, ritual-seeming lines, never schooling tightly, never panicking. A sudden movement would be wasteful. Panic belongs to brighter waters.

Some browse on plankton-fog in the circular basins. Some pick at fungal veils. Some wait at warm seams in the plant-floor—not heat, exactly, but a subtle metabolic softness—where larvae emerge from pores. These waiting fish are narrow and patient, with jaws that open like folded leaves. They may spend half a season beside one seam. Their young learn the same locations not by teaching but by drifting into the same currents and surviving there.

In the hollow towers live the tube-eels.

They are black as the water behind them, soft-bodied, nearly boneless, each one fitted to its reed-tube like a thought fitted to a habit. During rest they hang vertically, head upward or downward depending on the local flow, their jaws slightly open. They breathe the current and listen through the walls. Their bodies are marked by faint blue pinpricks that brighten only when another eel passes near.

They hunt by absence. A small fish enters a tube for shelter; the eel withdraws deeper, making the hollow seem empty. Then the current changes around the fish. The wall becomes mouth. The darkness folds. Nothing thrashes for long.

Yet even they are slow. A tube-eel may eat once and remain swollen for months, digesting in the dark while seed-cases tap against its chamber like distant knuckles.

The ceilings belong to the glass crustaceans.

They cling upside down from root-vaults and bridge undersides, jointed and transparent, their organs suspended inside them like colored smoke. Their legs are long combs. They unfold them into the current to sieve out drifting matter: spores, flakes of plant-skin, eggs, dust of shell, fragments of dead frond softened by years. When the water thickens, they feed. When it thins, they fold themselves into angles and become nearly invisible.

Their young are carried in beads beneath the abdomen, each bead with a dark speck inside. When released, the larvae drift upward, downward, sideways—direction means little here—until they catch on a film or are eaten. The survivors molt many times, leaving perfect transparent ghosts attached to ceilings. In some root-vaults the molts are layered so deep they resemble frost.

The bridges are lined with bell-animals.

They are small, soft, and colonial, shaped like inverted cups with fringed mouths. Their bodies are anchored to the underside of plant arches by threads stronger than they look. Most of the time they simply drink the passing water. But when pressure changes, when a large body passes nearby, when distant collapse sends a slow shock through the plateau, they ring.

Not loudly. Not like metal. More like the tremor of wet glass.

A whole bridge may answer a passing shadow with a faint chiming that travels from one end to the other. Other animals heed this. Fish alter course. Eels retract. Crustaceans fold. The bell-colonies do not mean to warn anyone. They are only made in such a way that the world plays them.

There are snails in the basin fields, pale and deliberate, with shells grown in low spirals to resist the current. They graze plankton-fog and bacterial films from the sunken rings of ancient trunks. Their mouths move constantly, making clean trails through the dimness. Some carry luminous specks in the folds of their mantles, not for display but digestion; the specks help break down old plant starches. When the snails gather in numbers, a basin glows faintly from within, like breath behind a closed hand.

There are many-legged road-followers, neither shrimp nor centipede, each with a narrow plated body and a procession of hooked feet. They live on root-roads and almost never leave them. Their entire sense of the world is longitudinal: forward along the road, backward along the road, food-film beneath, current overhead. Their backs are patterned with pale bars that match the seams of the floor, so a resting one disappears into the architecture.

They are eaten by the alcove mouths.

The alcove mouths are animals, though at first they seem like plant deformities: dark openings in walls, fringed with soft cilia, surrounded by mineralized lips. Inside each is a sack-body lodged deep in the architecture, fed by what passes close enough. They can wait for years. They taste the footsteps of road-followers, the scrape of snail shells, the tremor of larvae. When the right motion comes, the lip folds outward with unbearable slowness, widening, widening, until a patch of wall has become appetite.

Generations of prey die in the same alcoves. Generations of mouths inherit the same favorable hollows. Nothing needs to chase when the roads themselves deliver food.

Above the plateau hang the great filter-feeders.

They are not attached, though they may remain in one volume of water for so long that the plants grow in response to their shade. Their bodies are broad and ribbed, soft-edged, dark on top and pale beneath. Their mouths are vast permanent openings veiled with combs. The current enters them and leaves altered. Plankton, spores, larvae, fine rot, invisible nourishment—all of it is taken. What they cannot use falls as slow pellets that feed the terraces below.

They move by changing buoyancy and by a rare, solemn flex of their side-fins. To see one cross a colonnade is to misunderstand scale for a while. First there is only a deeper darkness between pillars. Then a row of cold internal lights appears, not shining outward but revealing chambers within the body. Then the pressure arrives. Fronds lean. Bell-colonies ring. Fish sink toward the floor. The grazer-processions pause without quite stopping.

These animals may live for millennia. Scars on their hides become ecosystems. Small eels nest in old wounds. Crustaceans clean their gill-combs. Thread-plants sometimes root in the calluses along their backs, feeding on waste until the animal sheds the patch of skin and the little forest falls away into the plateau below.

At the margins live the rim-watchers.

They are muscular, wedge-headed fish with wide mouths and fins adapted less for swimming than for holding position. They face the outflow where the plateau-water leans into the abyss. Their bodies tremble continuously in the rush. Their jaws gape, catching whatever the summit gives up: spores, larvae, fragments of frond, dying plankton, the occasional small animal swept too close to the edge.

They do not cross the rim. Their eggs are glued to roots just inland, in braided nurseries where the current is fierce but not fatal. Young rim-watchers begin life facing inward, feeding in protected slots. As they grow, they move outward by degrees, occupying stronger and stronger water, until they take their adult places at the edge of the world, staring forever into the lower dark.

Some are taken from below.

No one living on the plateau sees clearly what takes them. There is only a downward pull, a sudden loss of vibration, a widening blackness under the root-curtains. The rim-watchers do not flee. They adjust their fins and continue feeding. The abyss has its own laws, and the summit animals know the border by pressure, not fear.

There are also the quiet symbionts, without which the whole country would fail.

They live inside plant tissues, in animal guts, in the films coating old bark, in the pale veils that soften the dead. They are fungi-like, bacteria-like, neither and both. They digest what cold would otherwise preserve forever. They turn fallen fronds into pasture, carcasses into green seams, waste into mineral sweetness. Decay here is not rot in the warm sense. It is a slow negotiation. A dead thing remains itself for a long time while becoming useful by layers.

A fallen grazer may be visited for centuries. First the blind fish take the eyes and soft vents. Then glass crustaceans comb the loosened tissues. Then veils cover the shell seams. Then thread-plants root in the plates. Then snails polish the new growth. In the end the body is indistinguishable from a low bridge, and small animals pass beneath it.

If there is anything like intelligence here, it is distributed among habits.

No single creature appears to govern, remember, or plan. Yet the place behaves as though remembering is spread through routes, closures, scars, currents, and appetites. The grazers keep the plazas open. The plants answer wounds with form. The bell-animals translate pressure into tremor. The fish preserve pathways by using them. The eels maintain hollows by inhabiting them. The filter-feeders lift abundance out of darkness and return it as rain. Each life is narrow, patient, and exact; together they make a world that seems intentional from far enough away.

There may be one resident older than the others.

It lives under the woven floor, or partly within it, or perhaps it is only a misreading of many organisms acting together. In the root-vaults, where the roar is loudest, there are places where the plant matter responds too coherently to be ordinary growth. A corridor narrows after being overused. A blocked flume opens by the death of a wall. Seed chambers ripen along newly favorable currents. Animals avoid certain terraces before collapses happen. The plateau seems, in those districts, to feel its own hydraulics.

One could call this being the Underweave, though it has no center. It is the sum of roots fused through roots, fungal veils through cambium, old vascular channels still carrying chemical messages across distances no animal travels. Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, take centuries to complete. Hunger might appear as a new basin. Pain as a luminous seam. Memory as a road kept open long after the herd that made it has changed shape.

It does not know the animals as individuals. It knows pressures, losses, blockages, returns. It knows where water hesitates. It knows where light, however faint, can be conserved. It knows the rim by the continual subtraction of itself.

The creatures of this drowned height are therefore not dramatic. They do not leap, swarm, sing, or build fires. They glow only enough to locate one another in the dark. They kill without haste and feed without greed. They live as if interruption were the only sin.

They are pale processions, black sleepers in tubes, glass harvesters on ceilings, armored gardeners, ringing colonies, patient mouths, immense drifting sieves, and plants so old they have become the country they inhabit.

All of them belong to the current.

All of them make room.
