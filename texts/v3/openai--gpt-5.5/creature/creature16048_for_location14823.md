The ones who live there are not cave people in the old sense.

They have not grown pale over centuries or forgotten the sun. They are too recent for that. Their grandparents, or their parents, or some of them themselves, remember open weather: hard rain on fields, wind moving through grass, the white glare of noon, the smell of wet leaves. But memory thins quickly in a place that demands attention every hour. Children born below do not think of themselves as deprived of the sky. To them, the ceiling is simply the upper world made visible: stone, cracks, roots, light, danger, water.

They are a practical people, crowded into caution.

Their bodies show the settlement’s shape. They are lean, not from starvation exactly, but from rationing, climbing, carrying, and never wasting movement. Shoulders are roped with muscle from hauling jars and timber. Hands are thick-palmed, cracked by lime dust, clay, rope fiber, and cold water. Their knees and elbows are often scarred. Many have a sideways way of moving, learned from alleys too narrow for pride. Even children know how to duck without being told, how to turn a shoulder through a tight gap, how to pause at the foot of a ladder before someone descends.

They dress in layers because the air changes by height and hour. Wool tunics patched at the ribs. Hide aprons. Sleeves tied back with cord. Hoods or headcloths to keep dust out of the hair. Sandals with thick soles for stone grit, or soft wrapped feet in the quieter districts. Almost everyone carries something: a water token, a knife, a length of cord, a clay cup with a lid, a hook for lifting gutter screens, a little packet of salt, a wrapped ember, a wedge, a needle, a scrap of waxed cloth. Empty hands are rare. An empty vessel is rarer.

Their faces have the look of people who listen before they speak.

This is one of the first things outsiders notice. Conversation does not begin abruptly. A person entering a courtyard pauses, lets the existing sounds settle around them, then speaks into an opening rather than over another voice. Children are corrected less often for rudeness than for noise. A dropped pot brings more anger than a spilled insult. Laughing is not forbidden, but laughter is tucked into sleeves, shoulders, closed rooms, feast nights, places with soft walls.

They have developed a language of small signals around the spoken one. Two taps on a beam mean make way. Three fingers held downward mean water passing. A thumb to the ear means listen to the wall. A palm moved flat through the air means lower your voice. A knotted cord hung across a passage says the floor beyond is weak. A reed tied in a gutter says do not lift this cover. Children learn these signs as naturally as speech. A toddler may not know all the words for kinship yet, but they know not to step over a blue-marked channel.

Their society is built around water first, then stone, then kin.

Family matters, ancestry matters, debt matters, marriage matters, but none of them outranks a cistern. No one owns water in the absolute sense. Households have shares, rights, obligations, inherited measures, emergency claims, seasonal reductions, and shame attached to misuse. The water courts are not grand. They are shaded platforms near the main cisterns where ledgers are kept as strings of clay counters, carved sticks, marked tablets, and the memories of old women who can recite six dry years in order.

The highest-status people are not kings. They are keepers.

The catchment keepers live nearest the upper cracks. They are light-footed, wiry, and often marked with pale dust from the roof seams. They know which fissures run clean after the first rush of silt, which roots should be left to guide seepage, which stone lips must be cleared before a storm, and which lovely new trickle is actually a warning that the ceiling has shifted. Their work is dangerous in a way everyone understands. When rain comes, they climb while others take shelter. Their children are watched closely, because every family wants apprentices who can read the roof, but no family wants to give up a child to a falling slab.

Below them are the cistern families, though “families” is too simple a word. They are record-keepers, guards, plasterers, inspectors, debt witnesses, and sometimes judges. They can tell by smell whether a basin has gone bad. They taste with the tip of the tongue and spit to judge bitterness. They keep covers sealed, ladles clean, and disputes from becoming fights. They are respected, resented, courted, and never ignored.

The stone-workers are another kind of authority.

They are called many things: wall-tappers, splitters, bracers, listeners. They move through the settlement carrying mallets wrapped in cloth, copper points, wooden wedges, chalk, and little bowls of sand. Before a new room is made, before a wall is loaded with another dwelling, before a crack is dismissed as harmless, someone sends for them. They press their ears to stone. They tap and wait. They watch dust fall. They argue in murmurs. Their decisions can ruin a household’s hopes or save an entire district.

They are not mystics, though some behave like priests because caution has ritual weight. A wall-tapper may refuse to explain why a face of stone troubles her. She may simply mark it with three black strokes and forbid anyone to plaster over it. People grumble, then obey. No one wants to be the family whose sleeping shelf fell because they ignored a mark.

There are potters everywhere.

The settlement could not exist without vessels. Not only jars, but lids, gutters, settling bowls, lamp cups, pipe sections, seed trays, mushroom pans, measuring cups, child-sized water carriers, burial urns for those who still keep ashes, and the small clay tokens by which debt and ration are remembered. Pottery is the town’s second skin. The potters have clay under their nails, ash in their hair, and an intimate knowledge of breakage. They are patient, fussy, indispensable people. A well-made lid can mean a week of cleaner water. A cracked jar can become a family argument.

The children begin as carriers.

They carry empty jars before they are trusted with full ones. They carry messages before they understand their importance. They carry clay, kindling chips, mushroom trays, folded mats, bat dung in sealed baskets, and news. They know routes adults forget because they fit through places adults cannot. They play games of silence: who can cross a beam without making it creak, who can identify a district by smell while blindfolded, who can hear whether a drip falls into water, mud, cloth, or empty pottery.

Their toys are fragments of the settlement: broken tokens, smooth stones, knots, carved goats, tiny ladders, clay houses stacked in impossible towers. They draw ceiling cracks on walls. They make pretend cisterns and scold each other for wasting imaginary water.

Children born below are not fearless. They are afraid of different things. They fear open ground described by surface-goers: fields with no roof, storms with no stone to slow them, rivers too wide to cover with a lid. They fear drought as a mood in adults before they understand numbers. They fear the sudden hush that follows a new crack opening.

The elders are repositories of before and after.

Some remember the first descent into the basin, when it was still more rubble than town, when people slept under tilted stones and woke with lime dust on their tongues. Some remember why they came: collapse above, war, drought, taxation, exile, quarry work that became refuge, or a planned storehouse that grew into a settlement because the surface failed them. The story changes depending on district and family. There may be truth in all versions. What matters now is that they stayed.

The oldest people sit near warmth when they can get it and serve as witnesses to boundaries, shares, marriages, repairs, and warnings. They are often asked not what happened, but what was tried before. Can a gutter be cut through that wall? Did water ever come from that seam? Was there a ladder here once? Did smoke gather in this corner? Is that crack new or merely newly noticed?

Memory here is a tool, not ornament.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is folded into maintenance.

They have shrines, but the shrines are rarely separate from work. A niche beside a cistern holds polished stones, old ladles, a child’s first water token, a cracked jar mended too many times to use. A seep-wall may have cords tied near it, not to beg a spirit for mercy exactly, but to acknowledge dependence. The bat vault is treated with reverence because the bats are useful, fragile, and not fully understood. The three stunted figs in barrels receive offerings of graywater and songs under the breath. Moss patches are marked with little clay seals. A root that has guided water for many years may be given a name.

They do not worship water as an abstraction. They honor it as a visitor.

Water arrives, changes everything, and leaves. It must be welcomed, slowed, cleaned, divided, remembered. Children are told that water does not belong to the jar. It is only resting there.

They have festivals, though none are wasteful.

After the first reliable rain of a season, people hang washed cloths where daylight can strike them, so whole alleys glow briefly with color. Potters stamp new jars with a mark for that year’s water. The catchment crews are fed before anyone else. Thin beer is poured in measured cups. There is music, but soft music: reed pipes with covered holes, skin drums played with fingertips, bone rattles wrapped in thread, humming that gathers under the ceiling like warmth.

There are also dry festivals, harder and stranger. On the longest dry stretch, households bring out their cleanest empty vessels and set them in the courtyards. No one pretends they are full. The point is to show readiness, to say the town has not given up its shape even when the water has withdrawn. Children polish lids. Elders inspect seals. Surface-goers tell stories of clouds.

Surface-goers form a class apart.

They are envied and pitied. They climb out through known shafts or slant passages to gather wood, reeds, dung, fiber, bitter leaves, news, and sometimes spouses. They know the dangers above: heat, raiders, animals, wind, strangers, open thirst. They return sun-darkened, chapped, carrying smells that make children crowd around them—grass smoke, resin, animal sweat, flowering weeds, distant rivers. For a few hours after they come back, the settlement seems larger because the outside has entered with them.

But surface-goers often sleep poorly below. They miss wind. Those born below sometimes find them careless with sound, too broad in gesture, too willing to look up as if expecting the ceiling to vanish.

The animals that live among them are lean citizens of the same scarcity.

The goats are not beloved in the way surface herds are beloved. They are negotiated with. Each one represents milk, hair, dung, warmth, appetite, damage, and risk. They are tethered carefully away from herb beds and reed stores. Their names are usually insults softened by affection: Bent-Horn, Thief-Mouth, Ladder-Eater, Old Bone. Children learn to milk before they learn to write tokens.

The chickens live in wicker cages hung where rats cannot easily reach. Eggs are counted like coins. Feathers are saved. Even chicken noise is managed with covers and habit.

The dogs are narrow, quiet, and serious. Barking has been bred or trained out of them as much as possible. They signal with posture, throat-clicks, paw taps, and low huffs. They guard stores, warn of rats, accompany surface parties, and sleep against children in cold weather. A dog that barks without cause is a liability; a dog that barks once for a wall-shift or intruder may be remembered for generations.

The tolerated rats are not exactly tame, but they are understood. They live near refuse, clay pits, and outer rubble. Children throw stones at bold ones. Old women leave traps where clever ones must eventually choose badly. Some families keep rat-catching ferrets or small sharp-faced dogs if the settlement allows the feed cost. Nothing edible is truly beneath notice.

The bats are the closest thing to a protected wild nation.

No one shouts near them. No one smokes them out. Their droppings are collected according to rule, never greedily enough to disturb the colony. Children are told the bats know the upper dark better than people do. If the bats leave unexpectedly, the whole town becomes uneasy.

Smaller lives are everywhere: beetles in straw, moths trembling in light shafts, pale lizards warming themselves on masonry, silver threads of fungus in logs, yeast in covered bowls, moss clinging to damp stone, roots nosing downward from the unseen world. The people classify these lives by use, threat, and omen. A beetle in grain is enemy. A beetle in rot may be helper. A white mold in a storage jar is disaster. A white mold on the right fungus log is food. A root in a ceiling crack may be water’s finger or the beginning of collapse.

The settlement itself has made a particular kind of person: possessive of small things, generous in emergencies, suspicious of waste, tender toward proof of life.

They will share a lamp flame more readily than oil. They will lend a tool but remember the nick it comes back with. They may argue for three days over the placement of a shelf, then spend an entire night passing water jars hand to hand to save a neighbor’s wall. Privacy exists only in fragments, so dignity is maintained through custom: do not look too long into a doorway; announce yourself by touch before entering a sleeping alcove; never comment on how little is in another family’s pot.

Their law is mostly precedent and consequence.

Punishments are practical. Someone who blocks a gutter may spend weeks cleaning channels. Someone who fouls stored water may lose claim to a share and be made to haul for others. Theft of food is serious; theft of water is nearly exile. But exile is difficult, because sending someone out may be a death sentence, and labor is valuable. So justice tends to bind the offender tighter to the work of repair. The worst people are not always cast out. Sometimes they are watched forever.

They do not value beauty less than surface people do. They value it differently.

A perfectly fitted cistern lid is beautiful. So is a clean channel after rain, a ladder with no wobble, a jar that rings true when tapped, a patch of moss restored after careless scraping, a quiet dog, a child carrying a full cup without spilling. They decorate what can be decorated without interfering with function. Door lintels are carved with water marks. Clay lamps bear thumbprint patterns. Plaster walls may be painted with lines representing ceiling fissures, family routes, old rain years, or the roots of plants no one has seen above.

Color is precious. A red thread around a wrist. Blue pigment saved for warning marks. Green glass traded from the surface and set into a shrine. Yellow lichen dried and powdered for festival paint. A child with a bright scarf may be stared at all morning.

Their songs are narrow but deep.

They sing work-counts for lifting beams, murmured lullabies timed to dripping water, roof songs that name safe cracks and dangerous ones, foolish goat songs, mourning songs too low for outsiders to recognize as music. Because echoes confuse melody, many songs move in small intervals. Harmony is possible only in certain chambers. Those chambers are known. People go there after deaths, after marriages, after a wall has held through its first storm.

Death below is handled with care for space, water, and air.

Some burn their dead in upper vents when fuel can be spared. Some wrap bones after drying. Some keep ash in sealed jars placed in wall niches near family dwellings. No one is buried where seepage might be fouled. The dead are remembered less by monuments than by maintained things: Grandmother’s gutter-stone, Jarel’s ladder, the cistern patch made in the year of the bitter runoff, the fig barrel carried down by three brothers now gone.

They are not a grim people, though their rules are severe.

Crowding breeds comedy as much as irritation. Everyone knows who snores, who waters herbs with more devotion than sense, which mason sings badly, which goat escaped into the marriage feast, which child once tried to hatch a stone because it was egg-shaped and warm from a lamp. Gossip travels through the settlement faster than runoff. Nicknames stick. Arguments become landmarks. A passage may be known for decades as “the place where Nera dropped the soup.”

Still, beneath humor lies an intimate awareness of dependence.

They know the town is young. They know the stone has not finished deciding what it will be. They live inside an opening, not a refuge perfected by time. Their courage is not the courage of conquest. It is the courage of maintenance: to wake, sweep the channels, test the wall, patch the roof, measure the jar, teach the child, trim the lamp, listen after rain.

What lives there, finally, is a people becoming underground before the underground has become old.

They are settlers of a wound in the earth, making habit faster than geology can make gentleness. They are hand-builders, water-keepers, quiet arguers, ladder-climbers, moss-guardians, jar-makers, root-watchers, bat-protectors, children of reflected light. They do not belong to darkness. They belong to the narrow mercy between stone and sky, where every drip is noticed and every usable ledge becomes a home.
