What lives there is not a single species so much as an agreement between soft bodies, hot stone, captured water, and machines that have learned to behave like weather.

The people are the most visible inhabitants, though they do not seem to occupy the city in the easy way lowland people occupy a town. They do not spread into it, lounge through it, or decorate it as though the world had been made ready for them. They fit themselves into it. They are narrow-shouldered more often than broad, economical in gesture, with a habit of stillness that can be mistaken for severity. They move as if every unnecessary motion spends something measurable. Even their laughter tends to come in short dry bursts, quickly covered by a hand, not from shame but from instinctive conservation: breath has weight here, vapor has value.

Their skin is usually marked by the place. Not dramatically. No mythical scales, no glowing veins. Rather, a fine mineral roughness at the knuckles and elbows; pale salt traces at collars; heat-darkened forearms; eyes narrowed from glare; lips kept sealed against dryness. Many shave their heads or bind their hair close under cloth caps, because loose hair catches grit and drinks sweat. Children wear small collar condensers during the hot months, brass or ceramic rings that rest lightly against the throat and collect breath moisture in tiny glass bulbs. Adults joke that one can judge a child’s temperament by the clarity of the bulb: stormy children cloud it, liars fog it, patient ones make perfect beads.

They are patient because the city punishes haste.

Their tools are made for duration rather than speed: gauges that must be watched for hours, chisels that cut cooling stone by fractions, valves adjusted with both hands and then left alone, measuring rods hung in expansion seams for generations. Many trades are hereditary not by law but by the slowness of skill. A vent-listener’s apprentice may spend five years learning the difference between harmless subterranean settling and a pressure note that requires evacuation of three districts. A cistern accountant is trained to hear the thickness of water by the sound it makes entering a ceramic jar. A wallwright is expected to know which cracks are wounds, which are joints, and which are invitations.

They are not a loud people. Their ceremonies are quiet, precise, almost industrial.

A birth is registered not only by name but by water weight: how much was spent, how much recovered, whether condensation formed on the birth-room tiles. New parents receive a shallow black bowl, unglazed inside, meant to catch the infant’s first bathwater. That water is not discarded. It is filtered, recorded, and added to the household reserve. The gesture is practical, but it is also devotional. Nothing that has touched life is allowed to vanish carelessly.

Marriage, where it exists, is less a joining of bloodlines than a joining of surfaces. Two households prepare a wall, a pipe-run, a cistern face, or a cooling niche together. Whatever moisture that surface catches during the first condensation season belongs to both families. If it catches well, people say the marriage has good listening. If it stays dry, no one says much, but advice arrives quietly: change the angle of the plates, polish the stone differently, sleep elsewhere during pressure shifts.

Their dead are not buried. There is no soil for graves, and the stone is too young to accept sentimental digging. Bodies are dried, rendered, reduced, and divided according to custom and law. Water is reclaimed first. Salts are separated. Bone ash is mixed into heat-resistant ceramic, often used for threshold tiles or valve markers. A person may become the pale lip of a cistern, a handhold on a stair, a listening plate in a pressure hall. Lowlanders sometimes find this grim. The people of the shelf find burial wasteful and lonely. To become part of a surface that catches water is considered a gentle fate.

They do not worship the mountain in any simple sense. They negotiate with it, obey it, blame it, thank it, curse it under their breath, and teach their children never to interrupt it. Their religion, if named from outside, would look like civic engineering with pauses in it. Expansion shrines are kept empty not because emptiness is sacred in itself, but because arrogance is dangerous. A chamber left unfilled is a promise: when the stone moves, it will have somewhere to go besides through a nursery wall.

The mountain is not thought of as alive exactly. To call it alive would make it too small, too animal, too available to pleading. It is said to be “forming.” That word carries more force there than “living.” Living things end. Forming things may ignore endings altogether.

The children of the city know the mountain before they know maps. They learn which railings are safe to touch and which look cool but are not. They learn to stand still at the deep horn. They learn the smell of a valve about to fail: sharp metal, bitter steam, a sweetness like cracked ceramic. Their games imitate infrastructure. They build miniature catchment systems from tile shards and wager over which gutter will dry last. They play “pressure lull,” lying flat on warm paving stones with their ears pressed down, trying to hear the hidden pumps. They dare one another to touch the blue paint that marks future watercourses, as though those lines already contain rivers in ghost form.

Some of them become maintenance children before they are old enough for formal apprenticeships. Small bodies fit into the cistern throats, filter corridors, duct knees, and inspection shafts. They carry lamps with shielded flames, chalk markers, little hooked knives for scraping salt, and vials for reporting unauthorized seepage. They are the ones who see the blind porcelain things most often.

Those creatures live where the official diagrams insist nothing lives.

They are pale, jointed, and almost transparent, their bodies like wet folded ceramic, with limbs too delicate for the hot dry surface world. They cling to the undersides of cistern grates and filter stones. Their heads are blunt, if they have heads. They move by slow gripping contractions, then sudden tick-like darts when disturbed. They feed on the slick mineral films that form inside damp conduits, or perhaps on the breath-skins, or perhaps on the city’s waste salts. No one agrees. The cistern authorities deny their prevalence because prevalence implies an accounting failure. Children name them anyway: milk-clickers, pipe-ghosts, little elbows, wet saints.

When one appears in a public basin after rain, work stops. People gather around it with the uneasy tenderness reserved for impossible things. Someone fetches a damp cloth. Someone else fetches an official to insist that it is only filter matter. A child usually saves it before the argument concludes, lowering it back into a shaded drain with two careful fingers.

The breath-skins are stranger still because they do not behave enough like creatures to invite affection, and yet households become attached to them.

They grow in damp niches, along ceramic conduits, behind cooling jars, in the seam where a heated wall meets a shaded pipe. They are translucent, faintly opalescent when thick, with a texture somewhere between steamed glass and inner eyelid. They pulse when generator loads change. They spread toward warmth but retreat from direct dryness. They do not fruit, flower, bite, sing, or flee. They accept droplets and release a faint mineral smell when scraped. Some people cultivate them for practical reasons: a healthy breath-skin indicates stable humidity; a greying one warns of bad air; a sudden bloom may reveal a hidden leak.

But families also speak to them.

Not formally. Not in prayers. A woman rinsing jars may tell the wall-skin about a son working the western stacks. An old man may tap the film beside his bed after a pressure horn, as if reassuring both it and himself. Children press their palms near it, never quite touching, to feel the subtle warmth. In some houses a breath-skin older than any living family member is treated like an ancestor that forgot how to have opinions. It is trimmed, fed with condensation, shielded from dust, and shown to visitors with quiet pride.

There are guilds devoted to surfaces that catch life without becoming green. They cultivate thermal films, salt veils, and translucent colonies whose taxonomies remain disputed. Are they animals? Minerals persuaded into softness? Engine byproducts? The question is less urgent to the residents than it would be elsewhere. The city itself is full of things that cross categories: walls that sweat, stones that grow, machines that breathe, water that must be negotiated out of air. A life-form that behaves like a dampened wall seems not miraculous but locally appropriate.

Above all of this, the gliders wheel.

They are rarely seen clearly. Most sightings happen at the edge of glare, high over vent fields, where thermal columns climb out of the industrial shafts. A glider is a pale hinged thing with wings like scraped membrane stretched over black ribs. In some light it looks nearly invisible except for its shadow passing over a tower face. It does not flap often. It tilts, holds, trembles, and rises. Its body is narrow, almost weightless, with a mouth or feeding fringe that opens when it passes through mineral plumes. Some believe the gliders eat dust. Some say they eat the airborne young of breath-skins. Some say they are not animals at all but loose scraps of sky that learned hunger from the vents.

They never land in the city, which gives them status. Anything that refuses the city’s surfaces becomes a messenger, omen, or insult. A glider circling three times over a shaft before vanishing into white light is enough to start a day of speculation. Vent-workers mark them in logbooks beside pressure readings. Children carve their shapes into cooling plaster. Lovers compare their beloved’s hands to glider wings when they want to sound foolish and sincere.

At night, if night can be called cool, other lives become audible.

The drainage arches click softly. Not always, not predictably. The sound is like fingernails tapping inside a ceramic bowl. Maintenance crews blame contracting metal, then set traps anyway. The traps come back empty except for damp grit and sometimes a line of disturbed salt. There are stories of larger things moving below the gutter-basins after rare rain: soft-backed, many-legged animals that live suspended in the drainage darkness, waking only when water speaks loudly enough. Most adults dismiss these stories until a filter clogs with something that is not mineral and not machine.

There are also lives imported by people, though few thrive.

Fungal cultures are kept in tiled cupboards, fed on measured damp and processed waste. They are not lush mushrooms but disciplined mats and nodules, grey, amber, white, sometimes black-blue at the edges. They flavor broth, bind medicines, soften children’s food, and provide a kind of household companionship less ambiguous than breath-skins. A family’s culture-line may be older than its residence. Strains are traded at weddings, funerals, and apprenticeships. To let a culture dry out through negligence is a serious disgrace. To revive one from a sliver after a heat failure is cause for neighborhood celebration.

There are insects only where people bring them accidentally, and most die quickly. A lowland fly may arrive in a cargo crate, stagger in the brilliant air, land on a warm railing, and perish before it has found anything to want. The event will be noticed. Children will inspect it as one might inspect a fallen machine from another civilization. Its body may be placed under glass in a school cabinet beside samples of oxidized basalt and obsolete valve seals.

The city’s machines, too, have something like resident character, though no one admits this in formal reports.

The oldest pumps are addressed by names shortened over generations. A reciprocating lift engine that moves once every seven minutes may be called Grandmother No-Hands or Slow Red or simply Her. Workers know the moods of seal-housings, the stubbornness of ceramic gates, the slight cough in a condenser before it fouls. Such language begins as convenience and becomes relationship. When an engine is replaced, its parts are not scrapped all at once. A pressure plate may go to a school. A piston rod may become a bridge rail. A cracked housing may be set into a plaza as a shade base. Machines that have helped the city survive are dispersed like respected dead.

The inhabitants live by listening downward.

Their architecture has ears: pressure plates, resonance rods, hollow columns that hum when underground chambers shift, floors with tuned cavities beneath them. Some people are born with an unusual gift for reading these signals through their feet and palms. They become underlisteners. They sit in warm rooms below the public squares, hands resting on basalt tables, and write down what the mountain is doing in verbs too careful to be poetic: easing, drawing, shouldering, sealing, remembering heat, refusing load. No sane engineer makes a major plan without consulting them, though no engineer likes to say so.

The social order follows water and heat more than money. Wealth is catchment, shade, stable temperature, access to clean condensation, a house wall that sweats reliably but not dangerously. The poor live where vapor passes too fast to collect or where heat rises unpredictably through floors. The rich do not necessarily have spacious rooms; space is hard to cool and harder to justify. They have dependable surfaces. They have cistern shares. They have old tiles that bead at dawn. They have legal rights to condensation from the underside of particular bridges.

There are disputes of exquisite specificity.

Who owns droplets formed on a pipe passing through one household’s wall but fed by another district’s steam? If a child polishes an abandoned shade plate and it begins catching water, does the water belong to the child, the district, or the forgotten estate that installed the plate sixty years before? If unannounced water appears in a cellar, how quickly must one report it before gratitude becomes theft? The courts are full of such matters. Their judges are part jurist, part hydrologist, part priest.

Yet the people are not joyless. They simply find joy where the place permits it.

A cool wall at evening. A cistern tone that proves full depth. Six minutes of rain hammering every roof at once. A newly opened room that holds its temperature through a pressure change. The first bead of condensation on a surface prepared by one’s own hands. Pale tiles steaming under clear morning light. A glider shadow crossing a child’s face. The smell of boiled water made slightly sweet with fungus broth. A deep horn sounding and then, after the pause, nothing breaking.

Their music is built for thin air and thick walls. It favors struck ceramics, low reedless horns, bowed metal, and voices held close rather than projected. Songs often include long silences in which listeners can hear the city’s own ticks and drips entering the rhythm. Dancing is compact, footwork precise, arms controlled, bodies turning around one another without waste. During rain, however brief, restraint sometimes fails. People stand under gutters with bowls, sleeves, open mouths. Children shriek. Adults pretend not to. The rain ends quickly enough to forgive everyone.

Their myths do not begin with gardens.

They begin with a surface too hot to stand on, with people waiting at the edge carrying water in sealed vessels. They begin with a crack that exhaled steam for forty years. They begin with the first wall that did not split, the first cistern that held, the first child born above the cloud layer who did not descend. In lowland stories, civilization often rises from rivers, forests, fields. Here civilization condenses. It appears bead by bead on prepared stone.

What lives there, finally, is a population trained out of the illusion that life is separate from maintenance.

The people maintain machines, machines maintain air, air yields water, water maintains people, people make surfaces for small translucent things, those things warn of changes in the walls, the walls flex with the mountain, the mountain lifts and heats and interrupts all plans. Even the animals that cannot be admitted into the official ecology participate: the porcelain cistern-things cleaning or clogging filters, the unseen clickers of drainage arches, the gliders harvesting invisible abundance from exhaust.

No one there is merely resident. To live on the shelf is to be part of a managed exchange with unfinished geology. It is to understand that a home may be bolted to a heat exchanger, that a nursery may share a wall with a condensation duct, that an ancestor may be present as a threshold tile, that an empty chamber may be the most important room in a building.

They are not waiting for the city to become normal.

They know it will not. Normal belongs to lower altitudes, to soil, to weather that arrives without valves, to rivers that do not need persuading. Up here, life has accepted another grammar. It is dry, tiled, salt-edged, heat-listening, water-counting, mineral-fed. It grows without greenness. It loves without abundance. It builds heavily because everything moves. It speaks softly because the mountain speaks first.

And when a single drop forms on the underside of a black iron beam, there is always something alive nearby to notice: a child with a wager, a worker with a gauge, a breath-skin swelling in its niche, a porcelain creature waiting in the dark drain below, the city itself having prepared the channel.
