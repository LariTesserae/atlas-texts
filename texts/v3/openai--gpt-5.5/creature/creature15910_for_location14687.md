What lives there is not a civilization, and not quite a wilderness in the lowland sense. It is a society of restraint: organisms that have learned to make a body out of light, salt, cold, dust, pressure, and patience.

The oldest inhabitants are the crusts.

They are not passive groundcover but the plateau’s skin: black-green, ocher, violet, gray, thin as lacquer in some places and as tough as cured hide in others. They live slowly over the stone, sealing dust into place, knitting grit with threads finer than hair, taking centuries to cross the width of a hand. In the day they look dead, scorched flat against the glare. At night they loosen. Their membranes open by fractions. Minute colonies wake inside them: bacteria that taste iron, lichens that bind themselves to quartz grains, films that darken when the stars come out and pale again before noon.

If broken, they do not bleed. They powder. But the wound remains for generations.

Above them stand the groves, the true visible life of the plateau.

They are less like forests than like instruments built to play the wind. Their outer plants are low and defensive: thorn-fans, blade shrubs, mats of interlocked spines that catch blown dust and slow the air by the smallest possible degree. Their leaves are hard, reflective, edged with salt. When the wind moves through them, they click and tick and rasp, each grove making its own dry weather-music.

Deeper in are the cushion masses, dark and dense, green-black outside and copper within. They look simple from far away, like boulders furred with life, but their surfaces are crowded with pores, vents, lenses, and crystalline hairs. They drink almost nothing. Instead they harvest frost so fine it is barely water, mineral vapor suspended in night air, and salts drawn upward through stone. Their roots are pale, branching tongues that enter cracks and seams, dissolving the plateau grain by grain.

At the centers of old groves rise the columns.

Some are smooth, some ribbed, some branching like antlers or candelabra. In daylight they stand sealed and pale, their tissues armored against glare. At night their ribs become faintly translucent. Amber veins show inside them. Their vents open near the base and exhale cold gases that crawl along the ground, leaving pale rings of precipitate by dawn. Around these columns the air is slightly changed: stiller, sharper, resinous, carrying hints of alkali, flint, and something like bitter green smoke.

The groves are not individuals, exactly. Each is a compact world of alliances. Root cords braid through fungal sheaths. Salt tubes connect one plant to another. Dead stems remain standing as mineral scaffolds for new life. Seeds lodge in the hollows of old trunks. Lichens climb the shaded sides of branches. Small animals nest inside the hollowed remains of plants that died before their species existed.

There are also the vent-field organisms, stranger and paler.

They live where the plateau breathes from below. Around fissures in the black rock grow glassy forests with colorless stems and leaves like frozen flame. They glow weakly during pressure changes, not with warmth but with chemistry. Their roots descend into mineral cracks where hidden reactions feed them. They use sunlight when they can, but they are not wholly dependent on it. They taste the gases of the deep stone. They bloom in yellow, green, and violet crusts around vents, each bloom marking a different mineral hunger.

The animals are few because the land cannot afford many.

Most are small enough to hide beneath a stone scale.

There are translucent mites that live in the crust and move only under cold starlight. Their bodies are almost colorless except for dark mineral grains inside their guts. There are shell-worms no longer than a fingernail, plated with carbonate, that rasp films from rocks with slow, patient mouths. There are jointed grazers like fragments of thorn given legs, clinging to the underside of leaf-plates during the day and emerging at dusk to drink from plant exudates crystallizing at the roots.

Some insects, if they are insects, have wings like mica flakes. By day they hang under leaves, invisible among reflective hairs. At dusk they rise briefly. They do not beat against the wind so much as submit to it, angling themselves through the grove’s still pockets, carrying powder from one sealed flower to another. Their lives may be only a handful of flights long. They spend most of that life waiting for the right temperature, the right pressure, the right opening of plant mouths.

The larger grazers are rare and careful.

They are ash-gray, narrow-bodied, and high-legged, with padded feet broad enough to cross salt crusts without breaking through. Their coats are not soft fur but layered hollow fibers that trap cold at night and reflect light by day. Their eyes are mirrored, wide-set, unreadable. When they browse, they take only outer growth: a fan tip, a salt leaf, the edge of a cushion. Then they move on. They do not strip a grove bare. Nothing that lives here survives by greed.

They can stand motionless for hours, necks angled like dead branches, while the wind combs dust around their legs. From a distance they are almost indistinguishable from the plants they feed on. Their young are born already quiet. They learn stillness before they learn speed.

Predators exist, but they are not dramatic rulers of the place. They are intervals of hunger given shape.

One kind lies half-buried in gravel near the windward edges of groves. Its hide is mottled basalt, salt, and shadow. During the day only its eyes may be exposed, filmed against the glare. It waits for small grazers, burrowing animals, or the rare careless herbivore. It can wait so long that lichen begins to stipple its back.

Another hunts in the cold hours. It is narrow, low, and pale, with feet that spread like soft stars on dust. It follows scent, but scent on the plateau is thin and brittle, so it moves slowly, reading alkali, resin, warm scale, old breath. Its jaw is delicate rather than crushing. Its success depends on spending almost no energy until the final instant.

There are burrowers too, though their burrows are shallow and often more like sealed chambers beneath salt plates. They sleep through the bright season, curled inside mineral-lined cells, living on stored oils and slowed blood. When pressure falls or a crust cracks, they wake and emerge to feed on fallen plant scales, spores, and the bodies of smaller things caught in rime.

No creature here is loud for long.

A call would be costly. A chase would be costly. A wound would be costly. Even mating is timed to weather: to white hours, to wind reversals, to the nights when frost crystals form thickest on the plant hairs and enough trace moisture enters the living systems for brief extravagance. Then, for a few hours, a grove may become busy. Wings lift. Seed vanes turn. Small legs move through crust tunnels. Grazers arrive from separate horizons and stand facing one another among the thorn-fans, silent except for breath.

The plateau’s life is most abundant where it is least obvious.

Inside plant tissues live colonies that fix minerals into usable forms. In root sheaths, fungi trade acidity for carbon. In salt crusts, sleeping spores wait decades for a single correct night. Within dead columns, larvae chew tunnels through mineralized cellulose, and their dust becomes nursery soil. Under flat stones, pale eggs lie in clusters like grains of opal. In cracks too narrow for light, threads of life follow chemical gradients downward.

Nothing here is merely surviving. It is all participating in an old exchange.

The plants feed on light, dust, and stone. The crusts hold the land together so plants can root. The insects move pollen in the few hours when flowers dare open. The grazers prune the groves and carry seeds in gut and coat. The predators thin the grazers and return their minerals to the crust. The dead remain upright for years, then become architecture, then powder, then food.

If there is anything like a dominant being here, it is the grove itself: not one plant, not one species, but a slow communal organism made of trunks, crusts, roots, insects, salts, fungi, buried chambers, and altered air. Each grove is a city without builders, a weather-machine without metal, a memory of every wind that has crossed it.

From far away, the plateau seems almost empty.

From within one of its green constellations, it is densely inhabited—just not in the way wet worlds teach the eye to expect. Life here does not crowd, sing, gush, or swarm. It waits. It angles itself. It seals and opens. It shines faintly under stars. It makes a feast out of a crystal, a season out of a pressure change, a forest out of stone.
