What lives there is not one kind of creature, but a whole patience of life: dark-adapted, heat-reading, mineral-tolerant, quiet.

Nothing on the plateau has the extravagance of a forest animal or the quick arrogance of a creature born under reliable sun. The inhabitants are economical. They do not waste height, color, water, sound, or motion. Even the large ones seem partially withheld from themselves, as if each body is an agreement with scarcity.

Most life begins very low.

The plants are more like decisions made by the stone than like vegetation. They spread in mats, scales, cords, and sealed knots. A growth may look like a stain of soot across a warm slab until rain touches it; then it swells, greens inwardly, and exposes thousands of small mouths along its surface. These mats feed on thin light when there is any, but mostly on minerals, vent warmth, and the brief chemistry of wet dust. Their roots do not descend so much as infiltrate, entering pores and seams, gripping the rock from within.

Some are black and rubbery, storing water in flat bladders pressed against the ground. Some are pale, almost translucent, and live only where condensation repeatedly forms. Along drainage lines grow the taller things: reedlike stems with waxed skins, no higher than a forearm, trembling when underground water passes below. Their seed heads open only in darkness after storms, releasing hooked grains that cling to the legs of grazers and scavengers. A few plants flower, but secretly. Their blossoms are low, warm, and scented, shaped for insects that find them by heat rather than color.

The fungi are briefer and stranger. They appear after moisture as films, cups, threads, and luminous bruises on mineral crust. Some glow green-white for an hour, then collapse into black residue. Others form delicate lattices over bones or fallen reeds, drawing metals into themselves until they shine faintly like tarnished wire. In old vent fields there are fungal colonies that pulse with slow phosphorescence in response to pressure changes underground. These are among the closest things the plateau has to weather prophets.

The smallest animals rule the place whenever water returns.

Dormant eggs lie everywhere: in dust, in dry pool beds, in cracks, beneath salt crusts, under stones warm from below. They are like seeds but more impatient. A storm passes, a basin fills, and within hours the black water begins to move. Transparent larvae uncoil. Flat tadpole-things graze on microbial bloom. Threadlike swimmers gather in spirals around mineral seepage. Some live only a single night. Some grow legs, mate, and seal themselves back into crevices before the pool shrinks. Some never become adults unless a basin lasts long enough; in dry years their generations fail silently, but enough remain hidden for the next summons.

The insects are numerous, though seldom noisy.

There are lacquer-backed beetles that drink from metal seeps, their mouthparts clicking gently on stone. Their shells are dark red, black, or oil-blue, not for display but for heat management. Some carry mineral deposits on their backs until they resemble moving pebbles. There are moths with dust-colored wings and swollen abdomens that emit faint pulses, signals so dim they are invisible at a distance but unmistakable to another moth passing close by. Their wings do not flutter loudly; they lift and settle like scraps of warm cloth.

There are long-legged skitterers that hunt by pressure waves, standing motionless on crust plates until another creature steps nearby. There are vent crickets with translucent bodies, living in the warm collars around breathing holes, feeding on bacterial films and licking condensed minerals from stone. When a vent exhales, they retreat in perfect unison, not from fear but from practice. Their eggs require heat cycling; without the breath of the ground, they do not hatch.

In cracks live the soft-bodied things.

Some are pale, blind, and boneless except for a flexible inner rod. They move through fissures as if poured, widening themselves in chambers and narrowing to threads in seams. They feed on roots, fungi, dead insects, and the mineral-rich slime that coats underground water channels. Others have shells made not entirely by the body but by the plateau itself: plates of carbonate or iron collected over time, grown unevenly until the animal looks like a chipped piece of machinery trying to crawl away.

The burrowers are not deep diggers; the stone rarely permits that. They are users of existing gaps. They memorize networks of cracks, sinkhole lips, old root tunnels, and cooled vent channels. Many have whiskers longer than their bodies. These whiskers are not delicate ornaments but instruments: they taste humidity, read air movement, and detect the faint warmth of an approaching animal. The burrowers rarely squeak. Their language, if it can be called that, is tapping—tiny knocks made with claws, teeth, or shell edges against stone, spaced far apart so predators cannot easily locate them.

Then there are the grazers.

They are the most visible inhabitants of the plateau, though visible here means only that they alter the darkness when they pass.

They are tall without being bulky: long-legged, narrow-bodied animals with high shoulders and lowered heads. Their backs slope slightly, their ribs showing as shallow arcs beneath heat-dark skin. They walk on splayed, cushioned feet that spread their weight over crusted ground. The pads are thick and almost silent. Their legs can fold tightly under them when they rest, reducing heat loss and silhouette.

Their hides are marked with pale mineral bands and patches that absorb warmth from stone and release it slowly. In the faint thermal language of the plateau, a herd at rest looks like a scatter of cooling rocks. Their eyes are large but not bright. Sight matters less to them than heat, smell, vibration, and the pressure of air changing before rain. Along their faces grow sensory ridges, soft and folded, that can detect moisture from far off. Their nostrils close against mineral dust. Their tongues are broad, tough, and dark, built to scrape mats and lichens from stone without taking too much grit.

They do not graze continuously. They travel between known basins, seep lines, and condensation grounds, sometimes crossing enormous distances through open darkness. The old females remember wet years. The males remember vent belts and mineral flats where calves cannot safely lie down. Herds turn before water appears, responding to changes too subtle for most creatures: a cooling seam, an ion taste, a tremor beneath a dry basin. When rain comes, separate herds converge without calling, arriving from different directions like thoughts reaching the same conclusion.

Their calves are born in warm hollows near vent fields or under overhangs where condensation gathers. Newborns are dark and soft-footed, able to stand quickly but not yet able to read the ground. For the first days they carry a faint throat-glow, a residual bioluminescence that helps the mother find them in darkness. Predators know this too. Many calves die before they learn to keep their throats closed.

The predators have learned silence better than hunger.

Some are coursers: lean, low-headed animals with deep chests, long tails, and limbs built for endurance rather than sudden pounce. They follow herds for days, never rushing until the ground, weather, and weakness align. Their faces are narrow, their ears recessed, their mouths capable of opening wider than seems reasonable. They do not bark or howl. Pack coordination is done by posture, scent, and almost inaudible clicks made in the throat. Their hides are dull and heat-broken, patterned to disappear among cooling stones.

A courser does not chase blindly. It selects a calf, an old grazer, an animal with mineral crust between its toes, a pregnant female slowed by thirst. It waits until the herd must cross a plate field, a channel, a slope of loose pellets. Then the pack fans out, pressing not with noise but with presence. The kill, when it happens, is efficient. The plateau does not favor prolonged struggle.

Other predators are ambushers.

They live beside pools, under ledges, in dry basin mouths, and among folded rock. Their bodies are broad, low, and jointed oddly, able to flatten into shadow. Their skin grows mineral films; some deliberately press themselves into damp crust until they become coated with the same sheen as the stone. A drinking animal may stand within a body-length of one and notice nothing until the rock opens.

These pool-hunters have patient metabolisms. They can wait through dry periods in a state near sleep, heart slowed, limbs tucked, mouth sealed against dust. Rain wakes them. Water draws prey. Their jaws are hooked inward, not for chewing but for holding. Many have throat sacs that glow very faintly, used sometimes to lure insects, sometimes to confuse small amphibious animals, sometimes to signal a mate from one pool to another.

In the sinkholes live larger and older things.

They are rarely seen whole. A pale limb withdraws from the lip of a shaft. A wet flank shifts far below. A line of sensory tendrils hangs in warm air, tasting what passes above. These creatures feed on whatever falls in, but they are not merely traps. At night—or what passes for deeper night in a place without day—they climb partway up the smooth walls and harvest condensation, fungi, insects, and nestlings from cracks. Their bodies are soft underneath and plated on top, with mineral accretions so old they may be mistaken for the sinkhole wall itself.

Some grow very large because nothing requires them to hurry. They live where underground rivers pass near vent heat, taking food from both systems. Their young are small, mobile, and dangerous, wandering the plateau during wet seasons before settling into their own shafts. The old ones become almost geological. Animals detour around certain holes without knowing why.

The scavengers are the most dependable citizens.

They are pale, jointed, and methodical. Their bodies resemble a compromise between crustacean, insect, and bone. They travel in loose files across open stone, guided by scent, heat residue, and subtle chemical changes in the air after blood touches mineral dust. They do not swarm noisily. They arrive like a process.

A dead grazer will draw them from cracks, vents, and basin edges. First come the small cutters, with fine mouthparts that open the hide at soft places. Then larger hinge-backed forms brace themselves and pull. Some specialize in tendons. Some consume marrow by drilling through bone. Some scrape mineral-plated tissue others cannot digest. By morning there may be little left but clean structural remnants and a dark stain where fluids entered the stone.

They are not merely cleaners. They are movers of minerals, spreaders of spores, carriers of eggs. Many plants depend on their feet and mouthparts. Fungi bloom in their droppings. Some amphibious creatures lay eggs in the moist underside of scavenger carapaces, trusting them to travel between basins. The scavengers tolerate this, or do not notice.

Birdlike animals exist, but they are not creatures of sky in the usual sense.

The air above the plateau is too dark, too dusty, too often still near the ground and turbulent above vent fields. So the flyers are low and careful. Some are gliders with broad, silent membranes between elongated limbs, launching from ridges and riding warm exhalations. They feed on moths, vent insects, and small burrowers caught away from cracks. Their bones are thin but mineral-reinforced; broken wings often become sheathed in deposits and useless.

Others are more like walking birds, tall and spare, with probing beaks and heat-sensitive facial pits. They patrol temporary pools, plucking larvae and soft amphibious animals from shrinking water. Their calls are almost nonexistent. During mating season, instead of singing, they stand on resonant plates and drum with their feet, sending coded tremors through the ground. A receptive mate feels the pattern at a distance.

There are also the animals that seem less like individuals than events.

After certain rains, the black basins give birth to rafts: colonial organisms made of joined bodies, each no larger than a fingernail, collectively forming floating skins that pulse and fold. They graze on microbial blooms and dissolved minerals. When the pool evaporates, the raft thickens, darkens, and breaks apart into dry nodules that wait in the mud for years. In good seasons these rafts are eaten by everything. In bad seasons they are the only reason anything larger survives.

Around the vents live communities that almost do not belong to the surface.

There are bacterial veils in orange, black, violet, and dull silver, feeding on heat and chemical gradients. Tiny grazers browse these veils with comb-like mouths. Worms anchor themselves in warm cracks and extend crowns of filaments into vapor. Shell-backed things cluster at the edges, each positioned according to its tolerance: too close, and the breath cooks them; too far, and they starve. Predators here are small, armored, and slow, because the vent colonies reward steadiness more than speed.

Some vent fields have resident “choirs”: colonies of sac-bodied organisms rooted into mineral collars. They inflate when vapor pressure rises and deflate as it passes, producing low tones below easy hearing. The sound travels through stone more than air. Other creatures use these vibrations as timing devices. Eggs hatch after certain sequences. Insects emerge when the choir deepens. Herds avoid vent fields when the tone grows irregular.

If there is anything like intelligence here, it is distributed.

The grazers remember routes. The coursers remember individuals. The scavengers remember chemistry. The fungi remember moisture in the way their hidden threads persist and bloom only when the correct sequence returns. The vent colonies respond to underground rhythms older than any animal. No species builds towers or roads because the plateau itself is already a network: water routes, heat belts, storm corridors, mineral seams, migration lines, scent paths, pressure changes.

There may be no people.

Or, if there are people, they are not makers in the familiar sense. They would be listeners: narrow, dark, soft-footed beings moving between vents and basins, carrying little, leaving almost nothing. They would not build above ground, because anything raised becomes a silhouette and anything fixed is eventually plated, cracked, or buried. Their knowledge would be seasonal but not calendrical, measured by vent breath, condensation fronts, the taste of pools, the drumming of plate fields, the migrations of herds.

They might mark nothing except with temporary arrangements of stones that wind later scatters. They might speak rarely, and more often through touch on warm rock. They would know which lights are lures, which silences mean ambush, which pools are young and which have gone bad with minerals, which sinkholes are inhabited by old hunger. They would not think of the plateau as empty. They would think of it as crowded with distances.

But the place does not require them.

Its truest inhabitants are adapted not to possession, but to recurrence. They live by appearing at the right moment and vanishing before the wrong one. They sleep as eggs, knots, crusts, sealed bodies, spores, heat-slowed hearts. They wake when stone sweats. They travel when pressure changes. They breed in black water that may be gone by morning. They feed on minerals, darkness, warmth, and each other.

They are quiet because sound is expensive.

They are low because height is dangerous.

They are patient because the plateau is patient.

And when the rare water gathers in a basin, life comes to it from every scale at once: bacteria clouding the shallows, larvae writhing in the dark, reeds swelling, beetles clicking, grazers kneeling to drink, predators becoming stone at the edge, scavengers already approaching from far away, and beneath it all the ground breathing steadily, preparing to take the water back.
