The people who live there look, at first, like something the lake has remembered about humans.

They are human in the old shape: two arms, two legs, faces that can smile, hands fine enough to tie a seed-cord or remove a parasite from the root of a wall. But nothing about them has remained dry. Their bodies have been persuaded by generations of cold green water into another agreement.

They are small and dense-boned, rarely tall. A tall body is wasteful in water and clumsy in root-rooms. Their shoulders are narrow, their ribs flexible, their hands broad, with a slight webbing between the fingers that can fold away when they work. Their feet are longer than a lowlander’s, the toes strong and separated, useful for gripping vine-ladders and stone thresholds. The soles are tough, not from walking roads but from pressing against bark, grit, shell, and woven reed.

Their skin carries the colors of the town: brown-olive, grey-green, pale umber, deep tea, sometimes almost blue in the coldest families of the northern quarter. Children are often translucent at the ears and wrists, with visible veins like river-maps. Adults darken with age, especially along the back and shoulders, where the water-light touches them most. The old grow mottled with pale mineral freckles, and some are so long resident in the Lung that pearl-lichen will take to the ridges of their scars if they do not scrub it away.

They have hair, but little of it is loose. Loose hair is a nuisance in flowing rooms. It is braided tight against the skull, threaded with shell or seed-beads, or grown in heavy cords that float behind them like dark weed. Workers bind theirs with fish-skin bands. Children’s hair is cropped short until they learn not to get tangled in the nursery roots. Mourners leave one braid unbound, and in the water it moves constantly, a visible sign of grief.

Their eyes are large and dark, not because the place is lightless, but because the light is always scattered. They blink with two lids: one ordinary, one clear and sideways-drawn, which passes over the eye when silt rises or when a person swims into snowmelt current. Their ears are small and close to the head. They do not hear as dry people hear. They feel sound in the jaw, the throat, the breastbone. A bellfish court can make their teeth ache. A cracked root-mat has a note they can recognize before they see it.

They breathe in two ways.

At the surface of the lake, where leaf-platforms hold pockets of wet air, they can use lungs much like any mountain people might. But below, where most of life happens, they draw water across soft folded slits set low along the sides of the neck and beneath the ribs. These folds are not fish-gills exactly; they are slower and more fragile, and require clean cold water. A person raised here can live submerged for days without thought, sleeping with the faint pulse of the current moving through them. In warm stagnant water they sicken quickly. In dry air they become feverish, their skin tightening, their breathing laborious and loud.

The first lesson given to a child is not speech. It is flow.

A baby is born in the warmest part of the southern nurseries, among root-cradles lined with sponge and soft bladder-leaf. Newborns have feathery breathing folds, almost decorative, bright red for the first days. They do not cry in the way dry babies cry. They open their mouths and release a trembling string of bubbles. Their mothers, fathers, aunts, and appointed cradle-keepers hum against their small bodies so they know they have arrived among people.

No infant belongs only to its parents. A child belongs to a house, to a root-line, to a current, to the season of its birth, to the people who heard its first bubble-song. Children are passed from arm to arm, sling to sling, hip to hip. They sleep in hanging nets within sight of older hands. They learn the town as a creature learns its own body: where the smooth rail is, where the eels sleep, which walls will grip back if touched, which channels pull downward, which leaves sting, which turtles tolerate fingers and which do not.

They swim before they walk. Walking is a specialized skill, used for bearing baskets, scraping thresholds, hauling stone, tending lower gardens. Swimming is thought. Children dart through the lanes in flashes, bold and disorderly, pursued by older cousins with reed-switches when they disturb seed beds or wake the roof-eels. They play at being bellfish, striking hollow seed-pods together and booming through the courts. They race bubbles. They hide in abandoned lintels. They dare one another to touch the cold well and then come back shuddering and proud.

By adolescence they are given weights.

Not as punishment, but as citizenship. A belt of small stones, carved and smoothed, teaches a young person how to remain where work is needed. Without weight one is all impulse and drift. With weight one can prune a wall, carry silt, hold a beam in place, sit in council without floating away. The first weighted belt is a serious gift. It is made by relatives and neighbors, each stone taken from a place the child must learn to serve: the Lung, the animal quarter, the eastern spillway, the gardens, the dead-root shelves.

They wear little cloth. Cloth rots, tangles, and drinks too much water. What they do wear is practical: woven belts, shell aprons for tool work, fish-skin wraps for warmth, moss-padded shoulder guards for carrying frames, wrist loops for knives and scrapers. Ornament matters, but it is tied close to the body. Shells polished by generations hang at throats. Teeth of old pike or mountain cat are set into hair cords. Dyed threads mark skill, household, grief, vows, and dangerous work. A roof-grower may wear green knots at both wrists. A well-keeper wears pale stone against the sternum. Someone under a silence vow ties a strip of white bladder-leaf over the mouth and speaks only by hand.

Their speech is layered.

Close at hand, they use the mouth: short words, round vowels, clicks made with the tongue against the teeth, hums that carry through bone as much as water. Across distance they speak with hands, shoulders, and the angle of the head. Their sign-language is not silent exactly, because every motion trails bubbles and pressure, but it is precise. There are signs for eddies, for illness in a wall, for eel-right, for “come quickly but do not stir silt,” for “this is not mine to answer.”

For ceremony and work they sing through reeds. The reed-tubes deepen their voices until they become almost architectural, part of the room’s structure. A pruning song may last for hours, not as entertainment but as measurement: bend here, cut now, wait, pull together, bind, breathe, again. Their music has few sharp endings. It braids, overlaps, returns. When many sing in the Lung, the old trunks answer faintly, resonating through their hollow places.

They do not have rulers in the hard sense. No one can command a current, and so no one is permitted to command as if they were a current. Instead the town is governed by keepers of responsibilities.

There are root-tenders, who know the appetites and tempers of the living houses. There are silt-counters, who decide where the rich mud goes after storms. There are fish-speakers, not because fish obey them, but because they know breeding times, migrations, and the signs of disease. There are well-keepers, who tend the cold mineral spring and the baskets of the dead. There are bridge-wrights, nursery-aunts, moth-watchers, spillway-divers, compost-lawyers, seed-mothers, bellfish-listeners.

A council is not made of the important. It is made of the implicated.

If a channel is failing, those who use it, those whose gardens drink from it, those whose dead lie below it, and those who know its plants must all come. Decisions are slow because the town itself is slow. A proposal may be sung three times on three different days, once at morning bellfish, once at midday current, once at frog-dusk, so that no mood of the water is ignored.

They own tools, ornaments, songs, debts, scars, and promises. They do not own water. They do not truly own houses. A family may tend a dwelling for many generations, sleeping in its rooms, training its doors, guiding its fruiting stems, but the dwelling remains a living neighbor. If the house grows too greedily into a public lane, the family is corrected. If they starve its roots, they are shamed. If a house flowers well and shelters fish, the family is praised not for possession but for conduct.

They eat what the town and lake allow.

Their food is mostly soft, cold, and clean-tasting: sliced tubers grown in the western rafts, pale root-starch pressed into cakes, fish eggs, algae pastes, sweet bladder-leaf, snails, shrimp, moth larvae, turtle eggs when permitted, and fish taken by thorn-hook or hand-net. They ferment some foods inside sealed gourds weighted in the lower cold. These are opened only in gathering courts, because the smell travels strongly through water and would otherwise draw every crab in the neighborhood.

Meals are not served in bowls of liquid; liquid is the world. Food is wrapped, pressed, skewered, gelled, or eaten from the hand. Families gather in rooms where the current is gentle. They hook their toes beneath floor-vines and pass bundles from palm to palm. A child who lets food drift away must retrieve it unless a turtle reaches it first, in which case the turtle is said to have received the lesson.

They have humor, much of it physical and ecological. They laugh at people who misjudge buoyancy, at solemn speeches interrupted by shrimp, at proud young divers who surface with weed in their teeth, at outsiders who try to stand upright in a current and look offended when the water disagrees. Their laughter comes out as bursts of bubbles and low clicks. Among close kin, affection is often shown by grooming: cleaning silt from hair-braids, checking breathing folds, scraping mineral from the back, removing tiny hookworms from the ankles.

They fear dryness more than darkness.

Darkness has life in it: eels, roots, sleeping fish, the known deep. Dryness is empty pain. To be stranded above the lake-skin in cold air is a nightmare told to children who wander too high among the upper leaves. They have stories of ancestors who came from dry stone, but these are not heroic in the usual way. The dry ancestors are pitied for their thirst, their loud footfalls, their fires, their blindness to current. The great change—whether flood, choice, punishment, rescue, or all of these at once—is not remembered as a disaster. It is remembered as the treaty.

Their dead remain with them for a long time.

When someone dies, the body is not burned and not hurried away. It is washed in the cold well-stream, wrapped in openwork reed, and placed among the root-baskets below the Lung. The fungi take what is soft. The snails polish what is hard. The bones loosen slowly. After years, relatives return to gather the cleaned bones and sort them: some are strung into household memory-cords, some are powdered for wall-clay, some are placed in the foundations of new rooms, some remain in the well-roots. A person may become threshold, cup, bead, lintel, garden nutrient, song subject. To disappear into use is an honor.

They do not believe the dead are gone into a far country. The dead are redistributed.

There are also those who live at the edges of personhood.

The oldest trunks in the Lung are treated as citizens, though not as people. Each has a name, a temperament, and a legal memory. One is known to reject grafts from the southern gardens. One flowers only after hard winters. One has grown around a child’s lost shell bracelet and is considered to have adopted it. Disputes may be brought before the trunks, which means not that the trees answer, but that humans must speak slowly enough and truthfully enough to be heard by something that will outlive them.

The bellfish are not tame, but they are public. No one eats a bellfish from the central court. Their timing orders the day, and their thunder is treated almost as civic speech. A child who startles them deliberately is made to spend a morning cleaning algae from the court stones under the supervision of someone very old and very unimpressed.

The eels have rights of abandonment. If a wall collapses and eels nest in it, the people wait. If a roof-gutter becomes an eel nursery, the family reroutes their entrance. This is not sentimentality. Eels eat the things that would eat the roots. They are secret sanitation, white and patient.

The otter-like thieves are tolerated less nobly. They steal seed-cords, polished shells, young fish from pens, and occasionally tools. The people curse them fluently and set clever latches against them, but they also leave scraps near warm hollows in the bitterest months. An otter-beast that chooses a house repeatedly is considered either a nuisance or a sign that the house is healthy, depending on how much it steals.

Outsiders sometimes come.

Dry traders from the passes appear above the eastern spillway in summer, wrapped in wool and smelling of smoke, salt, metal, and mammal. They cannot enter far. The town sends up surface-speakers: men and women trained to endure air for a little while, their skins greased, their breathing folds covered with wet moss, their eyes narrowed against wind. They trade dried fish, medicinal lichen, pearl-shell, woven reed-cord, and cold-water dyes for obsidian, salt, rare metal, stories, and news of avalanches.

The surface-speakers are admired and pitied. They return exhausted, cracked at the lips, full of strange rumors: forests that stand in air, animals that run without swimming, fires kept inside stone rings, cities where walls are dead and must be repaired by force. Children beg for these stories. Adults listen politely and then touch the nearest living wall with gratitude.

The people of the drowned town are not innocent, and not idyllic. They quarrel over channels, inherit resentments, neglect duties, fall in love badly, take more than their share of sweet leaves, lie about tool debts, and sometimes prune in secret what should have been discussed. A family may shame another by implying their house flowers poorly. Young divers compete recklessly near the dark roots. Old councilors can become rigid as dead wood. There are punishments: loss of harvest rights, enforced silt-work, public correction songs, temporary exclusion from a current.

But cruelty cannot easily become architecture there. Everything leaves a trace. Poison spreads. Neglect clouds the water. A blocked passage is felt by all who swim it. A greedy house shades another’s garden. The town makes selfishness visible.

So its people are trained, from infancy, to notice consequence.

They know how to wait without being idle. They know how to touch a thing before changing it. They know that a wall is not merely a wall, but hunger, growth, shelter, future rot, fish passage, shade, memory. They know that water carries words farther than intended. They know that decay is not failure unless it is wasted.

They move through their green drowned streets with the ease of inheritance: pulling along root-rails, turning sideways through living doors, pausing to let fish pass before them, setting a palm to a trunk as one might greet an elder. They are gardeners of a city that gardens them back. They are mountain people who became lake people without ever leaving the mountain. They are citizens of a place where breathing is communal, where walls must be fed, where the dead enter the foundations, and where no current belongs to anyone.
