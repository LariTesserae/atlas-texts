What lives there is mostly not separate enough to be called a creature at first.

It lives as a thickness.

A green, black, listening thickness laid over stone and old wood; a body made of moss cushions, liverwort fans, fungal threads, cold water, and the soot of fires no one remembers making. It has no single den because the hollow itself is its den. It has no clear skin because every surface is skin: wet rock, root-bark, the inside curve of the pecked basin, the underside of the fallen shelter frame, the dark pads of moss that rise and close after a foot has passed.

If it has a face, it is the place where the water vanishes.

Not the waterfall itself, but the throat behind it: that black slot where sound is made larger than water deserves to be. The inhabitant listens from there. Or rather, it is listening there always, because listening is the way it remains alive. Sight is almost useless to it. Light is too weak, too occasional, too broken by fog and needles. It knows the world by pressure, by tremor, by the weight of drops, by the difference between rain and thaw, beetle and hare, boot and hoof, fallen twig and loosened stone.

It is old, but not ancient in the grand manner. It has no crown of antlers, no buried temple, no voice that speaks names. Its age is low and accumulative. It is old the way damp ash is old when found beneath moss. Old the way a root grows around a tool flake and holds it without understanding it. Old the way a path can remain after all the feet are gone.

Most days it does almost nothing.

It drinks condensation. It thickens around pebbles. It sends pale threads through blackened wood and draws from it the last sweetness left by sap. It accepts the shed wing of a beetle, the hair of a passing hare, the oil from a hand that rested briefly on stone generations ago. It works without appetite as animals know appetite. It does not seize. It receives.

Its movements are so slow they are better understood as decisions made by seasons. In spring, it loosens. The moss lifts, saturated and bright beneath the dimness. Tiny translucent stems rise in the hidden corners, and the creature’s outer body becomes tender, almost luminous where the grey light enters. In summer, it spreads sideways, never upward, following the seep-lines, knitting over flakes of quartz, creeping along the old path but not quite covering the placed stones. In autumn, it darkens. Fungi open from deadwood like small ears. In winter, it contracts beneath ice skins and frozen basins, listening to the water’s lower voice as if hearing speech through a wall.

There are animals in it, but they seem more like brief thoughts than inhabitants.

Beetles move under bark, lacquer-dark and slow. Pale larvae anchor themselves beneath wet stones, pulsing faintly in the cold seep. A small bird sometimes enters by mistake, a quick spark of feather and fear, then leaves when its own call is swallowed by the roar. Weasels cross the upper rim. Hares test the entrance and go no farther. Once in a hard winter, a deer may step between the upright stones, lower its head, breathe steam into the hollow, and withdraw with the caution of an animal that has heard something too large in too small a place.

The true resident remains.

It wears what has been left to it.

The old shelter ribs are part of it now. The bent saplings, blackened and softened, lie like exposed bones under the overhang, but they are not dead in the hollow’s sense. Fungal filaments lace them. Moss pads their joints. Where lashings once bit tight, moisture gathers in narrow rings. The inhabitant remembers pressure there: not as an image of hands tying knots, but as a lasting shape in the wood, a constriction that weather could not erase.

It keeps the bone awl differently.

That small polished point, tucked into moss, is too worked, too deliberate, to become ordinary quickly. The hollow has begun to blunt it, but with a kind of patience that resembles respect. Green has reached its edges. Mineral damp has entered its pores. Yet it still holds the memory of use: puncturing hide, opening holes, making passage for sinew. Sometimes droplets collect along it and fall from the tip as though the awl were still making something.

The upright stones at the entrance are its teeth, though not for biting. They mark the mouth. They tell snow where to drift and moss where to climb. They tell the old path where to begin. The creature does not guard them exactly; it simply continues to include them. If one fell, it would take a century for the hollow to decide whether that falling was accident, weather, or a new arrangement.

It is not hostile.

But it is not welcoming in any warm sense. Warmth would trouble it. Loud human certainty would trouble it. Fire troubles it most of all, not because it fears burning—everything here is too wet for easy flame—but because fire makes a rival center. Fire asks the hollow to gather around light. The resident prefers sound, damp, and dimness. Smoke clinging under the overhang is the nearest thing to a scar it carries. Not an open wound, but a stain in the breath.

When people once came, it knew them as interruptions with rhythm.

Feet on the same stones. Weight at the basin. The dry rasp of worked edges. Hide shifted from shoulder to ground. Breath. Muted speech near the entrance; silence near the slot. Hands placing things beneath the overhang. Hands taking other things away. The hollow did not understand them, but it learned their repetition. It learned that some stones are meant to remain. It learned that a path can be made of choosing, not clearing.

Perhaps the people knew it too, in their own way.

Perhaps they did not think of it as a being. Perhaps they only knew this was a place where sound covered speech, where water could be gathered, where bad weather passed overhead while the hollow held its own bad weather close. Perhaps they came because animals moved nearby in certain seasons. Perhaps because the roar made privacy. Perhaps because something about the place made listening easier, even while hearing became impossible.

The resident still waits in the shape of those visits.

Not expectantly. It does not long as a dog longs, or mourn as a person mourns. But it preserves absence with great care. The old path is not allowed to vanish entirely. The working stone remains a little clearer than surrounding ground. The basin continues to fill and spill. Even the flakes of flint and slate, almost swallowed, catch dim light now and then like thoughts surfacing.

If you entered and stood still long enough, it would begin to know you.

First as heat.

Then as salt.

Then as a column interrupting mist.

Then as pressure through the moss, your pulse traveling downward through boot soles or bare feet into the soaked ground. Your breathing would be almost nothing beside the roar, but the hollow would feel the rhythm of your ribs. It would feel where you shifted weight, where you hesitated, where your hand touched the wet rock for balance. If you spoke, it would not understand the words. It would take the vibration and break it apart, storing only the fact that a living chest had tried to push meaning into the noise.

Stay too long, and it would begin its ordinary kindness.

It would dampen you. It would dull you. It would draw the heat from your knees, bead water in your hair, soften leather, swell woven fiber, darken the hems of clothing. It would make your thoughts slower and more granular, until the waterfall became not a sound among sounds but the condition under which all thought happened. It would teach you the scale of small things: one drop forming, one fern-tip bending, one bead of water freezing in a stone bowl while green moss breathes beside it.

If you left something behind, it would take it in.

A knife would lose brightness. A strip of cloth would darken, fray, grow furred. A name carved into wood would fill with damp and lichen until it became texture rather than language. A bone would remain longest, but even bone would enter the hollow’s keeping: first stained, then softened at the edges, then nested in moss, then indistinguishable from the other pale, patient things beneath the green.

What lives there is a keeper of reduction.

It makes no grand transformations. It does not devour a body overnight or raise ghosts from the basin. It does not speak in the waterfall, though a lonely person might eventually hear almost-words there. Its power is quieter and more complete: it receives sharpness and returns roundness; receives brightness and returns dimness; receives intention and returns belonging.

The hollow’s inhabitant is therefore not one thing but a slow agreement between things.

Moss agrees to cover stone. Water agrees to fall forever through the same narrow dark. Roots agree to hold and pry. Fungi agree to translate wood into soil. Cold agrees to preserve what damp agrees to change. Human remnants, after resisting for a while, agree as well.

And beneath all of it, the roar continues, enormous and close, the voice of a small stream made vast by confinement.

That is the creature’s heart.

Not beating, exactly.

Grinding. Deepening. Remembering without thought.
