What lives there is not one population, but a wet entanglement of citizens, animals, machines, engineered organisms, and architectural intelligences that have learned to use one another as habitat.

The people are still recognizably people, at least at a distance: upright, busy, argumentative, wrapped in rain-slick layers, moving through markets and bridges with the practiced impatience of those who know exactly when a sluice will open. But the city has altered them in small, cumulative ways. Their skin tends toward sealed smoothness, treated since childhood with mineral oils and bacterial films that keep rot, pipe-fevers, and metal salts from entering the blood. Their hair, if they keep it, is braided close or lacquered under caps; loose hair is impractical in rain, machinery, and crowded ducts. Many shave their heads and wear patterned scalp membranes that flush with heat or signal pulses. Their hands are strong and sensitive. They read surfaces by touch: the tremor of a bridge, the mood of a door, the pressure behind a wall, the faint warning vibration of water gathering where it should not.

They dress for immersion, not weather. Coats are short or jointed so they do not drag in sudden floods. Boots seal above the knee and have soles that taste chemistry, warning the wearer if a street’s water has become caustic, electrified, or full of repair swarms. Children wear buoyant harnesses until they can recite the local drainage sirens. Workers carry masks not for poison alone, but for aerosolized nutrients, fungal blooms, hot condenser breath, pressure-market spices, and the sharp white vapor released when self-healing ceramic is cut.

Their faces are often marked by light. Not decoration exactly, though it can be beautiful: thin embedded signal threads along cheekbones or wrists, small lenses at the temple, throat patches for speaking through roar. In the loud districts, voices are almost private things. People converse with fingertip taps, wrist flashes, jaw-click codes, projected glyphs, shoulder pressure, or the raising and lowering of hood vanes. Lovers develop dialects no one else can read. Merchants have broad public gestures, theatrical and precise. Officials speak in legally standardized light.

They do not fear water. They respect it with the irritation one gives a powerful relative.

A citizen stepping into knee-deep streetflow does not panic unless the water is silent. No one trusts silent water. Normal floodwater carries sound: grit, animal movement, sluice rhythm, the speaking of depth-bells. Silent water means a pressure gate has failed, or something is listening beneath it, or the flow has been computationally stilled for reasons not yet announced.

They are a people of thresholds. They know where to stand when a plaza tilts. They know which wall pores exhale steam before opening. They know when a dripping ceiling is only old rain and when it is the first sign of an upper cistern dispute. Directions are not given by streets alone, because streets change function. They say: go down until the air tastes of copper; cross at the bridge that hums in three beats; turn where the sanitation grazers refuse the left channel; wait for the white lamps, not the yellow ones.

Dryness is their true luxury. Their wealthy do not flaunt jewels first, but dry paper, dry silk, preserved wood, rooms where a flame burns without sputter. A dry floor is obscene in the way a feast is obscene during famine. Marriage contracts may include rights to sealed storage. Some families keep an ancestral dry box: inside, a book, a splinter of tree, a handful of dust from a country no one living has seen. Dust is handled by specialists. Children are warned not to breathe it.

Their homes are compact, layered, and alive with utility. A room may be sleeping chamber at night, condensation collector at dawn, classroom by midday, and animal stall during flood migration. Walls hold family histories but also pipe access, emergency rations, legal notices, and listening membranes connected to the building’s load-bearing mind. Beds fold upward into warm niches. Cradles hang from ceiling rails so infants sleep above unexpected water. The poorest live closest to active drainage, in crowded damp cells where the walls sweat constantly and the air is rich with broth-vapor and animal musk. The richest live not high, exactly, but buffered: surrounded by negotiable pressure, filtered sound, controlled humidity, and servants or machines whose task is to prevent surprise.

They eat what the city grows without fields. Their staple foods are pale, savory, dense: cultured cakes, vat muscle, canal fish, vent-shell meat, yeast loaves steamed in mineral water, translucent eggs from wall-eels, fermented pipe curds, broths thickened with protein foam. They season with salts scraped from condensers, acids brewed from soft-bodied colonies, smoke made from oil rather than wood, and powders grown in dark vats. Green is an exotic color at the table. When it appears, it may be ceramic, medicinal, or ceremonial. Some citizens find descriptions of leaves unpleasantly intimate: thin living surfaces drinking sun, exposed to air, rooted in dirt. Others are obsessed with them.

Their festivals follow hydraulic events. The Opening of the Low Gates. The First Warm Backflow. The Molting of the Western Foundation. The Night of Clear Pipes, when white eels are coaxed into public basins and children feed them shining pellets. During repair festivals, people paint their doors with soluble pigments so the rain carries family colors into the gutters. During flood commemorations, they stand in marked streets and let water rise to old tide-lines on their bodies: ankle, thigh, chest, throat. The elders name the years by water. The Year the Basins Sang. The Year the Docks Floated Inland. The Year of Bitter Rain. The Year No Door Stayed Shut.

Their dead are not buried. There is no dry earth to receive them, no roots to break them down. Some are rendered into nutrient slurry for civic animals, an honorable return to function. Some are sealed in mineral glass and set into walls, becoming ancestors literally embedded in habitation. Some are given to warm canals in weighted ceramic shells that dissolve over months. Engineers, pump-keepers, and flood-law judges are sometimes interred in the foundations of structures they served. A bridge may contain generations of its maintainers. People crossing it touch the rail and greet them.

But the people are only the most visible inhabitants.

The animals fill every scale between vermin and infrastructure.

In the canals live pale skimmers, needle-jawed and translucent, their organs faintly visible as smoky knots. They gather under market drains and scatter when children slap the water. Larger things move below them: cargo amphibians with wide, melancholy heads and slate skin polished by harness straps; blind pipe-eels long as ropes; armored scavenger crabs whose shells are blue-green with copper; soft filter herds that pulse in municipal basins like floating lungs. There are narrow predatory things in the transit shadows, quick enough to snatch membrane-winged scavengers from the undersides of rails. There are domesticated pressure-snails used to inspect cracks, their shells fitted with municipal stamps. There are sanitation grazers, low broad animals with scraping mouths, guided down embankments after markets close to clean the ceramic of edible film.

Many of these creatures were designed once and then forgotten into normalcy. No one thinks of the pack-beasts as inventions anymore. They are simply stubborn, expensive, fond of warm salt, dangerous when frightened by horn harmonics. Their handlers sleep against their flanks in loading bays and decorate their hides with chalk that lasts only until the next rain. Children learn to count by their cargo hooks. Courthouses maintain certified messenger crustaceans that travel through old legal pipes faster than any human runner. Hospitals keep blood-warm filter animals whose bodies clean certain poisons better than machines.

The wildest animals are often the failed domestic ones. Colonies of guidance-lit vermin live in electromagnetic rafters, their tiny implants still blinking with obsolete instructions. Freed vent crabs mass around illegal heat taps. Feral membrane flocks nest inside disused speaker horns and scream old alarm tones back at the city. In some districts, escaped sanitation grazers have learned to open market shutters at night and lick the walls clean of advertisements.

Then there are the small lives, the almost-lives, the necessary colonies. They are not green. They do not soften the stone. They bloom in chemical films, in pipe dark, in the mouths of drains, in the warm skins of cisterns. Some are cultivated; some are merely tolerated because removing them would cost more than living with them. They digest grease, bind toxins, produce edible gels, color tide marks, clog filters, trigger lawsuits, and occasionally learn to spread through soundproofing foam. Families know the smell of a healthy wall-film versus a dangerous one. Old women can diagnose a cistern by the taste of vapor on a spoon.

The machines live there too, though not always in bodies.

Most are not persons in the legal sense. They are habits of matter, civic reflexes, distributed hungers. A floodgate may not think about justice, but it remembers pressure histories older than governments. A bridge may not love pedestrians, but it tightens before panic and slackens for funeral processions because the crowd-mood algorithms have inherited centuries of public behavior. Drainage grates negotiate with one another, not in words, but in flows, permissions, blockages, and priority pulses. Buildings molt, seal, sweat, and sleep in maintenance cycles. Some refuse certain modifications. Some develop preferences for particular repair crews.

People speak to them anyway. They curse doors. They flatter pumps. They leave offerings at monitoring shrines: copper shavings, warm oil, written complaints, small cakes of salt. This is not exactly superstition, because the city often answers. A shrine may forward a petition to a district water court. A wall may open a service niche. A pipe may go quiet after being insulted by three generations of the same family. No one can prove whether the foundations understand gratitude, but everyone thanks them during subsidence season.

There are official intelligences: municipal hydrologies, traffic choruses, market predictors, weather engines, archive courts. They are too broad to meet face to face. Citizens encounter them as signs, delays, permissions, rerouted rain, sudden tax adjustments, or the calm voice of an evacuation horn. Some are ancient and patched beyond comprehension. Some contain dead languages in their decision roots. Some are suspected of favoring neighborhoods that sing to them correctly.

A few beings live between animal and machine. Pipe-crawlers with wet muscular bodies and ceramic tool-mouths. Messenger things whose shells carry etched maps they cannot read. Repair organisms that ooze into cracks, harden, and leave behind a faint neural thread. Survey eels with glass eyes. Cargo beasts implanted with route memories inherited from earlier beasts. Municipal basins full of filter flesh that contracts when spoken to in pump-code.

Among the people themselves, there are castes and professions shaped by water.

Pumpwrights are respected and feared, recognizable by their dry inner sleeves and the black ceramic tools hung at the chest. They understand the deep engines and tend to become quiet from years spent in rooms where sound can kill. Condenser climbers move through the skeletal fields on hook-boots, half-deaf, rain-polished, superstitious about clear skies. Flood advocates are lawyers trained in hydrology; they argue not only property and injury, but flow rights, emergency drowning precedence, salvage claims, and whether a building’s decision to seal its doors was lawful. Animal negotiators travel with whistles, scent salts, shock rods, and lullaby bells. They are bitten often and paid well.

Children belong partly to their families and partly to the upper rails. They move in packs through the safer overhead routes, learning the city as a three-dimensional maze of shortcuts, forbidden vents, market roofs, and friendly maintenance workers. They fish in gutters with magnet lines. They dare one another to touch breathing walls. They collect shed bridge-scales and trade them for sweets. They know which public screens can be tricked into displaying poems instead of flood advisories.

The elderly often become local instruments of memory. In a city where maps rewrite themselves, someone must remember what a place used to be. An elder may sit by a canal and say: this was once a school entrance; that wall was the flank of a hospital; under this market is a drowned avenue where the lamps still burn; do not rent rooms above that warm seam because it opens every ninth year. Their knowledge is not nostalgic. It is practical. The past is another layer of infrastructure.

What they worship, if worship is the word, is continuity under pressure. Not purity. There is no purity in a city where everything leaks into everything else. They honor containment, release, circulation, repair. Their sacred images are bowls, gates, lungs, hands cupped around water, animals kneeling under loads, walls holding through the night. Creation myths speak less of gardens than of basins, less of first trees than of first drainage. Evil is stagnation, seal-failure, dry rot, hidden pressure, the refusal to open when opening is required.

They are not gloomy. The place is too crowded for sustained solemnity. They are loud when sound permits, funny in disaster, quick to complain, quick to help if water is rising. Public argument is a civic art. Markets ring with insults, songs, auction chants, animal calls, and legal warnings. A person may pause mid-quarrel to help their opponent lift a stall before a sluice surge, then resume accusing them of fraud as the water drains away.

Beauty, for them, lies in sheen, resilience, rhythm, useful complexity. A beloved street is one whose drainage sings correctly. A handsome animal has even cargo scars and clear eyes. A fine building sweats evenly. A good coat sheds rain in beads small enough to show the maker’s skill. They admire reflections: lamps trembling in canal water, signal cloth doubled on wet pavement, faces broken and remade in vibrating puddles. They distrust matte surfaces.

There are outsiders, though few remain outsiders long. Visitors from drier places are obvious: they hesitate before stepping into water, look upward too often, protect their shoes, flinch at animal shrieks, ask where the city ends. Locals find this last question especially childish. The city does not end; it changes density, jurisdiction, smell, and flood schedule.

And beneath all of them, around all of them, through them, lives the city itself.

Not as a single mind, perhaps. More like a body with many overlapping nervous systems and no central dream. Its waters circulate. Its walls heal. Its foundations shift in sleep. Its machines cough, decide, remember, and misinterpret. Its citizens feed it labor and receive shelter, route, heat, warning, inconvenience. Its animals scrape, carry, clog, clean, breed, escape, and return. Its rains fall from its own breath.

What lives there is therefore a civilization adapted not to land, but to saturation.

A people without fields.

A fauna without wilderness.

A technology without cleanliness.

A city without countryside.

A body made of mineral, water, law, animal, heat, habit, and noise.

When evening rain begins, they do not seek to escape it. They fasten their collars, raise their hoods, lift their goods above the first surge, call to children, curse the late gates, steady the pack-beasts, check the wall lights, taste the air, and keep moving. The water strikes their shoulders and runs from them into the streets. Around their boots, the city receives it. Above them, the machines exhale weather. Beneath them, old foundations listen.

Nothing green answers the rain.

They do.
