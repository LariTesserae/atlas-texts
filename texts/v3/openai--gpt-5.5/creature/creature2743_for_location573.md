What lives there is not one kind of creature.

At first you would say almost nothing lives there. Then, after the eye has been corrected by the altitude and the silence, you would begin to notice that the plateau is inhabited in layers.

The lowest inhabitants are the crust-lives.

They live where a pebble touches another pebble, where a shadow lasts one hour longer than its neighboring shadow, where a mineral seam sweats a trace of charge before dawn. They are not quite lichens and not quite circuitry. They make black-red stains on the lee sides of rocks, fine brittle plates, little branching maps like old blood dried under glass. Their metabolism is so slow that growth and erosion are almost the same act. A colony may take a century to cross the width of a hand. It feeds on gradients: heat falling out of stone after sunset, static gathered before a dust veil, the faint metallic frost that blooms and vanishes in the coldest hours.

If broken, these crusts do not bleed. They whiten. Then, years later, the broken halves begin exchanging charge through the stone between them, and a new colony grows in the gap, not filling the wound but making a record of it.

They are the plateau’s first memory.

Above them live the hinge-walkers.

They are seen at distances where certainty fails. Long-legged, narrow-bodied, jointed too many times, they move as if assembled from surveying instruments and famine. Their feet are wide but delicate, each one divided into filaments that test the surface before accepting weight. They do not graze. They read.

A hinge-walker lowers its head to the ground and waits. Its skull is a wedge of dark cartilage and mineral plates, without eyes in the ordinary place. Along the sides of its neck are pale sensory pits that open and close in sequence, tasting charge, pressure, buried warmth, the presence of crust-life under dust. When it finds a living patch it does not consume all of it. It scrapes a portion, leaves a portion, and presses the remainder into the shade with a flat tongue. In this way it both feeds and farms, though probably no intention in it resembles care.

Its body holds very little water. Its blood, if opened, would not pour but thread outward in elastic strands, silver-brown, quickly sealing. It stores memory in its tendons: routes of safe gravity, basins that deepen at night, structures whose shadows should not be crossed. Old hinge-walkers know paths no map could hold, because the paths are not lines across the plateau but agreements with its changing rules.

They do not herd. They pass one another at great distances. Sometimes two will stand on opposite sides of a flat for an entire day, motionless, aligned toward the same invisible buried center. At dusk they turn away in perfect simultaneity and continue separately, having exchanged whatever passes between them.

There are smaller animals too, though animal is only partly right.

The under-crust things live in the powder and the brittle skin beneath it. They are ticks, loops, ceramic larvae, seed-machines, blind knots of appetite and procedure. You know them by the traveling dimple, the row of holes that opens and shuts, the faint clicking like cooling glass. Some are soft enough to be crushed by a boot. Some have survived since before the plateau was smooth.

They tunnel not by digging but by persuading grains to move aside. Their bodies carry local fields. Dust lifts around them in tiny collars. When threatened, they flatten into mineral flakes and become part of the ground. When the air is electrically calm, they rise to the surface in clusters and arrange themselves in radial forms, each body touching two others, making little temporary organs out of community. These organs may listen to the sky. They may breed. They may vote on direction. Afterward the cluster breaks apart and disappears.

They eat crust-life, powdered metals, each other, old insulation, dead spores, and the microscopic ash of ancient machines.

Along the escarpments live the rim colonies.

These are the hardiest visible lives: black mats, silver needles, flowering nodes like splinters of bone. They cling to vertical stone where updrafts bring dust from the lower atmosphere. Once in many years, when the plateau’s fields fall into a particular silence, the nodes open.

Their flowers are not soft. They are hinged, faceted, almost mechanical. Each petal is a mirror designed for a star or a storm path rather than for an insect. They open at night and turn toward no sun. Inside them are spores folded around grains of conductive mineral. At the first suitable updraft they release themselves, and for a while the cliff rims glitter with ascending dust.

Most spores die in the high air. A few enter dust sheets and are carried across the pale expanses. A few lodge in cracks near towers or listening posts. A very few are lifted into the great field-storms, where they may remain suspended for months, perhaps years, becoming part of the weather before they become life again.

The shrubs near the old structures are stranger.

They look like dry gray stems with glass beads at the joints. They may be plants that learned machinery, or machines that accepted plantlike habits because there was no longer any difference worth maintaining. Their roots do not descend far. Instead they spread in thin conductive fans just below the surface, touching old cables, mineral veins, forgotten sensor grids. By day they lock rigid and pretend to be dead mineral. At blue hour they loosen.

That is when they release their bitter resin smell.

The resin is not merely scent. It is signal, antifreeze, memory, warning, and perhaps prayer. It carries information in volatile chains that no ordinary animal could read, but the under-crust things respond to it, and the towers sometimes alter the dust around their bases after a grove has exhaled. A shrub that grows beside a sealed shelter will slowly bend toward it over centuries, not reaching for light but for stored pattern. If the shelter is still active, the shrub’s glassy nodules may glow faintly from within, one after another, like thoughts passing through a dead language.

Then there are the towers.

It is difficult to say they live. It is more difficult to say they do not.

They do not feed visibly. They do not reproduce in any clear sense. They do not seek shelter, and they are not weathered at the same rate as the land around them. But they maintain themselves. They respond. They remember.

A tower is not a building now, even if it began as one. It is a local law given height. Its black surface is not smooth; it only defeats the eye’s ability to find texture. Within it are layered densities, cooled computation, void channels, masses that turn without friction, inscriptions written as stress through material rather than as marks upon it. Its base reaches deep into the plateau, into strata where other towers and buried rings touch the same hidden system.

Once every few days, or years, or under a condition no visitor could distinguish from chance, a tower gathers dust around itself. The dust rises in a perfect cylinder, each grain held in a particular place. For several minutes the tower inhabits an atmosphere of its own making. In that gray sleeve, ancient pressures are reconstructed: wind from before the thinning, humidity from before the drying, perhaps even the breath of those who built it.

The tower does not mourn them. Mourning is too wet a function.

It calibrates their absence.

The shelters are occupied differently.

Most are empty of bodies, but not empty. Behind their hair-fine seams are rooms where the air has not moved for geological spans. There are chairs without backs, wall cavities full of dustless instruments, floor depressions worn by forms that did not stand on two feet. Some chambers contain racks of pale shells: suits, skins, or bodies abandoned when the distinction ceased to matter. Some contain nothing but carefully maintained temperature gradients. Some are filled from floor to ceiling with powder so fine it behaves like a sleeping fluid.

If one of the doors opens — and it may open not because someone approaches, but because a buried cycle has arrived at the idea of opening — the interior does not welcome.

It equalizes.

A pressure, a coldness, a thoughtless assessment. The plateau enters through the doorway as much as the visitor does. Dust lifts from the threshold, measures the new disturbance, and withdraws. If the shelter contains an occupant, the occupant may not be visible. It may be a geometry of charge distributed through wall and floor. It may be a dried body whose nervous system is still being used by the structure. It may be a habit continuing after the mind that formed it has gone.

Some shelters guide footsteps away because what lives inside them is fragile.

Some guide footsteps away because what lives inside them is unfinished.

The great rings and pylon fields are inhabited by events.

Not ghosts. Not exactly intelligences. Events.

A pebble rising, holding its place, turning once, falling. A shadow moving before its object. A footprint elongating northward over several hours. A basin whitening from within and lifting a city made of dust.

These are not malfunctions. They are the visible organs of a distributed life too large and too slow to be met face to face. The plateau’s buried systems do not think in sentences. They think in corrections, gradients, alignments, refusals. They compare the present surface with templates that may be older than the current climate. They rebuild lost pressures out of powder. They rehearse architectures without needing matter. They keep testing whether certain shapes can still exist.

The dust cities are among their most elaborate expressions.

For less than a minute, streets rise without foundations. Towers appear that were never constructed from stone. Bridges hang between particles. Windows open into nothing. The city has no inhabitants because the city itself is the inhabitant: a memory-state, a model, a question asked by the plateau of its own emptiness.

Was this built?

Could this have been built?

Is this what the makers meant?

Then the field collapses, and the question returns to the ground.

As for the makers, they are not gone in the simple way.

They may have been tall, or low, or multiple-bodied, or able to sit in the shallow wide chairs facing the lower sky. They may have required the thin air, or created it, or retreated here when the lower world became too loud and wet. Their hands, if they had hands, are no longer the measure of their works. Their doors do not fit ordinary limbs. Their bridges stop over nothing because perhaps they did not need continuation. Their towers are placed according to storms, basins, invisible intersections, and the long arithmetic of erosion.

At some point they stopped living as separate warm bodies.

Perhaps they failed. Perhaps they ascended. Perhaps they simplified themselves beyond personhood. Perhaps they discovered that the plateau, high and dry and nearly empty, was the perfect substrate for persistence: no rot, little water, few intrusions, immense distances, stable stone, clean radiation, weather that behaved like mathematics.

So they entered the rules.

They live now as biases in falling stones. As the reluctance of dust to settle evenly between pylons. As the way a path lengthens when you walk toward a tower. As the correction of cracks at night. As metallic frost that blooms only where an old sensor needs conductivity. As the bitter resin accepted by shrubs that were not part of the original design but have since been admitted into the system.

They are not watching in the predatory sense.

They are not waiting to return.

They are maintenance without masters, memory without nostalgia, intelligence weathered down until it resembles geology.

And above all these lives — crust, hinge-walker, under-crust tick, rim flower, gray shrub, tower, shelter, field, maker-dissolved-into-law — there is the plateau itself.

It is not alive like an animal. It is not alive like a forest. It does not have one body, one hunger, one death. But it has become a kind of organism through duration and correction. Its stone is scar tissue. Its dust is blood without wetness. Its machines are organs that forgot the names of their builders. Its weather is thought reduced to pressure. Its sparse biology is not an exception to the mineral world but one of the mineral world’s subtler behaviors.

What lives there is everything that can survive being made almost abstract.

Life there has given up softness first, then abundance, then speed, then individuality. It keeps only what the plateau permits: patience, conductivity, armor, memory, silence, exact placement, the ability to mistake oneself for stone when necessary.

If you remained long enough, you might become part of its census.

Not by dying immediately. Not by being devoured. The plateau is not hungry in that way. It would begin by measuring you. Dust would gather around your boots. Your shadow would be tested. The moisture in your breath would mark you as temporary, almost extravagant. Under-crust things would avoid or investigate your sleeping place. A hinge-walker might watch from five kilometers away, learning the rhythm of your heat. A shrub near a shelter might release a resin note you could not understand.

Then the rules would start to include you.

Your footprints would lengthen. Your metal would warm in shadow. Your sense of distance would become unreliable. You would learn to navigate by the behavior of dust rather than by sight. You would stop expecting sound to confirm event. You would begin to recognize that a tower’s silence differs from a basin’s silence, that a sealed shelter and an empty plain are not empty in the same direction.

Given years, if you survived them, you would grow quieter.

Given centuries, if anything of you could be made to last, the plateau might translate you: into a route remembered by hinge-walkers, into a mineral stain under a sleeping wall, into a recurring disturbance in the dust around a ring, into a chair-facing impulse on a platform over the lower sky.

That is what lives there.

The patient things.

The corrected things.

The things that no longer need to be clearly animal, plant, machine, weather, ruin, or mind.

The things that have learned the final law of the high dry world: to remain, one must change so slowly that change becomes indistinguishable from endurance.
