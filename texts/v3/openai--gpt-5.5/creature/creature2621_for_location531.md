At first, what lives there seems to be moss.

That is the first impression: a city inhabited by green patience. Moss on thresholds, moss in seams, moss clasping old stone like a hand. Moss in bowls, in beds, in walls, in the dark felted places where water remembers to gather. But then the moss shifts, and a child’s eye appears between hanging roots; a cat moves along a ladder rail; a woman lifts a curtain of woven reed without making it whisper.

The people who live there are small only in the way burrowing animals are small: compact, economical, made for narrow passages and low roofs. They are not frail. Their bodies have learned compression. Shoulders slope easily. Knees fold without complaint. Hands are strong, broad across the knuckles, with careful fingers stained by lichen, peat, fish oil, root sap, and charcoal. Their feet are quiet and tough, often wrapped in soft hide or moss-padded shoes, though many go barefoot inside the warmer galleries, feeling for dampness, grit, tremor, change.

Their faces are pale in the city’s white light, but not bloodless. The cold has given them red eyelids, chapped lips, darkened cheeks, and noses that run in childhood and harden with age. Their hair is usually worn close, braided flat to the skull, tucked beneath caps, or wrapped in cloth, because loose hair catches in ladders, roots, hooks, bird nests, and other people’s business. Older people grow wisps of white hair that resemble the roof roots, and this is considered beautiful. A very old person with fine white hair and a soft tread is said to be “nearly rooted.”

They do not speak much with their mouths.

They can speak, and do, but voice is an intimate tool, like a needle or a cup of stored heat. Most daily conversation happens in hands, shoulders, brows, and touch. Their sign language is old, dense, and local. It has signs for varieties of water: roof-sweat, rain-drip, cistern-still, root-sour, pool-bright, melt-hurry, bad seep, patient seep. It has signs for kinds of silence: safe silence, listening silence, grief silence, angry silence, festival silence, silence-before-falling. It has signs for obligations too small for outsiders to notice: return this peg, shift that bowl, your flue smells bitter, the moss behind your wall is thirsty, your child has learned a dangerous crack.

Their spoken language is mostly used close to the ear. Lovers murmur into sleeves. Mothers speak into infants’ hair. A dying person’s last words are received by one listener, who repeats them later only if permitted. Public speech exists, but it is almost ritualized: a few low phrases at births, repairs, judgments, root permissions, and the sealing of the dead. A loud voice is not considered powerful. It is childish, wasteful, almost obscene. Authority belongs to those who can make a whole corridor understand them with two fingers and a tilted palm.

They are not a fearful people, though outsiders often mistake them for one. Their quiet is not shrinking. It is discipline, courtesy, engineering, worship, and common sense braided so tightly that no one bothers to separate them. Children are not told simply, “Be quiet.” They are taught why sound travels through root, roof, water, and bone. They learn by placing palms against walls while an elder taps a wrapped mallet at different distances. They learn which stone carries tremor, which clay drinks it, which hollow lies behind a patched surface. By the time they can climb alone, they understand that the city is an instrument, and every body inside it is always playing.

Their children are solemn in public and wild in secret.

They play in cracks too narrow for adults, in storage tunnels, under sleeping shelves, behind cistern walls where the white frogs sit like old thoughts. They invent silent games of balance, memory, mimicry, and theft-with-return. One game involves moving a feather from household to household without anyone seeing it travel. Another requires a child to cross three ladders, two bridges, and a market rail while carrying a bowl filled to the lip with water; spilling means beginning again, not because anyone scolds, but because wasting water ruins the game. They laugh with their whole bodies, knees drawn up, faces buried in sleeves, shaking like moths hatching.

A child’s first serious lesson is how to listen upward.

Not with ears alone. With scalp, teeth, fingertips, soles. They are taught to know the mood of the roof: frost-tight, thaw-loose, rain-fed, root-heavy, sleep-safe. Some children are better at it than others. Those who feel small shifts through stone become roof-listeners, an honored calling. They carry no badge except a cord of pale root fiber around one wrist. They move through the city touching walls, kneeling by seams, pressing their faces to cold stone. When they signal caution, even the cats seem to obey.

There are many trades, but few are separate from one another.

A mosskeeper is also a healer, a water-tender, and a keeper of memory, because moss remembers damp, smoke, blood, fever, and neglect. A flue-mender must understand peat, charcoal, clay, moths, breath, sleep, and quarrels between neighbors who accuse one another of stealing warmth. A root-binder must know which roots may be bent, which must be fed, which belong to houses above that no one has ever seen, and which are considered ancestral because generations have slept beneath them. A fish-tender works in darkness by touch, counting blind fish in stone bowls, judging their health by the pressure of their bodies against the palm.

Their leaders are not kings or chiefs. Leadership belongs to councils of competence: the Drip-Keepers, the Root-Askers, the Heat-Sharers, the Roof-Listeners, the Rememberers of Paths. These councils overlap. A person may serve one for a season and another in old age. Decisions are made slowly, sometimes over days, with tokens placed and moved in a shallow tray of pale clay. The tokens are bone, shell, seed, tooth, reed, copper scrap, and polished black peat. Each has history. Each has weight. Argument consists of rearrangement, pause, removal, replacement. Only when all hands withdraw does a decision exist.

They count wealth strangely.

A rich household is not one with many objects, because there is no room for useless abundance. Wealth is measured in reliability: a dry sleeping ledge, a clean flue, good moss, stored tubers, quiet children, healthy cats, uncracked bowls, a ladder others trust, a remembered debt of warmth. A family that owns a copper blade is fortunate, but a family whose wall catches three dependable drips after rain may be envied more. The most respected households are those that can take in guests during a cold spell without complaint.

They marry for many reasons, but always with attention to space.

Courtship is a delicate architecture. Two people must love, or at least incline toward one another, but their niches, duties, warmth allotments, and drip rights must also fit. A marriage proposal may involve the offering of a new shelf peg, a repaired boot sole, or a map-cord showing how two households might be joined without blocking a seep line. The most romantic gift is not jewelry. It is a place made usable: a sleeping alcove insulated, a ladder quieted, a wall garden coaxed back to life, a cistern cleaned by hand in winter.

When they join households, they do not feast loudly. They sit together in the corridor nearest their future home while neighbors pass small foods from hand to hand: fish paste, bitter greens, frost berries, mushroom cakes, slivers of smoked root. Then the couple shares a bowl of warmed water. Everyone watches the steam rise. That is the blessing.

The old are treasured because they know where things were before they became other things.

An old woman may remember when a pigeon shelf was a shrine, when the shrine was a seed vault, when the seed vault was a cradle niche, and whose child slept there during the long freeze. An old man may know that a certain wall bulges not because it is weak, but because a buried willow root thickened behind it fifty years ago and now helps hold the chamber. They keep genealogies not only of people, but of repairs, ladders, moss beds, cisterns, flues, cats, and leaks. Their memories are civic infrastructure.

The dead remain useful, but not crudely so.

Bodies are not buried deep, because earth is scarce and the marsh above is not easily opened. They are wrapped in moss, reed cloth, and root fiber, placed for a time in the coldest chamber, and visited in silence. Afterward, practices vary by family. Some bones are cleaned and kept in wall niches, named by touch. Some are carved into buttons, needles, prayer tabs, fish hooks, or door pegs, so that the dead continue to fasten the city together. This is not considered morbid. A grandmother’s finger bone made into a needle is still grandmother: still mending sleeves, still closing gaps, still keeping cold from a child’s wrist.

They have little metal, and therefore little arrogance about tools.

Everything is sharpened, patched, repurposed, softened, bound. A knife may be a great-grandparent’s flint set into a new bone handle with old hide glue. A bowl may have seven mended cracks, each repair recorded in red thread around the rim. A ladder rung worn low by feet is not replaced until it becomes unsafe; its smoothness is a form of ancestry. Waste is not sin because a god forbade it. Waste is sin because someone will be cold, thirsty, hungry, cramped, or endangered by your carelessness.

The animals live among them not as pets exactly, nor as livestock exactly, but as fellow citizens with simpler obligations.

The cats are the most visible after the people. They are round, short-legged, thick-pelted, and almost eerily silent. Their whiskers are long and often beaded with condensation. They sleep in warm flue pockets, patrol grain shelves, attend births, and judge strangers from high beams. No one owns a cat completely. A cat may favor a household for years and then transfer allegiance after a better sleeping place is constructed elsewhere. Kittens are given names only after they have survived their first winter and caught their first reed-mouse.

The reed-mice are pests, but not hated. They are clever, long-toed, soft-bellied, and able to flatten themselves into impossible seams. Children admire them. Grainkeepers do not. There are old stories in which mice taught the first people how to find hidden cracks, and other stories in which mice ate the winter seed and nearly ended the city. Both are believed. To live there is to understand that a creature may be teacher and thief at once.

The birds in the upper roots are treated with almost diplomatic respect.

They are tiny, gray-green, and nervous, with needle feet and black bead eyes. Their threadlike calls are among the only sounds permitted to travel freely. People leave hair combings, husks, and softened fiber near their nesting places. In return, the birds keep mites from the moss gardens and give warning when the roof shifts in unfamiliar ways. If the birds all move from one root braid to another, roof-listeners arrive within minutes. If they fall silent entirely, even work stops.

The white frogs are considered weather-thoughts.

They live in cisterns, basins, and cold dark bowls beneath grates. Their skin is almost translucent; their organs show faintly, like shadows behind scraped parchment. They make no croak. Their throats pulse, and the people read them: slow pulse for settled cold, fast pulse for rain above, frantic climbing for bad water or tremor. Children are forbidden to torment them. A household that kills a cistern frog by neglect must apologize publicly with water, which is one of the most serious forms of apology.

The blind fish are food, omen, and mystery. They turn in their black pools beneath the city, pale as peeled roots, sensing movement through water. Some are raised in stone basins, fed on insect meal, fungus scrap, and powdered shell. Others live in channels too deep or narrow to fully map. Occasionally one appears in the central bright pool, rising without ripple into the white glare. No one catches those fish. They are allowed to descend. What comes voluntarily into brightness is not taken.

The moths belong to warmth.

They sleep along flues in pale overlapping rows, wings marked with frostlike veins. When a flue burns too hot, they stir and crawl away before smoke changes; when a flue cools dangerously, they cluster closer. Flue-menders read moth behavior the way sailors might read clouds. Children collect fallen moth wings and press them into clay charms, not for luck, but for remembering gentleness.

There are stranger things farther out: translucent worms in seep lines, soft crabs with shells like wet fingernails, pale bats that crawl along the roof and fold themselves into root mats, beetles that shine only when wet, mites so important to soil that gardeners speak of them respectfully though they can barely be seen. Nothing is merely vermin until it breaks its bargain. Nothing is sacred if it threatens the city and cannot be turned aside.

And beneath all of them, or around all of them, or through all of them, lives the city itself.

The people do not say this plainly. They are too practical for that, or too reverent. But they behave as if the hollow has appetite, mood, memory, and skin. They feed walls with moss. They bandage cracks. They give roots space to breathe. They catch the roof’s sweat in cups. They warm only what can be warmed without starving another chamber. They stroke old stone when passing, not sentimentally, but the way one might touch the shoulder of a sleeping relative.

If there is a spirit there, it is not a towering god. It is a low, cold, luminous presence made of accumulated care. It lives in shared breath, in repaired ladders, in the hush before rainwater reaches the market gutters, in the hands that move silently to make room for someone carrying a full bowl. It has no temple because every wall is load-bearing, every drip has consequence, and every household is already praying by keeping something alive.

The inhabitants are therefore not merely people in a hidden city.

They are a quiet species of attention.

They live by noticing: the lean of reeds above, the taste of roots, the mood of frogs, the tremor of marl, the thirst of moss, the fatigue in a neighbor’s shoulders, the extra cold around a child’s bed. They have made survival into etiquette and etiquette into beauty. They are crowded, but seldom careless. Poor in metal, rich in memory. Primitive in tool, intricate in relation. Soft-footed, sharp-eyed, cold-fingered, green-thumbed, patient as condensation.

They do not dream of leaving for open fields.

Open fields, to them, are terrifyingly empty: no roof to read, no walls to touch, no moss to soften a fall, no known paths braided by generations of bodies. The sky is too loud even when silent. Wind is a kind of shouting. Distance is wasteful. A horizon is a place with no handholds.

Their world is narrow, but not small.

It contains water in a hundred moods, silence in a hundred meanings, green life in every crack, ancestry in every tool, and a whole people who have learned to live as lightly as breath beneath a listening roof.
