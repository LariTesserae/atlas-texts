What lives there is not quite hidden and not quite absent.

At first it seems the basin has no resident larger than a fly, no mind in it except the old intention left in the cut basins and stone rows. But after enough stillness, the signs begin to change character. A groove that looked made by water has the slight regularity of being kept open. A crust broken at the edge of a pool has been lifted, not cracked by chance. A thumb-sized plug of clay has been pressed recently into a seam where seepage ran too freely. The place is not abandoned. It is being maintained by something that has learned to resemble maintenance itself.

They live low.

They are small by human measure, though not tiny: perhaps the length of a forearm when stretched, but they are seldom stretched. They fold. They crouch. They move with elbows, knees, palms, and the slick undersides of long feet, staying close to stone as if height were a wasteful or dangerous habit. When one crosses open ground, it does not run. It pours itself from cover to cover, pausing in the wet shine between rocks until its outline breaks apart among clay ridges and mineral stains.

Their skin is the color of the basin: grey-beige, clay-white, iron-smudged in the creases, dark along the spine and shoulders where damp soot and mineral oils cling. It is not scaled exactly, but plated in soft, overlapping calluses, like old leather soaked and dried a thousand times. The young are paler, almost translucent around the throat and belly, with the blue-grey suggestion of inner vessels. The old become crusted. Salt feathers along their wrists, brow ridges, and the backs of their hands. Some carry pale fans of mineral growth on their shoulders the way neglected stones do, and these are not ornaments, though they may be marks of age.

Their heads are blunt and low, with no external ears. The eyes are set wide and shallow, filmed against vapor and mineral sting. In dim light the eyes do not flash; they merely gather brightness, becoming two dull wet points like water in drilled holes. The mouth is narrow until opened. Then it proves wide, soft-edged, useful for scraping films from stone, tasting seepage, and shaping wet clay with a precision hands alone cannot manage.

Their hands are their most human part, and the least easy to dismiss.

Four long fingers, one opposing thumb, broad pads darkened by use. The nails are flat, thick, and shovel-like, worn to exact curves by years of scraping, lifting, sealing, prying. They do not strike stone often. They worry it. They rub and coax and abrade. Their labor leaves no grand mark because grand marks do not last here. Instead they make small corrections continually: clearing a channel no wider than a finger, raising the lip of a basin by a handful of clay, resetting a fallen shale tile, packing warm mud around a leak until the seep resumes its proper thread.

They are not builders in the way people are builders. They do not seem to imagine completion as a separate state. A wall is not finished; it is being held in its degree of leaning. A pool is not made; it is persuaded to keep its depth. A shelter is not repaired; it is kept from becoming too much less than shelter. Their whole society, if that word can be used, is an agreement with slow failure.

They feed on what the basin provides reluctantly.

At dawn, or what passes for dawn under the dulled sky, they lift stones whose undersides are furred with pale films. They do this carefully, never stripping a surface bare. With shell scrapers, with tongues, with the edges of fired-clay spoons, they take a portion of the growth and set the stone back exactly as it was. In the milkier basins they lower shallow trays of porous clay, leave them for days, then draw them up coated in a faint living slick. This they dry over old warm ash, not by flame but by patient heat, until it becomes a thin brittle cake tasting of salt, chalk, and the hidden life of still water.

They also harvest crust.

The amber skins that gather on certain pools are prized. They are lifted with loops of cord or the broad back of a finger and laid over curved tiles to stiffen. Some become food. Some become sealant. Some are chewed and softened and used to mend cracks in bowls. Black mineral paste from the wet seams is kept in thumb-marked plugs and used to darken the skin around the eyes, coat tools, or mark stones whose balance must not be disturbed.

There is no cooking smoke most days. Fire is rare and treated as an impatient visitor. The old hearth rings are not dead, but neither are they places of daily flame. When fire is made, it is small, sheltered, and brief, fed with things brought from beyond the rim: dry dung, root knots, scraps of reed, slivers of wood traded or stolen or gathered on long excursions. Flame is used to harden clay, cauterize sickness, frighten burrowing vermin, and mark certain passages of life. It is never allowed to become cheerful.

They speak little.

Their voices, when heard, seem at first like the basin settling: soft clicks, throat pulses, wet taps made with tongue against palate. A group working together may communicate for an hour without any sound that would carry farther than a few paces. Much of their language is pressure and interruption: a hand placed over a seep to say wait; two fingers laid on a stone row to say this line must remain; a palm turned upward beside a basin to ask what has changed here.

They are excellent listeners, but not for birdsong or wind. They listen to levels. They hear the difference between a drop falling into a full pan and one falling into a pan that has lost a finger’s depth overnight. They hear whether mud releases a foot cleanly or with the hidden pull of a new cavity beneath. They put their cheeks to stone posts and feel deep tremors through the jaw. They can tell when a basin wall is preparing to slump days before it does.

Their dwellings are almost indistinguishable from the failed human works around them.

They occupy the roofless shelter at the eastern edge, but not as humans would. They do not mind the open top. They sleep under the raised stone floor, in the warm gaps between fist-sized rocks, wrapped in damp skins of woven fiber and mineral-stiff cloth. Other families live beneath collapsed platforms or inside the hollowed backs of clay ridges. Entrances are low crescents hidden behind leaning slabs. A human might see only a crack black with water. Inside are chambers no taller than a crouched body, smooth from generations of shoulders.

They keep their young in the warmest cracks near the western wall, where the pale lizard-things also live. The lizards are not pets, but they are tolerated. They eat flies and tiny blind grubs from the seams. Children watch them with solemn attention, learning from their pauses and vanishments. A child of the basin is taught early that suddenness is wasteful. Even play is quiet: floating shell chips down miniature channels, building tiny cairns and waiting to see which one the next drop destroys, pressing handprints into clay and then smoothing them away before they dry.

Their dead are not buried beyond the basin. Nothing important is taken out if it can be returned inward.

When one dies, the body is washed in three basins: one clear, one milky, one dark. The soft parts are wrapped in clay and laid beneath warm stones where seepage passes continually over them. Bones, when clean, are not assembled into skeletons. They are divided. Finger bones become burnishers. Ribs are set under basin lips to strengthen them. Skulls, if they remain whole, are packed with mineral paste and placed inside leaning cairns where water can enter and leave through the mouth. This is not reverence in the human sense, perhaps. Or perhaps it is reverence made practical. To remain useful is the form of peace they understand best.

They remember the former human workers, but not as ancestors exactly.

In their stories, the tall ones came and cut the first bowls, stacked the first rows, learned the first lessons badly and loudly. The tall ones wanted products: salt, pigment, curing clay, bitter water, black paste, pale shell-lime—whatever the basin gave in those days. They worked in seasons and left when weather changed. They built too high and too straight. They hurried evaporation. They widened channels that should have stayed narrow. But they also made the first shelter, the first stone court, the first hearth rings. For this they are neither cursed nor praised. They are included among the basin’s earlier disturbances.

The present inhabitants may have come after them, or may have been there all along, unnoticed in cracks and undersides. Their own account does not separate creature from place. They say, in their clicking way, that the basin grew hands because hands were needed.

Their rituals are acts of calibration.

At the lowest center, where the crowded court of shallow pools lies divided by raised clay lips, they gather at long intervals. Not many at once; the place is too small for crowds. They come in threes, fives, sevens, crouching at the edges of pools that touch but do not merge. Each brings a little water from a different seep, carried in a shell or the cupped hollow of a fired-clay shard. One by one they add these waters to different basins. Then they wait to see which surface clouds, which clears, which forms a skin, which accepts the addition without visible change.

This is how they make decisions.

Not by vote, not by command, but by response. If the dark central pool takes a poured thread and remains dark, a channel may be opened. If the oval basin near the old post clouds white, a birth is delayed, a journey postponed, a wall left untouched. If amber film breaks into islands, someone must climb the rim and bring back dry fuel. Outsiders would call this superstition, but the basin has rewarded attention for so long that attention and law have become the same thing.

They do go beyond the rim, though rarely.

Those who leave return dusted with the impossible colors of elsewhere: pollen yellow, leaf green, road white. They wash these colors off before entering the central court. The outside world is understood as excessive—too much air, too much height, too many changing smells. Trees are alarming because they grow upward without permission. Grass is disliked for hiding the ground. Birds are regarded as nearly weather, noisy and badly governed. Dryness cracks the skin and makes thought quick in an unpleasant way.

What they bring back from outside is stored sparingly: cord, bone, dry fuel, shells, sometimes news. But news has little standing unless it concerns water, stone, heat, or the approach of human feet.

If humans enter the basin now, the inhabitants withdraw below the evident world. A visitor may feel watched and see nothing. A tool left on a stone may be found later turned slightly, its rusting edge placed away from a seep. A footprint may be filled in overnight, not to erase the visitor but to restore the level. If the visitor damages a basin lip, it will be repaired by morning with such modesty that the repair looks older than the damage.

They are not hostile. Hostility is too hot a state. But they resist disturbance with the full force of persistence. Block a channel and they will reopen it. Drain a pool and they will feed it drop by drop. Build a high wall and they will undermine it by keeping the clay too wet on one side. Bring in seeds and they will lift them out, one by one, before they root.

They are the basin’s reluctance to change given flesh.

Or perhaps flesh is the wrong word. They are clay remembering touch, water learning patience, old labor continuing after the laborers have gone. They live there because nowhere else would allow them to be so nearly still and so completely occupied. Their lives are not empty. They are filled with levels, films, seams, temperatures, leanings, minute repairs, and the long discipline of not asking the place to become anything other than what it has already become.

When a drop falls in the central court and the rings spread outward, they notice.

They wait until the rings touch the clay edge and vanish.

Then, if nothing further is required, they resume.
