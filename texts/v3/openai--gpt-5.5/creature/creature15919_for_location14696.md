What lives there is not one creature first, but a tenancy.

The place is inhabited by a slow, thin, cold-adapted society of plants, fungi, small animals, and machines that have learned to share the same waste. Nothing there is sovereign. Nothing dominates in the way a forest dominates. The resident is distributed: a skin, a breath, a root system, a pattern of dampness, a set of habits repeated in the roar.

If it had to be named, it would be the undergrowth.

Not undergrowth as in brush and bramble, but the growth that belongs under things: under factories, under service yards, under rail lines, under concrete slabs, under human intention. It lives by entering what was not meant for it. It follows seams. It reads cracks better than maps. It does not break the place open all at once. It loosens it, accepts it, pads it, stains it, fattens around its leaks.

The moss is the most visible part of it. The moss is almost the face.

It lies over the floors in weathered pads and raised lips, with colors that change by chamber: cold ash-green in the draft passages, bottle-green near warm piping, black-green in old drainage cuts, sulfur-yellow where some bad mineral or vapor has poisoned it. It grows in a way that makes it seem intelligent only because it is so exact. It thickens around warmth. It climbs toward lamps but stops before dry heat scorches it. It bridges hairline cracks, covers discarded washers, wraps the feet of handrails, rounds the edges of broken concrete without hiding the fact of breakage. It is soft but not innocent. It hides voids. It turns ankle-deep water into ground. It makes a floor where there is no longer a floor.

Under the moss live the smallest animals: springtails, mites, threadlike worms, pale beetles with reduced eyes, larvae that eat films of fungus and bacterial slick. They are not plentiful in a cheerful way. They are present the way dust is present. A lifted mat would show them briefly as movement: gray specks withdrawing from light, translucent bodies flexing between mineral grains, bead-sized insects vanishing into wet black crumbs. Their world is only a few millimeters high, roofed with green stems and brown decay. To them, the chambers are continents of cold ceiling and thunder.

The fungi are deeper and quieter. They occupy what the plants cannot digest quickly: pallet wood, cardboard, labels, buried paper, insulation, the backs of old notice boards, dead roots, the soft interior of timber supports. Most are not dramatic. They do not make great glowing shelves or fairy rings. They appear as gray bloom, white threads, black freckles, rubbery beige lips on soaked wood, a floury dust under collapsed crates. Some fruit only when a warm draft changes the chamber for a few days. Then, overnight, little caps rise in ranks along a beam or between bricks, pale as fingernails, and by the end of the week they have liquefied back into stains.

The roots are the oldest residents and the newest arrivals at once. They belong to trees and weeds above, to scrub birch, willow, grass, dock, buddleia, whatever has found purchase in the industrial plain. Down here they seem separated from their owners, independent, exploratory. Fine roots hang in curtains and tremble continuously in fan wash. Thick roots enter like decisions, forcing themselves through fractures and around bolts, following moisture with a patience no animal could imitate. They descend from a world of wind and sky into the cold service dark, and there they become another kind of organ: drinking not rain, but delay.

Some of them die in the air and remain as wires. Some reach a leak and thicken. Some braid around cables and pipes, confusing the made and the grown. In places where a shaft lets down weak daylight, the root curtains are so dense that they resemble hair hanging from the scalp of the surface world.

The ferns are the nearest thing to citizens.

They stand apart in clumps, each one occupying a little district of tolerable conditions. They are small, dark, tough, and deliberate. Their fronds do not unfurl extravagantly; they ration themselves. A fern there may keep the same damaged leaf for months, its edge browned by frost, its underside stippled with spores that may or may not ever find another suitable patch. Near warm valves they grow higher and more confident, leaning all together in the draft like people listening. In the open cold chambers they crouch low against concrete, their stems wiry, their green almost metallic.

The birches on the old platform are a special colony, almost theatrical in their improbability. They are not healthy trees, but they are not dying either. They have the look of underfed children who have learned perfect stillness. Their white bark catches the maintenance light; their leaves are small, sparse, and careful. Their roots hang below the grating in long wet tassels, feeling the air for more soil that is not there. They live suspended between engineering and accident, a grove on a machine part.

There are rats, but they do not own the place.

They live near the service lift because the lift is a kind of seasonal river. Things come down in its shaft: paper, food wrappers, dead insects, salt, warm smells, sometimes human dirt. The rats are narrow-bodied, dark, and cold-lean. Their fur is not glossy. Their ears are nicked by frost and fighting. They keep to cable trays, wall edges, and the undersides of platforms where the moss is thin enough not to betray them. They have learned the machinery’s constancy so completely that they freeze only at changes: a fan slowing, a distant door opening, the sudden absence of vibration in a pipe.

Their colony is small because the place does not forgive abundance. Too many young, and they starve. Too bold, and they fall into sumps or meet the fox.

The fox is not truly a resident. It is a visitor from the margins, a red thought entering the gray system when hunger or weather presses it. It comes through a drainage opening with its belly low and its ears flattened by the roar. Its paws leave beautiful prints in frost and mineral dust. It hates the metal grates, distrusts the moss, and knows better than to cross the broad open floors without pausing. It takes rats when it can. It eats moths from warm lamps. It noses at fungal wood and damp roots, then retreats through culverts too small for any upright creature. It brings, briefly, the idea of the surface with it: fur, blood, alertness, a smell of snow or rain.

The moths live around heat and light as if around poor suns.

They are small and pale, their wings powdered with dust, their bodies warmed by sodium bulbs and blue-white strips. They do not fly much. Flying costs too much in the cold and is useless in the noise. They cling. They wait. They blunder from one lamp halo to another when disturbed, crossing enormous dark spaces like scraps of ash. Some have larvae in the moss, some in rotting paper, some in the felted matter around pipe brackets. Their lives are brief, but generations of them have made a faint pale litter below the fixtures: wings, husks, bodies dried by heat.

There are also bacteria and algae, of course, though no one would call them inhabitants unless thinking carefully. They are the first claimants of new wetness. They film the walls below warm pipes. They darken channels. They make slick black-green skins where condensation runs often enough. They prepare surfaces for others. They are the primer coat of life.

And then there are the machines.

They do not live, but in that place they are part of what lives. The ecology has grown around their breath. The fans are weather-makers. The pumps are seasonal hearts. Transformers are warm stones. Dehumidifiers make deserts and gardens depending on which side of them one stands. A bad bearing can change the moisture of a chamber. A repaired valve can starve a fern bank. A lamp replaced after years can wake algae on a wall that had gone almost dry and blind.

The living things respond to the machines with more fidelity than they respond to the sun. They know the old fixtures, the pipes that sweat, the cabinets that hum, the ducts that freeze, the rails that carry vibration. They are not separate from the infrastructure’s failure; they are its second use.

If there are people there, they are rare and temporary.

A maintenance worker may enter in orange waterproofs and hearing protection, moving with exaggerated care, reading the moss like a warning system. A surveyor may set up a tripod in a vault and find, weeks later, that a pale film has started on the rubber feet. Someone unhoused may once have tried to sleep near the warmth of a transformer room, then left because the roar made dreaming impossible. Teenagers may come down from the surface with lamps and leave quickly, frightened less by darkness than by the sense that everything here is using time differently.

No human belongs there for long. The place is too loud for conversation, too cold for rest, too damp for paper, too indifferent to comfort. Humans made it, but the current residents are the ones that can accept what humans left running.

The true inhabitant is therefore a cold garden without a gardener.

It has no central body, but it has behavior. It gathers at leaks. It retreats from poison. It thickens around warmth. It sends scouts through cracks. It digests neglect. It records changes slowly in color and height. It makes maps in moss, roots, fungal threads, rat paths, algae stains, and the leaning of fern fronds. It hears nothing, yet the machinery’s roar shapes every part of it.

It does not want the place restored. It does not want the place ruined faster. Wanting is too hot a word for it. It simply occupies the available margin between abandonment and operation.

Where a pipe sweats, it drinks.

Where a lamp burns, it greens.

Where concrete cracks, it enters.

Where metal rusts, it holds the moisture close.

Where humans meant emptiness, it makes surface.

It lives shallowly under the world, in the unfinished cold, growing over the fresh wounds of construction with the patience of something that has never needed permission.
