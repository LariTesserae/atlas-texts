What lives there is mostly a green pressure.

Not an animal, not a hidden watcher, not anything with eyes in the ordinary sense. The basin is inhabited by things that cling, mat, root, film, and return after being flattened. Its life has no single body. It is a crowded, low, patient organism made from many small ones, spread over stone as if the mountain has begun growing a skin.

The first residents are the mosses.

They occupy the basin with the confidence of things that do not need much permission. They swell in cracks, cushion the tops of stones, soften the undersides of ledges, and make dark pads in hollows where water pauses. In the dimmest places they are nearly black, dense as wet felt. Where seepage feeds them, they brighten to green-gold and seem almost luminous against the cold rock. They hold water the way lungs hold breath. After mist or rain they become heavy, saturated, vivid; when the wind strips moisture from them they shrink inward, tighten, wait.

They are not delicate. They are the first carpet over injury, the first living fabric pulled across broken mineral. They catch dust. They trap leaf fragments. They slow the trickles just enough for silt to settle. Grain by grain, rot by rot, they begin the basin’s first real soil.

With them live liverworts, slick and close to the stone, spread like dark green lacquer in shaded seams. They occupy the places too wet or too poor even for roots. Their bodies are simple, flattened, almost mineral in their obedience to the surface beneath them. They shine when the light catches them. They make the rock look damp even when it is only alive.

Lichens live higher, where exposure is too severe for softness. They are crusts, scales, pale maps on the black walls. Some are ash-white, some yellow-gray, some a faint oxidized orange that echoes the iron stains. They grow so slowly they seem less like plants than decisions made by time. They endure the wind by refusing height. They endure hunger by needing almost nothing. They eat light when it comes, stone by chemical patience, moisture from cloud and film. They are part fungus, part alga, a compact treaty between two forms of need.

In the lower basin the ferns dominate.

They rise from wet pockets and root-matted shelves in ranks and fans, their fronds overlapping until the broken ground disappears. In the dim light they are blue-green, almost metallic. Their stems are tough, dark, fibrous; their leaflets shiver constantly, combing the air. When gusts strike, the whole fern mass bows in one direction like fur stroked hard by an invisible hand. Then it rises again, rearranged but not defeated.

They are the basin’s most visible softness, but even they are armored in habit. Their roots grip beneath stones. Their old fronds collapse into the wet, becoming brown ribs in the forming soil. Their new fronds uncurl close to the ground, protected among moss and gravel until they can take the weather. They do not invite passage. They hide holes, cracks, loose rocks, sudden drops. They make the basin look fuller and safer than it is.

Among them grow tough grasses and sedges, blades flattened, polished, and re-lifted by wind. They live in clumps, each clump a fist of roots around a seam of grit. Their leaves rasp against stone until their tips fray. They flower briefly, if the season allows, with small plain heads that spend most of their time bent sideways. Their purpose is not display. Their purpose is to hold.

The dwarf shrubs are the nearest thing to trees, though they never become tree-like. They are willows made low by altitude, wind, and repeated injury. Their branches weave through rubble no higher than a knee, sometimes no higher than an ankle. They creep rather than stand. Their leaves are small, tough, glossy, and often silvered underneath so that gusts turn the whole shrubwork into a flicker of dark and pale.

These shrubs are old in a way the basin itself may not look old. A stem may be new, but the root behind it may have survived many breakages. The plant has learned to live by losing parts. Snow may press it flat. Stones may shear away a branch. Wind may strip leaves from one side. It answers by sending another shoot through another crack. It is less a shrub than a persistence strategy.

There are also broad-leaved alpine herbs in the wet pockets, taking advantage of the basin’s trapped moisture. Their leaves overlap, shine, bruise easily. They make small roof-tiles of green over black soil. Some may flower in brief, low bursts when the season opens: white, yellow, violet, not large, not fragrant enough to call anything from afar. Their blooms are almost private events. In a place without insects, or nearly without them, flowering feels less like invitation than habit—an inherited motion performed whether or not anyone arrives.

Below all this, in the leaf rot and water-dark seams, live the hidden ones.

Fungi thread the forming soil. They pass through dead stems, soaked leaves, root skins, and splintered wood no thicker than wire. They are the quiet digesters of the basin, turning fallen green into something roots can use. Most of them never show a cap. They remain as pale filaments, nets, soft fans beneath bark, stains in humus, invisible agreements with roots.

Bacteria live in the wet stone, in the black pools, in the mineral films where seepage slides down cliff faces. Algae darken trickle lines and shallow water pockets. Microscopic life gathers wherever water remains for even a little while. These are the inhabitants that do not care that the basin is small, exposed, unstable. To them, a hand-wide pool is a world; a film of water on rock is a season; a crack filled with silt is a valley.

Together they make a community without voices.

There are no birds nesting in the crags, no marmot or pika tucked under the stones, no moths lifting from the leaves, no flies hovering over the black pools. Nothing startles and runs. Nothing watches from a ledge. Nothing leaves prints. The place’s life does not withdraw when approached because it cannot withdraw. It holds its ground or is torn away.

So the resident presence is not a creature but a tenure: the tenure of rooted things in a place that does not promise to keep them.

They live by accepting interruption. A trickle appears and vanishes; they drink while it exists. Soil gathers and washes away; they root into what remains. A stone falls and crushes a patch of moss; spores and fragments begin again around the edge. A shrub is broken; its buried stem sends up another branch. A fern bed is flattened; new fronds rise through last year’s wreckage.

Their lives are not calm, but they are slow enough to seem calm beside the wind. The wind is always acting. The water is always testing. The cliffs are always shedding. The plants answer with attachment. They lace the rubble. They stitch the wet seams. They make nets, cushions, mats, skins, pads, braids. They are not conquering the basin. They are negotiating with it continuously.

If the place has a central inhabitant, it is the root mat beneath the ferns and shrubs: a dense, dark, living mesh wrapped around stones, threaded through gravel, glued with fungal strands, packed with old leaves and mineral dust. It is the basin’s first memory. It remembers every fallen frond, every washed-in grain, every brief pool, every season of thaw. It is not one organism, but it behaves like one—holding, drinking, rotting, feeding, binding.

Above it, the visible green is only the surface expression.

The true resident is the beginning of soil.

Not soil as a field has soil, deep and generous, but a thin black insistence in cracks and hollows. It is young, sour, cold, and alive. It smells of tannin, stone, and slow decay. It is made from whatever the basin can keep: fern stems, willow leaves, moss fragments, powdered rock, pollen blown from elsewhere, the bodies of microbes, the dust of broken cliff. Every plant there is both feeding on this soil and making it. Every root is an anchor and a tool. Every dead leaf is construction material.

The basin is inhabited, then, by builders too small and too low to look like builders.

They do not raise walls. They do not sing. They do not migrate through. They make no burrows and no nests. They build by remaining. They build by catching what falls. They build by slowing water, softening impact, splitting stone with root hair, trapping grit under leaf litter, and making darkness fertile.

In time, if the mountain allows it, more may come. Insects may begin to visit on warm still hours. A bird may land briefly among the shrubs. A small animal may one day cross the lip and leave a track in the wet gravel. But for now the basin belongs to plants, lichens, fungi, algae, and the unseen wet multitude.

It is a nursery without animals, a garden without gardener, a factory whose product is land.

What lives there is the first green claim on broken stone.
