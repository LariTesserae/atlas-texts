What lives there is mostly patience given bodies.

The people of the high country are difficult to notice at first. They have learned not to interrupt the plateau. They move low, wrapped in layered felted moss-cloth and root-fiber mantles the color of wet stone, hoarfrost, old wood, soot. From a distance they look like small standing cairns, or like dark shrubs that have decided to cross a plaza. They pause often. They listen more than they look. A stranger might think them fearful, but it is not fear exactly. It is courtesy toward a world that changes when hurried.

They are slight, broad-footed, and compact, with long hands made for knots, scraping, lifting wet slabs, reading seams in stone. Their faces are narrow and weather-pale, often marked with green-gray staining around the eyes, mouth, and nails from the lichens they handle and eat. Their hair, when they have it, is worn braided close to the skull or hidden beneath caps, because loose hair gathers frost and remembers too many winds. Their eyes are large, dark-adapted, and patient. They do not glance quickly. They watch until the thing watched has declared which version of itself it intends to be.

They are warm-blooded only by effort.

Their houses keep them alive, but not as shelters keep animals alive elsewhere. A house here is a second body, a stored climate, a remembered agreement with the ground. Families sleep in low chambers lined with peat brick and living moss. Hearthstones are carried from bed to bed at night and nested under quilts. Children are tucked beside warm cistern walls. Elders sit with their backs to chimney-flues, not for comfort only, but because their joints stiffen if separated too long from old heat.

Their food is quiet food. They eat root-starch from terrace beds, cakes of ground lichen and frost-grain, black fronds boiled until they soften, pale nodules grown in cellar dark, bitter leaves waxed with mineral salt. They brew sour drinks from dew-fed moss. They keep no herds, set no traps, tan no hides. There is no meat, no milk, no egg, no honey. Hunger has no animal face here. It is a thinning of stores, a failed fog, a cistern that reflects stars instead of filling.

They cultivate what will consent to cultivation. Gardens are less planted than persuaded. A gardener knows which wall sweats before dawn, which crack will open in late frost, which moss must be insulted with ash before it thickens, which vine grows best if no one looks at it directly for the first seven days. Seeds are not broadcast; they are placed, promised, weighed with pebbles, named into crevices. Some families inherit single cracks in stone the way others might inherit orchards.

They speak softly, but not because they are meek. Loudness is wasteful. Loudness travels poorly and sometimes returns changed.

Their languages are many and local. A valley may have its own grammar. A bridge may permit only nouns. A street may take offense at questions. So the people speak in layers: voice, gesture, cord, chalk, cairn, water mark, shared silence. Every adult carries knotted strings at the belt, not as decoration but as portable memory: routes, debts, weather, births, deaths, warnings, recipes, names of builders, names of wells, names never spoken aloud in certain districts.

Their stories are recited communally because writing cannot be fully trusted. Marks creep. Measures shorten. A carved date may wake up older than its monument. So a fact becomes durable only when many mouths hold it. In winter rooms, while frost feathers the roof-slabs, they repeat the city into shape: who laid this terrace, who first found water under the east stair, which tower must never be approached in fog, which dead woman’s name keeps the north roof from falling.

Memory is their chief technology.

They do make tools, but tools are humble. Scrapers of copper. Stone wedges. Bone-colored wooden levers polished by generations of palms. Nets for fog. Cords for measuring distance, though no cord is believed absolutely. Bowls cut from dark rock. Needles of hammered iron, guarded carefully because iron becomes moody in the damp and may turn green, black, or soft if left near certain pools.

They distrust clever mechanisms. A gear is a promise too small to survive the place. Springs forget tension. Hinges become opinions. Locks may decide they belong to another door. But a rope can be reknotted. A wall can be watched. A lever can be understood by the hand even after the world has altered the number of inches in a span.

Their cities are less inhabited than tended.

Everyone is a maintainer. Children scrape frost from gutters before they can read knots. Young people learn to carry dew from roof channels without spilling it, to reset paving stones that have drifted in the night, to test whether a staircase still arrives where it did yesterday. Adults serve seasons in cistern houses, fog nets, terrace crews, wall-listening guilds. The old, who no longer walk far, sit in plazas and remember buildings aloud. They are not useless. A roof may depend on one of them.

Their ceremonies are repairs performed with gravity.

At the first heavy condensation of the year, whole neighborhoods wake before light and go barefoot onto the roofs. They guide dew with bone paddles into channels, singing not to the water but to the stone beneath it, reminding it to slope. When a wall lengthens, a measuring procession walks beside it carrying lamps and wet moss, marking where doors used to be. When a road returns from fog in a slightly new place, the city does not condemn it. People bring charcoal, stakes, warm stones, and patient argument.

Birth is treated as an arrival from moisture.

A pregnant person is kept near filled basins and breathing walls. Names are not chosen until the child’s first dawn, when dew is gathered from the roof above the birth room and placed on the infant’s tongue. If the dew beads and rolls away, the child is believed to belong to roads and will one day travel. If it sinks into the lips, the child belongs to cisterns and memory. If it freezes, no one speaks for a while. Such children often become listeners at wells.

They do have children, but childhood is careful. Games are bounded by chalk circles, moss-lines, and rules repeated until they become instinct. Do not chase echoes. Do not step on a shadow that has detached. Do not answer if a familiar voice calls from a pool. Do not count the same tower twice in fog. Do not bring dry things across the damp bridge. Do not laugh at a door that has appeared where there was no door. It may open.

Their dead are not buried deeply. The ground is too old and too negotiable. Instead, bodies are wrapped in root cloth, packed with moss, salt, and powdered lichen, and placed into low stone houses outside the settled warmth. Families visit to scrape frost from the names. In some districts the dead gradually become part of the wall: not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. Their bones mineralize. Their names help hold lintels. Their remembered gestures are carved into roof beams. An ancestor is someone who may keep rain out if properly greeted.

But the people are not the only inhabitants.

The mosses live there with more authority than anyone. Some are soft as breath, others tough as old felt, others glassy and black, closing when touched like sleeping eyelids. There are mosses that drink smoke, mosses that fatten on silence, mosses that remember footprints and raise them in green relief weeks after the walker has gone. A few are kept in bowls indoors and consulted before journeys. If they darken, the road is long. If they silver, the fog is near. If they flower, someone has returned who has not yet arrived.

The lichens are older than the cities and are treated with formal respect. They grow in rings, maps, scripts, blazons, false constellations. Some colonize stone so slowly that a handspan is a dynasty. Others spread overnight across new masonry, claiming it for weather. People cut them only with apology. Certain red lichens are used as dye for mourning cords. Blue-white lichens mark places where distance is thin. Orange ones around pools are never eaten; they are said to feed on reflected skies.

The dwarf trees are stubborn, almost domestic, but never tame. They grow huddled against walls and in ravines, their trunks twisted like arthritic hands. Their wood is pale, dense, and warm to the touch, useful for bowls, cradles, levers, and doors that must not warp when reality does. They shed leaves reluctantly. A tree may hold the same black leaves for nine years, then drop them all in one windless hour. Children are told that if a tree grows straight, it is listening to something underground.

There are vines in the abandoned quarters that behave nearly like citizens. They block unsafe streets. They lace over roofs before collapse. They lower roots into cracked cisterns and bring up mineral water in slow pulses. Some districts feed them deliberately, threading them through broken aqueducts and around leaning walls. A vine that has held up a house for three generations is given a household name.

The fogs live there too.

Not as animals, not as spirits exactly, but as moving conditions with habits, hungers, and routes. The west fog is broad and cold and reliable. It fills cistern mouths and erases old mistakes. The narrow dawn fog creeps along gutters, wakes seeds, and leaves frost writing on copper. The black fog found in certain basins is avoided; it makes people remember valleys they have never entered. There are fog-watchers whose duty is to climb low towers and read the advancing whiteness the way sailors elsewhere might read sails.

Water lives there in separated minds. Pool water is not the same as cistern water, nor roof-dew as ravine-drip. Each must be handled according to its temperament. Water from a repeated valley is never mixed with water from the city center. Water that has climbed uphill is allowed to rest before drinking. Basin water reflecting stars at noon is used only for washing tools, never faces.

And beneath all of it, the stone lives most slowly.

The inhabitants know this. They do not say the stone is alive in the way a tree is alive, or a person, or moss. They mean that it changes by preference. It accepts certain walls and rejects others. It opens cracks where seeds have been promised. It lengthens roads, swallows thresholds, repeats valleys, lifts frost in the shape of future streets. It is not malicious. It is old beyond malice. It is tired of being only ground.

So the people have become specialists in coexistence with unfinished laws. They do not believe in mastery. They believe in maintenance, repetition, correction, apology, and warmth carried carefully through cold air.

They are a civilization of custodians.

Their heroes are not conquerors but those who kept a cistern sweet through a dry generation, who remembered a builder’s name when the roof began to sag, who crossed a road that had lost its destination and returned with the same number of companions, who taught a district how to grow food in a wall that had forgotten it was stone.

They live sparsely, but they are not lonely. Their companionship is distributed among people, houses, moss, water, names, rules, and weather. A person may have kin in another city three weeks away on clear days and one courtyard away in fog. A grandmother may speak every morning to a basin that reflects her childhood street. A mason may trust a certain lichen more than his own measuring cord. A child may grow up knowing that silence is not absence, only another kind of crowdedness.

What lives there, then, is not wildlife.

It is low human warmth in a country without animals.  
It is gardens that breed by mist and fracture.  
It is cities that survive by being repaired into existence every day.  
It is fog with memory, stone with habits, water with separate loyalties.  
It is a people who have endured so long among worn-down mountains that they too have become less like settlers than like a local condition: cautious, damp, quiet, persistent, and almost impossible to remove.
