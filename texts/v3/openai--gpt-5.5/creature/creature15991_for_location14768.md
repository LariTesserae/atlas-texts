What lives there is not gone. It is only difficult to catch in the same state as yourself.

The inhabitants are a small, cold-adapted people, slight but dense in the way mountain shrubs are dense: compact, resilient, close to the ground. They move with a careful economy, never placing weight on stone without first knowing how it has shifted since yesterday. Their feet are broad and often bare despite the cold, or wrapped in soft hide and moss-felt that drinks water rather than rejecting it. They have learned to feel new cracks through their soles, to tell a harmless settling from the beginning of a spring.

Their skin tends toward gray, brown, olive, and the pale tones of wet clay. Not because they are made of stone, exactly, but because everything here stains: peat smoke, mineral water, crushed lichen, copper salts, moss. Their hair is worn short or bound close beneath hoods of woven reed, wool, or bark fiber, since loose hair never dries in this place. Older ones often have greenish marks at their cuffs, throats, and temples from copper clasps and damp ornaments. Children are wrapped so thoroughly they resemble bundles of rushes with bright eyes.

They are quiet, but not silent. Their speech is low and full of water words. They have many names for dripping: the first drip from a roof after frost, the deep drip from a cistern lip, the nervous dripping of a new crack, the hollow knocking that means a wall has begun to loosen. They do not raise their voices near the black pool. They do not sing indoors unless the house has already stopped singing.

Their lives are organized around attention.

They are not farmers in the ordinary sense, though they grow food wherever stone permits it: cabbages black-green and tight-leaved, bitter herbs, watercress, root plants in raised beds lined with gravel, pale fungi in damp storerooms, vines trained along warmed hearth walls. They keep few animals. Perhaps small woolly goats are pastured lower down when the weather allows, and perhaps there are hutch-kept hares or water birds taken from the marsh ledges, but the town itself belongs too much to water for hooves and noise. Its true livestock are cisterns, channels, moss beds, seeded cracks, and carefully tended wetness.

They eat stews, dark leaves, boiled roots, sour cakes baked on hot stones, smoked fish brought from lower streams, and mineral-salt broths that taste faintly of the mountain. They drink constantly, but never casually. Water is drawn according to source and mood. Basin water is not drunk. New seam water is tasted only by elders. Roof water is for washing, unless it falls from a roof where someone has died. Water that has passed through moss is good for fevers. Water that knocks in pipes is not taken until it has quieted.

Their builders are also listeners. A mason here does not begin by measuring a wall; they begin by kneeling, pressing an ear to rock, and waiting. They carry wooden rods, copper bowls, clay plugs, lengths of reed, plumb lines weighted with smooth black stones. They build lightly when the mountain is restless and heavily only where the stone has slept for years. They know how to make a wall that can be taken apart without offense. They know how to abandon a room without calling it failure.

No one here believes a house is finished.

A house is a temporary agreement between people, stone, moss, timber, and water. It may hold for three generations. It may hold for three rains. If water enters, one does not curse it. One asks what it wants to become: a gutter, a basin, a shrine, a warning, a reason to move the sleeping mats elsewhere. The inhabitants do not repair as other people repair. They redirect. They accommodate. They translate pressure into channels.

They have offices, though not grand ones.

There are keepers of overflow, who inspect every spillway before thaw.

There are seam-markers, mostly old women and men with patient hands, who tie green boughs over new openings in the rock and sit beside them through the first three days.

There are basin-watchers, who never touch the black pool but read its surface for tremors, color, skinning ice, and the direction of falling drops.

There are child-guides, because children hear high thin water sounds adults miss.

There are empty-house listeners, who enter abandoned rooms at dawn, sit cross-legged in the wet, and decide whether the singing has changed enough for human sleep to return.

Their priests, if that is the word, do not command the weather. They maintain manners between the town and the unfinished mountain. They leave offerings that rot cleanly: alder twigs, white flowers, bowls of steamed grain, braids of moss, little carved teeth of stone placed near spillways. They recite the old prohibitions not as laws imposed from above, but as remembered accidents. Every rule was once a death, a flood, a cracked hearth, a child lost beneath a floor that had become water overnight.

The central black pool is their oldest citizen.

They do not call it a god, at least not in the way lowland people might. They call it something closer to Mouth, Ear, Unfinished Depth, or First Basin. It is not worshiped with spectacle. It is given space. No one casts anything into it. No one washes there. No one steps over its stone lip. Infants are shown their reflection in it from a distance. The dying are carried near enough to hear the pulse below it. When the deep boom comes from under the town, everyone pauses—not in terror, but in recognition, as if someone immensely slow has cleared its throat.

The town’s apparent emptiness is part of how they live.

They are often indoors but not visible: behind reed screens, below terraces, in mist rooms, in roofed passages, in the listening chambers built between walls. They travel at times when vapor thickens, because wet air carries sound differently and makes the stone easier to read. At midday, when the light is clearest, the place may seem deserted. At dawn, dusk, or during a fine cold rain, figures appear everywhere: one crossing a bridge with two clay jars, one crouched by a channel, one replacing moss between stones, three children floating leaf-boats in an overflow trench, an elder standing motionless with one palm on a wall.

They do not vanish by magic. They vanish by habit, architecture, and weather.

Still, they are not quite like ordinary humans anymore. Generations in the basin have altered their senses. They can sleep through a hundred kinds of dripping but wake instantly when one stops. They dislike dry rooms. A silence without water makes them uneasy, even ill. Lowland fire frightens them if it burns too cleanly. They prefer smoke that clings and peat that smolders. They read mineral stains the way others read writing. They know which moss can be stepped on, which moss marks unsafe stone, and which silvering growth means a threshold must be scraped before sickness enters.

Their memory is civic rather than genealogical. They remember where channels used to run, where a spring was expected but never came, where a wall was built too proudly and split in its first winter. Families are known by waterworks as much as by blood: the people of the north cistern, the line of the lower comb, the ones who keep the alder troughs, the descendants of those who moved when the singing began.

They build for descendants they may never meet and for streams that do not yet exist.

That is their strangest trait: faith in future water. They peg out channels toward dry stone. They raise aqueduct supports beneath cliffs that only glisten. They carve overflow notches in walls that have never flooded. Outsiders would call this superstition. The inhabitants call it courtesy. Water is coming. If there is no path ready, it will make one badly.

But people are not the only residents.

The moss lives there with something like authority. It is not decoration; it is witness, cushion, seal, calendar. Lichen takes possession of walls before history can. Ferns occupy rooms no one has condemned but no one has reclaimed. The translucent insects in the cisterns are tolerated as signs of good stillness. Pale moths under eaves are left undisturbed. The dwarf willows mark wind. The alders mark water fit for roots. Every living thing is small because the place itself is still deciding how large a life it can bear.

And beneath all of them lives the young mountain.

Not as a monster, not as a conscious giant, but as the largest inhabitant: shifting, cooling, swelling, exhaling water through its seams. The town is built on its skin, and the people know this. Their civilization is not founded against nature but within an ongoing negotiation with something that has not yet become landscape.

So whoever lives there is a people of intervals: between spring and river, between rock and soil, between settlement and ruin, between presence and absence. They are patient, damp, soft-spoken, ingenious, and slightly mineral in spirit. They do not conquer the mountain. They apprentice themselves to it.

If you waited in the plaza long enough, one of them would eventually appear beside you without hurry, carrying a covered bowl or a bundle of green boughs. They would not ask why you had come. They would listen first—to your breathing, to the pool, to the nearest channel. Then, if the water allowed speech, they might tell you where to stand so the stone beneath your feet would not change its mind.
