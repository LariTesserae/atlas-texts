What lives there does not present itself as wildlife at first.

It presents itself as a correction in the landscape: a rail kept clear of moss for ten paces and then allowed to vanish; a valve with every bolt furred green except the one that must still turn; a pool whose surface dimples in regular sequence though nothing touches it; a fresh landslide already stitched with roots in exactly the places where the shale would otherwise keep falling.

The plateau’s inhabitants are maintenance that learned to grow.

They are not machines, though some of them contain metal. They are not plants, though most of them root when still. They are not animals in any ordinary sense, though there are bodies among them that walk, feed, avoid notice, and leave tunnels through reed beds. They are a quiet ecology of inherited function: the old distributed industry translated into leaf, tendon, condensation, electrical sensitivity, and patient habit.

The most visible part of them is mistaken for vegetation.

The cable-vines are theirs. So are the silver mosses that grow in rectangular patches above warm buried conduits. So are the white enamel flowers around pressure valves, each with its dark, precise center. Those flowers are not blossoms in the usual way. They are eyes, gauges, listening cups, reproductive organs, and little weather stations. They open under hard brightness because brightness gives them contrast. They close in mist not to sleep, but to read moisture through their outer skins.

No insect visits them because they do not need insects. Their pollen is a dry black dust too heavy for wind and too fine for rain. It moves when the pylons hum. On the seventh or ninth day, when the hairs of stems lift and beads of water crawl uphill along cable sheaths, the flowers turn by a few degrees and exchange themselves across the plateau in a field too subtle to hear. Afterward, seeds appear in their cups: dark, polished, washer-shaped things with a pale seam around the edge. They lodge in bolt-holes, cracked insulators, pumphouse gutters, fresh shale, abandoned hinges. If they find warmth, mineral seepage, and silence, they begin.

Their young are the transparent larvae hanging under the pool surfaces.

They are almost nothing at first: threads of gel, small knots of nerve-colored tissue, motes of mineral appetite. They feed on dissolved metals, algae, transformer wax washed down in rain, the faint electrical leakage from submerged housings. For months or years they remain suspended, learning the rhythm of pumps beneath them. Their bodies pulse when relays click underground. If the pool dries, they harden into pale capsules and wait in the mud until vapor returns.

Later they crawl out at night or during the white noon glare, when everything is too bright to examine closely.

The walking forms are rare, or rarely seen whole. A person crossing the plateau might glimpse one only as a rearrangement of wet leaves. A flattened crown of shrubs moves against the wind. A length of old cable lifts, becomes a spine, and lowers again. Something pale passes behind a pumphouse window already bright with condensation. By the time one looks properly, there is only fern, rail, rock, and a crushed place in the moss that may have been made by anything.

They are built low and narrow, suited to gullies, service passages, and the undersides of catwalks. Some go on four limbs, some on six. The older ones seem to have forgotten the distinction between leg and root. Their feet are broad, soft, and many-toed, padded with living moss that drinks the sound out of each step. Where claws should be, they have curved pieces of dark ceramic or basalt-sharp keratin, useful for gripping wet concrete and young stone.

Their hides resemble the plateau itself: chalk-pale plates, shale-gray folds, bruised violet lichen, green-black seams where moisture gathers. Along their backs grow low mats of fern and dwarf grass. Very old individuals carry entire wind-flattened shrubs in the ridges of their shoulders, the roots sunk into their flesh as if into a cliff face. Their tendons are translucent. In bright weather one can see fluid moving through them in thin vertical threads, sometimes downward, sometimes not.

Their faces are difficult to remember.

Seen from the side, a head may look like a smooth stone knob, a broken insulator, the capped end of a pipe. When it turns, a dark point appears: glossy, circular, mechanically exact. Around it, small white petals or petal-like lids adjust to the light. Some have two such points. Some have many, arranged asymmetrically among moss and pale skin. Their mouths are quiet valves. They do not tear or chew loudly. They rasp minerals from stone, sip condensation from cable sheaths, take clean bites from swollen vines, and absorb oils from old rubber with slow visible satisfaction.

They do not call.

Sound is dangerous to them, or offensive, or simply outside the grammar of their lives. Their communication is conducted through changes of pressure, moisture, electrical inclination, and timing. A colony can pass alarm through a ravine by making fern-fronds turn their undersides outward. It can ask for warmth by opening silver moss above a conduit. It can direct a walking body toward a blocked grate by sending perfectly circular ripples across a pumphouse pool.

Their names, if they have names, are intervals: between drops, between ventings, between the noon unfolding of ferns and the evening contraction of cables.

They are intelligent, but not quickly.

Their thought resembles erosion before erosion has become confident. They test routes, abandon them, return after mist, choose again. They do not build monuments. They refine circumstances. A hatch that must remain accessible will be kept free of root mats by generations of tiny grazing mouths. A cliff door that no longer matters will be sealed under moss so completely that it becomes part of the slope. A drainage trench may be deepened into a streambed. An intake grate may be laced with living filters that remove silt better than any original screen.

They have inherited old purposes without inheriting old explanations.

One kind tends the vents. These are tall, narrow forms with reedlike limbs and backs covered in pale, flattened leaves. They gather on ridges before vapor release, still as marker posts. When the vents open, they stand in the spilling white breath and comb it with branching forelimbs, directing it downhill into ravines, orchards of lichen, and the mouths of thirsty service tunnels.

Another kind keeps to the rails. These are lower, heavier, dark-backed, with magnetite grains in their bones. They move along half-buried tracks, clearing root pressure from certain joins while encouraging it at others. They seem to know which stretches must remain conductive and which may become trellis. Their passage leaves two polished lines in the moss.

The smallest are everywhere: beetle-sized hinge-cleaners, pale mites that live under warning signs, filamentous things that taste cracks in concrete and decide whether to widen or seal them. A whole pumphouse may be occupied by millions of them, though it appears empty except for sedge on the roof and water on the glass.

The largest are the source of the rumors.

They live mostly below the young valleys, where the vegetation is stacked so thickly that daylight arrives green and delayed. They are called animals only because they move and leave signs. Their bodies are long and partially hollow, ribbed with old pipe, moss-grown along the flanks, rooted whenever they sleep. A large one may take a week to cross a service road. It does so at night, not from fear, but because night gives moisture time to gather under its weight.

When such a being feeds, it does not browse randomly. It removes a vine from a compressor wall because the vine has begun to choke a necessary seam. It bites through a shrub because the shrub is shading a solar gauge or, conversely, because the gauge has finally become irrelevant and should be shaded. It drinks from pools in circular motions, setting ripples that wake larvae beneath the surface.

A human observer would be tempted to call them caretakers.

That is almost right, but too sentimental. They do not preserve the plateau as it was. They do not restore the industry. They do not worship the machinery or mourn the workers. They use what still works, digest what does not, and translate both into further life. If a machine has purpose left, they keep it breathing. If a machine has only material, they feed it into moss, root, shell, and flower. They are not reclaiming the built world. They are continuing it by other means.

They are gentle only because gentleness is efficient here.

A loud intruder would not be attacked in any dramatic way. The plateau does not favor drama. Instead, paths would become confusing. Catwalks would bead with sudden slickness. Ferns would lean across sightlines. A relay might click once behind the intruder and never again. Moss would take the dropped tool. A bootprint would fill with pale water. The place would absorb the disturbance until it could no longer travel.

But a quiet visitor might be tolerated.

If one stood still beside a pumphouse pool long enough for breath to slow and clothing to stop whispering against wet leaves, the inhabitants might resume around them. A white flower would open a little wider. A vine would tighten on a cable. A rail-thing, no taller than a dog but much older in its movements, might step from the ferns, place one soft foot on exposed steel, and listen through its bones.

It would smell of crushed fern, cold stone, ozone, and faint warm rubber.

It would look newly made and anciently patient.

And if it turned its dark, exact eye toward the visitor, there would be no greeting in it, no threat, no curiosity as animals have curiosity. Only measurement. A question without urgency:

Can this, too, be used?

That is what lives on the plateau: a society of moss, valve, root, larva, cable, flower, and soft-footed bodies; an industrial afterlife gone cool and green; a maintenance crew whose tools are weather and growth. It lives quietly because the plateau permits no other kind of life. It lives brightly because nothing there has learned to hide in shadow. It lives slowly because the land is young, and something must teach it how to become old.
