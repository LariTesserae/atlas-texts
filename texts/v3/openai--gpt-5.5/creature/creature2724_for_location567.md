What lives there is difficult to separate from the place.

Not because the inhabitants are hidden—though many are—but because life on the plateau has learned not to stand apart. Nothing here keeps a clean outline for long. Fur greens over. Shells gather films. Antlers sprout moss. Nests become shrubs. Paths become roots. Bodies borrow from the forest until each creature seems less like an animal moving through vegetation than a brief decision made by the vegetation itself.

The oldest inhabitant is not an animal at all.

It lies under the central flats in layers: root, fungus, drowned wood, mineral seep, peat, thread, nerve-like filament, and the pale hair of living roots descending from trees whose first trunks are long gone. It is not one organism in the simple sense, but neither is it merely a forest. It is a continuity. A wet intelligence of exchange and pressure. It remembers in chemistry, in altered growth, in the refusal of roots to enter certain stones, in groves that bloom when thunder turns under the plateau. It does not think quickly. It may take a season to notice a fallen tree, a decade to answer drought, a century to close a wound in its own pattern. But it notices.

Animals that live above it are measured by this deep green mind and, in turn, have shaped it.

There are the moss-backed grazers: long-bodied, low-shouldered creatures with soft muzzles and wide, splayed feet that spread their weight across trembling ground. Their hides are gray-brown when young, but age makes them green. Algae grows in the hollows of their shoulders. Liverwort creeps along their spines. Small white flowers sometimes root in the matted hair behind their ears. Their horns curve forward and outward, not sharp, but broad and flattened, useful for parting vine-curtains, lifting wet branches, and scraping mineral films from stone.

They move in loose, patient bands. Not herds exactly; they do not crowd together unless the peat is unstable or a flowering migration calls them. They know the safe routes across bog-mats, though the routes vanish behind them. Calves learn by pressing their muzzles into the footprints of elders and tasting the water that gathers there. When one dies, the others do not flee the carcass. They stand around it for hours, chewing and breathing steam, while insects arrive, then fungi, then seedlings. Later the band will return to feed from the young growth rooted in the body of their dead.

In the canopy live the tappers.

They are seldom seen whole. A hand first: long-fingered, dark, damp, gripping a root-cable. Then a shoulder passing behind leaves. Then the shine of an eye, large and black, reflecting no light of its own. Their bodies are narrow and flexible, covered in dense water-shedding down that ranges from rust to deep green-gray. They have prehensile feet, soft throat-pouches, and thin crests of sensitive skin along their forearms with which they feel vibration through wood.

They travel the upper world without touching ground. Generations are born in hanging peat nests built in the forks of ancient trees. Their infants cling silently to the fur of adults for months, learning the forest first as rhythm: drip, creak, wingbeat, beetle-click, distant branch-fall, the hollow tone of root against root.

The tappers speak by percussion. Not language as humans would measure it, but not mere signal either. They drum on hollow stems, on swollen trunks, on fungus shelves dried just enough to resonate. A warning may pass across miles without a cry being raised. A courtship pattern can last three nights, answered from farther and farther away through fog. When fruiting begins in one district, the tappers announce it in uneven wooden rain. Other creatures learn these patterns. Grazers alter their paths. Moths rise early. Crabs leave the pools and climb the buttress roots to feed on fallen pulp.

The tappers do not build permanent structures. Permanence belongs to plants. But they encourage certain hollows, clear certain branch-forks, carry seeds in cheek-pouches, and chew openings into deadwood where rain can gather. A ring of trees may owe its beginning to generations of them sleeping in a circle around a safe interior, dropping seeds, dung, and scraps until the forest grew around their habit. If they have history, it is not carved. It is distributed among useful groves.

Lower down, in the padded dimness, live the white-faced watchers.

They are birds, though at rest they resemble rotten knots or damp fungus shelves more than anything feathered. Their heads are round, their eyes forward-facing, their beaks almost hidden in soft facial disks that collect sound. Over years of stillness, epiphytes take hold in the outer layers of their plumage. A very old watcher may carry moss on its back, tiny beetles in its wing-coverts, even a small fern rooted near the base of the neck. They permit this. The growth breaks their outline. It also feeds insects, and insects draw prey.

They hunt by waiting until waiting itself seems to have become a substance. A frog moving once too often. A beetle tapping under bark. A young tapper crossing a branch in fog. Then the bird drops without sound, opens talons the color of old ivory, and is gone again into leaves.

Yet they are not merely predators. Their abandoned roosts become rich gardens. Their pellets seed strange assemblages of bone-loving fungi. Some trees grow better beneath them, and there are groves where the watchers gather in numbers during certain hidden cycles, silent as old councils, while below them pale mushrooms push up in rings.

In the root-pools live the glass crabs.

They are descendants of something that once knew salt water, though no sea touches the plateau now. Their shells are translucent, clouded with mineral stains, so that their inner organs show as faint shadows when they move in clear seepage. They feed on fallen fruit, drowned insects, fungal lips, soft wood, and one another when hunger requires it. Their claws are delicate, almost surgical. They clean rot from the toes of grazers that stand patiently in pools. They harvest blue-green films from submerged roots and cultivate them in little scraped basins.

Some pools are entirely theirs: terraced, maintained, guarded. Not architecture, exactly, but arrangement. They push pebbles aside, wedge leaves to slow currents, prune fungal mats, and keep openings in the muck through which mineral water rises. When floods come, they climb trees in pale clusters and wait out the drowning, clinging to bark like beads of old milk.

The amphibians are everywhere.

Some are no larger than a fingernail and live inside the water-cups of single leaves, passing their entire lives in a suspended pond that sways above the ground. Some are broad and leaf-backed, indistinguishable from fallen foliage until their eyes open. Some carry eggs in moist folds along their sides. Some sing only when mist is thick enough to bead on their throats, their calls so soft and fluted that travelers mistake them for water moving through reeds.

The oldest frogs grow heavy and still. Moss adheres to their backs. Their skin patterns change slowly to match the bark they favor. They may occupy the same trunk for years, shifting only with the wet season, eating gnats, dreaming whatever amphibians dream in a world without sharp weather.

There are also the slow hunters of the moss: velvet worms, pale centipedes, root-eels in black seepage, blind salamanders with feathery external gills, and soft-bodied things without names that taste the world with their entire skins. They live by gradients: warmth, salt, pressure, decay. To them, the plateau is not seen but read. A footprint is a brief weather. A dead moth is a bright event. A buried fruit is a festival lasting years.

The insects form the plateau’s smallest nations.

Leaf-polishers keep the surfaces of certain giant plants clean, moving in orderly fronts, scraping away films but leaving the living skin unharmed. In exchange, the leaves secrete minute beads of sugar along their veins.

Pitcher-midges are born in carnivorous flowers but do not die there. Their larvae feed on what the plant has drowned. When they mature, they climb the slick inner wall along grooves only they can grip, emerge at dusk, and carry pollen to other pitchers whose throats smell precisely of rot, nectar, and warm rain.

Transparent moths rest on wet stone and vanish. Beetles pretend to be buds until real buds grow around them. Termites cultivate red fungi inside logs that are still half alive. Ants build floating rafts during floods, not from their own bodies but from trimmed moss and woven rootlets, ferrying eggs through temporary blackwater channels.

And then there are the almost-people.

They are not human, and nothing in them suggests lost humanity. They are plateau-born: amphibious, arboreal, deliberate. From a distance one might mistake them for hunched children covered in dark reed-cloaks, or for old stumps that have decided to walk.

Their bodies are compact, with long arms, short bowed legs, and broad hands webbed to the first knuckle. Their skin is smooth in youth, then becomes textured with age: mottled green, umber, gray, black. Lichens grow on the oldest among them, especially across the shoulders and scalp. They do not remove these growths unless they itch or interfere with movement. To be lightly overgrown is a sign not of neglect but of belonging.

Their faces are blunt and quiet. Nostrils high. Mouths wide but seldom open. Eyes large, lidless-looking, dark amber or green-black, adapted to dimness and near distances. They see motion well, color poorly, moisture exquisitely. Their hearing is less important than their sensitivity to vibration. They can place a palm against a trunk and know whether rain is falling high above, whether tappers have passed recently, whether beetle larvae are hollowing the inner wood.

They have no houses in the ordinary sense. They occupy intervals: dry hollows inside living trunks, root-chambers above seepage, leaf-roofed rings where the ground is firm enough to sleep. They improve these places, but so gently that after a generation their work cannot be distinguished from growth. They bend young stems into arcs and wait decades for them to thicken. They wedge stones where water must be slowed, not stopped. They plant poles in marshes knowing the poles will root, flower, and become something other than tools.

The stone-faced mounds on the drier rises may be theirs. Or may have been. They do not speak of origin as a line leading backward. Their memory is local. A mound is not “built by ancestors”; it is “where warmth stays after rain,” or “where the small white moths return,” or “where the buried ones feed the sour flowers.” They bury their dead shallowly, wrapped in moss and seeded with plants chosen for temperament: soft-rooted species for children, mineral-loving orchids for elders, sedges for those who traveled much.

Their speech is partly vocal, partly tactile, partly botanical.

Among themselves they use low throat tones, clicks made against the palate, and breath pushed through reed tubes. But important meanings are conveyed by touch: a hand pressed to the wrist, two fingers laid on the throat, palms placed together with moss between them. They also leave messages in growing things. A folded fern weighed down with a pebble. A vine redirected across a path. A cluster of pale fungi encouraged on one side of a trunk. Such signs are not commands but suggestions: unstable ground, bitter water, sleeping nest, flowering soon, do not disturb, someone died here, wait.

They do not farm as outsiders would understand it. They tend tendencies. They carry spores in damp pouches. They move crabs to pools where rot has become too thick. They scrape choking films from young trees. They break holes in fallen trunks so rain can enter and soften them for seedlings. They transplant useful moss to sleeping hollows. They know which carnivorous pools can be crossed after feeding and which remember heat for too long.

Their tools are few because tools do not last. Bone softens. Wood roots. Fiber sprouts. Stone disappears under film. They use knives made from the pale slabs of western ridges, kept wrapped in oily leaves. They use hooks grown from trained thorns. They use woven mats that must be kept either constantly damp or constantly in use, otherwise they become beds for seedlings. Possession is temporary. A thing not touched returns to the plateau.

They count poorly but remember cycles with great precision. Not numbers of years, but sequences: after the pepper flowers fail twice; after the black pools sweeten; when the tappers drum downward instead of across; when the watcher-birds gather without hunting; when thunder moves under the stone. Their lives are long, or seem long because they do not divide time sharply. A child may spend its first decade within a single grove. An elder may take thirty seasons to complete a journey from the central peat to the western ridges and back.

They fear dryness more than death.

A dry wind is an omen of exposure. A cracked tongue is a sickness. Fire is treated with solemn disgust, not panic. If lightning hollows a trunk and flame survives inside, they gather around it at a distance, feeding the surrounding wetness, packing moss, singing low until steam replaces smoke. Later they visit the blackened pocket and plant orchids there. In this way even injury is given a tenant.

Their rites are quiet.

At birth, a newborn is washed not in open water but in water collected from leaves, each drop having touched the canopy before touching earth. At maturity, one spends a night alone in a sound-dead hollow, listening to the body from within. At death, the body is opened only enough to invite roots. The dead are not sent away. They are assigned downward.

They are not numerous. The plateau could swallow a thousand peoples and still seem empty. Their bands are small, dispersed, and often unknown to one another except by signs left in growth. Sometimes two groups meet during a flowering that covers hundreds of miles in pale bloom. They exchange spores, songs, stones, children, memories of safe crossings. Then the flowers fall, pools sweeten, insects rise, and the bands separate into mist.

They did not make the plateau.

They are one of its ways of continuing.

If they built the stair descending into the bog, they no longer say so. More likely, they found the pale slabs and kept them clear for reasons that became ritual after the reason drowned. They descend only eleven steps. The twelfth belongs to black water. Offerings are placed there: fruit, feathers, polished beetle shells, the finger-bones of elders, small packets of seed. The water accepts them without ripple. Something below digests them slowly.

To outsiders, these almost-people would seem secretive. They are not. They simply have no habit of announcing themselves into distance. Sound is costly here, and attention has weight. They reveal themselves by allowing proximity. A face visible behind hanging roots. A hand withdrawing from a moss mat. A low click answered from the reeds. If approached clumsily, they vanish into the layered green, not by speed but by becoming indistinct: crouched beside trunks, submerged to the eyes in pools, wrapped in leaf-shadow, still enough for insects to resume walking over them.

Their wisdom is not abstract. It is intimate, wet, practical, and slow.

They know that a certain vine closes over warmth after repeated touch, so they brush it with heated stones before crossing beneath it. They know that some groves dislike iron-rich blood and will yellow where a wounded animal rests. They know which fungi glow before rain and which glow after death. They know that the central old trees should not be cut, not because of a law, but because their roots hold memories that keep the bogs from wandering.

They do not worship the plateau as a god. Worship implies distance. They are inside it. They bargain with it, feed it, avoid offending its hungers, and accept its claims. Their prayers, if prayers they are, sound like maintenance: clearing a blocked seep, pressing a fallen root back into mud, carrying a stranded crab to water, standing very still while a rare moth dries its wings on their shoulder.

Above them, the canopy animals drum and sing in fog.

Below them, blind lives consume the past.

Around them, plants remember pressure, stones weep minerals, pools wait with patient mouths, and the old green continuity beneath the peat receives everything eventually.

What lives on the plateau is not a kingdom of beasts, nor a hidden civilization, nor a monster wearing a forest as skin. It is a society without borders between body and dwelling. A damp republic of roots, grazers, crabs, moths, frogs, watchers, tappers, almost-people, fungi, and the immense slow mind of accumulation underneath them all.

Each inhabitant is temporary.

The wetness remains.
