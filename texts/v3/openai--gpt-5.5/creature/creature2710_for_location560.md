What lives there is not a people, though the place has the organization of a settlement.

It is a dry life, a life without sap, blood, slime, or breath as surface creatures know it. Everything in the chamber has learned to do without wetness. Its inhabitants are powdered, jointed, plated, shelled, vented. They do not drink. They sift. They do not graze. They abrade, absorb, resonate, crystallize. Their bodies are arrangements of mineral hunger and machine patience.

The smallest are almost not animals. They live inside the salt crusts, between the polygonal plates, in spaces too thin for a fingernail. They are pale ticks of motion, jointed like fragments of broken instruments. Their shells are translucent, with cloudy organs inside like inclusions in flawed quartz. They move only when the vibration is right. A footstep, a fan-blade wobble, the low pulse of the central cylinder: each sound opens a different pathway in their narrow world. They do not see. They sense pressure through their legs and chemistry through the fine combs around their mouths.

They eat edges.

Not food in any soft sense, but sharpness: newly split salt, oxidized metal bloom, the sugary crumble of alkali crystals, the powder that forms where black glass spines rub against one another. They polish the undersides of crust plates until they are dangerously thin, then seal the weakness with their own brittle secretions. Their colonies are maps of repairs no one ordered.

Above them move the glass-backed processions.

These are insect-like, but not quite insects. Their bodies are too regular, too much like beads threaded on wire. They travel in files over machines, up walls, along the rails that end in stone. Their carapaces are dark and lustrous, each one reflecting amber lamp-light as a tiny moving spark. They climb as if gravity is a question long ago settled in their favor.

They tend the machinery without understanding it as machinery. They clean the contacts of gauges. They pack dust around cracked housings in smooth collars. They gnaw rust only to a certain depth and then stop. They enter furnaces through hairline seams and emerge hours later dusted red and warm, carrying grains of something magnetic in the hollows beneath their thoraxes. When one dies, the others dismantle it into its useful textures: shell to insulation, leg-hooks to anchor points, inner chalk to feed the salt-mites below.

They are not busy. Busy implies urgency. They are exact.

From the ceiling hang the sifter-throats.

They are fat, soft creatures, although soft here means only less hard than stone. Their bodies are pear-colored in the weak light, blind and folded, with hook-feet sunk into mineral seams overhead. They cluster where heat rises: around lamp cages, vents, pipe elbows, transformer housings. Their throats tremble constantly, each at its appointed pitch. They take in the suspended mineral dust and sort it inside themselves by charge, taste, and grain.

What they release is not waste but weather made local: pale cones beneath them, each cone of a different fineness. Some piles are white as bone meal. Some are yellow-gray. Some are faintly violet and sting the skin. Other creatures visit these falls to feed, dust-bathe, lay eggs, sharpen mouthparts, or repair damaged shells. The sifter-throats are the chamber’s clouds, except they never move and never rain.

If disturbed, they do not flee. They tighten. Their throats stop for one impossible second, and the silence around them becomes a wound in the roar. Then they begin again, exactly where they left off.

In the trenches live the plated crawlers.

They are the largest moving things in the chamber, though none is larger than a dog, and most are lower to the ground than a sleeping child. Long-backed, blunt-headed, armored in overlapping flakes of mineralized skin, they pass through the narrow cuts in the floor with the sound of a file drawn across bone. Their eyes are sealed beneath plates. Their mouths are underneath them: broad, rasping, lined with black ceramic teeth.

They scrape the hardened blooms from ridge bases and furnace feet. They widen some trenches and fill others. Their routes look aimless until one watches long enough to see that they follow temperature gradients, vibration lines, invisible deposits of charge. They are slow but never hesitant. When two meet in a passage too narrow for both, they do not fight. They press head to head, hum faintly through their armor, and one reverses after the proper interval.

Their young grow inside red-brown nodules that look like mineral tumors. For years, or what passes for years there, the nodule sits sealed and silent. Then a seam opens. A small plated thing unfolds, already dusted, already armored, already knowing the trench-pattern beneath its feet.

Near the warm pipes surface the star-mouthed burrowers.

Most of their lives are spent under the crust, where they move through powdered mineral the way fish might move through silt, if there were fish here, and silt, and water. Their bodies are pale, muscular, ringed with stiff bristles that push dust backward as they tunnel. They come up only where heat gathers. First the crust trembles. Then a round plug lifts. Then the mouth opens.

The mouth is all one sees: a star of dry red tissue, petal-like and toothless, widening to expose dark folds that pulse around hot air. They feed on warmth, perhaps, or on the electrical taste of buried pipes. They may be breathing, but if so it is not for air. After a time they close, seal themselves with a disk of mineral paste, and sink away. The plugs they leave are nearly invisible except to the salt-mites, who harvest their edges with great care.

Fixed to the walls are the bellow-shells.

They are sessile creatures, fist-sized and innumerable. At first they seem like deposits: fluted shells, ceramic knots, old valves mineralized into the rock. Then they open. Each has a narrow slit or round dark aperture rimmed with hard lips. They flex in slow sequence, drawing in vibration and releasing a softened countertone. The walls do not breathe; the bellow-shells make it seem that they do.

They face inward, toward the central cylinder. Not all at the same angle, but with the distributed attention of a crowd arranged around a speaker. They feed on resonance. Their bodies are chambers within chambers, small acoustical organs lined with metallic hairs. A loud impact fattens them. A steady engine note keeps them alive. A broken rhythm causes them to seal shut until the room corrects itself.

They reproduce by sound. When the lowest machine-note thickens at certain intervals, the oldest shells release a dust finer than ash and heavier than pollen. It travels nowhere by breeze—there is no breeze worth trusting—but by tremor, skittering along walls, catching in cracks, settling around bolts and pipe brackets. There it hardens into tiny listening mouths.

The plants are less plant than intention.

Black cords creep along conduits, always near current. Gray mats spread over ceramic insulators and taste the leakage there. Brittle fans unfold from alkaline shelves, their surfaces veined with dull metal. Translucent needle-clusters root in machine grime and grow toward humming. They do not seek light. Light is incidental. Heat matters. Charge matters. Certain salts matter. The memory of hands on a rail, perhaps, matters.

The squat stalks rising from old drums are the closest things to flowers. Their waxy skins split to reveal red interiors, not petals but exposed sealing surfaces, glossy and dry. Sometimes a glass-backed procession pauses around one of these openings, and the stalk trembles as if being read. Sometimes the stalk closes around a small insect and keeps it for a long time, not digesting it exactly, but copying some mineral fact from its shell.

All of these lives are separate only at the scale of a glance. Given time, they become parts of one larger resident.

The chamber itself lives there.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. Its life is distributed through furnace, salt, shell, pipe, animal, lamp, dust, echo. The creatures are organs of maintenance. The machines are bones that still remember labor. The mineral growths are slow tissues. The air is a circulating medium, dry instead of wet, carrying charge and powder where blood would carry warmth and food. Nothing rules from above. Nothing commands. But everything answers.

At the center is the thing they answer to.

The black cylinder is not their king. Kings require obedience, and there is no disobedience here to define it. It is not their god, because worship would imply distance. It is more like a heart that has never been inside a body, or a seed that grew machinery instead of leaves, or an organ the earth invented after seeing a factory once.

It lives by holding.

It holds the dryness. It holds the warmth. It holds decay at the lip of occurring. It holds the engines behind the wall in their endless, unchanging labor. It holds every sound in its allotted place. Its note is below hearing but not below knowledge; every creature carries that note in its joints, shells, roots, throat-sacs, plates. The sifter-throats tremble in sympathy with it. The wall-shells face it. The plated crawlers route themselves around it. The smallest salt-things hatch according to fluctuations no visitor could detect.

Nothing touches it. Not because it is forbidden, but because contact has no role in the system. Around it is a ring of respectful function: animals crowding near enough to receive, far enough not to interrupt. Pipes and rootlike growths braid upward from it, making a single trunk of industry and tissue. Lamps brighten near it with the faint, embarrassed glow of attention.

If it thinks, its thoughts are not images. They are tolerances. Pressures. Frequencies. Ratios of dust intake to dust fall. Acceptable warmth. Acceptable sharpness. The maintenance of corners. The prevention of damp. The continuance of the chamber as itself.

There may once have been human beings here. The place remembers the size of hands, the height of platforms, the angle of ladders, the language of arrows and stamped numbers. But humans do not live here now, except as a shape the chamber knows how to incorporate. A visitor would not be attacked first. They would be measured by dust. Sampled at the boots. Mapped by echoes. Warmed, coated, listened to. The animals would make allowances in their paths. The bellow-shells would learn the new obstruction’s effect on resonance. The central note would include the body’s water as a temporary error, then begin the slow work of making it irrelevant.

What lives there is an ecosystem that has forgotten softness but not care.

It cares for continuance. It cares that the belts move, though they carry nothing. It cares that salt breaks and is remade. It cares that heat is gathered, dust sorted, sound caught, sharpness maintained. It cares with the blind precision of a sealed process.

No creature there is lonely, because none is singular enough to be alone. No creature is free, because freedom would require an elsewhere. They live in assignment, in fit, in station. They are not trapped. Trap implies a prior open world. They are native to the locked condition.

They are the dry congregation of the under-crust: mites of salt, glass-backed tenders, throat-hung sifters, armored trench-scrapers, star-mouthed heat-burrowers, listening shells, electrical weeds, red-cored stalks, and the black central organ whose hum makes them all possible.

They do not wait.

They continue.
