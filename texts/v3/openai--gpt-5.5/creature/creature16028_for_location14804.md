What lives there is not one species, though the townspeople will pretend it is for legal convenience.

The most visible inhabitants are people, or something close enough to people that distance and custom have softened the question. They are slight, damp-skinned, patient-faced beings with long hands and wide, flexible feet made for uncertain surfaces. Their bones seem to have learned compromise. Few of them stand perfectly straight; they carry themselves with the practical bend of reed-cutters, bridge-crossers, root-listeners. Their shoulders are narrow, their hips strong, their balance excellent. Children learn to walk twice: once on solid ground, and once on ground that may change its mind.

Their skin ranges through mud-browns, olive-greens, pale clay, dark bark, and the faint bluish grey of old water. Many have subtle mottling along their limbs, not painted but inherited or acquired from lifelong contact with sap, spores, mineral seepage, and the town’s medicinal molds. Their hair is usually kept bound, lacquered, braided with beads that chime against sudden shifts in architecture. Some wear living ornaments: small moss-collars that cool the neck, fern-hats rooted into shallow trays, bracelets of pale vine that tighten gently when flood pressure changes.

They are not primitive. They simply do not separate tool, animal, plant, and machine the way outsiders might.

A resident may carry a knife with a bone handle, a spool of self-knotting fiber, a pouch of salt, three beetles trained to clean infected wood, and a ceramic charm containing a gravity exception valid only for municipal bridges. Their clothing is light, layered, waxed against rain, and cut to be repaired quickly. Children’s garments are sewn with bright warning threads so they can be seen through mist or foliage. Elders wear darker, heavier cloth printed with maps that are no longer accurate but still respected.

Their faces are calm, but not placid. They watch constantly. They notice the angle of a doorway, the taste of fog, the mood of roots underfoot, the absence of an insect that should be present. They do not stare upward much. Height is unreliable. They look down, sideways, into water, into bark seams, at the underside of leaves.

They speak in a language full of conditionals.

Not “the road goes there,” but “the road is going there this morning.”

Not “my house,” but “the chamber that keeps my family.”

Not “dead,” but “unresponsive,” “released,” “composting,” “waiting for beetles,” or “politically inert.”

They have many words for wetness: clean wet, hungry wet, fever-wet, engine-wet, root-wet, memory-wet, rain that has fallen, rain that has been exhaled, rain that has not yet chosen a direction.

Their society is built around attendance rather than ownership. To live in a house is to keep up relations with it: scrape fungal overgrowth from its breathing pores, sing its door-membranes open in the morning, feed mineral paste into weak wall-veins, invite diagnostic insects after long rains, thank it when it holds through flood season. A neglectful family may wake to find their back room quietly reassigned as public passage, nursery, shrine, or eel court.

They are governed, but not by rulers in any sharp sense. The closest thing to authority is a rotating group of map-keepers, canal negotiators, root advocates, flood-callers, archive tasters, and engine interpreters. Their meetings are held in different places depending on which building is most willing to remain still. Decisions are spoken aloud, scratched into soft bark, tasted into the archive grove, and released as scent markers for nonhuman parties.

No law is considered complete until at least three forms of life can obey it.

The children are bold in the way of children raised among living hazards. They know which berries teach nightmares, which gutters conceal eels, which vines enjoy being swung from, and which only pretend to. They race beetles down polished root-roads. They dare each other to whisper jokes into municipal drains. They collect abandoned door-teeth and trade them like coins. They are taught early how to count breaths, read flood-bells, identify false reflections, and apologize to a bridge.

Their games become civic training. A common children’s game involves crossing a courtyard while older children shout changing rules: “Grey stones only!” “Green if flowering!” “No shadows!” “The third step is a canal!” The best players grow into guides, builders, midwives, and negotiators.

The elders are not merely old people. Age changes a resident’s relationship with the town. Their bodies become repositories of tolerated spores, benign symbiotes, weather aches, and sensory habits too subtle for youth. An elder can place a palm on a wall and tell whether it is tired, pregnant with rooms, lying, or remembering fire. Some have bark-thick patches at the spine or wrists from decades of leaning against listening trees. Others keep small rootlets coiled beneath their skin, a voluntary bond with archive groves or healing pools. When they die, the question is not burial or burning, but affiliation: marsh, garden, wall, insectary, engine, family tree, public bench.

There are also specialists who are almost priest, almost mechanic, almost gardener.

The gravity-menders tend the shimmering bridge fields. They wear veils of glassy thread and carry tuning weights in nested baskets. Their work is quiet, dangerous, and beautiful: they adjust the local manners of falling things.

The eel-consuls maintain relations with wall-channel colonies. They go barefoot, smell faintly of brine, and speak by tapping ceramic rods against submerged pipes. No canal project proceeds without them.

The mouth-answerers are called when a wall grows speech organs. They are trained never to answer personal questions. Their official vocabulary consists mostly of weather reports, crop conditions, tide lies, and compliments about drainage.

The archive tasters press their tongues to encoded bark and read civic memory as bitterness, mineral sequence, numbness, sweetness, and aftertaste. They seldom eat strongly flavored food. Many of them cry sap-colored tears after difficult records.

The release-wardens oversee buildings that have gone fully dead. They decide whether a structure can be revived, given to insects, dismantled into nutrient, or allowed to sink into marsh. They are practical people with gentle hands.

But the humanlike residents are only one civic body among many.

The town’s plants live there in the fullest sense. They are not background, nor livestock, nor architecture alone. They are neighbors with slow tempers and long obligations. The great tree-buildings remember families by pressure, salt, and footfall. Seed-cap houses adjust their inner ribs around sleeping infants. Root-streets prefer certain festivals and become difficult during others. Benches grow leaves when they approve of conversation. Doorways sulk. Stairs breed alternatives.

Some plants are considered citizens. Some are minors. Some are public utilities. Some are criminals, usually for unauthorized expansion, predatory fragrance, or attempting to digest municipal signage.

There are animals who belong to the town as firmly as any family.

The wall-eels are white, clever, and vain. They keep the channels open, eat mineral clots, and attend festivals wearing children’s paint. They understand contracts, though not urgency.

The polishing beetles are fist-sized and glossy-backed, moving in patient processions along root-roads. They feed on the residue of foot traffic: skin flakes, oil, spilled starch, pollen, machine dust. Children name them; adults pretend not to.

The cup-leaf crabs are nearly transparent, each no larger than a thumb joint. They live in rainwater caught above doors and wave their claws at passersby with the solemnity of guards.

The roof frogs are loud, territorial, and indispensable. Their barking predicts pressure changes better than instruments.

The salt-birds are clever nuisances with hooked beaks, soft ears, and long memories. They repeat names, debts, secrets, legal clauses, lullabies, and occasionally the last words of people who are not yet dead. To silence one, you pay salt. To offend one, you may hear about it for years.

The spark-coughing reptiles in the heat vents are tolerated because they eat wire-worms and warning moths. Their nests are marked with red clay circles. Startling them indoors is considered bad manners and poor survival sense.

There are monkeys, or monkey-equivalents: shrieking, social, quick-handed creatures that live along the vine cables and steal polished things. They sometimes deliver messages, though never for the reason requested.

There are marsh deer with delicate legs and solemn eyes, moving through plazas as though remembering older roads beneath the current ones. They browse only where permitted, except during mating season, when they ignore all signage and must be negotiated with by song.

The great flat-backed grazers are visitors but honored ones. When one arrives from the marsh carrying sedge, flowers, parasites, and small weather systems on its shell, the town treats it as a temporary district. Permits are issued. Harvesters climb ladders onto its back. Children are warned not to fall asleep there, because the grazer may leave without announcement.

Beneath the pools, something larger lives, though no census includes it. The townspeople speak of it indirectly. They call it the Underturner, the Deep Tenant, the One Below the Reflections, or simply “that which must not be woken by drums.” It sleeps under the oldest water and dreams with enough force to lift every pool at once. Some say it is an animal. Some say an engine. Some say it is the original basin remembering the sea. Offerings are made to it not from worship, exactly, but from prudence.

The civic engines live too.

No one agrees whether they are machines, organs, ancestors, weather institutions, or a retired species of public servant. Their domes breathe at the marsh edge. Their diagnostic insects stream out in glittering swarms and return with maps in their stomachs. They do not speak in words, but they issue fog, tone, pressure, heat, and correction. The townspeople respect them the way one respects a mountain, a grandmother, a sleeping crocodile, and a power plant.

The engines are old enough to be beyond blame. When they fail, the town mourns. When they alter a district without notice, people curse them while obeying.

The smallest inhabitants may be the most numerous: repair spores, civic nanites, engineered pollens, memory yeasts, mites that groom sap-veins, bacteria that digest old plastics into root food, dust-sized surveyors that flash once before dying. They pass through bodies, buildings, water, and archives. Every resident is a weather system of microbes and machines. Illness is often a jurisdictional dispute.

And then there is the town itself.

Not metaphorically. Not exactly.

It lives in the combined pulse of root-pressure, drainage, insect traffic, civic memory, engine breath, human habit, animal paths, and laws repeated often enough that even trees grow around them. It has moods. It has reflexes. It can be startled, soothed, infected, embarrassed, made generous. It remembers not as a person remembers, but as a wetland remembers a channel, as a tree remembers a wound, as a machine remembers calibration.

The people do not say they live in the town.

They say they live with it, under it, through it, by its leave, in its current version.

They are citizens of a place that never stops becoming itself. They are gardeners of streets, tenants of organisms, mechanics of rain, relatives of walls, translators for eels, witnesses for trees, and temporary shapes in a much older patience.
