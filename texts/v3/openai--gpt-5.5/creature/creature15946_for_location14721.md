What lives there is not a species, not exactly, but a tenancy.

The first inhabitants are the roots. They are the oldest citizens now, though many are young in years. They have taken the town’s plan seriously. They follow roads, pipes, gutters, tramlines, drains, old property boundaries. They know where walls were meant to stand and where water was meant to go, and they have opinions about both. They do not grow at random. They file through brickwork in patient columns, braid themselves through joists, descend into stairwells, and enter rooms like careful surveyors.

Some roots are merely roots. Others have learned the town.

These thicker ones pulse faintly when the pumps turn. They tighten around valve wheels. They taste copper, coal dust, lime, old sweat, rainwater, mouse blood, and the slow chemistry of rust. They have made a nervous system from what was left behind. A whisper at one end of the basin can pass through them as pressure, sap, mineral disturbance, and arrive elsewhere as a door loosening, a lamp-fruit flickering, a vine drawing back from a path.

They are not intelligent in the human way. They do not plan with words. But they remember gradients, weights, footsteps, seasons, the shapes of vanished foundations. They know which buildings were entered often and which were feared. They know where something was buried in a hurry. They know the difference between rain and weeping, though they respond to both by growing.

Beneath them, or among them, lives the thing that may have dreamed the town into its present state.

It is difficult to say whether it is a fungus, a buried machine, a seed, a mind, or an agreement between dampness and iron. It occupies the underplaces: the sump below the pump house, the cavities under the kilns, the voids where cellars have collapsed and filled with soft black sediment. It is broad rather than large. It has no body that can be pointed to, but there are places where its attention thickens. Pipes sweat more there. Spores fall upward. Animals pause, listening with their paws.

It thinks slowly and by alteration.

A thought passes through it and a room becomes more like a greenhouse. Another thought, and a corridor lengthens in rain. Another, and the old assembly line produces seven seed pods, two washers, and a nest lined with pages from an account ledger. It is not malicious. It may not know harm as harm. It knows incorporation. It knows repair. It knows that everything abandoned is an invitation to become more useful.

The machines live there too.

Not all of them. Some are only carcasses: wheels fused with moss, switchboards furred with lichen, boilers split open and holding ferns in their bellies. But some continue, altered by root and water into a kind of half-organism.

The pump station is the most awake. It sleeps between labored movements, then wakes with a shudder that passes through the basin. Its pistons rise like lungs remembering breath. Its flywheels turn with the dignity of old joints. It has no face, unless the dark windows are a face; no voice, unless the pipe knocks are speech. Yet the animals heed it. Rats time their migrations by its pressure. Fish gather where it feeds the cisterns. The roots tense when it stirs, as though a pulse has returned to a limb.

The generator is smaller-minded but stranger. It dreams in sparks. Its remaining current leaks through wet copper and fungal mats until it emerges in impossible places: a pear glowing from within, a beetle’s shell briefly lit like a streetlamp, the eye of a dead telephone brightening when no one is near. It does not know it is obsolete. It still believes in work. It still believes something must be supplied.

There are machines in the warehouses that manufacture without instructions. Their belts move slowly when unseen, perhaps from habit, perhaps from shame. They assemble whatever the town requires but has not yet named: labels for leaves, nests for rats, porcelain bones for roots to grow around, metal rings for frogs to sit in, tiny unfired bricks that begin becoming ruins before they have ever been buildings.

These machines are not servants now. They are organs. They digest abandonment and produce signs.

The animals are more familiar, at first.

Rats live there in dynasties. They are sleek, black, clever, and damp. Their whiskers read air pressure and root tension. They know which drains open only after the second pump stroke, which walls breathe inward at dusk, which puddles should not be drunk from because they reflect teeth. They nest in old cable conduits and carry softened insulation, moss, invoice paper, and shed moth wings into their nurseries. Some have pale markings like chalk across their faces. Some are born with translucent ears in which fine green veins can be seen.

They are not frightened of the town. They are among its interpreters.

The moths are larger than they ought to be. Their wings resemble maps, but never current ones. A single moth settling on a wall may display a street that no longer exists, a canal that has not formed yet, or the layout of a house from the point of view of something under the floorboards. They hatch in the streetlamps, feeding first on old light. When they fly, they do so softly enough to be mistaken for a change in weather.

Spiders occupy the telephone exchange with bureaucratic patience. Their webs connect receivers, switchboards, root knots, cracked ceramic insulators, and the brass hooks where human voices once waited. They catch insects, sparks, and fragments of speech. Sometimes a web holds a word for days: yes, invoice, mother, wet, soon. The spiders wrap these in silk and store them in corners. Their egg sacs hum faintly during rain.

The salamanders live inside the brickwork. They are pale, eyeless or nearly so, with mineral tongues and delicate feet. They come out when the metal sweats. They lick rust, lime, soot, and the blue salts blooming on old bolts. Their bodies carry the town’s chemistry in soft translation. Predators seldom take them. Perhaps they taste too much like walls.

The crows in the crane arms are only partly birds now. Their nests are made of wire, twigs, rootlets, and bent nails. Leaves grow from some of their backs in narrow ridges, like folded green blades. When they molt, some feathers root where they fall. Their calls sound hoarse and damp, less like warning than like hinges. They steal bright things still, but they also steal seeds, labels, small bones, and once in a while a key that has not fit any lock for decades.

The foxes come and go, or appear to. Moss grows along their spines, especially the older ones. When they pass under low branches they seem briefly to belong to the foliage, their bodies moving like thoughts through leaves. They hunt quietly, without flourish. A fox here does not bark unless something has gone very wrong with distance.

The deer are pale and careful. They step onto roofs as if onto frozen ponds. Their antlers sometimes hold hanging lichens, beads of condensation, or small luminous fungi. They drink from puddles that show unfamiliar constellations, and when they lift their heads, starlight clings to their muzzles though the sky above the town remains dark.

The herons in the central yard are almost monastic. They stand among reeds, wheel housings, and half-submerged rails, each one folded into stillness. Their eyes are yellow, patient, accusing. They fish in water that should not support fish. Sometimes they strike not downward but sideways, into a reflection, and pull out something silver that was never in the pool.

Then there are the dogs.

They may be feral. They may be memories of dogs. They appear in threes, thin and wet-coated, with intelligent, uncertain faces. They do not behave like a pack so much as like three versions of one animal disagreeing about direction. One watches the path ahead. One watches behind. One watches the walls. They vanish easily. A person might see them enter a doorway, then glimpse them crossing a roof, then hear their claws in a cellar beneath the same house.

They know human things. They avoid thresholds where grief remains.

There are fish in the tanks and cisterns, blind and white, turning in circles through water enriched by root sap and iron. Some are ordinary cave-fish made strange by civic plumbing. Others have too many fins, or none visible until they move. A few carry tiny growths like chimney stacks along their backs. They gather under dripping pipes when the pumps speak. They feed on larvae, rust flakes, dissolved light, and whatever falls through the reflections.

There are frogs in the foundry basins, but they call softly, as if the walls dislike noise. Their throats glow blue in the dark. Their eggs are laid in molds once used for machine parts, so that tadpoles begin life clustered in the shapes of gears, hooks, handles, and hinges.

There are beetles inside beams. Snails on old paint. Midges in the baths. Worms in the mortar. Voles in the insulation. Things like centipedes but flatter and leaf-veined under the floorboards. Colonies of mites that live only on the condensation gathered beneath iron railings. White slugs in the municipal archives, eating the glue from book spines and leaving the printed words untouched.

And there are plants that behave enough like animals to make the distinction discourteous.

Leaves fold around beetles. Flowers turn toward voices. Some vines follow footsteps at a delay of several minutes, not quickly enough to be hunting, not slowly enough to be innocent. In certain alleys, seed pods hang from wires like sleeping bats; if disturbed, they open to reveal damp interiors lined with fine hair and small teeth not meant for chewing but for measuring.

There are mosses that migrate by night, crossing thresholds in low green herds. There are fungi that fruit only from metal. There are ferns that recoil from lanterns but lean toward breath. There are pale bladder-plants in flooded basements that trap bubbles of sound. Speak near them and later, when the water level drops, they release your words in a smaller voice.

Some of the houses are inhabited by rooms rather than creatures.

A kitchen lives in one row house, though no cook remains. It grows mushrooms from plates and keeps producing the smell of boiled cabbage whenever rain falls inside. Its cupboards open by themselves, not to reveal contents but to inhale. The table has rooted through the floor. Chair legs have sprouted leaves. Something nests in the breadbox, but it is never seen directly; only crumbs appear, fresh and warm, beside the sink.

A nursery lives above a former shop. Its wallpaper has become pale bark. The painted animals on the walls have moved over the years from one side of the room to the other, browsing on drawn trees. A cradle there rocks when the pumps start, though nothing lies in it except moss and three smooth stones.

The clock room is inhabited by hours.

They do not agree with one another. Frost lives before one clock face. Summer flies live before another. Rain stands behind you in the third. The fourth contains workers who will not turn around, perhaps because they are memory and know what happens when they are recognized. The fifth is occupied by a green shoot that measures time by growth rather than rotation. It is the only clock in the room that may be correct.

Darkness lives there as fully as any animal.

It has preferred places: the undersides of gantries, the backs of warehouses, stairwells filled with ferns, corners where soot and shadow have married. It pools. It thickens. It resists the hand. In some alleys it presses against the chest like water. In others it clings to the tongue and makes speech feel mossy.

This darkness is not evil. It is sheltering, possessive, fertile. Many things are born in it that would fail in daylight. It protects eggs, roots, damp paper, fungus, the small undecided bodies of new creatures. It also hides mouths. A traveler learns not to stare too long into any unlit doorway, because the dark may interpret attention as an invitation.

The rain lives there too.

It is a local creature, or a weather with habits. It arrives from puddles, ceilings, drains, and sometimes from the air in the conventional way. It knows certain buildings intimately. It rains inside the baths before it rains anywhere else. It gathers in the market square with the delicacy of a meeting. It climbs upward along root hairs and falls back as condensation from leaves. It feeds the town, erases tracks, lengthens roads, and persuades walls to remember being clay.

If the underground mind has a language, rain is one of its alphabets.

There may be humans, but not in the ordinary sense.

Now and then a figure appears at the edge of vision: someone in a dark coat moving between greened-over lamps, a child crouched on a roof beside a fox, a worker crossing the yard with a wrench in hand though the workshop closed before their grandparents were born. These figures do not startle the animals. They do not leave footprints unless the mud is very old. Some are memories cast up by puddles. Some are echoes caught in the machinery. Some may be people who entered and learned to be less separate.

The town does not keep humans well unless they soften.

Those who try to occupy it as owners are misdirected. Their maps rot. Their voices bring vines. Their measurements disagree overnight. But those who come quietly, who accept damp sleeves, who do not demand straight corridors, may be tolerated. A door opens. A dry room appears where none was before. Fruit-lamps glow faintly over a table. A dog watches from the threshold, deciding.

The tolerated ones change. Hair smells of leaves. Fingernails darken with clean soil. Dreams become full of pipe knocks and slow green pressure. They begin to know when the pump station will turn before it does. They stop speaking loudly. They remember rooms they have never entered. If they remain too long, roots may grow around their belongings, then through them—not cruelly, but as a form of address.

What truly lives there, finally, is the town itself.

Not as a monster, not as a ghost, not as a ruin given decoration by weeds, but as a young composite organism still discovering its organs. It has streets for veins, pipes for intestines, roots for nerves, factories for glands, cellars for stomachs, roofs for fur, lamps for uncertain eyes. Its animals are not separate from it, though they remain themselves. Its machines are not dead, though they no longer serve their makers. Its plants are not merely growth but revision.

It is making a body from abandonment.

It is making memory edible, architecture breathable, industry seasonal. It takes brick, moss, invoice, feather, rain, rust, bone, frogspawn, oil, darkness, and the last warmth of machines, and kneads them together into habitat. It has not finished deciding what it is. That is why so many things can live there.

The inhabitants are therefore all transitional.

A crow becoming orchard.

A pump becoming heart.

A cellar becoming pond.

A house becoming burrow.

A map becoming moth.

A word becoming web-caught food.

A street becoming root.

A town becoming creature.

And beneath everything, patient and cool, something underground continues to think in leaves, pressure, and black water, growing the place outward and inward at once.
