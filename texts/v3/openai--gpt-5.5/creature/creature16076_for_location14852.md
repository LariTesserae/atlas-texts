No single creature owns the plateau. It is not the country of one great beast, nor the hidden garden of a people. It is inhabited in layers, like the place itself: by films, mats, roots, insects, frogs, fish, birds, furtive browsers, and patient hunters. The life there is mostly small, wet-skinned, adhesive, and heat-wise. Everything that lives on the hot upland has learned to read water.

The first residents are almost not animals or plants at all. They live in the orange rims of the warm pools, in the silver slicks where minerals dry and reform, in the black jelly on stones below vents. They are microbial mats, soft crusts, threads, grains, and skins that thicken where the mountain breathes. Some are rust-colored, some bottle-green, some pale as curdled milk. They take warmth, sulfur, iron, rot, and light, and turn them into surfaces that other things can graze. Tiny snails rasp at them. Midge larvae tunnel through them. Crabs pick them apart with neat, nervous claws. The vents are not barren mouths of heat; they are farms without farmers.

Above them grows the green machinery of the plateau.

The plants are tough in a lush way. Their leaves look extravagant, but they are built for punishment: waxed, ribbed, sharply drained, able to take rain all day and steam all night. Ferns dominate the wet hollows, growing in ranks and crowded bowls, some fine as hair, some with fronds broad enough to hold sleeping frogs. Mosses make deep, treacherous carpets over peat and stone. Liverworts glaze the shaded rock like wet scales. Dwarf trees twist together into low roofs, their trunks swollen with stored water, their bark hidden under moss, lichens, orchids, and clinging ferns.

There are pitcher plants in the poorer soils, but they are not gaudy monsters. They are squat, dark-lipped vessels tucked between sedges and roots, collecting rain, insects, pollen, and the occasional drowned tadpole. Their mouths steam faintly in the morning, not because they generate heat, but because they hold the night’s warmth longer than the air. Small frogs shelter under their hoods. Certain ants nest in their stems and defend them from caterpillars, while still stealing from their traps.

Along streams, the vegetation becomes nearly muscular. Cane-stems knock together in mist. Broad leaves funnel water into their own root crowns. Flower spikes rise out of black mud in sudden colors: waxy orange, violet, white, scarlet. Many blossoms hang downward, made for birds that feed while clinging under them, or for heavy beetles that crawl inside and emerge dusted gold.

The insects are everywhere, though the place is too wet for great dusty swarms. They are beadlike, armored, lacquered, precise. Small metallic flies gather over warm seepage, flashing blue and green when sun breaks through. Long-legged water striders skate the cooler margins of pools but avoid the central trembling patches where heat rises. Black beetles climb steaming stones and fold themselves into mineral cracks when rain strikes. Pale moths sleep under leaves by day, their wings patterned like lichen maps. Ants build no tall mounds; the ground is too unstable. Instead they inhabit hanging gardens, dead branches, root-balls, and the dry interiors of epiphyte clumps.

There are dragonflies in the open marsh basins, large and glass-winged, patrolling the air lanes between reed beds. Their nymphs live in the warm shallows and are among the first predators many tadpoles ever meet. There are also vent midges whose larvae tolerate heat that would kill most small life. They cling in writhing red seams where hot water thins into cooler runnels, feeding on mineral films until a rain pulse washes them downstream.

The freshwater crabs are among the most characteristic animals of the upland. They are low, dark, and quick, with pale joints and delicate claws. Some live in the warm rivulets below cliff springs; others occupy peat pools under fern roots. They sort constantly: leaf fragments, algae, dead insects, shed frog skins, fallen fruit pulp. When disturbed, they do not flee far. They wedge themselves beneath stones and raise one claw slightly, as if holding a thought. After rain they emerge in numbers, crossing wet moss with surprising confidence. Their young begin life in hidden pools caught among roots, where the water is warm, low in current, and rich with rot.

The frogs are the plateau’s small voices.

They are many kinds. Some are leaf-clingers with round toe pads and skins like polished fruit: green, amber, brown, black with yellow flecks. They sleep flattened under leaves during the brightest hours, then inflate their throats at dusk and call from above the pools. Their notes are short and hollow, suited to mist and clutter; a long song would be swallowed by dripping leaves. Others live down in the moss and peat, squat frogs with blunt heads and dark backs mottled like wet bark. They do not leap far. They vanish instead, sinking into root tangles as if absorbed by the ground.

A few breed only in warm water. Their eggs cling to submerged stems near mineral springs, where the temperature stays steady through cold cloud nights. The tadpoles graze on orange film and black algae, developing wide mouths and heat-tolerant bellies. But the adults must be exact. Too close to a vent and the clutch cooks. Too far and fungus takes it. These frogs know the gradients of warmth with their whole bodies. They choose pools by touch.

Small fish live in the clear trickles and shallow marsh channels, though their world is broken into fragments by peat shelves, falls, and hot seepage. They are narrow, quick, brown-backed, and reflective along the flank. In cooler streams they move in loose shoals, darting under floating roots at the passing shadow of a bird. In warmer water they become solitary, each fish holding a pocket behind stone or root where current brings food. Some have blunt heads for grazing film. Some pick larvae from submerged moss. After heavy rains, channels join temporarily, and fish cross from one basin to another through brown rushing water, recolonizing pools that had been isolated for weeks.

Lizards own the black rocks when the sun appears. They are dark, flat-bodied animals that vanish into cracks with liquid speed. Their scales shine with constant damp. They bask not only for sunlight but for stored geological warmth, spreading themselves on stones over shallow heat seams. Their feet are wide and gripping, good for slick mineral crust and mossy bark. In the windward scrub they become paler and harder, with blunt tails and low heads, living among cushion plants where insects collect after fog.

Snakes are present, but rarely announced. One kind lies along warm branches, narrow and green-brown, indistinguishable from a vine until it shifts its head. It takes frogs, nestlings, and lizards. Another is heavier and more aquatic, living at the margins of mineral pools and braided streams. It hunts fish and crabs, waiting with only the nostrils and eyes lifted above brown water. Both are heat-wise. Neither crosses the pale crusts around the hottest vents unless rain has cooled them.

Birds pass through the plateau in flashes and interruptions. The nectar-feeders are small, iridescent, and quarrelsome, visiting the waxy flowers along streams and cliff gardens. They hover briefly but prefer to perch, clinging sideways beneath blossoms while rain beads on their backs. Fruit-pickers move more quietly, swallowing small dark berries from dwarf trees and leaving seeds in droppings along fern tunnels and rock ledges. Their bills are thick, their feet strong, their colors often hidden until they open their wings.

There is a seed-knocking bird, heavy-headed and deliberate, that works the canopy like a woodpecker works bark. It hammers pods and hard fruits against branches. The fallen pieces splash into pools, where fish, crabs, insects, and frogs all investigate. Its feeding can be heard farther than its voice.

The hunters are quieter. A heron-like bird stalks warm shallows, slate-backed, long-toed, with a pale throat and a bill fine enough to take tadpoles from between stems. It stands motionless beside mineral pools, steam feathering around its legs. Narrow-winged raptors hunt the open lanes above marsh basins, appearing when mist lifts, taking dragonflies, small birds, and the occasional lizard from exposed rock. At dusk, soft-winged night birds skim low over pools, catching insects rising from the day’s stored heat.

The larger animals are mostly known by evidence.

There are splay-footed browsers that move through the fern beds at night and in early morning. They are low-shouldered, dark-coated, and rounded, something between a small tapir, a marsh deer, and a pig in their habits, though not exactly any of these. Their feet spread wide on peat. Their muzzles are mobile and wet, made for stripping fern stems, fallen fruit, sedges, young leaves, and mineral-rich moss. They make tunnels through vegetation that other animals use: frogs in the damp prints, crabs in the ruts after rain, snakes along the dry raised edges. Their droppings are full of seeds, and wherever they sleep, seedlings later rise in small crowded patches.

A more delicate hoofed animal uses the firmer ridges and cliff margins. It has narrow legs, rubbery split hooves, and a coat the color of wet bark. It browses orchids, young leaves, and salt-loving plants around cool mineral seeps. It is almost never seen in the open except in fog, when its shape appears briefly between steam plumes and then becomes a leaning stump or a shadow. Its young hide in moss hummocks and give off little scent. Its ears are large, not for distance, but for sorting close sounds: water under roots, wingbeats, a snake shifting on bark.

There are burrowers too, though the ground makes true burrows difficult. Small blunt-snouted mammals live in root masses and old peat shelves above the waterline. They eat grubs, fallen fruit, fungus, and the soft bodies of snails. Their tunnels are temporary, repaired constantly after rain. They line chambers with dry fern fibers stolen from under overhangs and move their nests often, following the changing firmness of the ground.

The chief mammalian predator is rarely seen. It is a long-bodied, low-moving animal with a dark waterproof coat and pale whiskers, equally at home on roots, rocks, and shallow water. It hunts crabs, fish, frogs, nestlings, and the young of the smaller browsers when it can get them. It does not chase far. It knows crossings, waits at constrictions, and uses the noise of rain to cover movement. Its tracks appear beside warm streams after storms: five-toed, neat, already filling with tea-colored water.

There may be a larger catlike hunter in the outer forested ridges, but if it enters the plateau, it does so like weather: briefly, without explanation. The resident animals respond less with panic than with disappearance. Birds fall silent. Frogs stop calling. The browsers stand knee-deep in fern, nostrils lifted. Then, after a while, water resumes its ticking and the place begins again.

Everything that lives there is adapted not simply to wetness, but to uncertainty under wetness. Solid ground is a rumor to be tested. A mat may hold or give way. A pool may be warm, healing, rich with food, or hot enough below the surface to kill. A spring may run clear for months and then pulse mineral-white after an underground shift. Animals learn the plateau as a map of temperatures, textures, smells, and remembered dangers.

They also live by pulses. Rain opens corridors. Mist hides movement. Sun exposes insects. Steam warms night feeding grounds. Flowering draws birds. Fruitfall draws browsers. Browser dung feeds beetles. Beetles feed frogs and birds. Dead wood feeds fungus, ants, moss, seedlings. A vent changes course and kills a patch of sedge; mineral-loving plants colonize it; insects arrive; crabs work the edges; frogs lay eggs in the cooled water a season later. Loss is quickly inhabited.

The plateau’s life is not loud, not grand in the way of plains herds or tall forests full of calling monkeys. It is intimate, crowded, and continuous. Its inhabitants cling, sip, graze, filter, tunnel, wait, and release. They are polish-skinned, moss-backed, heat-tolerant, soft-footed, quick to vanish. They do not build the engine, but they live in every pipe and seam of it. The mountain warms the water; the water feeds the green; the green shelters the small; the small feed the quick; the quick feed the patient; and all of them, dying, return to the black wet ground, where the slowest residents begin their work again.
