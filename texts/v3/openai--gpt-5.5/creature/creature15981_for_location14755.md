What lives there is not a single kind of creature, but an arrangement of appetites old enough to look like geography.

The first inhabitants are the plants, though calling them plants makes them sound too still. They are the plateau’s elders and engineers: slow-bodied, heat-fed beings with territories, defenses, hungers, and long memories written in root and rind. A fan-tree may live for a thousand years and spend three hundred of them leaning one ribbed crown toward a particular wind. Its trunk is not wood in the lowland sense but layered cork, mineral grit, old insect galleries, healed wounds, and water channels under pressure. Animals nest in it, drink from it, court on it, die inside it, and the tree incorporates all of them. It is less a tree than a district.

The water-trees are among the most respected and feared. They stand alone on shallow rises, glossy and swollen, with translucent sacs nested among hooked leaves. Their crowns glitter at dawn with condensation. Some permit drinking. Some tolerate only certain birds or armored beasts whose saliva triggers the sacs to open harmlessly. Others defend their hoards with falling fruit full of venomous grubs, with whip-roots under the mat, or with stones lifted slowly over years and released when a shadow lingers too long below. The people of the plateau say a water-tree must be introduced to you by an animal before you may approach it.

There are vine-colonies that migrate across the open root plains in generations-long journeys, each stem living only a season but the body of the vine continuing like a thought passed from mouth to mouth. They browse on dead crust, fungal bloom, old moss, shed skin, dung, and sometimes the sleeping. Their fronts are soft and exploratory, feathered with tasting hairs. Their rears are pale scars where the land has been scraped clean. If watched at noon they appear motionless; if watched by moonrise they have rearranged a whole slope.

The red moss is not one organism but a pact: moss, fungus, adhesive algae, and minute hunting worms living in a shared skin. Dry, it is velvet and brittle underfoot. Wet, it swells into a red-black gel that grips anything warm. Small animals vanish there in frantic stages: first feet, then belly, then muzzle. The moss does not hurry. Scavenger beetles, needle-mouthed birds, and pale lizards come to feed, and their leavings feed the moss in turn. Old paths bend widely around red plains, even when they look dry.

The reed beds are hollow cities. Each stem is wide enough to hide a child, a leopard-cat, a shrine, or a clutch of eggs. Wind makes them sing, but so do the animals inside them. Some insects carve resonating chambers into the inner walls and use them to broadcast mating pulses across miles. Frogs paste their eggs above the damp nodes. Small people sleep in the higher hollows during floods of ants. At certain seasons the reeds synchronize with buried colonies below, and the whole basin becomes an instrument, a single note rising until bones ache.

Beneath all of it is the old root mass, the under-country. It is crossed by blind streams, burrows, fungal corridors, collapsed trunks, larval chambers, and pockets of air warm as breath. Things live there that seldom see the sun: white-shelled crabs with root-cutting claws, glassy worms as long as a man, eyeless grazers with velvet snouts, colonies of heat-making beetles that ventilate through chimneys of packed fiber. The surface rises and falls above them. Travelers call it buried forest-breath, and perhaps that is enough.

Among the visible animals, the most numerous are the small ones. Bowl-sized beetles push through moss like armored thoughts, clicking and shoving with polished mandibles. They come in lacquered black, ocher, green, and ash-white, depending on the plants they eat. Some carry gardens of fungus under their wing-cases. Some roll pellets of dung and pollen up ridges to hatch their young in warmth. Some are followed by thin, nervous birds that pick mites from their joints.

The lizards are everywhere once you learn how to see them. Pale ones lie flat on leaf plates, their skins matching the waxy glare. Black-throated ones live in the cracks of succulent slopes and flash blue tongues before they flee. Long, ribbon-bodied water lizards haunt condensation channels and root streams, their limbs reduced almost to hooks. They wait under leaf-drips for moths, birds, or the noses of thirsty mammals. The largest are old, scarred animals that bask along ridges at dawn, mouths open, steam lifting from their tongues.

There are grazers built for the open heat: tall, narrow animals with high shoulders, slit nostrils, and lips divided into delicate fingers. They move in loose diagonal herds, never straight toward water, never straight away from danger. Their hides are striped in the colors of dry grass and shadow. They feed on fungal rind and the thin living crust that grows over old root plains. Their teeth grow continuously and are worn down by grit. Calves are born already long-legged and silent, able to stand between their mothers’ legs and disappear into the geometry of stems.

The armored browsers are lower, heavier, and more influential. They look almost made of the plateau itself: plated backs, blunt wedge heads, forelimbs thick enough to crack hardened leaf-ribs. They kneel to break water bladders and can smell poison blooms before opening them. Other creatures watch them constantly. When browsers drink safely, birds descend, stilt-beasts approach, insects swarm the wet scars, and even predators delay their strike until the first danger has passed. Their dung is rich, fibrous, full of viable seed, and often sprouts before it dries.

The stilt-legged animals are the plateau’s ghosts of grace. They step on the broad leaves without triggering cilia, distribute their weight over soft toes, and pluck dew beads one by one from flower throats. Their eyes sit high and sideways. Their ribs show. Their young are hidden not in nests but in suspended hammocks of chewed fiber, tied between thorn stems above the reach of crawling mouths. They are fragile only in appearance; their kicks can split a lizard’s skull, and their alarm cries carry farther than thunder.

The birds are less a class than a weather system. There are stilt-birds that know every water pocket and every poisonous basin. They are followed by people, cats, and desperate herds. There are night screamers with long hooked wings that roost in fan-tree crowns and drop at dusk on bats, frogs, and large insects. There are nectar birds small as thumbs, dusted copper and emerald, living half their lives inside flowers large enough to drown them. There are carrion kites that circle so high they seem like flecks in the eye until something dies, at which point they fall from the white sky in silence.

Many winged things are not birds. Membranous gliders cling under leaf-ribs by day, their bodies furred against the high chill of night despite the daytime heat. Beetle-winged pollinators pour from shaded creases at dusk, each species answering a different flower’s temperature, color, or scent. Broad, soft-bodied moths migrate between fan-trees in pale rivers. Some carry pollen. Some carry parasites. Some live only one night, mate in the copper haze, and fall by morning in such numbers that the ground seems dusted with torn petals.

Predators belong to every layer.

The heat-drunk cats are the most admired because they are beautiful and rarely seen honestly. Their hides imitate bark, lichen, and broken shade. Their paws are wide and quiet on root crust. They sleep in fan-tree ribs with their tails hanging like vines, then descend at evening to follow herds. They do not waste effort. A cat may trail a wounded browser for two days, drinking dew and waiting for the land itself to finish the work. During the hottest hours they seem senseless, sprawled and panting, but their ears continue to turn.

The serpentine hunters are water-thieves and ambush-makers. Some are true reptiles, some amphibious, some difficult to classify. They live in hidden streams and condensation gutters, with nostrils that close like seeds and jaws hinged too far back. They strike upward at drinking birds, sideways at calves, downward into burrows. Their shed skins are prized by the plateau people because they predict rain: fresh skins curl tight before a dry spell and relax before storms.

There are mantis-creatures that wear seed husks, dry leaves, insect shells, and sometimes the finger bones of animals that have died nearby. They stand beside pollinator trails, indistinguishable from debris until their forelimbs open. Their patience is nearly vegetal. Some spend so long among flowers that the flowers grow around them, accepting them as part of the architecture. A few have learned to tap flower stems in patterns that summon nectar insects.

Other predators blur the boundary between animal and plant. A sweet-smelling shade under an overhanging mat may be cast by leaves whose undersides are furred with digestive hairs. Warm fruits may pulse not with ripeness but with larvae inside, each larva mimicking the heat of mammal flesh to attract egg-laying flies, then birds, then larger scavengers. Some flowers close too quickly around pollinators; some do not close at all but drug the animal until it remains willingly, dusted and dazed, while rootlets enter its skin.

The scavengers are the plateau’s priests of usefulness. Nothing dies unused because too many mouths are waiting. Bone beetles arrive first, guided by vibration and the salts of blood. Then come tearer-flies, carrion kites, pale lizards, red-jawed ants, fungal fans, and root hairs fine as smoke. In a day a small carcass becomes a stain of motion. In a week a large one becomes a mound of sprouting heat. Horns, plates, and teeth last longer, but even those are bored through, nested in, polished by tongues, carried away as tools or ornaments.

There are people too, though not many, and they do not behave as if the plateau belongs to them.

They live lightly, in scattered bands and seasonal households, following water, flowering, insect emergence, and herd routes. Their villages are not villages in the lowland sense. A settlement may be three platforms in a fan-tree, two ash-cleared sleeping ovals, a raised rack of gourds, and a ring of carved stakes warning where the ground has mouths. They abandon and return to places over years. Their architecture is repaired by growth as much as by hand. A platform left alone may become a branch. A wall may root. A roof may flower.

They are lean, scarred, observant people with a culture of waiting. Children learn before speech which leaves may be stepped on, which shadows are wrong, which insects mean water, which silence means a cat. They are taught to watch an animal drink before drinking, to test shade with smoke, to listen with the soles of their feet. Speed is considered childish except in storms, fires, and mating swarms. Patience is a survival skill and a moral virtue.

Their tools are portable because permanence is arrogance here. They carry obsidian flakes wrapped in fiber, bone needles, hooks made from stilt-bird leg bones, nets woven for particular insects, gourds sealed with resin, ash packets, thorn awls, reed flutes, and water bladders traded from safe trees. Metal is rare and powerful: a knife of sky-iron, a copper ring from the lowlands, a broken buckle hammered into a charm. Metal is not wasted on ordinary cutting if shell, glass, tooth, or stone will do.

They harvest by relationship. A vine that gives fiber is marked and revisited, never stripped bare. A fan-tree whose yellowing ribs are taken receives ash, dung, songs, or the bodies of pests removed from its bark. Water bladders are pierced with narrow hollow thorns in places that heal. Reed stems are cut above living nodes. Certain flowers are never touched except by widows, certain mosses except after first thunder, certain fruits except when the stilt-birds have finished with them.

Whether the plants understand apology is debated by outsiders and not by the people who live there. The people know the practical answer: those who take wrongly die more often.

They have specialists. Water-listeners lie flat with one ear to the root crust and hear the cool mutter of hidden streams. Spore-readers can tell from the taste of evening dust which flowers will open and which animals will follow. Cat-watchers mark the sleep trees of the mottled predators and advise when herds should be avoided. Reed-callers use carved flutes to speak with distant camps through wind beds, though messages are never exact. Ash-keepers maintain bare sleeping grounds and know how to burn without waking the wrong seeds.

Their dead are not buried deep. The root layers are too hungry and too sacred. Some are placed in high platforms for birds. Some are wrapped in cured leaves and given to particular trees. Some are laid on red moss before rain, an offering both feared and honored. Bones may be recovered later as needles, whistles, beads, or memory pieces. To leave a body unused would be an obscenity.

They tell stories in which the plateau was once an animal that lay down and became green, or a forest that climbed a mountain and forgot how to descend, or the back of a sleeping being whose breath makes clouds. None of these stories exclude the others. Their myths are practical maps disguised as origins. A tale about a greedy hunter who drank before the armored browser is also a warning about poison blooms. A song about a bride taken by sweet shade teaches children to distrust certain flowers. A comic story about a man courting a reed explains wind direction, echo, and hollow stems.

They do not hate the lowlands, but they distrust lowland certainty. Lowlanders think in roads, walls, ownership, wells. Plateau people think in routes, permissions, ripeness, signs. They know that a path may be valid one month and lethal the next. They know that shade can move, water can lie, and open ground can be more dangerous than thicket. Their hospitality is generous but conditional: guests are fed, watched, corrected sharply, and sometimes tied by the ankle at night so they do not wander into breathing ground.

Other intelligences may live there too, though not in a way that admits easy proof.

Some fan-trees flower in sequences that match old route songs. Certain reed beds answer flutes with tones no wind made. Herds sometimes change course before sporefall as if warned. There are troughs where the root mat rises beneath a sleeping traveler and settles again only after the traveler speaks aloud, offering name, origin, and intention. The plateau’s people do not say the land is one mind. They say minds are not always shaped like heads.

The true resident, then, is the whole interlocked heat of it: plants that build weather, insects that tune the air, grazers that read poison, predators that imitate shade, fungi that remember every death, people who survive by courtesy, and roots below all of them digesting centuries into a future surface.

Everything there lives by restraint until the moment of abundance. It waits through glare. It seals its moisture. It tastes the air. It listens through bark, shell, paw, cilium, and bare foot. Then rain crosses a valley for ten minutes, and the plateau opens—flowers heating, frogs hammering, beetles climbing, cats waking, water sacs swelling, spores lifting into copper light—and every hidden life declares itself at once.
