What lives there is not a people, though the city has the shape of having expected one.

It is inhabited by continuities.

The first and largest resident is the city itself: not a ruin, not a plant, not a mineral formation, but a settled agreement between stone, heat, water, root, and time. It does not think in sentences. It thinks in drainage, in pressure, in the placement of openings, in the exact curve by which a stair becomes a bridge. Its intelligence is not quick enough to be called thought and not blind enough to be called accident. It has arranged itself into boulevards and cisterns for the same reason a shell arranges itself into chambers, for the same reason a fern unrolls, for the same reason water finds a low place. Form is its appetite.

The towers are its tall organs. The plazas are its lungs. The canals are veins carrying warm mineral blood from cliff to marsh and back into hidden caverns. The root bridges are tendons. The amber sap in the walls is a memory-fluid, thick and golden, holding in suspension whatever decision first caused growth to become architecture. The city does not expand. It no longer needs to. It lives by maintaining the exactness of itself.

Beneath the streets lives the fungal undercity.

It is pale, fibrous, soft as soaked paper and old as the basin. It fills the dark between paving roots and mineral ribs. It threads through wall, cistern, hollow pillar, hanging garden, shrine, marsh platform, and cave-mouth. It is the closest thing here to a nervous system, though it has no center and no urgency. If anything perceives visitors, it is this hidden mesh: not as strangers, not as bodies, but as disturbances in humidity, salt, warmth, pressure. It registers a footprint as a temporary weather. It receives breath as a small, animal storm. Then it absorbs the fact of it and returns to equilibrium.

The fungi are not decomposers, because nothing here properly dies. They are preservers. They hold leaves at the instant before yellowing, petals at the instant after opening, fallen matter at the instant before becoming refuse. Their work is the prevention of aftermath. They are the custodians of “not yet.”

The ferns live there like green aristocrats in a court without genealogy.

They stand on terraces, in windows, along canal edges and stair landings, each frond lacquered, immense, motionless, beaded with perfect water. They do not seed. They do not spread. They occupy. Their curled new growths remain curled forever, a perpetual promise of unfurling that never needs fulfillment. Their undersides are red, almost fleshy, but there is no flesh in them; only the city’s heat showing through leaf.

They are not passive. Their stillness has weight. A fern may lean over a balcony for centuries with the exact attitude of someone listening from an upper room. A row of them may flank an avenue with the solemnity of officials. In the central courts, where their crowns gather above shallow pools, they seem to deliberate. What they deliberate is not policy, not fate, not judgment, but posture: how to hold the basin in its completed condition.

The vines are the binders.

They climb walls that do not need support and support walls that should not still be standing. They cross gaps as bridges, lace openings as curtains, lock towers to cliffs, loop through colonnades and under canals. Some are thick as the bodies of trees. Some are delicate as wet hair. They do not strangle. They restrain. Their purpose is not conquest but insistence: this arch shall remain an arch; this stair shall remain a stair; this crack shall widen no farther; this collapse shall be delayed into architecture.

If the city has laws, the vines enforce them.

They know the precise boundary between ruin and design. They let stones sag, but not fall. They permit roots to split terraces, but only until the split becomes a channel. They wrap statues that were never carved and doorframes that were never fitted. They are the city’s ligatures, its signatures, its long green handwriting.

The lichens live as maps.

They spread over walls in pale green, gray, yellow, and black islands, resembling continents on a drowned globe. They are thin lives, almost weather, almost stain. Yet they are among the most attentive inhabitants. They remember exposure, seepage, mineral taste, the angle of diffuse light, the path of water down a particular stone face. They do not record history as events, because there have been no events for them. They record conditions. They are archives of sameness.

On some walls they gather into patterns so close to script that the mind aches trying to read them. But the lichens never become language. They live at the edge of meaning and refuse to cross. Their message, if they have one, is that form can be exact without becoming speech.

The mosses are the city’s sleep.

They cover steps, benches, parapets, roofs, the lips of wells, the backs of bridges. They are deep and layered, velvet-dark, swollen with warm water. They soften all thresholds. They make every surface seem touched by long use, though nothing has used it. They accept the endless rain, the endless mist, the endless drip from eaves and leaves. They cushion the city against its own hardness.

If one kneels near them, one might feel that they are breathing. But they are not. They are holding breath on behalf of a world that no longer exhales.

The orchids are the most nearly personal of the residents.

They bloom in cracks and high ledges, in the mouths of drains, in the hollows of root-pillars, above black pools where nothing comes to drink. Their flowers have the colors of injury and moonlight: livid violet, arterial red, yellow-white, translucent green. They seem obscene only because they are so complete in a place without pollinators. They advertise to no one. Their petals are invitations addressed to an extinct possibility.

Each flower is always newly open. Not opening. Not fading. Open. They live in the condition after desire but before consequence. Their suspended fallen petals—those impossible fragments hovering just below the bloom—are not failures of gravity so much as the orchids’ second blossoms: the bloom of almost-loss.

The reeds at the marsh edge are the border people.

They stand in exact ranks, black-green and taller than houses, their stems dense enough to make corridors, chambers, processional ways. They are too orderly to be wilderness and too alive to be construction. They divide the marsh into routes that may never have been traveled. They make privacy in a city without bodies. They form neighborhoods for mist.

The reeds think in straight lines. They know alignment, adjacency, repetition. They are the keepers of approach and refusal. To enter their grids is to feel oneself being arranged rather than guided. They do not trap. They make escape geometrically uncertain.

Among them stand the small root-shrines with no doors. These are also alive, though not as houses and not as plants. They are sealed gestures. They are dwellings for absence. Their interiors, if they have interiors, belong to whatever kind of inhabitant does not require entry.

The pools are inhabited by reflections.

These are not illusions exactly. They are not spirits either. They are possibilities retained in water after the world declined to enact them. In the pools live the shapes of populations the city might have housed: silhouettes at balconies, processions crossing bridges, dark fish under canal surfaces, birds folding above towers, animals lowering their heads to drink, figures gathered in amphitheaters, lamps shining in market arcades where no fire has ever existed.

None of them are present.

But the pools remember the architecture’s invitation. A doorway implies someone passing through; a plaza implies assembly; a bridge implies crossing. The reflections are the city answering its own implications. They vanish when directly pursued because they are not hiding. They simply cannot become more real than the rule of the place permits.

The droplets in the air are another kind of resident: sealed, tiny worlds.

Each bead contains the city inverted, miniaturized, altered. Some hold dry versions, burning versions, inhabited versions, ruined versions, cities overrun by beasts, cities under stars, cities buried in snow, cities still growing, cities never grown. These droplets do not fall because falling would choose one world over another. They remain suspended, each one a closed census of alternatives.

They are the city’s unborn histories.

The shadows live there too, and they are not always loyal.

Some cling properly beneath arches and leaves. Others belong to objects that are not present. A tower may cast the shadow of a tree. A tree may cast the shadow of a tower. A colonnade may darken the ground in the shape of a procession. These shadows are perhaps the oldest inhabitants, older than the visible structures, older than the plants. They are forms remembered from before light agreed to match matter.

They do not move across the day, because the day does not progress. Instead they persist like legal claims. This place was also otherwise, they seem to say. This shape has another owner.

The roar lives everywhere.

It is not merely sound made by water. It is a resident force, a pressure-being, a body without outline. It fills halls, presses through moss, vibrates in root-bridges, enters the cavities of bone and tower alike. It has no source because every source shares it. The cliff seep, the canal, the pore, the hidden river, the suspended rain—all are mouths of the same vast utterance.

The roar is the city speaking without language and without pause. It says only one thing, but says it with infinite density:

Here. Here. Here. Here.

In the caves behind the waterfalls live the almost-letters.

They appear in mineral veins, root knots, stalactite joins, stains of iron and calcite. They gather into near-script along the walls and then dissolve back into geology. They are not writing, but they exert the pressure of writing. They make the observer feel that meaning is present and withheld, not because it is secret but because it has not separated from matter.

These almost-letters are the city’s dreams of language. They live where water, root, and stone touch in darkness. They never quite say what they know. Perhaps because the city has no need to tell itself anything. Perhaps because to become readable would be to begin time.

Nothing there lives as an animal lives.

Nothing hunts, mates, calls, flees, nests, gnaws, sleeps, or dies. There are no eyes opening in the fern-dark, no larvae under bark, no wings in the vapor, no teeth in the marsh water. The city has rejected hunger. It has rejected pursuit. It has rejected the small panic by which animal life usually proves itself alive.

Instead, life there is positional.

Life is the holding of a curve.

Life is the maintenance of wetness.

Life is the refusal of a petal to finish falling.

Life is the continued agreement between a root and a wall.

Life is a bridge remaining crossable though no one crosses it.

Life is an empty amphitheater retaining the exact acoustics of a crowd.

If the place has inhabitants in the ordinary sense, they are the offices of habitation rather than the inhabitants themselves: passage, gathering, watching, waiting, sheltering, remembering, receiving. A doorway lives by being passable. A cistern lives by being full. A balcony lives by facing outward. A stair lives by implying ascent. These functions have become the citizens. They occupy the city more completely than bodies could.

And above all, there is the basin-presence: the total life made from all the others.

It is not a god, though it is large enough to be mistaken for one. It is not a mind, though it arranges. It is not asleep, though it does not act. It is the condition that allowed architecture to grow without builders and ecology to exist without animals. It is a climate that became civic. A wet heat that learned proportion. A mountain’s breath that took the form of streets.

It does not wait for visitors. It does not mourn the absent. It does not desire completion.

It is complete.

Whoever lives there is what happens after becoming has been stopped without being killed: a city-population of roots, mosses, fungi, reflections, suspended drops, obedient waters, disobedient shadows, and forms too intentional to be wilderness but too innocent to be made.

They do not inhabit the city.

They are the city’s way of inhabiting itself.
