The ones who live there are not a people apart from the city. They are a pressure in it, a warmth under its double walls, a habit of repair moving through stone.

At first you might not notice them as individuals. In the morning fog they are shapes with hoods up and shoulders rounded against the wet, climbing stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, the other holding a tin pail, a tool roll, a bundle of greens, a gauge case strapped against the ribs. They do not hurry unless something has already gone wrong. Even then, their urgency is contained. They have the gait of people who know that breath is costly, that wet stone betrays the careless, that old mechanisms respond badly to panic.

They are compact more often than tall, though there are long-boned families from the outer terraces whose limbs seem made for ladders and ravine paths. Most have strong calves, narrow chests made efficient by altitude, and hands roughened not only by work but by damp: cracked knuckles, moss-stained nails, the pale scars of cold metal. Their faces weather early. Fine lines gather around eyes from squinting through mist and gauge-glow. Their lips chap blue in winter. Their hair is usually kept bound, braided, wrapped, or cut close, because machinery, wind, and constant moisture punish vanity.

Yet they are not dour. Quiet is not the same as sadness. They have made a culture out of low voices, small jokes, patient songs, and the shared pleasure of things that continue to work.

They speak in short exchanges in public, especially outdoors, where breath plumes and words fall flat. In kitchens, furnace bays, drying rooms, and tram shelters, conversation thickens. There the people become more animated: arguing over valve timings, orchard grafts, old family debts, weather signs, whether the east cistern is filling too quickly again, whether a fox seen near the battery sheds means frost or thaw. Their speech has many words for dampness, for kinds of fog, for water that clings, water that beads, water that runs true, water that runs wrong. They distinguish the silence of empty pipes from the silence of full ones. They can hear a loose bracket through a wall.

Children learn the city before they learn its map.

They are carried first in slings under waxed coats, breathing wool, lamp-oil, rain, and skin. Later they crawl on warm floors above machine rooms and are scolded away from condensation gutters. By five or six they know which stairways are honest in frost, which roofs will shed rime without warning, which handrails grow the harmless green moss and which carry the numbing black film that causes fever if it enters a cut. They play at being maintenance crews. They build tiny aqueducts in drainage channels, whistle at fog to make it tremble, dare each other to stand beside the old bald circles in factory floors. Their games often involve waiting: waiting for a drip to fall, for a pipe to knock, for a tram light to change, for the pool in the civic platform to show its darker sky.

They grow up with the idea that every object has two lives: the intended one and the one it will have after wearing out.

A cracked bowl becomes a seed tray. A worn gear becomes a shrine weight. Broken tram glass becomes greenhouse patchwork. Old clothes are felted into insulation. The dead are wrapped in cloth that can decay, but their tools are kept, named, and redistributed. In some houses there are racks of inherited tools more carefully tended than portraits: a grandmother’s pressure key, an uncle’s moss knife, a mother’s lamp wrench, a child’s first fitting hammer hung after the child grew into heavier work.

The dead themselves are not thought to leave quickly.

Not as ghosts in white shapes, not as voices in the hall, but as tolerances within things. A pipe adjusted by a careful hand may keep that hand’s judgment for decades. A stair repaired badly is said to reject the mason and loosen beneath his descendants. A well-kept orchard remembers the breath of those who pruned it. People speak to machines without embarrassment, especially when starting them in cold weather. They greet cisterns, apologize to boilers, thank stubborn doors. This is courtesy, not superstition, though no one there draws a clean line between the two.

Their houses are layered like nests.

Outer walls of stone take the weather. Inner walls of felt, timber, reed mat, and packed moss hold the human warmth. Window ledges are crowded with drying herbs, folded cloth, tools left where they can be reached quickly, jars collecting seepage, black bees asleep in little comb-boxes through the coldest months. Beds are built into alcoves. Kitchens are often below street level or against a shared heat wall. The best place in a house is not the largest room but the driest bench, the one near enough to warmth without being damp enough to mildew papers.

Meals are plain, fragrant, and intensely local. They eat dark bread steamed rather than baked when fuel is short, mushroom cakes dried in fan houses, sour plum paste, bitter greens, smoked root, pressed nut-pine oil, grey apples kept through winter in moss-lined drawers, cheeses made from the milk of small terrace goats where such animals are still kept. Meat is rare enough to be ceremonial. Eggs are rarer. Honey from the insulated hives is medicine, gift, and festival food. Hot broth is almost sacred: mineral, fungal, herbal, sometimes with shavings of dried meat floating like relics.

Their festivals follow utility so closely that outsiders might mistake them for work.

There is a first-fog day, when the upper nets are inspected and children tie scraps of bright cloth to the lower stays. There is a cistern-opening, after the long cold, when the stored water is measured and a cup from each public tank is poured into the still pool at the old civic center. There is a pruning week with songs too soft to travel beyond each terrace. There are furnace nights when metalworkers fire several shops at once and the whole district glows red under the fog, and people come not merely to watch but to bring broken things: hinges, pans, rail fittings, charms, buckles, needles, toys.

Their music is built for poor acoustics.

They use close harmonies, throat-hummed drones, small bells, breath pipes, stone idiophones, and stringed instruments with muted bodies. Loud drums are disliked except in the pressure halls, where rhythm is used to coordinate valve teams. A common song may sound unfinished to a stranger: a rising line, a pause for breath, a low answer from another room, then the scrape of work resuming. Many songs include long rests. The rests matter. The city itself is expected to answer with drip, knock, hiss, rail-whine, or wind-hum.

They do not worship abundance. They distrust it.

A river in flood, a roaring fire, a clear blue sky that lasts too long—these things make them uneasy. Their reverence is for recurrence, circulation, return. The water that is caught, used, filtered, lifted, condensed, and caught again has a moral force. Waste is not merely foolish but indecent. To leave a tap weeping uncontrolled is a kind of public insult. To burn dry wood for spectacle would be remembered for years. Children are praised not for owning things but for knowing how to keep them in service.

Still, they love beauty, and because they cannot spend much on display, they hide it in edges.

The inside of a valve housing may be etched with a family pattern. Tram handles are braided in colored fiber according to district. Rain capes are patched with deliberate geometry. Tool boxes are carved with contour lines. Greenhouse panes are arranged so that when the weak sun strikes, the wall briefly becomes amber, blue, and pale rose. People cultivate miniature gardens in broken insulators and old lamp cups. A perfect line of moss along a parapet can be admired the way other places admire marble.

There are guilds, though they are less like merchant houses than lineages of obligation.

The Water Office families are famous for memory. They know cistern capacities by sound. Some can tell by taste whether a tank has taken fog-water, roof-water, ravine seep, or old quarry standing water. The Condenser crews are wiry and weather-burned, spending much of their lives among high nets where the fog pulls hair and lashes into beads. They carry tuning forks, pressure whistles, and long-handled combs for rime. The Tram people have black crescents under their nails and a tendency to glance at rails while speaking. The Gardeners are considered both practical and slightly dangerous, because they know roots, poisons, grafts, medicinal molds, and the moods of bees. The Deep Exchange workers, who tend heat shafts and thermal engines, are pale even by city standards and speak least of all.

The Archive keepers are stranger.

They live near the old black platform, in rooms lined with waxed paper, slate records, pressure charts, plant histories, maps corrected so many times the original city is hidden under ink. They record weather not as daily fact but as negotiation: which walls wept, which cisterns lied, which moss crossed iron, which children were born during upward flooding in the alleys, which chimneys exhaled after being cold for years. They are trained to read stains. They consult retired machinists the way other cities consult philosophers. They preserve obsolete instructions because nothing is ever fully obsolete there.

The infirmary workers keep their own quiet mysteries. Illness at that height is often a matter of breath, damp, mineral imbalance, mold, cold entering the joints, or pressure confusion. They prescribe bitter infusions, heated stones, fungal poultices, compressed rest, lamp exposure, and careful relocation from one level of the city to another. There are rooms where patients sit beside warm pipes and breathe through cloth steeped in herbs. There are wards for those who have heard too much from cisterns, or who cannot stop feeling the city’s pressure changes in their bones.

Outsiders come rarely, and when they do, they are obvious.

They speak too loudly. They underestimate stairs. They ask where the river is. They think a machine that is not running is dead. They touch moss without permission. They are startled by the way residents pause mid-sentence when a pipe knocks, all heads tilting slightly, listening together. The city’s people are polite to them, but politeness there has a measuring quality. A visitor is weighed by whether they close doors, wipe condensation from shared benches, return cups dry, and step aside for maintenance crews.

Some outsiders stay. They do not become native quickly. Their lungs may adapt in a year, their walking in three, their speech in five. But the deeper change takes longer: learning that survival is not conquest, that maintenance is not failure, that quiet may be full of decisions.

The people have a civic temperament shaped by compromise with stone. They prefer councils to proclamations, schedules to commands, repairs to revolutions. This does not mean they are meek. Their anger is slow and formidable. A person who steals water, damages a shared stair, tampers with pressure controls, or neglects a public mechanism can be shunned with a thoroughness more frightening than prison. The punishment is usually restoration: months of supervised labor cleaning culverts, rewrapping rails, scraping mineral bloom, carrying night lamps in dangerous fog. To harm the systems that keep others alive is to be made intimate with them.

Their politics are hydraulic.

Power belongs to those who understand flows: water, heat, labor, information, obligation. No single office can rule absolutely because no single system can sustain the city. The Water Office needs the machinists. The machinists need the gardeners for insulation fiber, oils, and food. The gardeners need heat schedules and pipe maps. The tram crews need stonecutters, electricians, weather readers. The archive remembers who owes whom. Authority is therefore distributed, redundant, patched, and periodically leaky, like everything else.

There are also those who live at the margins of the city’s permission.

Ravine walkers descend where most people do not. They maintain old pipeways, salvage metal from collapsed lifts, read hoofmarks in mud near aqueduct mouths, and return with stories they do not always share. Their coats are longer, their boots hobnailed, their faces often veiled against mineral spray. Some say they trade with settlements lower down. Some say they leave offerings to whatever moves along the ravine walls at night with unscheduled lamps. They smell of wet fern, rust, and depth.

There are roof-hermits too: old condenser workers, failed inventors, widows of tram accidents, people whose lungs cannot bear the lower damp. They live in legal ambiguities among tanks, pigeonless coops, net towers, and abandoned signal rooms. They keep weather journals. They mend things for neighbors by basket-line. Children believe they can speak directly to fog.

But humans are only the loudest inhabitants.

The moss lives there with intention, or something close enough to intention that the distinction has become academic. It is not a single organism but a citizenship of greens, blacks, silvers, ochres, and pale blue films, each with its own appetites. Some are insulation, some warning, some slow destruction disguised as softness. The people name them with affectionate caution: handmoss, rail felt, widow’s velvet, pipehair, bitter green, switch-rot, sleepmoss, old-touch. Moss grows where breath gathers. It thickens along thresholds, under sleeping-room eaves, beside benches where workers rest. In houses inhabited by quarrelsome families it sometimes yellows at the seams. In abandoned houses it waits, thin and dusty, as if refusing to waste itself on emptiness.

Fungi occupy the hidden city.

They live under slate covers, in cellar beds, behind warm walls, in the damp seams of old stone, in archives despite all vigilance, in the lungs of the careless, in carefully tended edible mats that feed whole districts. Some are cultivated like livestock. Some are feared like fire. The drying houses smell of them: nutty, loamy, medicinal, slightly sweet. The best fungal keepers can tell by touch whether a bed is hungry, fevered, contaminated, or ready. They speak of strains as if speaking of temperamental relatives.

The trees are citizens of another pace.

Low orchard trees bend under nets, their bark blackened by cold and lichen. They flower cautiously. They fruit sourly. Their roots know the retaining walls better than masons do. A tree that has held a terrace for eighty years is not cut down merely because it no longer bears. It may be braced, fed, hollowed into a shrine, threaded with condensation line, used as a living post for laundry or bee boxes. In some districts, family descent is reckoned not by surname but by tree: those of the Third Plum Shelf, those under the Bent Pear, those of the Five Apples Above the Tram Turn.

The bees are dark, furred, and half-domestic. They live in insulated hives mounted near warm pipes and are released only during certain hours, when the cloud lifts or the gardens steam. Their keepers wear felt masks and carry little heated stones in pockets to revive chilled workers. The bees make bitter honey from alpine blossoms, moss flowers, herb beds, and whatever strange sweetness gathers near leaking condensers. Their hum is one of the few summer sounds that can make a whole court fall silent with pleasure.

The moths come in damp afternoons: soft-bodied, grey, brown, or moon-pale, clinging to workshop glass and the undersides of bridges. Children collect their shed wing-dust on black paper. Beetles emerge from stone joints and vanish. Mice live carefully in granaries, never quite numerous enough to become plague, never absent enough to be forgotten. Cats, if they exist, are rare, prized, and mostly decorative, too cold-footed and offended by damp to be reliable hunters.

The ash-colored fox-creatures are not owned by anyone. They appear at thresholds of industry, especially before changes in weather. Long-legged, narrow-faced, with ears too large for their heads and eyes like wet iron, they move as though listening through their paws. No one kills them. Seeing one near a cistern is a bad sign. Seeing one cross a tramline before dawn means the day will be mild. Seeing one look into a house is said to mean someone inside has been called by the ravine.

And there are the machines.

Not alive in the way a goat is alive, nor in the way moss is alive, but not dead either. Machines there sleep, wake, cough, resist, accept coaxing, remember mistreatment, and develop preferences. A turbine that has turned under the same district for a century acquires a personality no engineer will officially acknowledge and no engineer will ignore. Some motors start only if warmed in a particular sequence. Some valves need to be tapped twice, then left alone. A tram known as Low Seven hums differently when carrying mourners. A belt drive in the old weaving hall stops every year on the same date until someone oils the bearing and says the name of the woman who lost her hand there.

Small household machines also gather intimacy. Kettle-pumps, lamp regulators, drying fans, window winches, boot warmers, gauge clocks: each is patched, cursed, praised, inherited. The people’s relationship with them is practical devotion. They do not imagine the machines benevolent. They imagine them companionable under correct conditions.

Deeper still, the stone itself lives by refusing to be inert.

It does not move quickly enough to satisfy ordinary definitions. But it sweats iron, holds cold, misleads compasses, delays water, releases seepage in response to sound. It hums faintly under pressure halls. It remembers erosion patterns older than roads and persuades new streets to follow them. Families who have lived there longest are said to resemble the stone: not hard exactly, but slow to declare, slow to abandon, able to bear weather without pretending not to feel it.

Then there are the things reflected in the civic pool.

No one agrees what they are. The archive has records; the priests, if they are priests, have prohibitions; the maintenance crews have anecdotes; children have the clearest descriptions and are not believed after puberty.

When fog is heavy and the shallow pool on the black platform goes still, it reflects a darker sky than the one overhead. In that dark, slow lights move. Sometimes they arrange themselves like tram signals. Sometimes like windows on a descending lift. Sometimes like eyes seen through water. Those who keep night watch say that if a lamp is lowered near the pool, the reflected lamp arrives a moment late. If a name is spoken too clearly, ripples form from below.

No one worships the pool openly. But people lower their voices there. Newly appointed water officers stand beside it before taking their keys. Children who recover from lung fever are brought to its edge and allowed to drop in a bead of honey. Old machinists sometimes sit near it when they know they are dying, listening to the city breathe through stone.

Perhaps what lives there most fully is not any species or guild, but a form of attention.

It occupies humans, moss, pipes, trees, machines, water, and stone alike. It is the habit of noticing before acting. The people listen before opening valves. Plants find warmth by fractions. Water waits in grooves until vibration frees it. Machines wake when pressure permits. Fox-creatures appear where change is about to happen. Even decay proceeds with deliberation, becoming use before ruin.

So the inhabitants are patient, but not passive. They are damp, durable, ingenious, wary, and tender toward continuance. They know how to live with little flame, little sun, little certainty. They trust redundancy more than promise. They prefer a repaired thing to a new one unless the new thing has already proven it can endure fog.

They are a city of listeners: to pipes, to roots, to weather, to old stone, to each other’s breathing on the stairs.

And when evening comes, they gather themselves into lamplit rooms and sheltered courts. A woman unwinds wet cable fiber by a stove. A boy counts drops from a roof gutter into a measuring cup. A tram mechanic eats mushroom broth with blackened hands. Bees sleep in their heated hives. Moss thickens invisibly along a handrail used all day. A turbine turns below, not loudly, but enough. In high windows, lamps appear one by one through the greenish dim.

They live there as if the world is not ending, but being maintained.
