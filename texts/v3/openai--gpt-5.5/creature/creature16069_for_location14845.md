What lives there is not a people, but a set of habits that have learned to use bodies.

The largest inhabitant is the city itself.

Not as a mind, not exactly, and not as a beast hidden under the paving, but as a distributed, mineral organism whose body is basin, wall, vent, street, and powder. Its metabolism is pressure. Its sleep is contraction. Its growth is the hardening of vapor into ribs and panes. It eats its own fallen edges, draws them back into dust, seam, crust, and extrusion, and forgets no material for long.

It has no heart, unless the sealed well in the central square can be called one. It has no breath, unless the nightly loosening of metallic vapor counts. It has no memory, unless repeated form is memory: the doorways, the niches, the steps, the empty rooms arranged around spaces where no one has ever stood.

The city is alive in the way frost is alive on glass, but older in intention and slower in appetite. It wants edges. It wants intervals. It wants corridors that narrow and then open. It wants voids separated by thin partitions. It wants, above all, to continue making interiors.

The smaller lives use those interiors without ever needing to understand them.

Closest to the stone are the black-green crusts.

They are the city’s first vegetation, if vegetation is the right word for growths that neither drink nor root in soil. They lie in shaded angles like soot deposited by an invisible fire. Their surfaces are matte from a distance, but up close they are intricate: scales, pits, minute glassy hairs, dark plates overlapping like armor. They grow where charge pools, where trace gases linger, where one mineral surface has just begun to disagree with another.

They are patient things. A patch may take a season to cross the width of a hand. It may spend weeks motionless, then suddenly thicken overnight into a raised, enamel-like ridge. Some patches remain flat and scriptlike, especially around the dry well, arranging themselves in radial bands and branching marks. Others lift themselves into brittle fronds, black on one side, green-black on the other, each frond edged with silver dust. These upright forms are the nearest the basin comes to leaves.

They do not turn toward light. Light is almost incidental to them. They turn toward differences: a colder wall, a warmer seam, a change in static tension, the faint metallic taste of a new exhalation from below. When the city tightens at night, the crusts flatten. When the vapor comes, they open their pores—not wet pores, not breathing pores, but mineral gates—and take in what they can.

They are not softening the city. They are annotating it.

The glass reeds are stranger.

They stand in the stillest alleys, rooted in faults and seams, arranged sometimes in sparse rows like abandoned instruments. They are hollow and transparent, and their walls are so thin that a stronger wind, if such a thing existed there, would probably splinter them. But the air does not trouble them. They endure by being nearly absent.

Their roots are not roots but clamps of clear mineral fiber sunk into charged cracks. Through these they gather tension from the plates beneath the city. During the coldest hours the charge climbs them. It appears first as a faint violet thread near the base, then forks upward, dividing inside the hollow stem. For a moment each reed contains a map of lightning too quiet to happen. The branching tips brighten. The alley fills with a color that casts no true shadow.

Then it is gone.

The reeds feed other things indirectly. They change the charge of the air. They wake dormant crusts. They draw mica-grazers into their alleys. They mark safe passages for the gliders that move at dusk. A reed-bed is not a forest, but it is a neighborhood. More life passes there than elsewhere, though even there the city remains mostly empty.

The mica-grazers are the commonest animals, which means one may see three in a long day.

They are flat, translucent, and jointed along lines that look less grown than scored. When pressed against a wall they become almost impossible to distinguish from the mineral sheets around them. Their bodies catch the dim light in panes. Their organs, if they can be called organs, are milky flecks suspended between layers. Sometimes a faint granular pulse moves through them after feeding, as if powder is being sorted by invisible combs.

They have many legs, but the legs are not arranged like those of insects or crabs. They unfold from the underside only when needed, thin and angular, each ending in a dry pad that grips by static rather than pressure. They do not tap, scrape, or click. Even when they climb vertical walls, the sound is no more than the idea of a sound.

They graze the crusts in shallow arcs. A feeding trail looks like a clean crescent cut through black enamel. The grazer lowers its front edge, seals itself to the growth, and abrades the surface with a mouth too fine to see. It takes only the charged upper layer, leaving the deeper crust alive. In this way it prunes more than destroys. Old grazing grounds often return denser, darker, more strongly patterned.

If startled, a mica-grazer does not flee. Flight belongs to wetter worlds. Instead it folds.

Its panels collapse along prepared hinges. Its legs tuck away. Its body kinks into an irregular shard, a broken plate among broken plates. The change is instant and almost contemptuous. A predator may pass over it. A drifting sheet of dust may half-cover it. Hours later, if the charge is right, the shard opens and continues feeding.

The larger hunters are the dusk gliders.

They are seen mostly as absences passing across pale walls.

Their bodies are narrow, no thicker than a wrist, but long—sometimes longer than a person’s armspan. Their skins are ash-white above and smoke-gray below, mottled with translucent patches that mimic openings in mineral walls. Between their limbs stretch cold-stiffened membranes, not soft wings but tense sheets like oiled glass or thin mica. They do not flap often. They launch from towers, fall without haste, and make use of the basin’s faint thermal differences, sliding from one layer of cool air to another.

Their heads are wedge-shaped and nearly blind-looking. Sight, for them, is not merely image but temperature shadow. They hunt the mica-grazers by the interruption a body makes against a wall’s slow radiation. A folded grazer is almost safe; a moving one is bright as a shout.

The gliders make no cry. Their mouths open only when striking. Inside are combs of pale mineral teeth, suited less for tearing than for separating layers. They take a grazer against a wall, pin it with two forelimbs, and peel it apart silently. What they cannot digest they drop as clear flakes, which later join the dust.

They nest in upper tower holes where the mineral skins are warmest after dusk. Their nests are not woven. They are accreted. The glider returns to the same aperture, exudes a faint reactive powder from glands along its belly, and lets the city build around it. Over time, a shallow cup forms—a cradle of altered salt-glass, brittle but exactly fitted to the body that rests there. If the tower grows, the nest may be sealed inside it. Some towers contain old nests like fossils of intention, empty chambers shaped by vanished animals and continued by stone.

There are lesser lives too, almost too slight to call creatures.

In the dust live angle-colonies: shifting films of microscopic mineral feeders that move not by crawling but by changing cohesion. They make certain drifts sharpen overnight. They sort grains by charge, by taste, by crystalline ancestry. When a wall opens a hairline crack, they enter first, lining the new seam with a faint gray bloom. Later, crusts may follow. Later still, a reed may find the place. The angle-colonies are the city’s smallest surveyors.

There are pale seam-worms, though they are not worms. They inhabit the thin darkness between plates, bodies no wider than threads and sometimes several body-lengths long. They travel through cracks that seem sealed, digesting volatile salts and leaving behind smoother channels. Once in a long while one emerges by mistake into open air, curls into a spiral, and hardens almost at once into a white pinwheel.

There are hollow mites inside the reeds, transparent specks that drift up and down with charge changes. They clean the inner walls of the stems, feeding on mineral films that would otherwise cloud the violet discharge. During a release they freeze in place, each mite becoming a bright bead in the branching current. Afterward they resume their slow vertical migrations.

Around the dry well lives the densest and oldest community.

The crusts there are thick, arranged in radial bands, so dark they seem to drink the dimness. Mica-grazers approach the well cautiously, often pausing at the rim as if at a boundary. The charge is stronger there. The air tastes metallic even when the vent is dormant. Glass reeds near the square grow shorter and more branched, like transparent antlers. Their discharges are not violet but blue-white.

At night, when the city exhales, the well’s rim becomes crowded by things usually hidden.

Seam-worms extend from cracks, tasting vapor. Folded grazers open one panel at a time. Dust films draw themselves into branching deltas. The black crusts lift their fronds and expose green-black undersides veined with silver. From the tower mouths, gliders watch without calling.

For a little while, the central square is almost inhabited in the ordinary sense. Bodies face inward. Forms gather around a center. No voice passes between them, but exchanges occur: charge, vapor, trace minerals, heat. The city’s old imitation of a meeting place becomes, briefly, a meeting place.

And beneath all of them, or among all of them, the city continues to make rooms.

This is what binds the lives there together: none of them truly oppose the architecture. They are not intruders in a dead ruin. They are participants in a mineral weather that happens to resemble settlement. The crusts darken walls and teach grazers where to move. The grazers prune the crusts and carry their dust on silent feet. The gliders thin the grazers and seed towers with altered powders. The reeds conduct charge that awakens chemical skins. The angle-colonies prepare seams. The city absorbs every husk, every flake, every abandoned nest, and builds with it.

There are no citizens, but there are occupations.

The crusts listen.

The reeds conduct.

The grazers erase.

The gliders subtract.

The dust remembers shape.

The city encloses.

Nothing there lives warmly. Nothing there hurries. Life in the basin is not lushness but precision: a tendency to persist at the edge of chemistry, to make use of cold, silence, dryness, and fracture. Its creatures are not adapted to absence; they are made of it. They need the lack of water, the muffled air, the unwettable surfaces, the dim light softened by ash. Bring rain there and it would be catastrophe. Bring a river and it would be fire by another name.

So whoever lives in the city lives thinly, angularly, almost invisibly.

They live in cracks rather than burrows, on charge rather than sap, by folding rather than fleeing, by brightening rather than singing. They leave marks that resemble writing but are only feeding, rooms that resemble dwellings but are only growth, gatherings that resemble ceremony but are only the nightly negotiation of vapor and cold.

And yet, from far above, if anything could watch the basin through the ash veil, it might see a settlement after all: dark patches tended by pale animals, towers occupied at dusk, alleys lit from within by violet reeds, the central square waking in blue silence.

A city with no builders.

A city with inhabitants that do not know they are inhabitants.

A city alive enough to make room for more.
