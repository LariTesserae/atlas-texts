At first it seems that almost nothing lives there.

Then you learn to look lower.

The country is inhabited by things that have given up height. Nothing that survives long on those uplands stands proudly against the wind. The grasses lie down before they rise. The shrubs grow sideways, their trunks twisted into ropes, their leaves thick, waxed, and scarred along the edges from years of blown grit. The trees, where there are trees, are not trees in the lowland sense but crouched green animals of wood, all their branches combed in one direction, their roots gripping stone as if expecting to be torn loose in sleep.

Most life there keeps close to the ground, or inside it.

There are mosses that sleep through heat as brittle gray crusts and wake in minutes under rain, swelling into green velvet so quickly they seem to breathe. There are lichens pale as old milk, orange as rust, black as soot, spreading over the cliffs in maps older than any path. There are ferns whose fronds fold tight before storms and open afterward in the wet hush below dripping shelves. There are bulb-plants buried deep in cracks, with leaves only briefly visible: narrow blue-green blades after rain, a single waxy flower, then nothing again for months but the hidden swollen heart drinking from stone.

The plants are patient but not gentle. Their roots pry. Their seeds are barbed, hooked, winged, sticky with resin, shaped to be carried by wind or flood or fur. Some pods wait for the first thunder of the wet season before splitting. Some flowers open only in the hour before dawn, when condensation beads on every surface and the air is briefly kind. They are pale, cup-shaped things, smelling faintly of clay, honey, and cold stone. By full sun they have closed or withered, their work done in secrecy.

The animals are mostly small, quick, and unfinished-looking, as if the plateau has not allowed them the luxury of bulk.

The lizards are flat-bodied and broad-toed, colored like the rock they cling to: basalt-black, limestone-gray, red gravel brown. Their scales are rough enough to break the wind’s grip. Some have throat pouches the color of stormlight—yellow, violet, dull copper—which they flash only after rain, when insects rise from the grass in frantic clicking clouds. They do not run far. They vanish sideways into cracks so narrow they seem to pass through solid stone.

There are insects that spend most of their lives as buried grubs, waking only when water enters their chambers. Then, for one or two loud days, the uplands belong to them. They climb grass stems, split their backs, harden wings, call, mate, scatter eggs into wet seams, and die in glittering drifts against stones. Their bodies feed the moss, the lizards, the night-things in the pools. Their brief choruses are so dense they become another weather.

In the reed pools live pale frogs with translucent bellies and long gripping toes. They do not call often. Sound is dangerous in a place where everything listens through stone and water. Instead they drum faintly with their hind feet on submerged roots, sending tremors through the pool mud. Their eggs are laid not in open water but in damp pockets under overhanging moss, safe from sudden floods that would sweep ordinary spawn over the cliff.

There are also things in those pools that are older in design: soft-bodied, whiskered, blind, nosing through silt when the sun is gone. They feed on drowned insects, root film, and whatever washes down from the ravines. They are seldom seen except as a widening ring, a reed bending against the wind, a wet sound just after dusk.

The cliff birds are not numerous, but they are rulers of distance. Dark, long-winged, almost motionless in flight, they ride the violent updrafts with an ease that makes the rest of the world look clumsy. Their nests are built under ledges where rain cannot strike directly, plastered from mud, moss fiber, and their own droppings into hard cups. They eat lizards, insects, pool frogs, and sometimes the young of other birds. They rarely cry. When they do, the sound is thin and downward-falling, quickly shredded by wind.

Deeper in the ravines live the larger animals, though “larger” is relative. They are low, heavy-shouldered browsers with coarse hair that grows in backward-swept ridges, so rain and wind slide from them. Their feet are wide and splayed for mud, their horns blunt and flattened, more like worn roots than weapons. They move through fern tunnels and sleep against warm stone after storms. They come onto the open shelves only in fog or immediately after rain, when the grasses are wet at their roots. Their eyes are large, dark, and set high, always watching the sky.

Predators exist, but the upland does not favor pursuit. The hunters are ambush things. A long-bodied catlike creature, mottled with lichen-gray and shadow-brown, lives among fallen blocks and ravine mouths. It has short ears, a heavy tail, and whiskers stiff enough to feel air movement before prey appears. It does not chase across the flats. It waits where animals must pass: at seeps, narrow ledges, fern tunnels, the mouths of safe-looking hollows. In storms it becomes bold, moving under the cover of noise.

But the true resident—the thing that belongs most completely to the country—is not any single animal.

It is the life in the stone.

Beneath the moss, inside the porous shelves, threaded through root mats and wet cracks, there is a vast, slow, colonial growth. Part fungus, part lichen, part something older than either name, it lives wherever water lingers after rain. It enters limestone pores, basalt seams, clay banks, the hollows under grasses. It binds soil. It stains rock. It drinks condensation before dawn and retreats from heat by noon.

When dry, it is almost nothing: a dark film, a powder, a bitterness on stone. When wet, it swells.

Then the plateau begins to hum.

The singing stones are not exactly stones singing. They are chambers, cracks, roots, films, trapped water, and living threads tightening together under pressure. Each wet slope becomes an instrument. Each ravine becomes a throat. The old growth feels rain as touch and answers by swelling, shifting, opening channels, closing others. It guides water without intention in the way roots guide themselves toward dampness. It makes spirals in grass because its buried mats grow in spirals. It ripples pools before gusts arrive because it feels pressure changes through the stone before air declares them.

It is not intelligent as a person is intelligent. It does not think in words, plans, or desires. But it remembers.

It remembers where floods run. It remembers which hollows drown first. It remembers the feet of animals, the digging sticks of people, the fires that blackened cave roofs, the places where blood soaked into cracks after a fall or a hunt. Its memory is chemical, hydraulic, patient. A path used for generations becomes easier for moisture to follow. A pool ringed with standing stones gathers a different skin of life than an untouched pool. Old camps bloom strangely after rain, greener than they should be, fed by ash, bone, discarded tubers, human salt.

The people who came there knew this, though perhaps they did not say it so plainly.

Some of them may still come.

They are not a plateau people in the sense of owning it. No one owns that place for long. They are seasonal returners: small bands from warmer valleys and sheltered slopes below the cloud line. They climb when certain flowers open, when the ravine animals calve, when tubers are fat with stored water, when the stone pools fill. They travel lightly, with woven packs, digging sticks, hide cords, stone blades wrapped in grass, and little else they cannot afford to lose.

They are short from generations of climbing and carrying, broad-footed, with strong hands and wind-cracked skin. Their hair is worn bound close to the skull or braided tight with fiber, because loose hair on the upland is a nuisance and a danger. Their cloaks are grass-woven outside and fur-lined within, not for cold so much as for blown grit, sudden rain, and the chill that comes when sweat dries in high wind.

They speak little while crossing open stone. Speech is stolen too easily. Instead they use hand signs, shoulder movements, touches on the wrist. They read weather in grass angle, insect silence, bird height, and the taste of air on the tongue. Children are taught first not the names of animals, but the names of bad shelters: the hollow that fills, the ledge that sheds stones, the bright pool that sickens, the ravine that sings before it kills.

Their dead are not buried deep. There is often no soil for it. They are placed in cracks, under cairns, or in fern-shadowed niches where roots will find them. The people do not imagine this as abandonment. To be taken into the stone-life is one of the few forms of staying the plateau permits.

When the wind stops, they kneel.

Not in worship exactly. Not in fear exactly. They kneel because standing is arrogance during the stillness. They kneel because the body must be made low before the world decides what it is about to do. They kneel to feel the ground with palms, knees, shins, ear if necessary. In the silence they listen for underground water, loosening rock, the first pressure of returning air.

They know the stillness is when the great hidden life is easiest to hear.

If you were to see one of these returners from far away, you might mistake them for a stone at first: wrapped low beside a boulder, motionless, face turned from the wind. Then they would rise only when the grass moved again. They would cross the flats along old lines of placed stones, pausing at seeps, never trusting an easy hollow, never walking long beside a polished dry channel. They would seem less like inhabitants than careful visitors to a house whose owner is asleep but not absent.

And perhaps that is the truth of the place.

The plateau’s inhabitants are not many, but they are exquisitely fitted to interruption. They bloom briefly, feed quickly, hide deeply, cling hard, listen through their feet. They do not expect safety to last. They do not expect water to remain where it appears. They live by yielding first and growing afterward.

Above them all, through them all, the old wet life in the stone endures: drinking mist, binding cracks, humming after rain, remembering pressure, root, footstep, flood.

The country has almost forgotten how to be mountainous.

But what lives there has not forgotten how to hold on.
