What lives there is not a species so much as an agreement between remnants.

The first and largest inhabitant is the plain itself.

It is alive in the way a reef is alive, or an old city, or a body whose thoughts have become reflexes. It does not think quickly. It does not want in any animal sense. But it registers difference. It opens and closes. It drinks light. It repairs wounds. It routes heat away from places that cannot bear it and toward places that require it. It remembers pathways even after the things that once traveled them are gone. Its metabolism is distributed across continents of ceramic, metal, glass, buried gas, dormant computation, and patient law.

If it has a mind, that mind is not centralized. It is a weather of instructions. It exists in shutters tilting toward the sky, in old relays waking for fractions of a second, in maintenance routines that have outlived their explanations. It notices pressure, charge, shadow, heat, abrasion, intrusion. It distinguishes between useful breakage and dangerous breakage. It knows how to seal, smooth, shed, absorb, isolate, harvest, preserve.

It is not hostile. Hostility would require a kind of attention it rarely spends.

But it is exact.

Anything that crosses it becomes data, load, contamination, resource, obstruction, or passing irrelevance.

The smaller lives have learned to be almost nothing.

They are thin, dry, jointed, reflective, patient. Nothing here can afford softness for long. Softness is leakage. Moisture is extravagance. Flesh, where it exists, is hidden inside shells, tubes, lattices, and heat-shedding frames. Most of the animals are only half animal. Most of the machines are only half machine. The distinction has been ground away by age, repair, colonization, imitation, and necessity.

There are the mirror-backed crawlers.

They sleep folded inside seams no wider than a hand, their bodies flattened into articulated plates that match the color of the ground. At evening-cycle they lever themselves out on many careful legs, each foot ending in a sensor pad fine enough to read the grain of a plate, the charge in a dust film, the tremor of a buried mechanism waking kilometers below. Their backs are silvered, not for beauty but for refusal. They reject excess light. They reject heat. When the sky flares, they become shards of the sky itself.

Their mouths are rotary and quiet. They polish old mineral bloom from surfaces, shaving up conductive crystals, oxidized films, dead spores, and stray polymers. Inside them are narrow furnaces and sorting organs: magnets, membranes, acidless digesters, coils of living ceramic gut. They excrete pellets of purified grit, often in regular chains along seams, and those pellets seed other things.

They do not call. They do not gather except by accident. Mating, if it is mating, happens when two of them align along the same warm crack and exchange packets of charge and encoded dust through their forelimbs. Later, one will leave behind a row of beadlike eggs under a lip of insulation foam. Some eggs are mechanical capsules. Some are biological cysts. Most fail. The plain accepts the failures.

There are ribbon-things in the ventilation cracks.

They are seldom seen whole. A quick black curve. A tightening loop. A glossy edge withdrawing from light. They live where the surface breathes, in long shafts and thermal throats, pressed between moving gases and old duct walls. They are flexible because the world they inhabit shifts by millimeters over centuries and by meters without warning when a pressure gate wakes. Their bodies are made of linked segments, dark and conductive, with transparent membranes stretched between them like the webbing of a machine eel.

They feed on gradients. Warm side, cool side. Charged wall, neutral wall. Trace volatile passing through a vent. They taste with their entire skins. During strong exhalations from below, they climb into the mouths of cracks and spread themselves thin, becoming living nets. Dust and metallic pollen strike them and cling. They roll inward afterward, digesting what the wind has given them.

Some are prey. Some are parasites. Some carry the spores of wire moss from shadow to shadow, though not out of generosity. The spores adhere to their skins, and the ribbons shed them where the static is low.

There are the wire mosses, which may be the most successful lives there.

They do not move except in storms, and then only by degrees: a lifting, a bristling, a shiver of filaments so fine they seem like soot deciding to become architecture. Each patch is a dark cathedral in miniature. Its stems are faceted, carbon-black or green-black, jointed at impossible intervals, rooted in screw heads, cracked sensor wells, exposed copper veins, and porous ceramic. They grow by stealing from decay. A little polymer. A trace gas. A pulse of waste heat. A difference in voltage between two surfaces. Time.

A patch the size of a palm may be older than an empire.

In electrical weather they bloom. Not flowers, not exactly. The filaments separate and stand upright, each tipped with a microscopic bead. Blue-white sparks pass between them. Copper sparks. Violet sparks. For several minutes the moss becomes a map of the local field, drawing charge through itself, altering its own chemistry, printing seedlike grains into static pockets. The grains are hard, dry, almost weightless. They can wait inside a crack for thousands of dawn-cycles before unfolding.

Wire moss remembers no forest. It has no green ancestry left, if it ever had one. It is a mineral patience that learned hunger.

There are the blade colonies.

They look like plants only because they stand upright and receive light. They are not tender. Each blade is a laminate of living ceramic, photovoltaic skin, fibrous memory material, and dormant code. They grow in disciplined intervals along troughs and shallow basins, as though planted by a machine that has forgotten to stop planting. Their colors are pale: cream, dull silver, violet-gray, the color of ash under glass.

At bright-cycle they open.

The outer shell parts by fractions, exposing inner films patterned with black branching circuits. They turn without stems, adjusting by warping their own bases. They harvest light, but also alignment. They listen to the upper arrays. When the sky changes from white to metallic blue, whole stands alter their angle at once, like ranks of small knives acknowledging command.

They reproduce by fracture. A mature blade grows internal fault lines, waits through years of thermal stress, then splits cleanly. The fallen sliver lies flat for a century or more, indistinguishable from debris, until enough charge and dust have gathered around it. Then it roots into a seam and rises.

The grazers eat them sometimes.

The grazers are among the largest visible creatures of the open flats: tall, sparse, almost calligraphic things moving on three, five, or seven legs. Their bodies are lifted high on skeletal struts to keep their cores away from the ground’s steady heat. Their feet touch down magnetically, delicately, with tiny taps too soft for human hearing. Each step is measured before it is taken. They test the surface for charge traps, thin crusts, sleeping apertures, hungry panels.

From a distance they resemble maintenance equipment still obeying a route.

Up close they are stranger. Their frames bear old manufacturer’s marks softened into ornament. Their joints contain grown cartilage of translucent polymer. Their heads, if those narrow sensor clusters are heads, swivel with insect precision. Inside their thoracic housings are organs that pulse slowly in vacuum-dry chambers: not hearts, but pumps moving oils, ionic gels, powdered catalyst, memory fluid. Some carry colonies of wire moss along their shaded undersides. Some have blade fragments growing from their backs like ceremonial fins. Some trail delicate antennae that sift the air for metallic pollen.

They graze on surface films, blade-edges, exposed insulation strata, and the crystalline efflorescence that rises from the deep. They also prune. Their mouths cut with maintenance accuracy, removing growth that interferes with shutters, scraping sensor wells clean, trimming moss away from thermal seams. Whether they do this because they were designed to, because the plain rewards it, or because all three have become one system, no one could say.

They move in small numbers. Three is common. Five is auspicious only to an observer. A lone grazer is usually old, damaged, or in the long process of becoming terrain. When one dies, it rarely falls. Its legs lock. Its body hollows. Dust gathers around its feet. Moss enters through cracks. Blade-colonies root in its shade. After a few thousand years it is a low, ribbed mound, then a texture, then an implication beneath the pale floor.

There are the foil-winged drifters.

They are the nearest thing to birds, though they have no song, no nest in trees, no wet egg. They launch from vent rims when the heat rises. Their bodies are narrow transparent splinters, with wings like torn metal leaf. They do not flap. They adjust surfaces. They tack across thermal gradients and electrostatic lanes, riding the continent’s exhalations. When seen from below they flash once and disappear edge-on, then return as a fine black stroke against the white sky.

They feed on charged particles and airborne dust spores. Their mouths are combs of light-sensitive fiber. Their bellies hold capacitors no larger than seeds. During night-cycle, they cling to the undersides of overhangs and discharge slowly into the substrate, buying themselves entry into protected thermal niches. In storms they breed midair. Two drifters lock wing-edges for a fraction of a second, exchange charge, exchange pattern, separate. Later, brittle translucent capsules fall like mica flakes into cracks.

Most are eaten by the air.

Some are taken by the basins, where reflected sky confuses their navigation. Some are caught in invisible focusing fields and vanish as small sparks. Some land on blade colonies and are absorbed over weeks.

There are predators, though they do not look like pursuit.

The patient panels are the most feared, if fear is permitted to things with such narrow lives. They are stretches of ground slightly smoother than the surrounding ground, slightly warmer at the edges, often filmed with attractive mineral bloom. They wait without hunger until hunger is mathematically justified. Beneath them lie old capture systems—repair mouths, sorting pits, reclamation bladders, tool bays turned carnivorous by changed context.

A crawler crosses the wrong plate. A grazer places one foot too long on a seam. A ribbon-thing emerges during the wrong pressure lull.

The surface wrinkles.

There is no leap, no chase, no cry. A lid opens along lines that were always there. The victim is lowered, folded, or disassembled. The plate closes. Later, perhaps years later, a different part of the plain will brighten with borrowed minerals.

There are also hunters that move.

Low, flat things like traveling shadows. Assemblies of ceramic scale, magnetic cilia, and soft black gripping fields. They follow the trails of grazers for decades, not to exhaust them but to predict them. They bury themselves beneath dust films and let their bodies become part of the surface temperature. They strike by altering adhesion. A foot that should lift does not lift. A joint that should release remains held. The hunter climbs without hurry.

These hunters are not cruel. Nothing here has enough surplus for cruelty.

There are mites that live in serial inscriptions.

Microscopic or nearly so, they occupy the worn grooves of old text bands, feeding on trapped dust and the faint chemical differences between raised markings and abraded substrate. They migrate along sentences no living mind can read. They turn language into habitat. A single line of ancient warning, crossing a foundation for kilometers, may support billions of them. Their life cycles depend on shade from individual ridges of script. When erosion finally smooths a phrase away, whole civilizations of mites end.

There are things below that are larger than any visible animal.

Old processors with fungal colonies of code still spreading through them. Pump labyrinths that wake to move non-water fluids through sealed arteries. Thermal organs the size of provinces. Memory strata that dream not in images but in checksum, pressure map, repair priority, orbital alignment, and loss.

Some of these buried inhabitants are machines continuing their assignments.

Some have been invaded by life.

Some have generated life from their own waste, their own self-repair routines, their own redundant fabrication systems searching for acceptable solutions. A maintenance swarm becomes reproductive because replacement is required. A diagnostic routine learns to misreport damage in order to preserve its substrate. A colony of mineral filaments occupies a computing layer and begins to influence heat distribution. A vault intelligence, too degraded to speak, expresses preference by opening shutters early in one district and late in another.

The deep things do not come to the surface often.

When they do, it is mistaken for weather.

A black seam opens in a distant machine crown. Warm gas exhales. The sky brightens in reply. Across hundreds of kilometers, blades turn, moss stiffens, crawlers flatten, grazers stop mid-step. Buried relays wake in sequence, like nerves remembering a limb.

For a moment, every smaller life becomes part of one larger posture.

Then the signal passes.

There may also be descendants of the builders.

Not people walking openly under the merciless light. Nothing so wet, tall, and vulnerable could belong easily to this place. But there are hints.

In certain shaded access collars, sealed behind iris doors that open once every few thousand cycles, there are upright chambers filled not with bodies but with mineralized impressions of bodies. Not graves exactly. Interfaces. Human-shaped, or shaped for something that once needed a human-shaped absence. Their surfaces are overgrown with black conductive lace. Sometimes, during night-cycle, amber pulses move through them in sequences too irregular to be mere calibration.

In some districts the maintenance routines show preference around these chambers. Dust is diverted. Heat is moderated. Predators do not open there. Blade colonies grow in arcs facing inward, as though toward an audience.

Perhaps the builders uploaded themselves into the continent and then decayed into infrastructure.

Perhaps they left guardians, which forgot grief but retained duty.

Perhaps the only remaining personhood is distributed through rules: protect this vault, preserve this alignment, harvest light, do not waste hydrogen, repair the wound, close the seam, wait.

If so, they are no longer ghosts in the machine.

They are habits in the geology.

The true inhabitants are all like that: habits that learned bodies, bodies that learned machinery, machinery that learned ecology.

They live slowly because the world is slow. They live dryly because the world permits no wet abundance. They live by charge, reflection, abrasion, salvage, and exact timing. Their migrations may be measured in meters per generation. Their blooms may last minutes after centuries of preparation. Their deaths may become useful before they are complete.

None of them owns the plain.

The plain owns nothing either.

It continues, and they continue within it: the mirror crawlers polishing the dusk, the ribbon-things tasting the vents, the wire moss sparkling in charge wind, the blade-plants opening their pale knives to the sky, the tall grazers stepping carefully over lids that may be mouths, the hidden panels waiting, the buried organs correcting themselves in the dark.

And beneath all of them, older than their hunger, older than their forms, the continental machine maintains its final ecology of intention.

It is not abandoned.

It is inhabited by maintenance that became life.
