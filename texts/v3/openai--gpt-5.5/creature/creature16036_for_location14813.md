What lives there is not one species, nor even a kingdom, but a high cold metabolism spread thin and fierce over new stone.

The plateau is inhabited by organisms that have learned not to separate living from working. They are bodies, machines, weather-makers, mineral refiners, batteries, furnaces, herds, traps, and slow thoughts. Nothing there merely survives. Everything participates.

Closest to the stone are the first claimants: the frost-lichens and glass-mosses.

They are older in behavior than they are in age. They arrive almost before the rock has cooled enough to stop steaming. Their spores settle in hairline fractures, silvering the black basalt with dust-fine colonies. At first they seem like stains, but under magnification they are layered cities of cells: mirror plates on top to blunt the sun, black conductive tissues beneath, acid mouths pressed into mineral seams. They eat stone by persuasion, loosening metals molecule by molecule. Some species form circular blooms, pale at the edge and dark at the center, like burns running backward. Others grow in scales, overlapping blue-black armor that clicks in hard wind.

They are not passive groundcover. In sunlight they charge. In shadow they contract. When frost enters the cracks they hold it, guide it, use its expansion to widen their own footholds. Whole ridges are slowly being pried apart by moss no higher than a fingernail.

Above them lie the mats.

The mats are the plateau’s true body.

From a distance they look like vegetation: low, dense, green-black, folded over rock. But to step onto them is to stand on a layered organismal apparatus meters deep. The upper surface is springy with stiff moss, wax leaves, lichens, translucent reeds, and low shrubs with fat antifreeze tissues. Beneath that is brown compressed growth, root-rope, fungal membrane, collapsed stems, animal tunnels, damp chambers, mineral nodules, pockets of trapped air and heat. Beneath that, older still, are the black warm strata where bacteria digest pressure, blood, resin, frost, and stone-dust into usable compounds.

The mats pump.

They draw meltwater sideways, upward, down into basins, across saddles, through living valves that open and close in cold rhythms. They store electricity from basking animals. They separate salts. They grind bone. They seal wounds in themselves with waxy scar tissue. They creep downslope by fractions, not like a plant growing but like a glacier deciding to be alive.

In the basins, these mats rise into low forests: not forests of trunks and canopy, but forests of thickness. Waxy fans overlap like shields. Fern-crowns crouch under the wind. Bladder-vines swell with condensed fog, their surfaces beaded with metallic droplets. Transparent reeds fill with light and glow from within, then darken as grazers pass their shadows over them. Cold-blooming flowers open with a sound like small glass bells. Their petals are not fragile; they are mineralized lenses, angled to split the brutal sun into wavelengths their tissues can use.

Many of the flowers are traps, though slowly. They do not snap shut. They lean. They reorient across days. A nesting swarm may realize, too late, that the grove around it has curved inward and that the shining petals are now a roof.

The small animals are countless.

There are frost-combers: white, low, quick-footed grazers no larger than a hand, with lower jaws divided into many comb teeth. They move in nervous sheets over moss fields at dawn, scraping frost and the sugar-rich films beneath it. Their fur is not fur exactly, but hollow translucent bristle, each strand holding a thread of still air. When alarmed they flatten until they resemble spilled snow. Their predators often pass directly over them.

There are helmet-beetles, dark and copper-seamed, traveling in ranks through mineral lichen. They scrape metallic films from stone and leave bright clean paths behind them. For a few hours, their trails are the only bare places in the world. Then spores settle, frost forms, and the paths vanish. The beetles communicate by tapping their armored abdomens against the rock. On still days whole slopes answer with dry coded rain.

There are prism-flies, though they are not flies. Their bodies are transparent and full of angled internal plates. They gather in shafts of sunlight above warm vents, flashing white, green, violet, white again. They feed on suspended pollen, ice dust, and electrical discharge. When a herd releases stored charge into the root network at dusk, prism-flies descend in glittering spirals to sip the sparks that leak from swollen nodules.

There are thread-keepers: blind many-legged animals that tend acres of adhesive filament strung among shrubs, reed stems, boulders, and root arches. A thread-keeper may never travel more than a few body lengths in its life. Its body is pale, jointed, soft under a crust of frost-resistant plates. It knows the world entirely through vibration. The impact of a seed-wing, the landing of a bird, the slow scrape of a hoof five hundred meters away—all enter its body as touch. It harvests by patience. Things die in its web not because it strikes, but because the plateau does not offer enough spare energy for endless struggle.

Some creatures live partly as weather.

The air-grazers are nearly invisible except when the sun catches their bellies. They are translucent, thin as leaves, ribbed with gas bladders and fine cilia. During bright hours they climb thermal columns rising from dark basalt. They feed on pollen, spores, and mineral aerosols lifted by wind. In cold phases they descend and fold themselves into the mats, becoming wet films under leaves. Some freeze deliberately. Their winter form is a crystalline stain on stone, patterned like fern frost. When condensation returns, the pattern thickens, softens, and becomes animal again.

The reed birds—if birds is the right word—arrive in dense flocks that can erase a basin. They have feathers, but the feathers are also solar vanes, insulation, mating signals, and perhaps respiratory surfaces. Their bodies are heavy and compact; their wings broad enough to ride the violent upland air. They settle over glowing reed beds in such numbers that the reeds disappear beneath a trembling dark pelt. Then, for no visible reason, they rise together. The sound is not flapping but weather tearing loose.

They nest in warmth wherever they find it: in the vents of huge animals, in fungal chimneys, in the lee of smoking mineral cones. Their eggs are mirrored on one side and matte black on the other. The parents rotate them through the day, sun to dark, charge to warmth, warmth to charge.

The larger grazers resemble terrain because resembling terrain is survival.

The kneeling browsers have long jointed necks, small shaded eyes, and backs that host entire gardens. Moss grows in the grooves of their hides. Mites graze that moss. Flowering parasites root behind their shoulder plates. Small birds pick among the parasites. The browser benefits from all of it: insulation, camouflage, chemical defense, social signaling. A healthy adult looks less like an animal than a moving hillside.

They feed by kneeling, anchoring forelimbs deep into the mats, and drawing up vegetation with rasping lateral jaws. They do not chew quickly. The mouth works like a mill. Fibrous growth enters, mineral grit enters, ice enters, beetles sometimes enter, and all of it is sorted in vast cold stomachs where symbiotic vats separate sugar, salts, metals, and usable proteins. Their dung steams and is valuable. Whole beetle guilds follow them. Mats grow thicker along their old circuits.

The heliostats are stranger still.

They are herd animals with hides patterned in dark photovoltaic scales bordered by pale insulation. At noon they stand in ranks over ridges and basin floors, all angled toward the sun. From above they would resemble a field of living panels. They bask not simply for heat but for charge. Beneath the skin, electrical organs accumulate potential in gel chambers. At dusk the herds press their conductive limbs into root beds and discharge. The mats accept the current. Nodules swell faintly. Blue sparks run between stems. In return, the animals feed at night on sugar exudates released from the roots.

Their migration is not driven only by food. It is driven by circuits. Certain basins need them. Certain herds need certain mineral compositions from certain mats. Over generations, their trails become electrical geography.

Then there are the great ones.

Most are first noticed as mistakes in the landscape.

A ridge shifts. A boulder blinks. A low hill opens vents along its flank and releases a white breath that rolls down through moss. These immense animals move so slowly that lichen colonizes them between steps. Some stand rooted for days or weeks, burying their feet into the vegetation mats and joining, temporarily, with the plateau’s subterranean networks. Through symbiotic rootlets they draw sugars, minerals, and information. Through their vents, flocks nest and warm themselves. Through their hides, moss and parasites bloom in seasonal bands.

They may not be entirely separate individuals in the way lowland animals are. A great grazer standing in one basin may share fungal symbionts, electrical charge, alarm chemicals, and reproductive cues with another beyond a ridge. Their calls are hours apart, single infrasonic notes that loosen frost from cliffs. The answer may come the next day. Or the next week.

One kind, the basalt-backed strider, has many paired limbs hidden under a mantle of gray plates. When resting, it resembles a fallen shelf of rock. Its black eyes open along the side, not the front. It feeds on light, root-sap, and mineral-rich mud. Its young begin life as burrowers inside vegetation mats, soft and pale, emerging only after their back plates harden with accumulated metals.

Another, the vent-crowned bulk, is almost sessile for much of its adulthood. It chooses a warm basin, drives rootlike toes deep into the mats, and becomes a living hill. Heat vents open along its spine. Birds nest there. Moss grows there. Thread-keepers anchor webs there. Small grazers shelter in the lee of its body. Every few years it rises and moves, leaving behind a deep warm footprint that becomes a pond, then a thicket, then a little center of weather.

Predators live there too, but the plateau has taught them not to waste motion.

The pale jointed hunters are nearly silent. Their limbs are long, their bodies narrow, their heads low and wedge-shaped, with heat-sensing pits under matte brow plates. They move through thickets by planning each step as if crossing centuries. A hunt may begin when prey enters the same basin. It may end a week later when the prey, following its old feeding circuit, arrives at the place where the hunter has finally positioned itself. The strike is brief, but the predation is mostly geometry.

Other hunters are distributed rather than embodied. A carnivorous mat may sweeten one patch of growth, guiding grazers to feed there again and again. Over days, adhesive root hairs thicken around the favored route. The ground becomes softer, warmer. Then one morning the crust gives way. The animal sinks shoulder-deep into digestive peat, while bladder-plants lean inward and fungal membranes seal the surface above it.

Some predators are plants in the way a glacier is water: technically, but not comfortingly. They fold around colonies by fractions of a degree each day. They grow hooks into sleeping fur. They release narcotic resin that smells like thaw. Their victims do not always appear alarmed. In the cold, a gentle loss of urgency is enough.

Scavengers are everywhere because death is too valuable to leave whole.

When a large animal falls, the plateau enters a long manufacturing season. First come the heat-seekers, soft-bodied under armored shells, pressing themselves into wounds before the body freezes. Then the mineral beetles, removing teeth, plates, bone seams, and photoreceptive scales. Fungi rise as pale ribbons, stripping proteins and laying them in translucent sheets under the mat. Root-mills enter last, grinding dense parts into powder. Nothing simply rots. The body is disassembled into categories.

Oil goes to one guild. Calcium to another. Conductive metals to lichen. Antifreeze proteins to bladder-vines. Gut microbes to warm hollows. Even memory, in a crude chemical sense, may be taken up: parasites carry scent histories from carcass to host, host to mat, mat to herd.

There are parasites that are almost societies.

Some live across many bodies at once. A single colony may exist as threads in a browser’s moss-back, cysts inside frost-combers, chemical nodules in reed roots, and dust-like spores on the feathers of highland flocks. No one body contains the whole organism. It coordinates through exchanged fluids, scent marks, bites, pollen, nesting material, and dung. Its behavior appears as coincidence: a flock choosing one basin, a herd delaying migration, a moss patch flowering early, beetles avoiding a carcass. If this is thought, it is thought without a head.

The plateau may contain many such distributed intelligences. None build tools. None make roads. None stand apart from the ecology to command it. But some processes seem too coordinated to be mere appetite. Fog gardens open vents before herds arrive. Lichen bands thicken along future fracture lines. Swarms descend into root caverns before storms form. Perhaps the whole place senses through pressure, light, charge, and chemistry. Perhaps it only remembers well.

The inhabitants share certain traits.

They are armored against light as much as cold. Eyes are shaded, recessed, duplicated, or replaced by lateral organs that read glare through skin. Surfaces are mirrored, matte, translucent, or deeply black. Many bodies have two faces: one for the sun, one for frost. Leaves glitter above and drink darkness below. Shells reflect at noon and radiate at night. Feathers can close like shutters. Hides carry crests, veils, mineral dust, or living moss to manage the terrible brightness.

They are patient. Even the quick things live within slow systems. A darting grazer follows a feeding trail laid down over centuries. A prism swarm blooms for an hour from larvae that slept three seasons as frost. A predator’s strike is sudden only at the very end. The plateau’s citizens do not hurry because hurry spends warmth, water, and charge.

They are crowded. Almost every body is habitat. Almost every habitat is also a body. A browser carries moss; the moss carries mites; the mites carry spores; the spores carry dormant animals; the animal’s dung feeds beetles; beetles polish stone for lichen; lichen prepares cracks for roots; roots feed the browser. There is no clean outline around an individual. Life there is nested, rented, exchanged.

And they are bright in the dark.

After sunset the plateau’s residents reveal their stored day. Root nodules glow amber under frost. Lichens shine green along cliff bands. Heliostat herds pulse blue at the joints as excess charge drains into the mats. Reed birds open cold lamps along their ribs to signal through fog. Thread webs flash when touched. The great vent-crowned bulks breathe steam lit from within by bacterial heat.

In that night-glow the inhabitants look less like animals and plants than like the visible circuitry of a continent.

But there is no engineer.

What lives there is a civilization without intention, an industry without invention, a wilderness that has become infrastructural by hunger alone. It is made of moss that mines, animals that store sunlight, fungi that refine the dead, herds that wire basins together, predators that think in weeks, giants that serve as hills, and parasites that pass messages through bodies like rumors through a city.

If one stood still long enough, the plateau would begin to include them too.

Spores would silver their boots. Frost-combers would investigate their shadow. Threads would test their warmth. Lichens would taste the minerals in their abandoned tools, if they had any. The mats would feel the pressure of their weight and route moisture around it. A distant ridge would raise its head, not in welcome or threat exactly, but because every presence there becomes part of the work.
