What lives there is mostly small, low, and patient.

The first inhabitants are not creatures one would notice as individuals. They are films: green-brown algae spread thinly over submerged stone, black moss packed into seams, lichens growing so slowly on the wind-dried faces that their lives resemble weathering more than growth. In the clear pools, transparent crustaceans hang in the water like bits of loosened glass. Snails move over the mineral shelves in pale, deliberate arcs, rasping at the slick films and leaving behind cleaner crescents that cloud again by the next season.

The basins hold amphibians, but not loud ones. They are narrow-bodied, cool-skinned things, colored like wet stone: gray, olive, milk-green, rust at the joints. Their eyes sit high and still. They spend much of the day under lips of travertine or in the shadow beneath submerged steps, pressed flat where the water is coldest. Their eggs cling in strings and clusters to mineral shelves, clear beads with dark commas inside, trembling when a spring pulses below them. The young are almost invisible until they move.

There are insects in the warmer hours, but few enough that each one seems purposeful. Small black flies rise above the sedges. Long-legged midges stand on the skin of still pools. Beetles shelter in damp cracks and under mats of moss. Spiders live where dry stone overhangs wet stone, stringing short, practical webs across seams no wider than a hand.

Larger animals pass through rather than own the place. A fox might use the pale upper shelves at dawn, its paws choosing the dry rims with practiced care. A marsh-cat, lean and gray-brown, may hunt among the sunken courts, pausing at pool edges where amphibians breathe under weed. Birds cross overhead or stop briefly: a highland wader probing silt traps, a small cold-country finch picking seeds from sedge heads, once in a while a silent raptor reading the terraces from above as if they were a map.

But the true residents are lower and stranger than those visitors.

They are not tall enough to interrupt the horizon. At first they look like pieces of the place that have learned to hesitate before flowing: flat-backed, pale, damp-bodied beings that lie along ledges, half in water and half out, their outlines broken by mineral crust and mossy staining. Their skins are not smooth exactly. They carry a fine roughness, a lime-softened hide that takes the color of whatever district they inhabit. In the white upper shelves they are chalk-pale and almost translucent at the extremities. In the fog courts they darken, green-black along the spine and throat. Near spring mouths they grow bluish, cold-veined, as if the water below has tinted them from within.

They are shaped by the rules of the plateau.

They stay low. Their limbs splay outward rather than downward, giving them a broad, gripping posture on slick stone. Their fingers and toes are long, webbed only partly, with soft pads that seal briefly to wet surfaces. They can climb the shallow walls of basins, but they do it slowly, with the care of something that knows a fall will echo through bone. Their bellies are pale and sensitive. Their backs are tougher, sometimes ridged with thin mineral plates, not true shells but hardened layers where their bodies have accepted the water’s habit of deposition.

Their heads are wide and blunt. The mouth is modest, usually closed, with a lip that can press to stone and draw food from it: algae, bacterial films, the smallest drifting life caught in mineral scum. They eat like the snails eat, but more thoughtfully, returning to old feeding places after days or weeks, never stripping them bare. Their nostrils seal when submerged. Their eyes are dark, beadlike, and set high enough that a body hidden under a ledge can watch the water-street above it.

They hear poorly in air but exquisitely through stone and water. A falling chip of limestone, a fox’s careful footfall, the sudden rush of a spring changing pressure under a basin floor — all of it reaches them. Their language, if it can be called language, is made of contact: throat-clicks transmitted into wet rock, faint knocks given with the knuckles or tail, pulses made by closing and opening the mouth against a pool’s surface. Much of what they say is not sentence but adjustment: danger above, cold rising, water clouding, eggs ready, channel open, do not cross, come low.

They do not build, because the place builds continuously and better than they could. They do not cut doors or stack walls. Instead they inherit temporary rooms from water. A shelf undercut by seepage becomes a nursery. A cracked rim becomes a sleeping line. A sunken court with late fog becomes a summer gathering place. When mineral accretion narrows a channel, they remember it. When frost opens a new passage, they use it. Their knowledge is not architectural but hydrological. They know where water will be tomorrow by how it tastes today.

They live in small dispersed groups, though they are not solitary. A group may occupy a chain of basins connected by narrow runnels and hidden underflows. They keep close to spring mouths in winter, where the water remains liquid and slightly warmer than the ice-edged shelves. In brighter seasons they spread outward into the shallow terraces, grazing, mating, following insect hatches, attending to eggs, or lying motionless in the clean glare with only their eyes above water.

Their young are soft, quick, and nearly transparent. They begin life fast by the standards of the plateau, darting through algae fur and silt threads, hiding in the angles of submerged stone. As they age, they slow. Their bodies thicken. Their backs roughen. They acquire stains from districts, scars from frost-shattered edges, mineral pale along the limbs. The oldest among them are difficult to distinguish from ledges until they open their eyes.

They have no fire. Fire would be absurd here, a feverish visitor with nowhere to take hold. They have no metal, no pottery, no lamps. They keep no dry stores. Their possessions are routes, sheltered hollows, memorized spring rhythms, and certain stones that sound properly when tapped. A resonant ledge may be used for gatherings over many generations. A sheltered basin where eggs survive the late frosts may be guarded with quiet ferocity. A crack that leads beneath a terrace to a warmer underflow may be known to only one lineage.

They mark little. Sometimes they smear mineral paste along the edge of a channel, a pale stroke that lasts until the next overflow. Sometimes they arrange small flat stones at the mouth of a hidden runnel, not as a wall but as a sign to those who know how water should sound there. Their marks are made to be erased. Permanence belongs to the plateau, not to them.

Their dead are given to water. A body is placed in a slow basin or under a mineral lip where springs pass over it. Flesh goes first to insects, crustaceans, bacteria, and the quiet mouths of kin. Bone remains longer, furred with lime. In time it becomes part of the shelf, a faint pale complication inside the stone. The living do not visit graves, exactly. They visit flow.

They are cautious with visitors. A fox sends them under ledges. A bird-shadow flattens them instantly. Heavy footsteps from an unfamiliar animal make whole courts empty at once, bodies sliding soundlessly into cloudy pools. But they are curious in their slow way. If something stands still long enough, they will watch it from cracks and waterline hollows. If it returns without harm, they may come closer over days, then weeks. Their trust is accretive, like travertine.

They do not see the place as a city, though their lives give it the feeling of one. To them it is a body of passages: cold veins, blind pockets, breathing walls, shelves that grow, hollows that remember. They know districts by taste and pressure. The upper shelves taste bright and thin. The fog courts taste of old leaves and black moss. The causeway ridges taste of hidden seepage. The collapsed terraces taste unstable after rain. The spring mouths taste of depth.

At evening they become more visible, though only slightly. As mist rises and the hard edges of stone soften, they climb from the basins to the damp rims. One lies along a parapet with its chin on its hands. Another moves through a water-street, leaving a widening V behind it. Several gather around a shallow shelf where eggs shine under the surface. Their throats pulse. Now and then one taps the stone, and somewhere beyond a wall another answers.

They are not many. The plateau could never feed multitudes. Their lives are measured, interrupted, careful, and long. They belong to scarcity without seeming deprived by it. They have taken the instruction of the place completely into themselves:

stay low, hold fast, waste nothing, remember water.
