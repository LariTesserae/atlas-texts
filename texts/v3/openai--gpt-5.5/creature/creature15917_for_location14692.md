What lives there is not a people, but a pressure-born multitude: a society of soft mouths, blind feelers, filtering throats, hinged shells, coiled hunger, eggs in jelly, and bodies so small they make weather by moving.

They are citizens only because they share the chamber and have learned its routes.

Most of them are pale, or transparent, or the color of old water. Color is expensive here. Pattern is almost useless. Many have given themselves over to translucence: glass-bodied larvae with black pinheads; water fleas bright as splinters of mica; small fish or fishlike descendants whose skins remember light only as a weakness. Their organs show faintly through them, little smoky knots of digestion and pulse. Their eyes, where they have eyes, are reduced to beads, shadows, guesses. They read the world by pressure, taste, vibration, the faint directional tug of current.

The mussels are the city’s old households. They do not move much, but everything must account for them. Half-buried in the soft floor, wedged against one another in dark-lipped rows, they open and close with the patience of gates. Water enters them carrying clay, rot, bacteria, algal dust, the powdered remains of the world above. Water leaves them altered. They are not rulers, but they are infrastructure. Their shells make ridges and alleys. Their dead make walls. Their living bodies measure the pulse of the chamber. When they close all at once, even the hunters understand that something has changed.

Among them work the worms.

Some live in the mud, stitching the bottom to itself. Some build chimneys from silt and mucus, little gray flutes standing in neighborhoods so close-packed they resemble organ pipes or drowned reeds. They are soft, retractable citizens, mostly hidden, known by their openings and by the faint exhalations of cleaner or dirtier water from the tubes. They ventilate the unfinished ground. They bring the buried upward and take the surface down. Without meaning to, they keep the city from suffocating in its own sleep.

The caddis larvae are the masons. Each carries a house of stolen fragments: mica, seed coat, sand grain, fishbone, husk, shell chip. Their corridors and cases are made one decision at a time, chosen by touch, fitted by instinct. They drag their little architecture with them or anchor it into collective pavements. To them, the city is not one city but a material field: this grain useful, this sliver too large, this black sand fitting nicely beside the pale. They are artisans without memory of artistry, builders whose whole bodies are plans.

The sponges are slow towers. They rise where the current feeds them, knuckled and porous, more colony than creature, more appetite than intention. Their bodies are full of chambers smaller than thought. They drink continuously. Minute lives pass into them and vanish. Other minute lives settle on them and graze them. Their surfaces become districts: snails crossing, larvae clinging, bacteria filming them in faint velvet. A sponge is a building that eats and is eaten, a monument made of hunger arranged into holes.

The snails are the scribes. They write in glaze. They pass over shell, clay, sponge, egg-cord, abandoned casing, leaving trails that briefly catch what little light exists. Their rasping is one of the city’s quietest sounds, a steady erasure and renewal of films. They do not hurry. They inherit every surface and reduce it to nourishment by increments. Where they have been, the world is cleaner, shinier, marked by a language no one reads.

The amphipods are the nervous system. They swarm under ledges and in root tangles, curled like commas, springing and folding, legs going in constant panic. They are scavengers, cleaners, fugitives, messengers of disturbance. One darts, and then ten dart, and the motion spreads like alarm through a neighborhood. They feed on what loosens, what dies, what softens. They make use of collapse quickly. When a wall slumps and a district is buried, the amphipods wait at the edges for the first edible pieces to lift free.

The water fleas are weather. They travel in loose, shining storms, each individual a twitch, together a drifting shimmer. They rise when the currents bring food; they scatter when a shadow tightens; they vanish into the grayness by dispersing into too many selves. They have no streets, but their swarms reveal streets: the invisible lanes where water passes, the slow curls behind sponge towers, the upward leakage near root curtains. They are the city’s visible circulation.

There are flatworms too, quiet and almost elegant, slipping over walls like scraps of living dusk. Their bodies fold around irregularities and unfold again. They seem less like animals than decisions made by darkness: to glide, to taste, to engulf. They enter egg-clusters and leave them emptier. They cross the caddis pavements without lifting themselves from the world. They are soft knives.

The leeches wait vertically from roots and shell lips, elongating into questions. They are patient in a different way from mussels. A mussel waits because the world must come through it. A leech waits because something may pass close enough to claim. They sense warmth, damage, salt, motion. Then the still line becomes a loop, the loop becomes a contraction, and the body moves with muscular certainty. After feeding, they withdraw into creases and become dark marks again.

There are hydra, if one looks closely enough: little predatory flowers rooted to shells and stems, their tentacles spread into the current. They are almost beautiful, almost plantlike, and entirely treacherous. A larva touches one and the softness closes. The victim struggles in a scale too small to disturb the city, and the flower eats.

The larvae are everywhere. Some hang upright in ranks, glassy and still, breathing through feathery parts. Some creep in cases. Some burrow. Some are temporary forms of creatures that may never see the surface they were shaped for. Mosquito-like, midge-like, beetle-like, gnat-like, they inhabit postponement. They are not children exactly; they are a season of bodies. Many will be eaten before transformation. Some will transform in the chamber and find no air, no place to fly, no exit into the world their bodies expect. The city keeps even failed metamorphoses. A winged body drowned before flight becomes food, then wall, then film, then egg-nourishment again.

The fish, if they are fish, are pale needles of appetite. They thread through the districts with oversized fins, correcting themselves constantly in the crowded water. Their mouths taste before they bite. Their sides flash only when they turn, a brief silver admission that they exist. Generations here have thinned them toward secrecy. They do not chase for long; there is no room for pursuit. They angle, hover, sip, vanish. Their hunger is precise because wasteful motion clouds the water and summons larger hunger.

The larval salamander is one of those larger hungers.

It is not large in the way of the upper world, but here it is a moving catastrophe, a pale lord of pressure. Its external gills feather beside its head like red-gray weeds. Its limbs are delicate, almost unfinished, but its mouth is absolute. When it enters an avenue, the avenue becomes aware of itself. Worms retreat. Amphipods pack into cracks. Water fleas split apart into glittering confusion. The salamander pauses, and in that pause the city tightens. Then the mouth opens and the water itself seems to betray one of its inhabitants, folding prey inward without splash or cry.

It does not rule. It merely passes through, and that is enough.

Below all of them are the bacteria, the oldest and most numerous residents, though almost none can be seen as individuals. They are the undercity, the chemistry, the dim metabolism of the drowned pocket. They fur roots in pale haze. They silver the mud with gases. They soften leaves, bloom on carcasses, blacken the undersides of shells, turn what falls into what can rise again as food. Some need the faint oxygen brought by currents and roots. Some work deeper, where light and breath are rumors. They make life possible by taking apart the dead so thoroughly that nothing remains only itself.

There are also the almost-plants: algae, drowned grass, rootlets, failed leaves. They are not citizens in the restless animal way. They are remnants and conduits. The rootlets descend from above like blind fingers, bringing trace sugars, trace oxygen, trace news of seasons no inhabitant understands. Algae cling wherever the faintest permission of light remains. Thin leaves, water-emptied and translucent, continue their ghost-work in crevices. They are the city’s memory of sky, though the city has never had one.

What lives there, then, is a layered covenant of smallness.

No creature knows the whole chamber. A water flea’s city is a column of edible suspension and sudden shadows. A mussel’s city is whatever the current brings to its lips. A worm’s city is mud density, oxygen gradient, the safety of retreat. A leech’s city is waiting. A salamander’s city is intervals of stillness broken by intake. The bacteria’s city is every surface becoming edible in time.

They do not love the place, but they fit it. They are shaped to its cold, its dimness, its seal. Their bodies are instructions written by pressure: flatten, cling, filter, burrow, taste, retract, drift. They build by living and live by building. They make districts from shells and tubes, streets from currents, ceilings from danger, monuments from the dead.

They are not primitive. They are exact.

They have no history except layers. A collapse is recorded as a buried neighborhood. A feeding bloom as a band of richer sediment. A generation of mussels as a ridge. A drought above, perhaps, as a narrowing of roots. A flood as new clay. A death as a scaffold. Their archive is physical and edible. Their ruins are immediately inhabited.

If one could become small enough and still keep wonder, one would find no emptiness there. Every apparent surface would reveal residents: ciliates shivering in films, eggs darkening toward motion, mites crawling through sponge pores, fungal threads entering husks, minute crustaceans grazing bacterial velvet. The city is crowded beyond sight. It continues inward past what a human eye would call the smallest thing, into motile specks, dividing cells, chemical hungers, invisible exchanges.

They are a civilization without intention, a metropolis of instinctive agreements.

Open when fed.

Close when warned.

Anchor where the current permits.

Eat what softens.

Hide when the water darkens.

Lay eggs where stillness gathers.

Use the dead.

Do not break the ceiling.

And because none of them can leave, the chamber is not merely where they live. It is their climate, their map, their ancestor, their future fossil. It enters their bodies as cold and clay and returns from them as shell, tube, mucus, egg, gas, movement. They are making the city by surviving it.

What lives there is the drowned ground learning to have inhabitants before it has finished becoming a place.
