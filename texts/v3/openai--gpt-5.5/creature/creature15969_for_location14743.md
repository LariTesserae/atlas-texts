What lives there does not look, at first, like a population. It looks like weather having learned habits.

The most numerous inhabitants are low, dark, and patient. They live pressed against stone in skins of wax, hair, glass, and mineral crust. They are plants only by the old habit of naming things that do not walk: knee-high forests of black-green stems, frost-edged leaves folded tight as fists, mats that breathe slowly under their own weight. Their roots are not merely roots. They are grips, pumps, tongues, wires. They taste iron. They draw vapor out of cloud. They clasp quartz and basalt and persuade them, over years, to give up what can be eaten.

Some of them open only when the sky is darkest. Then the thickets loosen. Fronds unfold from knots no larger than a hand, clear as wet horn, ribbed with pale conductive veins. They do not seek sunlight. They drink fog, static, mineral breath. When storm charge crawls through the plateau, these plants lift their edges and tremble, not with fear but appetite. Blue sparks pass from leaf to leaf. Whole groves briefly show their hidden geometry: circles, lines, branching lattices, as though thought has moved through them.

Among these dark growths live the grazers.

They are tall only because the plants are low. Narrow-bodied, white-backed, long-legged, they move over the tableland with the delicacy of animals crossing thin ice. Their hooves spread on contact, softening over moss, gripping shale, folding again on bare stone. Their heads are long and fine, with nostrils that can close against freezing fog. They crop the alpine thickets sparingly, never stripping one place bare. They know the plateau as a sequence of remembered warmths: a vent under lichen, a gravel hollow where frost melts first, a salt seam exposed by winter, a black pool that does not seal over.

They do not herd closely. Each keeps a careful distance from the others, pale shapes scattered over the slopes like remnants of snow. Yet they are not solitary. They listen through the ground. A shift in hoof-pressure travels through moss and root and shale. A warning passes without voice. One lifts its head, then another two hundred meters away, then a third beyond the ridge. In this way the herd turns as one without seeming to gather.

Their young are born dark, almost invisible among wet stones. For the first weeks they do not stand in open ground. They lie folded in moss cradles that warm around them slightly, fed by the slow respiration of fungal tubes below. The mothers visit rarely and briefly, leaving milk rich with mineral salts and a resinous bitterness that keeps parasites from the skin.

There are birds, but they are not singers.

Small black birds nest in the mouths of warm cracks. Their feathers are oily, their eyes bright, their feet bare and heat-loving. They build no elaborate nests, only rings of fiber, insect shell, and mineral wool gathered from vent-crusts. Their eggs are mottled like basalt and are warmed from below by the mountain’s breath. They fly low and fast, using the broken currents along cliffs. Their wingbeats seem louder than their calls, and their calls are little more than clicks: stone sounds, frost sounds, the kind of noises the place permits.

In the pools live the pale ones.

Blind salamanders drift under black water, nearly transparent except for the ink-thread of spine and the slow pulse of organs. Their limbs are fine and spread like roots. Some have external gills shaped like frost-ferns. They feed on mineral films, insect larvae, and the soft white colonies that grow where warm seep meets ice-water. When disturbed, they give off a faint greenish light, not enough to illuminate the pool, only enough to reveal that the pool contains depth, movement, and more bodies than expected.

Beneath stones are insects like filings of bone and amber. They gather at resin seeps, cut droplets loose with small black mandibles, and carry the beads away in processions that rearrange themselves whenever the electrical fields shift. Their colonies are built inside old root hollows and abandoned mineral vents. They farm pale molds. They lacquer their tunnels. Some have wings that unfold only during ground-fog, when they rise in glittering swarms no higher than a person’s knee and vanish into the vegetation before frost can take them.

There are predators, though the plateau has taught them restraint.

The frost-cats are the easiest to understand, if one is lucky enough not to meet them closely. They are low, heavy-shouldered mammals with short ears, wide paws, and fur that gathers rime until they resemble broken patches of snow-shadow. They hunt by waiting where warmth forces traffic: at the margins of vents, beside unfrozen pools, along the narrow passes between thicket hollows. They do not chase far. Expenditure is a kind of death here. A failed leap may cost more than hunger. So they become stones. They become frost. They let the grazer come to them.

Other hunters are less animal.

Under overhangs cling flat-bodied things the color of wet shale. Their limbs are jointed too many times, their undersides soft and adhesive. They wait for days, sometimes weeks, drinking condensation from the rock. When something warm passes below, they release all at once, falling without sound, spreading like a cloak. Their mouths are not in one place but many, small and circular, opening along the underside.

There are plants that hunt by thirst. They grow beside meltwater threads and alter the water’s taste with invisible salts, leading insects, rodents, and small birds to linger. Their leaves are lined with inward-pointing hairs. Their flowers are not bright, but warm. In the dark, a moth can mistake them for the flank of another living thing.

And there are the web-makers.

They are neither spider nor fungus, though they borrow from both. Their bodies remain hidden in moss-caverns and root-knots while their filaments travel outward, anchored from shrub to shrub, stone to stem, ridge to ridge. A single organism may tension an entire hollow. Its threads are nearly invisible except when frost forms on them, and then a valley will briefly reveal itself as strung with silver geometry. Small prey are held outright. Larger animals are only slowed, redirected, made to stumble where another hunter waits. The web-makers feed as much on vibration as flesh. They know footsteps. They know rain. They know the deep turning below.

But the largest resident is not one creature.

It is the joined life of the plateau itself: root and fungus, mineral vein and warm crack, lichen grid and moss-bellows, insect colony and conductive grove. It has no mind in the quick animal sense, no face, no central chamber where intention sits. Yet it responds. It stores water before dry cold. It vents heat where groves can use it. It plates bark with metals. It routes moisture underground and releases it far away. It grows traps along animal paths and softer moss where young are often hidden. It is not benevolent. It is not cruel. It is an economy so old that cooperation and predation have become difficult to separate.

The beings that live there are quiet because loudness is waste. They are dark because color is costly. They are slow because speed belongs to places with surplus warmth. Even the killers conserve themselves. Even the flowers do not spend beauty freely.

They live by condensation, by patience, by theft from stone, by the brief availability of thaw. They live in pockets and crossings: the lush hollows, the vent-mouths, the under-moss streams, the black basins of water, the pale mineral seams. The open gravel between these places is not empty; it is a test. To cross it is to be seen, chilled, measured by eyes, roots, webs, and pressure-sensitive moss.

Nothing there announces itself as master of the plateau. No animal calls dominion from a ridge. No tree rises above the rest. No nest city, no burrow town, no visible architecture declares occupation.

Yet everything is occupied.

A stone may have lichen thinking slowly across it. A frost shrub may contain sleeping moths. A pool may hold blind white bodies layered below its black reflection. A seam of warmth may be lined with eggs. A patch of moss may be breathing for a fungal lung beneath. A slope that seems vacant may already have heard you, tasted your heat, and begun passing the fact of you from root to root.

What lives there is life reduced to its most durable agreements: keep low, keep close, waste nothing, speak softly, use the dark.
