What lives there is mostly not wild in the old sense. It is not a people of forests or caves, not burrowers in earth, not things that grew up with stone above them and soil below. The inhabitants are creatures of systems: of condensation, access routes, thermal leaks, shift bells, sump tides, and numbered valves. They live by reading what the place is doing before the alarms decide to say it aloud.

At first, the most visible inhabitants are the workers.

They look temporary, even when they have spent years below. Their clothing makes them seem unfinished as bodies: bulked out by insulation, sealed at the wrists and throat, helmeted, lamp-eyed, with respirators clipped to their chests like second mouths. Their boots are heavy and always marked by the gray manufactured mud of the basin. They carry meters, wrenches, sample vials, thermal probes, folded maps laminated against damp, and grease pencils for writing on pipes.

They do not walk casually. They move as if crossing a sleeping animal.

A worker from this place learns to pause at thresholds. Not out of superstition, though superstition grows easily here, but because air has information. Cold flowing across the ankles means one thing. A warm draft at face height means another. A sweet metallic smell may mean coolant. A sharp clean smell may mean ozone. Rottenness may be bacterial bloom, or a dead animal, or a sealed chamber breathing after months of stillness. Their heads tilt often, listening. They can hear differences in pumps the way sailors hear weather in rigging.

They speak little in the open basin. Not because they are unfriendly, but because the machinery humiliates speech. Words become small, torn things. They use hand signs, helmet lamps, short radio calls, coded taps on railing. In the settlement decks they talk more, but even there their voices remain compressed, economical, as if the walls are still listening for mistakes.

They divide the world by routes and risks rather than by rooms. North field. Baffle seven. Blue stairs. The hot seam. The throat. Catwalk twelve if the frost is light. Western gallery only with visor down. Don’t cross the old membrane after ceiling rain. Don’t trust dry concrete near the black basins. Don’t lean on yellow railings unless you saw someone test them that week.

They are not exactly miners, though they live below. Not exactly factory workers, though machinery rules them. Not exactly mariners, though water determines their lives. They are keepers of an artificial watershed. Sump shepherds. Leak readers. Weather mechanics.

Their faces, when uncovered, are pale in the way of people who know lamps better than sunlight. Their eyes narrow against glare and mist. Their skin cracks at the knuckles from cold, disinfectant, sealant dust, and wet gloves. Many develop a slight forward lean, a habit from passing under pipes and suspended loads, even in spaces where they could stand straight. Aboveground, if they go there, they often look up too much. Open sky makes some of them uneasy. It gives no ceiling from which danger might be mapped.

They keep private calendars of the place’s moods.

There are official schedules: pump cycles, treatment rotations, ventilation purges, structural inspections, delivery windows. But every long-term worker knows the other calendar. When rain above will become ceiling rain below. When the gray breath thickens near the western galleries. When a basin begins to ring before a pressure shift. When frost will form on the high stairs. When sump tide will reverse the channels just long enough to fool an inattentive person into thinking the world has changed its mind.

They have jokes about it, but the jokes are careful. They laugh at failed valves, at bad coffee, at new engineers who step into clean-looking puddles, at drones that wedge themselves upside down in cable trays and blink helplessly. They do not joke much about the places taped off in black and yellow where water behaves incorrectly.

Children do not belong there, but there are sometimes children in the settlement decks.

Not many. They are the children of long contracts, of maintenance families, of people whose work became life before anyone admitted it. They are raised above the wet on pilings and enclosed walkways. They learn early not to touch condensation on walls, not to chase the maintenance cats, not to drop anything through grating, not to follow a light unless they know who carries it. They draw suns as circles with hazard stripes around them. They know the names of pumps before the names of birds.

The children play quiet games adapted to the architecture: counting beacons through fogged windows, racing droplets down reinforced glass, building miniature sluices from food trays, daring one another to stand barefoot for three seconds on the cold floor of a laundry module. They collect fallen labels, stripped wire, colored gasket rings, and flakes of mineral crust. A green leaf from above, if one appears in a drainage basket or stuck to a boot, becomes treasure, then mold, then lesson.

They grow up with a different sense of nature. For them, weather is something that happens inside after something else happens outside. Rain is not falling from clouds; rain is the ceiling deciding to answer. Wind is louvers opening. Fog is heat meeting regulation. A river is water that has been assigned a path and still tries to misbehave.

Some leave when they can. Some return, because the upper world is too unmeasured.

The settlement decks have their own domestic species of behavior. Laundry never fully dries unless placed near the warm duct behind module C. Boots stand upside down on heated rails. Every bed has a condensation cover folded nearby. People hang charms from pipe brackets: old pressure tags, bright ribbons from supply crates, stamped tokens from decommissioned valves. Not everyone believes in them. Almost everyone leaves them where they are.

Food tastes faintly of the place. Even sealed stores seem to acquire a metallic edge. Hot meals are valued not for flavor alone but for steam, for the brief luxury of warmth that is allowed to rise instead of condense immediately into trouble. Tea mugs fog face shields. Soup is eaten slowly with both hands around the bowl.

The oldest workers become nearly amphibious in habit, though not in body. They know which surfaces sweat first. They recognize corrosion by color and texture: orange freckles, black blooms, green salts, white lace. They can tell whether a drip has fallen for minutes or months by the shape of its stain. Some claim they can hear a dry bearing through a wall. Some claim they know when a pump is lonely, which is only another way of saying unbalanced, underloaded, cavitating, but the word persists.

They do not worship the pumps, exactly.

But the main pumps have gathered something like reverence around them. No one enters that chamber frivolously. No one speaks there, because speech is useless, but also because the pressure in the chest feels like being addressed by something too large to answer. The pumps are machines, numbered and serviceable, with inspection schedules and replacement parts. They are also the reason the place is not drowned. Their slow beat is the underground country’s pulse.

Around them, people become solemn. Even the careless straighten.

Then there are the cats.

Whether they are cats in the ordinary sense is a matter of argument. They look like cats when seen briefly: lean bodies, triangular heads, pale eyes, thick winter coats darkened by damp. But they move too easily along pipe racks, too knowingly across cables, too silently among drones. Their paws are broad and often gray to the ankles with synthetic silt. Their whiskers are long, sometimes beaded with condensation. Their ears are scarred by cold and metal edges.

They do not live on the floor if they can avoid it. The floor belongs to water, current, wheels, boots, chemistry, and surprise. The cats belong to the middle height: handrails, pipe bridges, cable trays, warm housings, the tops of insulated ducts. They travel the layered overhead territory like another species of maintenance crew.

They hunt moths, sump gnats, heat-loving beetles that arrive in packaging, and the tiny blind things that gather around algae lamps. They drink from known clean drips. They sleep in valve blankets and beneath control kiosks where transformers give off a low, steady warmth. They appear in pairs on fogged windows of the settlement decks, watching people eat.

No one admits to feeding them, because feeding them is discouraged. Everyone feeds them.

They have names, though the names shift by crew and district. Flange. Static. Blue-Eye. Mister Condensate. Little Warning. The black one near filtration four is called Sluice by one shift and Bad Luck by another. A pale, wide-faced female with one torn ear is known everywhere as Supervisor, because she appears before accidents and watches repairs with insulting calm.

There are stories that the cats know when the ceiling will shed ice. Stories that they avoid places before sensors register gas. Stories that they sit facing walls where leaks will open. These stories are likely exaggerations. They are also used operationally. If three cats leave a warm pump room at once, someone checks the room.

Below the cats, in the water and manufactured mud, live smaller lives.

The bacterial mats are everywhere once one learns to see them. Pale films in drainage margins. Rust-colored slicks under slow leaks. Black velvet inside still channels. Green-gold stains beneath lamps. Some were introduced deliberately, engineered to digest spills, bind minerals, or soften industrial residues into something removable. Others arrived as contamination and were tolerated because eradication would require shutting down too much.

They are the place’s first true colonists.

They do not care that the ground is artificial. Concrete dust, carbon residue, polymer threads, iron, lime, coolant trace, warmth from a pipe, darkness, wetness: these are enough. They make skins over puddles. They lace themselves through manufactured silt. They breathe chemistry. They turn leaks into territories.

In the treatment bays, biofilm curtains hang like drowned tapestries. They are supposed to be maintained at specified thicknesses, seeded with approved strains, trimmed, sampled, and replaced. In practice, they grow personalities. One curtain fattens in cold weather and becomes translucent as fat. Another darkens whenever the western fabrication galleries are active. A third retracts from copper runoff as if offended. Workers call them by bay number when managers are present and by nicknames when they are not.

There are floating reed-analogs too, rooted in mesh rafts: pale segmented stems, rubbery leaves, hollow capillaries designed to pull minerals from water. They resemble plants only enough to make their not-plantness unsettling. They do not sway like reeds unless the pumps start. Their leaves are too evenly spaced. Their roots are white and bundled, like cables pretending to be organic. Small organisms shelter among them anyway. Life accepts bad imitations if the imitations provide cover.

In the older sumps live translucent crustaceans, no longer than a fingernail clipping. They are almost invisible until light hits them, and then they become commas of glass flicking away. They feed on bacterial film, shed skins too delicate to survive retrieval, and gather in seams where warm water enters cold. They have no pigment because there is no need for color. Their bodies are little decisions made by darkness.

Some workers insist they are descendants of something introduced. Others say they came in with construction water, or on boots, or in tanks, or from above after a flood. The truth may be several truths braided together. The sumps have been young for years, but water makes ancestry quickly.

There are moths, though they seem impossible.

They appear near warm lamps and control kiosks, powdery white or gray, their wings dusted with concrete and mineral bloom. They fly badly, as if the air is too heavy to understand. They cling to screens, helmet rims, foam insulation, the undersides of switch boxes. Their larvae probably live in damp packaging, cloth fiber, fungal mats, forgotten stores of cellulose, the cardboard that is never supposed to remain below and always does.

A moth in that place seems holy for half a second, then doomed. Its softness is extravagant. Its wings make no industrial sense.

Some die in condensation. Some are eaten by cats. Some vanish into cable forests and return generations later as paler versions of themselves, bred by accident for cold, lamps, and dust.

There are fungi beneath pallets and in wall seams where warmth persists. They are thin, translucent, almost architectural: shelves like waxed paper, cords like exposed nerves, small cups full of water. They consume forgotten wood, cloth, insulation backing, adhesive, the paper labels on old crates. They like the underside of things. They are patient with poisons. They fruit after heat pulses.

Once in a while, maintenance opens a sealed service void and finds an entire pale garden grown on discarded packing material: fungal fans, black algae, mineral whiskers, droplets trembling from every edge. For a day it is documented. For an hour it is beautiful. Then it is scraped, bagged, sterilized, and entered into a report as contamination.

In the channels there are stranger animals.

The eel-like cleaners are officially unconfirmed in some districts and officially decommissioned in others. Something long and dark moves under grates, leaving a wake through oil film. It bends without surfacing. It noses along sediment banks and vanishes into pipes narrower than seems possible. Perhaps it is a designed organism, released to consume sludge and forgotten when a contract changed. Perhaps it is a flexible inspection machine whose casing has gone soft with bacterial growth. Perhaps there are both, and the distinction has become irrelevant in the water.

Those who have seen one describe no eyes, or too many small eyes, or sensor pits, or a pale mouth working at residue on the wall of a channel. The accounts do not agree. The wakes agree.

They are not aggressive. They are worse than aggressive: they are unaccounted for. They live in the part of the system where maps become recommendations.

There are drones as well, and they are a kind of fauna whether anyone admits it.

Maintenance drones cling to walls and overhead beams with magnetized feet. They are compact, jointed, sensor-faced, often streaked with mineral deposits like old insects. They scrape buildup from gauges, sample leaks, weld hairline cracks, map corrosion, chase thermal anomalies, and die in inconvenient places. Their routes are scheduled, but the place interferes with schedules. A drone delayed by condensation may shelter under a pipe until its sensors clear. Another may circle a basin for hours because reflected lamps confuse its navigation. Another may mistake a biofilm curtain for a sealant failure and politely attempt to repair it until recalled.

They have no instinct, but repeated errors become behavior.

Workers curse them, rescue them, decorate them, and sometimes speak to them in the tones reserved for stubborn animals. A drone that regularly services the high catwalks above the central pump plain has a strip of red tape tied around one leg and is known as Auntie. A small wall-crawler in the filtration fields chirps before taking samples and has taught several cats to avoid it. One obsolete ceiling unit continues to travel a retired inspection loop above a sealed corridor, blinking green at doors no one uses.

The drones do not live biologically, but they inhabit. They leave scratches, clean paths, failed welds, nests of spare parts, charging stains. Their dead bodies collect in retrieval bins and occasionally under grates, where bacterial mats soften their labels.

The machines themselves live most largely.

Not as minds, perhaps. Not as spirits, unless one has worked too many night shifts and slept too little. But they live as organized insistence. Pumps feed channels. Channels feed sumps. Fans make climates. Heat exchangers create fog. Valves cough and hold back pressure. Gates open like jaws. Sensors blink their small judgments into the dark. The whole region has metabolism: intake, sorting, filtration, excretion, repair.

It eats water and contamination. It exhales mist. It sweats from ceilings. It grows frost. It sheds rust. It scars itself with cuts and channels. It replaces its own organs while running.

The people are its attendants and sometimes its immune system. The bacteria are its gut. The cats are its rumor. The drones are its grooming insects. The pumps are its heart. The channels are veins and arguments. The false geology is its young bone, still too sharp at the edges, still wet with manufacture.

There may also be something else there, though no responsible worker would phrase it that way.

It is not a monster in the channels or a ghost in the walls. It is a pattern born from repetition: water behaving almost naturally in a place that denies nature; machines correcting failures so often that failure becomes a climate; condensation remembering shapes no one designed. It lives in upward-falling droplets, in basins that ripple before pumps engage, in clean freshwater sweating from a wall with no reservoir behind it. It lives in the mismatch between plan and behavior.

The workers mark such places with tape. They file reports. They recalibrate sensors. They avoid giving the phenomena names because names invite habits of attention, and attention invites relationship.

But privately they remember.

They remember the corridor colder than its pipes. The reflection of a lamp that had been removed the week before. The basin that rang when no gate moved. The inspection drone that returned with its camera full of black water and one frame of white moth wings deep under the grating. The cat sitting at the edge of a dry channel, staring down into it as if watching something pass beneath the concrete.

What lives there, then, is not one species but an ecology of the made and the nearly-made.

People live there conditionally, by procedure.

Cats live there by route and secrecy.

Bacteria live there by chemistry.

Fungi live there by patience.

Crustaceans live there by invisibility.

Moths live there by mistake.

Engineered reeds live there by assignment and then by adaptation.

Drones live there by instruction until instruction frays into habit.

The pumps live there by force.

And the place itself lives there by becoming less like a facility and more like a weather system with walls.

Nothing in it is ancient, but everything is already acquiring memory. Every stain lengthens into history. Every leak found twice becomes a landmark. Every repaired seam waits to become a seam again. The inhabitants do not belong to earth, exactly, nor to industry alone. They belong to the cold interval where construction begins to behave like landscape, and where landscape, denied soil and sky, learns to grow from metal, water, dust, and repetition.
