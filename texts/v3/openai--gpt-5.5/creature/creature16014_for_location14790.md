They are a people of ledges, cisterns, and resonant floors.

Not mountain folk, exactly. They have no ancestral stone, no sacred spring, no memory of a valley river cutting its own way through the world. Their oldest stories begin with foundations, with the first decks bolted into the air, with a generation that learned to trust a floor because someone had calculated it and someone else had listened to it through a brass rod. To them, the world is not divided into nature and construction. A gutter is as natural as a creek if it has carried water longer than anyone’s grandmother can remember. A vine around a pipe is not an invasion. It is an agreement.

They are lean, sure-footed, and practical in the way of people who grow up beside drops. Even the children move with a certain inherited caution: one hand free near railings, toes finding the dry parts of algae-slick stairs, bodies tilting automatically when a freight platform shudders past. They are not fearful of height. They are intimate with it. They know the personality of gaps, the moods of bridges, the difference between a safe creak and a warning one. A child of six can tell which ladder rungs are municipal, which belong to a house, which are shrine-access only, and which will get you cursed at by condenser workers.

Their clothing is layered but light: wrapped trousers, sleeveless work coats, sun-cloths, waterproof shoulder capes that can be folded into catchments, belts crowded with hooks, chalk, seed packets, valve keys, twine, and small brass gauges. Many wear sandals or soft-soled boots made for wet tile and hot metal. Hats are common on the upper decks—wide, stiff, and lacquered against sudden rain. In lower damp galleries, people tie their hair close and oil their sleeves against mold.

Their hands are distinctive. Nearly everyone has the hands of someone who has repaired something: thickened palms, stained nails, little burns, resin scars, cuts from sheet metal, green crescents from pruning, black crescents from machine work. Even scholars and singers know how to patch a leak. Even priests carry spanners. The place does not allow any caste to remain purely ornamental.

They speak in a language full of gradients, flows, and loads. A person may be called “well-braced” as praise, “overhung” as criticism, “dry-chain” if unreliable, “good overflow” if generous under pressure. They do not say someone has died; often they say the person has “gone below the deck,” or “entered the foundation,” or, for the very old, “settled into bearing.” To waste water is a moral ugliness. To overload a balcony is nearly a crime of violence. To ignore a changed bridge-tone is a sin.

Their society is arranged less by streets than by systems.

There are gutter families, who inherit rights to certain clean flows and maintain the lips, chains, mesh, and settling jars that make them drinkable. There are root-pruners, who know how to keep a fig alive without letting it split a retaining wall beyond tolerance. There are bell-listeners, trained from childhood to hear structural fatigue in the note of a suspended bronze plate. There are fog-combers, who climb before dawn to unfurl silver mesh from the high towers and return glittering with condensation. There are sump-divers, algae cooks, liftwrights, crab-herders, pipe cartographers, trellis-weavers, and old women who can identify a leak by taste.

The highest respect is given to maintainers.

Not rulers. Not merchants. Maintainers.

The ones who keep water sweet, decks balanced, engines patient, roots disciplined, and paths open. Their marks are everywhere: painted triangles, dots, saints’ eyes, repair dates, pressure numerals, signatures written over the signatures of dead masters. A wall in this place is also an archive. A staircase is a lineage. A valve may have more biography than a noble house.

Their priests are engineers with dirty hems. Their engineers are half-priests whether they admit it or not. Shrines sit at junctions where overflow channels meet, beneath counterweights, beside ancient pump housings, in small alcoves warm from transformer hum. Offerings are simple: polished washers, first fruit, bowls of clean water, little folded plans of repairs hoped for, feathers from birds nesting under bridge ribs. The gods, if gods they are, are not remote sky-beings. They are pressure, balance, condensation, corrosion, germination, and return.

They farm upward and sideways.

Their orchards grow in troughs; their fields hang in trays; their root vegetables swell in sacks of manufactured soil stamped by compost houses. Children learn to mix substrate as other children might learn to bake bread. There are recipes for it: fungal fiber, mineral dust, crab-shell lime, leaf mold, spent algae, powdered ceramic, old soot in careful quantities. Soil is never just dirt. It is wealth, inheritance, medicine, weight, and promise.

They eat brightly and damply: fruits warmed by sun, sour greens from greenhouse stacks, steamed fungi, gutter-herbs, terrace beans, fish from aqueduct troughs, eggs from roof birds, fermented algae cakes, milk from narrow-footed stair beasts, crabs from the lower drains when the water is clean enough. Their markets smell of lime, oil, wet rope, fried batter, crushed leaves, engine grease, and flowers. Every stall has hooks overhead in case of rain. Every market has a public scale, and beside the scale a posted load limit for the deck.

They are not delicate people, but they love delicate things.

They cage songbirds near machine shops because a bright voice makes labor human. They paint tiny flowers on inspection hatches. They carve vines into pipe collars. They hang prayer ribbons from pressure valves and polish them with the same care given to tools. Their festivals use mirrors, lenses, colored mist, reflected lamps, and captive sparks behind glass. Open flame is too dangerous to be beautiful without supervision.

Their music is full of rhythm learned from work: pump-throb, chain-clack, drip-count, belt-patter, lift-groan. They sing in stairwells because the echoes climb. They play flutes cut from service reeds, drums made from stretched waterproof cloth over ceramic basins, chimes tuned to bridge-bells. Some songs are practical. A maintenance crew may sing a measuring song to keep timing while opening a sluice. Some are old enough that nobody understands the terms in them anymore, only that they must be sung during the first fog harvest or the mesh will tear.

They marry across elevations carefully. A person from the dry high decks may find the lower galleries oppressive, too rich with mold and machinery. Someone raised near the sump can think the upper brightness cruel and exposed. Courtship often involves learning the other’s routes: which stairs to trust, which vendors to greet, where to stand when the afternoon glare becomes unbearable, which gutter tastes faintly of copper after rain.

Their homes are fitted into the made mountain like nests in a cliff. Some are bright rooms behind tiled terraces, with fruiting vines shading the windows. Some are deep vault dwellings where condensation patters all night into household jars. Some occupy former machine housings with walls too thick to alter, so families hang curtains and shelves from old bolt-holes. Floors are often uneven because no one wastes effort correcting a harmless tilt. Windows look into other levels of life: a kitchen may face the underside of an aqueduct where swifts nest; a bedroom may open onto steam from a bakery three decks down.

They keep animals that have become almost citizens.

The stair beasts are patient, warm-blooded, padded-footed creatures with low shoulders and long balancing tails, bred to carry sacks of soil, pipe lengths, ceramic plates, sleeping children, baskets of wet greens. They know routes no engine can manage. Some refuse unsafe bridges before human inspectors notice the problem, and such animals are retired into honored idleness.

There are cats everywhere, narrow as poured shadow, moving through grates and trellises. They belong to houses, shops, temples, and no one. Their coats are often rust-colored, soot-gray, cream, or greenish at the paws from moss. A cat that keeps condenser mesh free of nesting vermin may receive a name and a ribbon from the maintenance guild.

The drain-crabs are less loved but deeply respected. They tap through wet service corridors and ceramic floodways, cleaning what should not remain. Children are told not to tease them, not because they are sacred, but because a crab population collapse can foul three districts before anyone understands why.

Birds are everywhere: swifts, swallows, clever hook-beaked scavengers, domestic songbirds, fat roof fowl, bright pollinators, escaped aviary breeds that now own whole façades. Some birds have learned the lift schedules and ride warm drafts from industrial vents at the same hour every day. Some steal brass washers and fruit skins. Some mimic pressure whistles so accurately that apprentices are warned never to trust one signal alone.

Insects are tolerated when useful, cursed when not, and studied always. Pollinator hives are mounted beside factories where waste heat keeps them active. Certain beetles are released into planter troughs to aerate soil. Bright-winged gnats appear after fog weeks in such numbers that children chase them with nets of old filter cloth, not to kill them, but to gather pigment dust from their wings for festival paint.

There are also creatures no one fully accounts for.

Translucent salamanders in the old condensation galleries, blind and soft, moving like living water over mineral-streaked plates. Pale moths that hatch only when a disused boiler warms for the first time in years. Tiny lizards striped like hazard markings. Root-rats that know how to open seed lockers. Silver fish in aqueducts that flash under inspection lamps and vanish into inaccessible channels.

Some are engineered remnants. Some are imports. Some are accidents. After enough centuries, the distinction matters mostly to archivists.

The people themselves may have changed too, though they do not speak of it dramatically. Many have wide tolerance for damp heat and thin air. Their lungs are strong. Their balance is uncanny. Their eyes narrow easily against glare. Families from the upper decks often have darkened, weather-tough faces and pale lines where goggles sit. Lower-gallery families are softer-voiced, better at hearing through machine noise, better at judging humidity by skin. No one calls these separate peoples. They are elevations of one body.

Their politics are hydraulic.

Arguments begin over overflow and end in genealogy. Water rights can be older than neighborhoods. A household may own the first draw from a condensation wall because an ancestor repaired it during a famine. A factory may be required to feed warm vapor into a greenhouse because of a treaty signed four hundred years ago after a boiler fire. Legal disputes involve maps so layered that they must be unfolded across entire courtyards and weighted with stones, tools, fruit, sleeping cats.

No one is allowed to claim rain by mere luck. Water falling on a roof belongs first to that roof’s maintenance, then to its assigned cistern, then to public overflow, then to root beds, then to emergency channels, depending on season, purity, and ancient promises. Theft of clean water is severe. Theft of dirty water is still theft, but sometimes understandable.

Their funerals are careful about weight.

The dead are rarely buried in any ordinary sense. There is no earth to spare for the gesture. Some are composted in sealed civic houses and later returned as soil to family trees. Some become part of memorial planters, their names stamped onto ceramic root-guards. Maintainers may have their tools hung in public passages until rust takes them. Bell-listeners are sometimes remembered with small tuned plates under bridges. When rain strikes those plates, the dead speak in faint, irregular notes.

They do not dream of untouched wilderness. That idea, if explained to them, would seem lonely and wasteful: a place with water not caught, shade not shared, fruit unpruned, paths unmarked, danger unlistened to. Their ideal of beauty is not purity. It is function ripened into grace. A perfect thing, to them, is an aqueduct lip fringed with moss, repaired in three colors of ceramic, feeding a cistern, a fern wall, a drinking spout, and a fish trough, while swallows nest underneath and someone has painted the date of the last inspection in blue.

They are busy, but not hurried. The whole place teaches long attention. A crack may be watched for thirty years. A tree may be trained over a century to shade a stair without lifting the paving. A bridge may acquire a shrine, then a tea stall, then a trellis, then a schoolroom, until no one remembers when it was only a way across.

Their children play at collapse and repair. They build toy terraces from broken tile. They stage mock disputes over drip rights. They dare one another to cross fog bridges and climb forbidden ladders. They learn bird calls, valve calls, warning bells, and the sound a good cistern makes when struck. They grow up knowing the world can hold them only because someone is always holding the world.

And beneath them all, around them all, living with them rather than under their command, is the made mountain itself.

It is not alive as an animal is alive, though at times it breathes like one: warm vents exhaling, fog screens drinking, drains swallowing, pumps pulsing in the depths. It has circulation, appetite, fever, memory. It hosts parasites and symbionts. It scars and heals. It sheds obsolete structures the way trees shed limbs, slowly and with warning. Its inhabitants do not worship it exactly. They maintain it, fear it, feed it, argue with it, inherit it.

They are its weather-minded custodians: gardeners of iron, farmers of runoff, shepherds of load, citizens of a vertical ecology. They live among vines and valves, frogs and turbines, rust and fruit, with clouds passing below their balconies and water ticking all night through chains.

They have made a homeland out of balance.
