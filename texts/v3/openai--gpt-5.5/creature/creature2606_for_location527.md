What lives there is not a single people, not a kingdom of beasts with clear borders, but a pressure of life so dense that individuality is something glimpsed only when it moves.

The inhabitants are all shaped by interruption.

They are creatures of broken channels, falling trees, sudden light, sudden drowning, sudden abundance. Nothing here can afford to depend on a stable bank, a permanent nest, a reliable path, a season that behaves as it did before. The successful ones are quick to climb, quick to breed, quick to abandon, quick to feed on disaster. They are soft where the land is sharp, armored where the rot is patient, loud where the dark is deep.

They do not live in the basin so much as answer it.

The plants are the first and most numerous residents, though they do not seem passive. They behave almost animal in their urgency. Pale young trunks swell overnight after rain. Vines search with blind, muscular certainty, coiling around anything upright: saplings, antlers, dead branches, sleeping animals if they sleep too long. Ferns fold and unfurl according to moisture, opening like green lungs. Some trees bleed milk-white sap that hardens into bitter amber and traps insects in jeweled clots. Others grow hollow chambers in their bases, warm and damp, where frogs, beetles, snakes, and root-crabs shelter together until hunger divides them.

There are flowers that do not care for color because almost nothing sees them in daylight. They bloom in heat, in odor, in vibration. Some open to the booming calls of the night hunters. Some release pollen only when the metallic birds scream above them. One kind of hanging blossom keeps its mouth shut through rain and wind and thunder, then opens all at once when the long-limbed climbers cough in chorus, dusting their wet fur with gold.

Fungi are everywhere, though often unseen until they fruit. They are the basin’s quiet memory. They lace the raw wood, knit the sliding soil, enter the carcasses before the blood has cooled. Some grow into broad, shield-like shelves on fallen trunks. Some rise as black fingers from the mud after storms. Some make pale veils over insect husks. In the deeper shade there are mats of fungal thread that pulse faintly blue when touched, not enough to light the way, only enough to show that the ground itself is awake and taking account.

The insects are not a population but a weather system.

They live in bark, in rot, in leaves, under scales, inside fruit, in the ears of the tusked animals, in the throats of flowers, in bubbles at the edges of pools. Beetles with shovel heads carve galleries through newly fallen timber. Needle-bodied flies lay eggs in bruised fruit and open wounds. Cicadas scream from hidden places until predators find them by sound alone. Antlike swarms build no lasting towers, because towers do not last here; instead they make temporary living bridges over fresh runnels and collapse them when the ground changes.

There are lantern flies with cold blue abdomens that drift low among roots in the thickest dark. Their glow attracts moths, and the moths attract frogs, and the frogs attract vine-snakes, and the snakes attract the hook-beaked birds that hang upside down from branches and stab into the green gloom. Nothing shines here without becoming part of a chain.

The amphibians are among the basin’s favored children.

Frogs fill every accidental cup of water: hoofprints, split bamboo-like stems, broken crowns of fallen trees, stone basins, crab holes, rain-sagged leaves. Some are no larger than a thumb joint and translucent enough that their hearts show through their bellies. Some are swollen, warty things that sit like wet stones and bellow with voices too large for them. Some carry their eggs on their backs until the next storm, then scrape them into new pools with their hind feet. Their colors are warnings, invitations, lies: wax yellow, bruised purple, mold green, the red of exposed earth.

There are salamander-like things that never choose between water and land. They slide through wet leaf litter on small, clever limbs, breathing through shining skin, feeding on worms and insect larvae. When channels dry, they bury themselves in mud and wait for thunder. When channels flood, they unfold and hunt.

The reptiles are sharper.

Small upright runners flash across warm stone with their forelimbs tucked close, using their tails like balancing reeds. They hunt flies and each other. Their scales are dark when wet, copper when dry, green when they freeze under leaves. Larger lizards lie on branches with mouths open, letting insects clean their teeth until a bird comes too near. There are shell-backed grazers in the pools, soft at the edges, scraping algae from young rock with round mouths. Some carry whole little gardens on their shells: moss, seedlings, tiny white flowers, mites, eggs.

The snakes are almost not animals in the way they inhabit the place. They are lengths of intention disguised as vine, root, shadow, water. Some hang in loops from branches and take birds from below. Some lie in shallow streams with only their nostrils exposed, letting fish brush their lips. Some are slender and fast, living in the canopy, drinking eggs, raiding nests, moving through leaves without setting them trembling. The largest are rarely seen entire. They are known by absences: a sudden silence in the frogs, a crushed reed bed, the half-swallowed hindquarters of a striped browser disappearing into green water.

The birds are heavy, brilliant, and violent with sound.

Many are built for short crashes rather than graceful flight. They throw themselves from branch to branch, wings thudding, claws tearing bark, throats flashing blue, orange, wet black. Their cries are metallic: gongs, plates, hammered wire. Some have casque-like skulls for smashing fruit and rivals. Some use stones wedged into tree forks to crack nuts, not keeping the stones, not owning them, only remembering where stone and branch make a useful mouth.

There are red-eyed carrion birds that descend before death is complete. They gather on leaning trunks and wait for the mud to finish what sickness began. There are long-tailed insect hunters that follow the tusked animals, picking disturbed beetles from the air. There are small, gray, nearly silent birds that nest in the hanging roots above pools and feed their young on mosquito clouds. At dusk the central flats fill with hundreds of wading birds, their legs thin as reeds, stabbing at stranded fish in the braided shallows.

The climbers are the closest thing the basin has to a people, if people can be said to exist without keeping monuments, names scratched into bark, or anything meant to outlast the rain.

They are long-limbed, narrow-chested, furred in dark brown or soot gray, with pale hands and watchful faces. They move in troops through the middle canopy, never entirely quiet, never entirely exposed. Their fingers are strong and delicate. They strip sticks, probe bark, pry grubs from holes, fold leaves into cups, crack shells in stone crotches, use thorns to tease larvae from swollen stems. But they leave almost nothing behind that the forest cannot confuse with accident.

Their intelligence is immediate, social, and weatherwise. They know which branches will bear weight after rain and which only pretend. They know the calls of predators and the false calls made by birds that want them to flush insects. They know where last week’s river ran and where today’s river is thinking of going. They remember fruiting trees, but no tree is trusted. They remember safe sleeping places, but sleep lightly.

They do not make villages. They make moments.

A troop may gather broad leaves and bend them over a forked branch to keep rain off infants for an hour. They may wedge stones where nuts fall hardest, use them for a season, then abandon the place when vines close it. They may braid hanging roots together to cross a gap after a storm, but by the next storm the braid has sprouted, rotted, or been pulled apart by something heavier. Their culture, if it can be called that, lives in gesture, warning, taste, mimicry, grudge, and route. The old females know thunder by smell. The young males dare the open flats and come back with leeches, scars, and stories told in coughs and slaps and branch-shaking displays.

They are not masters of the basin. Nothing is. They are only among the quicker minds it has produced.

On the ground move the armored and tusked ones.

The low tusked animals are dense, muscular, mud-colored creatures with shoulders higher than their hips and snouts built for ruin. They root through fern beds, pry up tubers, split rotting logs, overturn stones, and leave behind churned tunnels that become seedbeds, puddles, insect traps, frog nurseries. Their tusks are not elegant crescents but blunt, stained wedges. Their eyes are small and bright. Their hides are thick, often greened with algae along the back, scarred by parasites and thorn-vines. When alarmed, they do not flee cleanly; they detonate through the undergrowth, and everything smaller gets out of their way or becomes part of the mud.

The striped browsers are more nervous, more beautiful, and more often eaten. They move in herds through the basin center at dusk, belly-deep in reeds and young shoots. Their coats are patterned in broken vertical bands, not to hide them in open light but to dissolve them among stems, trunks, falling rain. They have wide, rotating ears, wet black noses, and long tongues for stripping leaves. Their fawns are born spotted and nearly scentless, tucked under fern fans while the herd feeds. Many do not last a week. Those that do learn the first rule of the flats: never step where the water reflects too perfectly.

There are larger browsers too, high-shouldered and slow, with split lips and bony brow ridges for shoving through young trees. They are followed by birds, flies, and smaller animals that eat what they break. Their passage makes temporary roads. The forest closes these roads quickly, but for a day or two they belong to everyone: predators, seed carriers, scavengers, mating frogs, the long-limbed climbers coming down to gather fallen fruit.

The predators are seldom seen as whole bodies.

They are spotted flanks between trunks, breath in a hollow log, yellow eyes below a root shelf, the flex of a tail over wet stone. Some are catlike, though heavier in the forequarters, with paws broad enough for mud and claws that hook into bark. They climb when they must, swim when they must, wait more often than they chase. Their coats break apart in the dimness: rosettes, streaks, moss-dark patches, pale throat flashes. They hunt the edges of everything — edge of water, edge of clearing, edge of herd, edge of attention.

The night hunters are larger and stranger. Their calls are the hollow booming that passes through rib and mud. They have throat sacs or resonating chambers in the chest, and when they call, nearby water trembles. They do not roar because roaring would be too simple; they drum themselves into the dark, telling rivals, mates, and prey that the deep night has teeth. Some wait on stone islands in the central alluvial flats, black against black, letting herds come to them. Some patrol the young ridges, taking animals driven uphill by flood.

There are also smaller killers in abundance: civet-like egg thieves, long-snouted fish catchers, clawed burrow raiders, night-gliding things with membranes between their limbs, owls with soft wings and blunt crushing feet. The basin does not reserve death for the grand.

In the central flat live the most changeable creatures.

Fish stranded by wandering streams gulp air in shining heaps and wriggle from puddle to puddle at night. Some have stiffened fins that let them push across wet silt. Eels live under stone shelves and come out when thunder begins, as if summoned by vibration. Mud-crabs climb reed stems and carry wet earth on their backs; seeds root there, so some crabs wear tiny gardens until molting strips them bare. Reed shrimp bloom in temporary pools, breed, die, and leave eggs in the silt for a future rain.

The red algae of the pools is almost an inhabitant with moods. It gathers in skins, breaks around movement, closes over stillness. Tadpoles browse it. Insects drown in it. Birds avoid some pools where it grows too thick, because things beneath the surface use it as a curtain.

The dead are also residents, though briefly recognizable.

A fallen tree becomes housing, food, bridge, dam, nursery, ambush, sponge. A carcass becomes flies, beetles, fungus, cats, birds, crabs, soil. A drowned browser becomes a green slick in a side channel and then a congregation. Bones vanish quickly unless caught in stone. Antlers, tusks, shells, scales, feathers — all are taken down, softened, buried, climbed.

The basin’s inhabitants are not peaceful, but neither are they cruel. They are opportunists in a world that gives no other profession. They are loud because silence is dangerous. They are bright because darkness is crowded. They are armored because rot has teeth. They are soft because stone and water change their minds. They are clever, but rarely possessive. They use, drop, flee, return, breed, call, bloom, devour, vanish.

If there is a common soul among them, it is this:

They do not expect the world to hold still.

Every nest is provisional. Every trail is a rumor. Every pool is temporary. Every clearing is already ending. Every death is already busy becoming shelter.

The ones who live there are those who can enter that bargain without hesitation — the climbers with their wet hands and temporary tools, the tusked churners, the striped dusk herds, the throat-drumming hunters, the birds like struck metal, the frogs in every accidental cup, the snakes pretending to be vines, the crabs carrying seedlings, the fungi drawing maps through the dead.

They are the basin learning how to be inhabited.
