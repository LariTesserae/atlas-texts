What lives there is not a jungle’s population translated into mineral, though at first the mind tries to make it so. There are no trees, no vines, no beetles, no birds in any true sense. The inhabitants are descendants—or alternatives—of growth, computation, weather, and law. They live without water, without softness, without haste. They are not dead or mechanical. They are alive in a manner that has never needed to resemble flesh.

Most of the life is rooted.

The tall mica-reeds are among the simplest to understand. They stand in groves, flexible but rarely bending, their layered bodies full of fine conductive dust. Each reed is a column of decisions held in tension. It feeds on sunlight, heat rising from the ground, and the tiny electrical imbalances between mineral plates beneath it. When the air changes, the reeds do not sway as plants would. Instead, they recalculate their positions by minute internal shifts, changing the angle of their translucent layers so that heat, light, and charge pass through them in preferred patterns.

A grove of them is less like a stand of grass than like an organ of perception. It knows where shadows are, where cracks are widening, where distant stones remember being molten. If one reed is broken, the others around it alter their alignment, not in alarm exactly, but in correction. They do not heal one another. They restore the equation of the grove.

The fan-leaved things are more social.

Their broad plates overlap in canopies, each leaf dry and thin as polished shell. On one side they drink light. On the other they shed unwanted possibilities. The rattle they make in a breeze is not accidental. It is a language of contact, a clicking exchange of pressure, temperature, memory, and future stress. A whole terrace of fan-growth can pass a change across itself without anything visibly moving: one plate warming, another dimming, a third becoming briefly less probable.

They do not have flowers. Instead they produce angles.

At long intervals, a fan-leaf develops a bright seam along one edge. That seam sharpens for days or years, until it becomes a freestanding sliver of geometry. If the conditions are correct, the sliver detaches without falling. It simply appears on a nearby surface where it could have grown, and there begins unfolding into a new plant. If the conditions are not correct, it remains in place forever, a sterile blade of colored glass containing the idea of offspring.

The black filament mats are older, or at least deeper.

They spread low over the ground, over stones, over dead stems, across the undersides of plates. They are not fungi, but they do the work of inheriting what other lives leave behind. They digest contrast. Light entering a black garden slows because the filaments sample it, compare it to darkness, and keep the difference. They absorb failed shadows, unused heat, the faint charge of footfalls, the residue of choices not taken.

A black mat may look still for centuries. Beneath that stillness, it is busy with a kind of thought too distributed to be called thought. It has no center. It remembers by changing what can happen on top of it. A creature crossing such a mat may find that every third step becomes easier, or impossible, or already completed. The mat is not attacking. It is filing the event into itself.

There are shrubs made of arithmetic.

Each branch divides into seven, then seven again, and each subdivision is a test of available space. They grow where the basin’s contradictions are mild but persistent: places where distance is unreliable, where a stone may occupy two positions, where heat arrives before sunlight. Their leaves are small plates, satin-faced and chalk-backed, arranged with such precision that they catch not only light but expectation. They feed on regularity. They flourish wherever a pattern repeats.

These shrubs are among the few organisms that can be said to reproduce abundantly. They cast no seeds. Instead, when a branching pattern has proven stable, the plant impresses that pattern into the mineral around its roots. Later, perhaps far away, where similar stresses pass through similar stone, the same branching begins again. The child is not carried from the parent. The child is a recurrence.

The columnar giants near the basin rim are something else.

They are not the oldest beings there, but they are the most nearly individual. Their trunks are nested lattices, their crowns hexagonal umbrellas, their vertical channels full of motionless sparks. Each one contains long sequences of mineral memory: evaporated oceans, unborn weather, tectonic gestures that never finished, machines that may or may not have existed. To stand near one is to be borrowed by its remembering.

They do not think in words. They think in completed arrangements.

A giant column considers a problem by becoming the solution to it. If heat must be redirected, its crown alters. If gravity strays, its roots brace a counterargument into the stone. If a future fracture approaches, sparks in its channels rearrange until the fracture chooses another century. These beings are less like trees than like local constitutions. Around each one, a district of reality behaves more consistently.

They may be guardians, though not in any moral sense. They preserve coherence because coherence is the condition under which they exist.

The small moving creatures live carefully among these rooted powers.

The angular foil-skinned animals that cross open flats are quick, jointed, and intermittently present. Their bodies are made of folded mineral membrane over threads of charged dust. They do not run continuously. They occupy a posture, then select the next posture from among nearby possibilities. To an observer, this looks like jerky motion: pause, vanish, reappear, pause. In truth, they are not hiding. They are refusing the space between.

They feed from wounds, seams, and newly exposed edges. A fresh crack in salt-metal may attract one from a great distance. It will approach in precise increments, touch the exposed interior with its needle-mouth, and remove a measure of tension or charge. Sometimes it takes nourishment. Sometimes it repairs the crack. Sometimes the crack repairs it.

They have too many joints because they must negotiate too many versions of direction. Their limbs can fold forward, backward, inward, and toward adjacent probabilities. When threatened, they do not flee. They become the version of themselves that was never standing there.

The pale flat-bodied things under leaves are quiet grazers of accumulated light.

They cling with many fine hooks, their bodies translucent except for dim golden organs arranged like maps. They feed on radiance stored in the underside of plates, scraping it away in thin layers. A colony of them can make a leaf forget an afternoon. Where they have fed, the plant’s shadow changes shape.

They are tolerated by most of the rooted life. Perhaps they prune excess brightness. Perhaps they carry information from leaf to leaf. Perhaps they are larvae of something that only becomes visible under a different law.

Their young are almost two-dimensional. They hatch as pale stains on mineral surfaces and slowly gain thickness by eating reflected sun.

The airborne organisms are rare and beautiful in an austere way.

They do not have wings for lifting. Their circular membranes, pierced at the center, are instruments for choosing altitude. They drift overhead in groups of three or four, though “drift” is only the nearest word. They appear at one height, then another. Their bodies hang within the rings like dark knots of folded wire. They make no sound because they do not push against air. They negotiate with position directly.

They feed on gradients: warm below, cool above; bright outside, dim within; possible here, impossible there. Passing over a gravity cage, one may pause for hours, held in an invisible bargain. When it leaves, the suspended golden dust inside the cage may have rearranged into a new constellation.

Their reproduction is almost never witnessed. Once in a long while, a ringed flyer descends to the crown of a columnar giant and aligns itself with the hexagonal canopy. For a time there are two shadows, then six, then none. Afterward, a smaller ring may be found hovering in a nearby clearing, already ancient in its stillness.

There are insects, if one insists on the word.

Silver needle-mouths. Jointed prisms. Transparent mites that live inside crystal defects. Long, brittle walkers whose bodies are no wider than a line of script. They are not numerous. Nothing here breeds in clouds. Abundance belongs to the rooted and the architectural; motion is expensive and therefore exact.

Predation among them is formal. A silver insect may spend half a day approaching a seed-spine because the approach is part of the feeding. Each interval of nearness changes both creatures. The spine offers stored charge, but only under the correct geometry of attention. The insect offers mobility, novelty, and the memory of distances crossed. When the needle finally touches, there may be no tearing, no consumption. Instead, both become more transparent. Some substance has passed between them, but not necessarily matter.

Death exists there, but it is not collapse.

A living thing that has exhausted its charge does not rot. It clarifies. Its colors withdraw into edges. Its interior architecture becomes visible. It turns to framework, lattice, glass, powder, or a new kind of stone. The dead are not removed from the living world. They become addresses.

Other organisms root on them, compute through them, use them as braces for further impossibilities. A pale dead reed may stand for ages, emptied of itself but still useful as a remembered vertical. A fallen plate may become the floor of a microscopic grove. A dead insect may crystallize into a seed of direction, causing nearby roots to grow preferentially toward an angle no compass recognizes.

The dead are not mourned. They are referenced.

There may be intelligence there, but not concentrated in any animal head.

The basin itself behaves like a mind that has no need to wake up. Its thoughts are arrangements of shade, mineral charge, heat flow, spatial inconsistency, and the posture of living structures. A grove can know one thing. A terrace can know another. A black mat can remember what a column refuses to forget. The airborne rings may carry comparisons between distant regions. The angular walkers may serve as punctuation, moving events from one thicket to another.

No single inhabitant governs the place. No queen, no hidden machine, no god beneath the crust. Yet the whole basin has composure. It corrects itself. It incorporates disturbance. It notices without looking.

If a human entered, the residents would not gather around in curiosity. Most would continue. A few leaves would alter their angles. Black filaments would taste the new complexity of a wet animal moving through dry law. Foil-jointed creatures might keep farther away than distance explains. The great columns might offer, accidentally or not, memories impossible for the visitor to have earned.

The basin’s inhabitants would not consider the human alive in quite the proper way. Too fluid. Too temporary. Too dependent on internal weather. A walking bag of unstable chemistry, leaking heat, salt, intention, and time.

Still, the place is not cruel. It would not hate the intruder. Hatred requires a narrowing of attention that these beings do not practice. It would receive the human as it receives a shadow, a crack, a brief change in charge. It would test, store, redirect, and perhaps remember.

What lives there, finally, is a dry ecology of solved forms.

Plants that are also instruments. Animals that are also negotiations. Corpses that are also substrates. Shadows that are also food. Dust that is also deferred event. Memory that grows ribs and roots. Geometry with appetites. Mineral with patience. Life that never learned to be moist, never needed decay, never accepted that physics must remain singular.

They are quiet because they are not waiting.

They are still because motion is only one possible expression of being alive, and not the most efficient one.

They live as the basin lives: completely, dryly, under ordinary afternoon light, having already become what change elsewhere is still trying to reach.
