What lives there is not a people in the surface sense.

At first you would say: plants live there. Then, after a while, that would seem too small a statement, like saying a body is inhabited by flesh.

The true resident is a vast under-earth vegetal organism, though not one plant and not many in any easy way. It is a federation of roots, leaves, fungi, mineral skins, warm pools, slow sap-columns, and sealed chambers of growing tissue. It has no trunk that can be pointed to as its center. Its mind, if it has one, is distributed through pressure, moisture, light, and chemical patience. It thinks by allowing and refusing. A corridor remains open because it has not been closed. A pool stays clear because certain roots have chosen not to cloud it. A stone wall is kept standing because something in the green has decided it is useful as a rib.

It lives as the country itself.

Its surfaces are its senses. The pale hairs along the root tunnels feel footsteps long before anything arrives. Hanging veils of creeper taste the air for salt, breath, fear, pollen, rot. The luminous plates under the leaves widen or dim in answer to pressure changes too small for animals to notice. Nothing turns its head because there are no heads. Nothing watches, yet every open space is known.

The plants are not aggressive. That would require haste. They do not pounce, strangle, snap, or hunt in the theatrical manner of hungry things. Instead they revise. A dead moth is revised into a stain of nourishment. A fallen leaf is revised into floor. A wall is revised into trellis. A path unused for long enough is revised into tissue. Anything that remains too still is gradually given a place in the anatomy.

The smaller creatures live there by understanding this.

The pale lizards are nearly glass, with bones like faint writing inside them. They cling to warm seams of new stone and flatten themselves until they are no more than a change in sheen. Their eyes are dark pinpoints under translucent lids, but they do not seem to look outward often. They listen through their bellies. They feed on the soft fungal bloom that gathers where light leaks from leaf-veins, scraping it away with delicate, colorless tongues. When startled, they do not run. They go dim. Blood withdraws from their skin, and the body becomes a smear of mineral pallor against mineral pallor.

There are moths too, blind or nearly blind, bred for a world without night. Their wings are broad and powdery, patterned in rings and freckles that resemble spores, leaf-scars, old pollen burns. They hang upside down beneath glowing leaves, drinking condensed sweetness. They do not swarm. Each one keeps to its own dim territory of underside and vein. Their larvae are small, soft, and greenish white; they live inside the dry golden linings of split seed-pods, eating only what the pod has already abandoned.

In the pools are thread-creatures, long as fingers or long as hair, depending on age. They have no faces that can be recognized. They move by changing their idea of straightness. Most of the time they lie suspended in the warm clear water, each body a pale filament among rootlets. When something passes above, they alter the reflection without breaking it, making the ceiling waver, making leaves appear to breathe. They eat mineral milk, loosened skin from roots, and the fine dust of fungus that falls from the glowing vaults. Some become transparent with age. Some acquire a faint green core and eventually attach themselves to the roots, after which it is hard to say whether they remain animals.

There are also the slow shell-bearers.

They are seldom seen because they resemble seed-cases or knots of bark. Each carries on its back a little cup of hardened secretion, cream-colored and ridged like new limestone. Their bodies are dark, moist, and soft beneath. They move along the undersides of root bridges, polishing them with their passage. Their work is maintenance: they graze away excess films, open clogged pores, clean the breathing surfaces of the great plant-structure. If lifted, they withdraw completely into their pale cups and wait for days. The plants do not digest them. The shell-bearers are part of the permission.

But there are larger inhabitants, though they are difficult to separate from architecture.

In the central bright reaches, among the waxy spires and moss plazas, stand forms that might once have been people or might only be the country’s imitation of people after encountering their absence.

They are called nothing, because no one there calls.

They are upright growths, usually found in threes or fives, near old stone platforms or clay alcoves. At a distance they resemble narrow trunks wrapped in dry fiber. Closer, one sees suggestions of shoulders, folded arms, bowed heads. Their surfaces are bark-smooth in some places, root-matted in others. Pale mineral plaques form along their faces like unfinished masks. There are hollows where eyes would be, filled not with darkness but with a soft green-gold glow.

They do not walk. They do not speak. Yet they are not dead.

Moisture beads on them in patterns that change. Fine rootlets enter and leave the clay beneath their bases. Their inner fibers tense when footsteps cross a nearby court. If a moth settles on one, its surface loosens to accept the touch. If a lizard climbs it, a little warmth rises under the lizard’s feet. They seem to be stations of memory: places where the basin remembers uprightness, shelter, gathering, perhaps worship. They may be what became of the makers of the low walls. They may be the plant-country’s answer to them.

If they were once inhabitants, they did not vanish. They were included.

Their nests remain in alcoves, but empty nests here are not evidence of departure. Fiber bowls, seed cups, stones set in threes—these may be tools, offerings, organs, or thoughts hardened into arrangement. The upright growths keep near them as if tending a custom after forgetting its reason. Sometimes a split seed-pod above one of these alcoves releases a dry thread, and the thread drapes itself over the stone ring below. Nothing more happens for a very long time. That may be ceremony.

The whole country has a ceremonial quality, but without performance. It does not enact rites. It holds conditions in which rites could have happened, or may still be happening too slowly to witness.

The dominant life is patient beyond animal comprehension. It does not sleep, because sleep belongs to creatures divided from their surroundings. It does not wake, because waking implies interruption. Its days are not marked by sun but by intensities of inner radiance, by condensation forming and being reabsorbed, by the minute swelling of a pod, by the sealing of a fallen frond into the floor.

Its hunger is equally quiet. It wants warmth, mineral, vapor, stillness, and time. It prefers that things remain close. It dislikes running water because running water is escape. It dislikes wind because wind is rumor. It tolerates animals that move lightly and return what they borrow. It tolerates stone because stone gives brightness back. It tolerates ruins because ruins are shapes already willing to be held.

A stranger entering would not be attacked. Not at first. The place would test them by contact.

The floor would remember the weight of their steps. Leaves would gather the salt of their breath. Root-hairs would sample the oils of their hands if they touched the walls. Pools would hold their reflection and pass it downward into pale root systems. If the stranger kept moving, kept warm, kept separate, they might be allowed to pass through as one more temporary animal.

But if they slept too long in a moss court, the country would begin to interpret them.

First warmth would gather around them, comforting as blankets. Then tiny rootlets would grow into the edges of their clothing, not piercing, only fastening. Their exhalations would be caught and sweetened. Their hair would dampen with condensation. Dreams would become crowded with yellow-white light, with corridors that opened only where the plants permitted. On waking, they might find a green film over their boots, a fine net across their hands, a taste of sap in the mouth.

If they did not leave, the basin would make them less foreign.

That is how things live there: by becoming less separate.

Even the rare animals are not independent citizens but small clauses in a larger sentence. The lizards warm the stones and carry spores. The moths pollinate the undersides of leaves that never see wind. The thread-creatures stir the pools without disturbing them. The shell-bearers clean the breathing walls. The upright almost-people hold memory in place. Each is granted a niche so precise it feels less like habitat than role.

And beneath them all, around them all, through them all, lives the country of plants: bright, hot, enclosed, and unfinished-looking, though it may be older than weather. It is not waiting for inhabitants. It is inhabited by its own continuance.

It does not need to move in order to approach.

It does not need a voice in order to answer.

It does not need to kill what enters it.

It only needs to keep touching, feeding, shining, sealing, remembering—until every separate thing has been given a green place in the pause.
