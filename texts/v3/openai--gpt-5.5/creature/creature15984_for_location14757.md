The ones who live there are easiest to miss when they are still.

At a distance they look like part of the plain’s debris: a low stone heap, a collapsed trap, a sheet of slate tilted against another. They crouch so close to the ground that height seems foreign to them. Their backs are broad and shallow-arched, plated not with true shell but with overlapping callused skin hardened by mineral deposits, gray-black, blue-gray, sometimes pale where limestone has crusted into old scars. Rain collects on them exactly as it collects on the rock. In fog, a family at rest can vanish into the landscape so completely that only their breathing betrays them: a soft double-click from the throat, followed by the wet settling of gill-folds along the ribs.

They are not quite human, though some of their habits are human enough to trouble the distinction.

Their bodies are made for the horizontal world. Their legs are short, strong, and splayed, better for bracing on slick stone than for striding. Their feet have wide gripping pads and small hooked nails that find purchase in cracks too fine for a finger. Their hands are more delicate than their bodies suggest: long-palmed, six-fingered, with two opposed digits on each hand, capable of tying gut-cord, shaping clay seals, teasing translucent eels from fissures, and cutting warnings into stone before rain softens the mark. The palms are roughened like rasps. Children learn to read by touch before they learn to read by sight.

Their heads sit low between heavy shoulders. The face is blunt and downward-angled, made for looking at pools, channels, tracks, slick films on rock. Their eyes are large but dull-colored, without whites, filmed by a clear inner lid that slides sideways against rain and grit. They see movement well. They see shine better than shape. A still predator under a shelf may be invisible to them, but a tremor in a puddle, a new ripple crossing against the wind, a bead of condensation flowing the wrong way — these they notice instantly.

Their ears are not obvious. Sound enters through seams along the jaw and collarbone, through membranes protected under folds of skin. They listen with the sides of their heads, with their teeth, with the bones of their chests pressed to stone. When they are uncertain, they kneel and lay both hands flat on the ground. A person standing upright is considered inattentive, almost rude. The proper posture for thought is crouching.

They call themselves something that cannot be carried well into air-language: a sequence of throat pulses, tongue clicks, and a low vibration made with the sternum against resonant stone. Outsiders, if any survive long enough to need a name for them, might call them the Low Hearers, or the Seepfolk, or simply the Crouched.

They do not build upward unless they must. Height attracts wind, lightning, screaming birds, and notice. Their shelters are tucked into the lee of shelves or sunk partly below the surface, roofed with overlapping slabs so rain runs across them rather than through them. Entrances are narrow and angled, never facing the same direction twice in a settlement. A dwelling from outside may resemble a cairn or a trap. Inside it is warmer than expected, close with body heat, mineral damp, stored shell oil, cured gut, and the faint bitter smell of seep-films drying on racks.

They sleep in clusters, not for tenderness alone but for warning. One sleeper feels vibration through another. A tremor passing under the flats may move through the group before it reaches the ear. Their dreams are said to be communal during heavy rain. Whether this is belief or biology is hard to say.

They wear little that is woven, because there are no plants to give fiber. Their garments are layered from eel-skin, scraped membrane, gut-thread, softened throat sacs of the hook-beaked cairn nesters, and the flexible inner plates of shield-mollusks. Children wear strings of fired-clay beads that knock softly when they move; the sound lets adults locate them in fog. Adults often coat their shoulders and backs in pale mineral paste. It seals small cuts, disguises scent, and over years becomes part of their skin.

They do not farm. Nothing there will accept a seed.

Instead they tend seepage.

A good seep is inherited like a house, a parent, a history. Iron cracks, warm cracks, bitter cracks, blue cracks: each has its season and temperament. The Seepfolk scrape living tarnish from stone with bone combs, never taking all of it. They smear some into prepared hollows to encourage new growth. They block certain trickles with clay for a day or a week to thicken the film. They know which mineral skins feed children, which strengthen pregnant bodies, which cause shaking fevers, which bring visions, which preserve meat, which poison the shell-grazers but draw the blue eels up in knots.

Their herding is patient and indirect. Low crescent walls guide armored grazers into pools. Clay plugs redirect rainwater. A line of perforated stones can persuade mud-runners to veer without ever touching them. The Seepfolk do not think of themselves as masters of animals. Mastery is a vertical idea. They think in terms of invitation, pressure, interruption, and release.

Their language is mostly sound, but not loud sound. The plain is already loud enough. Among themselves they speak close to the body: clicks under the tongue, closed-mouth hums, little wet consonants shaped in the cheek, palm taps on stone, finger drags through water. Much can be said by striking a bowl and touching the listener’s wrist before the note fades.

Names are not fixed. A child’s first name is usually a weather event: Rain-That-Fell-Sideways, Warm-Sweat-Under-Stone, Three-Eels-Rising. Later names are earned from listening. One who hears gas under rock before it blooms may become Pale-Flame-Spared-Us. One who mistakes a predator’s silence for ordinary quiet may carry No-Sound-Bit-Deep for years. A very old person may have so many names that no one speaks them all except at death, and even then only if the weather allows.

They count, but not high. High numbers are less useful than patterns. Seven channels meeting. Three low hums before fog. Two days of bitter seep after blue eel knots. A silence lasting longer than one breath but shorter than fear. Their tallies are carved in stone, tied in cords, pressed into clay weights, or remembered as sequences of knocks. A good memory is not one that stores many things; it is one that does not confuse similar sounds.

They are cautious with fire. Flame is not sacred to them. It is a useful danger, treated with the same respect as a sleeping predator. Most cooking is done with heated stones carried from safe vents, or by placing sealed bowls in warm cracks. When fire is necessary, they light it in shallow clay basins set on tested slabs, with children sent far away and elders listening for the soft subterranean gathering of gas. A person who lights fire carelessly is not merely foolish. They are almost unthinkable.

The dead are not buried deeply. The plain does not welcome digging, and the understone things notice disturbance. Bodies are laid on high shelves during low rain, covered with flat stones, shell plates, and the broken tools of their last season. Over time water enters. Minerals enter. The body stiffens, pales, becomes something between fossil and memory. Later, descendants may remove finger bones or jaw plates to make listening charms. This is not considered desecration. Hearing continues where flesh does not.

Their children hatch in water, though they are born from bodies.

An infant is carried first as a swelling beneath the parent’s side plates, then released into a warm seep-pool lined with softened membrane. For several weeks it is more tadpole than child: blind, frilled, translucent enough that its small organs flicker blue in dim light. Several adults tend the pool. They sing into the water, not melodies but pressure-patterns, teaching the young the first grammar of vibration. When limbs unfold, the child is lifted out during rain and pressed belly-down to stone. The first lesson is always the same:

Listen before moving.

Their games are imitations of survival. Children race mud-runners across shallow pans. They hide among slate plates and try to fool the hook-beaked birds. They make miniature cairns and learn which holes whistle, which moan, which scream. They dare each other to touch bitter seep with one finger. They are scolded not for fear, but for untested courage.

The Seepfolk have priests, though they would not separate priest from engineer, musician, weather-reader, and mourner. These are the Basin Keepers, the ones who tend the ring of short pillars where seven channels meet. They clean the cups atop the stones. They replace cracked bowls. They keep old tools arranged not as offerings exactly, but as records of hands that failed, hands that learned, hands that survived a season long enough to leave something behind.

When the water runs high and the seven channels pour together, the Keepers crouch inside the roar with their chests against the pillars. The sound is too large for ears. It enters the bones. It blurs thought. Some come away bleeding from the nose and jaw seams. Some come away with weather knowledge. Some come away unable to speak for days except in clicks. A few do not come away at all, though no wound can be found on them.

No one agrees what the pillars are for.

The young say they are an instrument.

The old say they are a question.

The dying say they are a mouth.

Beneath all their customs lies the one fact they never forget: they are not the largest inhabitants of the plain.

There are things below.

The Seepfolk do not name them directly. Names invite attention. They describe effects instead: Water-Drawn-Down, Stone-Knock, Puddle-Ring, Silence-Under-Singers. Whatever moves beneath the surface-stone is too large, too slow, or too unlike ordinary animals to be hunted or worshiped safely. It passes through hidden veins and old hollows, pulling water after it. Sometimes it leaves circular pits where none were before. Sometimes it exhales through fractures, and the whole basin smells of hot iron and old eggs. Sometimes a channel becomes a throat.

The Seepfolk survive by yielding. They keep hands out of water during passages. They stop speaking. They lower their bodies until their mineral-crusted backs resemble stone. Even infants are taught not to cry when the ground knocks.

Their fear is practical, not superstitious. They know the plain is alive in many sizes.

To them, the world is not divided into living and dead. Stone feeds film. Film feeds grazer. Grazer feeds skulker. Skulker feeds bird. Bird bone becomes awl. Awl opens eel. Eel blood stains tally marks. Rain blurs the tally. The blurred groove holds water. In the water, new skin grows.

So the stone is not dead. It is slow food.

Their art is almost entirely acoustic. A beautifully made thing is one that sounds correctly in weather. Cairns are tuned to fog. Traps are tuned to tide. Bowls are shaped to reveal bitterness by pitch when struck. A shelter with a poor roof is not one that leaks — all roofs leak eventually — but one that drips without rhythm, making sleep and listening difficult.

They sing rarely in the open, because the blind amphibian choirs and screaming cairn birds own most of the air. Their private songs are low and percussive, shared inside shelters during long rain. They sing histories as changes in pressure: the year the warm cracks soured, the season of shell-plague, the child found alive in a bird cairn, the three families taken when someone lit fire over breathing gas. A beautiful voice is not sweet. It is accurate. It can imitate the warning hum of a coming fog so precisely that listeners feel cold before understanding why.

They have no word for wilderness. This is not because they feel safe. It is because nothing around them is unused. Every pool may be a nursery, trap, grave, mirror, poison source, drinking bowl, listening place, or mouth. Every slick stain may be food or fever. Every silence has shape.

They also have no word for conquest. The plain cannot be conquered. It can only be misheard.

In old age, they grow paler. Their plates thicken. Movement becomes difficult. Lichen-colored mineral crust gathers at joints and along the spine until some elders resemble carved stones more than animals. These elders spend much of their time at listening screens, palms resting on propped slabs, interpreting distant tremors for the settlement. They become, gradually, less like leaders than instruments.

When one can no longer move from the rain, they are carried to a sheltered shelf and set facing the basin. People bring them bowls of seep-water, softened grazer flesh, the clicking toys of children. The elder listens until listening stops. After death, their back plates may be set into a cairn. Wind moving through the holes will pass over them for years, making a tone the family recognizes.

The Seepfolk do not believe the dead go upward.

Nothing important goes upward.

Rain comes down. Seep rises. Sound travels through. Bodies return across. Memory remains in hollows.

If they have a prayer, it is not a plea but a discipline:

Hear before hunger.  
Test before fire.  
Leave film in the crack.  
Do not trust a quiet pool.  
Crouch when the stone knocks.  
Remember that the plain is older than fear.
