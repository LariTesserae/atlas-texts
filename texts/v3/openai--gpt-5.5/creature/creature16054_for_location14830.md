What lives there is not one species, not one kingdom, not even one layer of life. The inhabitants are a compressed society of cold, wet persistence: rooted, burrowing, swimming, clinging, nesting, dissolving, hunting, waiting.

The first citizens are the plants.

They do not merely grow in the sunken country; they hold it together. Moss is the oldest-looking resident even when it has arrived only recently. It lies over stone like memory, thick-backed and dark, swallowing edges, softening fractures, making ledges passable and walls habitable. In some places it is velvet; in others it is a waterlogged fleece that gives underfoot and exhales black soil. It shelters eggs, seeds, insects, sleeping voles, and the first pale threads of fungi.

The ferns are the visible crowd. They occupy the terraces and balconies, leaning out over water lanes in ranks, every frond damp and trembling. Some are small enough to fit in the palm; others grow shoulder-high where light strikes reliably, forming green rooms that animals enter and disappear inside. Their undersides are freckled with spores. When brushed, they release a faint brown dust that vanishes into the wet air.

The roots of surface trees are stranger residents: half-foreign, half-native. Above, their trunks may stand in wind and snow; below, their roots become architecture. They descend through cracks as ropes, ribs, curtains, pillars. Some are dark and barked over, thick as limbs. Others are newly exposed, raw, white, tender, searching blindly through air that should have been soil. They drink from the basin and feed the roof. Birds nest in them, beetles tunnel through their dead portions, fungi lace them, and small mammals use them as roads.

In the dimmer districts live the fungi.

They are patient roof-makers, ledge-makers, undertakers. Cold keeps them slow, but not weak. They climb dead wood in ivory shelves. They seam the undersides of stones with blue-gray crust. They send amber ears out from rotten roots and brown fans from buried branches. Their real bodies are hidden: pale threads running through leaf mold, moss, dung, drowned stems, abandoned nests. They bind the city from underneath, digesting what falls, making softness out of structure and soil out of endings.

The lichens are the dry-side dwellers, though dryness here is only a relative condition. They cling to undersides of shelves and high stone lips where water passes less often. They make maps in gray, black, green, and rust. They are slow enough to seem like stains, but they are settlers too, eating light and mineral, preparing stone for others.

In the pools and channels, algae live as green veils, brown slicks, transparent threads. Duckweed gathers in sheltered corners, opening and closing its small floating leaves as water rises and falls. Rootlets hang into the current like combs. The water is never just water; it is crowded with mouths too small to see.

Beneath all of this are the minute inhabitants: bacteria in the mud, rotifers turning in droplets, nematodes in films of water, mites among moss stems, springtails flashing pale and dark over decay, transparent worms in silt, eggs tucked into slime, larvae curled under leaves. They are the true multitude. Every handful of moss is a district. Every drop is a room.

The larger animals live as if sound were dangerous.

Small birds nest near the skylights, where root curtains catch enough warmth and light. They are dark-backed, compact, quick, with pale throats or faint speckling that vanishes in shadow. Their voices are not songs so much as signals: a tick, a threadlike fall of notes, a brief scrape of warning. They fly low between ledges, take midges from light shafts, drink from beads caught on moss, and vanish into root tangles when a shadow crosses the roof.

There are also birds that arrive only to hunt. A small owl may use the upper shelves, round and silent, its feathers made for this muffled country. It does not call often. It waits where a vole-run crosses an open patch of mud, where shrews must pass between fern roots, where the light from a crack lays a faint strip over moving ground.

The amphibians belong deeply to the place. They are bronze, olive, gray, sometimes almost black, with pebble-colored backs and cold golden eyes. They sit under moss lips, in reed shadow, half-submerged among stems. Their stillness is not sleep. A midge lowers too near; a beetle misjudges the water’s edge; something soft-bodied passes over a stone—and the amphibian is suddenly exact, then still again. In breeding season, their eggs hang in clear clusters among submerged roots, each dark center suspended in cold jelly.

The fish are pale but not ghostly. They are the color of gravel seen through water: silver-gray, brown-spotted, dimly banded, with wide eyes adapted to reflected light. They live in deeper channels, beneath root mats, under slab shadows. They know the tremor of each drip. A falling insect brings them up in quick tilts. When an otter-like hunter enters the lane, they scatter into side cracks and buried hollows where only water can follow.

Water beetles row through still pools with polished backs. Caddis larvae drag little cases of grit and stem. Cranefly larvae hang in mud, soft and hidden. Midges rise where light comes down, forming thin, wavering columns that look almost like smoke until a bird cuts through them.

The mammals are warm interruptions in the wetness.

Voles live in the moss banks and root mats, making tunnels that steam faintly with body heat. They chew sedges, seeds, fern bases, softened bark. Their paths are narrow but durable, pressed through generations of use. In winter, when the surface is sealed hard above, their hidden runs become especially important: warm corridors under a green roof.

Shrews are everywhere and almost nowhere, too restless to seem like residents. They hunt along the edges of pools, under dead leaves, through fern litter, inside root curtains. Their lives are hunger made into motion. They are small, sharp, damp-furred, with snouts that read the world faster than eyes could. Many things eat them, but while alive they are fierce little governors of the under-leaf world.

The swimming mammals are among the basin’s great travelers. They are otter-like, low and sleek, with dense fur that sheds water in black shining beads. They use the canals as streets, the central pool as crossing-place, and hidden chambers under overhanging roots as dens. Their slides polish mud banks. Their paws leave crescent marks in silt. They eat fish, amphibians, beetles, eggs, whatever the water yields. They are playful only when safe; otherwise they move with a narrow, muscular seriousness, as if the whole basin were a remembered map in their bones.

Foxes, or something close to foxes, come down from above. They are not true citizens in the way moss and shrews are, but they know the entrances. Lean winter bodies, wet paws, ears pricked forward, they descend along slumped cracks and high shelves to hunt. The basin gives them shelter from wind and scent-rich trails through mud. They do not linger in the lowest wet places. They keep to stone, root, and moss, appearing suddenly in a shaft of cold light, then passing out of it without sound.

Bats sleep in the upper dark. Not in great colonies, but in small folded gatherings tucked into dry seams, their bodies like dead leaves pressed to stone. At dusk and dawn they wake, loosen, drop, and skim the brighter hollows. Their wings write quick black curves through midge clouds. They drink by touching the pool surface mid-flight, leaving one ring among many.

There may be beavers, but if so they have become subtle here. They do not need to conquer a river; the basin is already half-engineered by water. They drag deadfall into useful narrows, wedge branches beneath root masses, encourage silt to gather, deepen a channel by returning to it. Their dams are not monuments but suggestions, collaborations with collapse and thaw. A person might walk past one and see only a tangle of old sticks doing what old sticks do—until the raised pool behind it, the new sedge bed, and the changed route of water reveal the animal mind in the arrangement.

No one species rules the place.

Light is contested by leaves. Water is contested by roots, larvae, fish, moss, mud. Warmth is hoarded in burrows, bodies, rotting wood, folded wings. Shelter is made and remade: a fallen slab becomes a fox path, then a fern wall, then a vole city, then a fungal shelf, then soil. The dead do not depart. They become housing, food, footing, shade.

If there is an intelligence here, it is distributed. It is not the intelligence of speech or tools, but of repeated acts fitting themselves to repeated pressures. The vole knows the safe moss tunnel. The fern knows the angle of light. The root knows water by touch. The fish knows the footfall through current. The fungus knows the fallen branch. The otter knows which channels remain open after thaw. The whole basin thinks in use.

Its inhabitants are quiet not because they are few, but because they are many and close together. Noise would travel poorly and betray too much. So they live by tremor, scent, moisture, pressure, dimness, habit. A drip can mean rain above, a shifted pebble, a fish strike, a paw on stone. A moving shadow can mean cloud, bird, fox, collapse. Every creature is listening, even the rooted ones in their slower way.

What lives there, finally, is a cold ecology in the shape of a city.

Its citizens have no walls except roots, no lamps except broken daylight, no streets except channels and worn moss paths, no monuments except slabs where the roof fell in. They are small, damp, watchful, and innumerable. They live by occupying every surface before emptiness can return. They are the basin teaching stone to become habitat.
