What lives there is mostly low, wet, patient, and hard to distinguish from the weather.

Life in the basin does not stand upright unless it must. It crouches, mats, clings, roots, floats, burrows, and slides. Anything too tall is punished by wind; anything too dry cracks; anything too slow to adjust is undercut, drowned, frozen, or buried. The successful creatures have learned to give way without surrendering. They bend, detach, re-root, migrate a few meters overnight, or let the water pass through them.

The oldest inhabitants are not the largest ones. They are the dark skin of the country: mosses, liverworts, lichens, cold algae, iron bacteria, fungal nets, engineered root-filaments so old and overgrown that no one remembers which were seeded and which arrived by spore. They live over everything. Stone, clay, polymer, metal, old concrete, sensor glass, bone. Their mats breathe slowly under rain and ice. They drink mineral seepage from below and cloudwater from above. In the dimness they shine with a drowned green-black light, not bright but persistent, like banked fire under wet ash.

Some of these mats are natural. Some are not. The difference has become mostly academic.

There are lichens that read acidity and open their pale cups only when the meltwater is safe for young roots. There are mosses with dark conductive threads that carry tiny charges during storms, feeding embedded sensors or warning nearby growth of flood pulses. There are floating plant islands whose root-beards include both living fiber and artificial filament, braided so closely that the islands steer a little when the current changes. They are not intelligent in a human way, but they are not passive. They gather around snags, pylons, warm vents, carcasses, fallen panels. They thicken where the water slows. They break apart before pressure can tear them completely and meet again downstream in altered forms.

The reeds are the basin’s loudest vegetation. They grow in black-green walls, jointed and sharp-edged, their stems coated in wax and ice. Their roots grip new sediment with a violence that looks almost angry. In storms they flatten in long waves and rise again, whispering and clacking. Birds nest inside them. Insects overwinter in their hollow stems. Small mammals tunnel at their feet. The reeds are less a plant community than a city with no streets, only vertical shafts, flooded rooms, and trembling corridors.

Among them live the little dark birds, more often heard as wing-flutter than seen. They are compact, waterproof things, slate, umber, or reed-brown, with pale eye-rings and narrow beaks for prying insects from wet sheaths. They move in bursts. A whole thicket may appear empty until one stem touches another in the wrong way, and then thirty birds spill sideways through the reeds without ever truly entering open air. They do not sing much. Song is wasted here. Instead they use hard clicks, wing-snaps, and flashes of white under-tail feathers, brief signals that can survive the wind.

The waterfowl are heavier, lower, almost sullen. They sit in the channels like stones until approached, then erupt with shocking force. Their bodies are dark and thickly insulated; their feet are broad, black, and warm-veined. Many have white or silver facial patches that show in mist like torn paper. They feed on marsh herbs, invertebrates, mineral algae, and the tender roots exposed after bank collapse. Their nests are anchored to floating mats, never trusted to one shoreline. The females nudge eggs deeper into moss when cold fronts come; the males stand like sodden sentries, vanishing when they turn sideways.

In the pools are frogs, or something close enough that the name has endured. They are small, blunt-headed, and wax-pale underneath, dark above, with skin that can endure both near-freezing water and brief warmth from vapor vents. Their calls are not croaks but ticks, clacks, and little knocking pulses, timed between gusts. Some inflate throat sacs not to amplify sound but to send vibrations through water and mud. In spring thaw, if the basin can be said to have spring, the pools tremble with their signaling. Their eggs cling to submerged moss in clear ropes, many of them lost when channels change course overnight.

There are insects in impossible numbers, though the cold slows them into strange manners. Pale midges collect under stone lips. Black gnats rise in dense, reluctant clouds during warmer vapor events. Beetles with oiled backs skate the margins of flooded ruts. Thin-legged flies lay eggs in the seams between ice and mud. Some insects have adapted to machinery as if to bark or stone: they pupate in the warmth of housings, drink condensation from sensor lenses, and cluster around faint status lights during the long blue dimness.

The long-bodied mammals own the channels.

They are sleek, low, muscular creatures, somewhere between otter, mink, and something more ancient. Their fur is so dark it seems blue when wet. Their ears are small; their whiskers are heavy and pale. They slide down clay banks on habitual routes polished smooth by generations. They hunt fish where there are fish, frogs where there are frogs, birds when they can catch them, and the soft bodies of cold marsh invertebrates when they cannot. They den in raised banks stabilized by roots and filament lattice, though they abandon dens quickly when the walls begin to slump.

They are clever in the way water animals are clever: not tool-makers, but route-rememberers, current-readers, thieves of warmth. They know which vents open after pressure drops. They know which maintenance stations hum through the night. They have learned to pry open certain sampling traps and to ignore others. Their tracks are among the most reliable writing in the basin: five toes, belly-drag, tail-line, claw-scrape, then the sudden disappearance into black water.

In the alder thickets move the larger animals.

They are seldom seen whole. A shoulder passes between wet trunks. Antlers or branch-like headgrowth lift, vanish, lift again. A flank glistens under rain. They may be descendants of deer, elk, reindeer, or some introduced browsing stock reshaped by generations of cold marsh and bad footing. Their hooves are wide and splayed, with flexible outer rims that spread on moss and grip stone. Their legs are scarred from hidden shale. Their coats are shaggy, dark at the roots and pale-tipped, so rain beads and freezes along them like seed pearls.

The antlers are not clean crowns. They are irregular, useful, often hung with moss, bark strips, and filament snagged from repair nets. In older animals, lichens grow on the rough bases. Small birds pick there. The animals browse alder, dwarf willow, reed shoots, cold ferns, and the mineral-rich herbs that flourish near vents. During floods they climb the black ridges and stand in groups with their heads down, steaming faintly, while water reorganizes the world below.

Their calves are born on the most temporary ground: floating mats, gravel bars, raised causeways, collapsed machinery roofs. This is not carelessness. Predators have trouble reaching such places quickly. The basin teaches from birth that safety is not a location but a timing.

There are predators besides the channel mammals. Some are birds: broad-winged marsh hawks that fly low because the cloud ceiling gives them no height; pale-faced owls that hunt in the dimmest hours, listening not for sound but for movement through reed tension; black gull-like scavengers that appear after floods to inspect stranded fish, drowned rodents, and uprooted nests. There are fox-sized things in the ridges, red-brown or charcoal, with narrow feet and ice-rimmed muzzles. They patrol the transitions: reed to gravel, bank to channel, thicket to causeway. They know the human stations and keep just outside the light.

Fish live where the channels have persisted long enough. They are lean, cold-water forms, black-backed and silver-sided, with large fins for holding position in sudden flows. Some feed on mineral films scraped from stone. Some are blind in one eye more often than chance would suggest, injured by gravel torrents, ice shards, or turbine vanes. Eels or eel-like things pass through seasonally, using flooded causeways and new channels as if the entire basin were a single breathing gill.

And then there are the human inhabitants, if they are still best called human.

They are few. The basin does not support crowds, and it punishes permanence. They live in raised stations, sealed huts, movable gantries, half-buried service shells, and narrow clusters of rooms built onto the lee sides of instrument pylons. No settlement grows large enough to become a town. A family, a crew, a research lineage, a maintenance order, a weather-keeping clan: these are the human scales here.

They are not pioneers in the triumphant sense. They do not clear the marsh or try to make the basin behave. They have inherited, or been assigned, a humbler task: to keep the corrections small enough that the land can continue becoming itself without tearing away the structures that listen to it. They watch water levels, root binding, bank failure, vent heat, species movement, ice formation, sediment load. They repair causeways that will be submerged again next season. They retrieve drowned sensors. They replace filament lattices. They mark new channels and erase old maps.

Their bodies and habits show the place.

They dress in layered skins of waxed fiber, smart membrane, felted insulation, and dull ceramic scales at knee, shoulder, and shin. Nothing shines unless it is meant to be found in rain. Their boots are wide, cleated, and flexible; many carry detachable marsh soles like small dark snowshoes. Gloves are never fully removed outdoors. Hoods are deep. Faces are wind-burned, eyes narrowed, lashes often beaded with water. Children learn balance before speed. They learn which moss will hold, which black water has no bottom, which fresh gravel sings hollow underfoot.

They do not shout much. Shouting is useless and tiring. Their speech has become mixed with gesture, lamp-flash, touch codes, and short-range bone-conduction murmurs. On the causeways they use hand signs broad enough to read through rain: stop, sink, current, vent, line live, animal near, return. Indoors, in the artificial quiet of sealed rooms, their voices seem too soft at first, as if they are afraid of waking the weather.

Many wear instruments as another people might wear jewelry: pressure beads at the throat, water-chemistry strips woven into cuffs, small anemometers on packs, old family altimeters polished by handling. Children are given their first personal flood alarm early. Elders carry analog staffs marked with historic water heights, because batteries fail and memory matters.

Their culture is hydrological. They name not hills but flows: the channel that opened in the Red Thaw; the pool where the west mast fell; the ridge that steams before a north-front; the reedfield that eats causeways; the clay bank that calved three times in one night. A person’s reliability is judged by whether they remember ground as changeable. To say someone “walks like stone” is not praise. To say they “hears water under moss” is.

They keep few large animals. Dogs, if present, are low, rough-coated, and quiet, trained to freeze rather than bark. Pack beasts fare poorly except on the firmer ridges. Small domestic fowl are kept in suspended coops near stations, though the marsh hawks make this difficult. More often the people rely on preserved stores, cultivated cold greens, vat-grown protein, fish traps, and negotiated harvests from the basin itself. They gather herbs during brief warm venting. They cut reed only in rotating plots. They take moss plugs the way others take cuttings from orchards, replanting as they go.

They are intimate with machines, but not dependent on domination.

The machines are residents too.

Some are obvious: leaning masts, sealed buildings, black windows, warning lights, pump throats, maintenance drones crouched under overhangs like metal crabs. Others are nearly indistinguishable from habitat. A bank-stabilizing filament web tightens when clay slumps, then loosens as roots take hold. Current vanes flex like submerged leaves. Sensor grains migrate through runnels, gathering data, releasing trace nutrients, stiffening where erosion accelerates. Little repair crawlers live under stones and in conduit hollows, emerging after storms to mend breaks in lines no human could reach safely.

They are not masters of the place. They are more like a nervous system the basin has reluctantly grown around. They feel pressure, salinity, vibration, temperature, root density, ice shear. They respond slowly unless crisis demands speed. Their intelligence, if it can be called that, is distributed and maintenance-minded. They prefer prevention to action. They have no monuments, no voices except hum, click, pulse, and the faint glow that runs along slack lines when storms press low.

The humans speak to them as one speaks to working animals, weather, and old relatives: practically, sometimes affectionately, sometimes with irritation.

“West lattice held.”

“North vane jammed again.”

“Grains seeded too far downslope.”

“Old mast is singing before the pressure drop.”

Whether the machines understand tone is uncertain. The people use tone anyway.

There are also those who live between categories. Not mythical beings, not ghosts, but the ambiguous products of a landscape where biology and maintenance technology have touched for generations. Reedbeds that route electrical charge away from nests. Fungal masses that preferentially grow over cracked housings and keep them insulated. Marsh herbs that accumulate rare metals around sensor fields. Fish that spawn in the warm outflow of pressure vents according to schedules shaped by machine maintenance cycles. Birds that weave colored wire into nests because it sheds water better than grass.

The basin does not care about purity. If a thing holds, feeds, signals, shelters, or adapts, it is accepted until the next flood tests it.

The dead are important inhabitants too. Fallen birds become insect bloom. Drowned mammals feed channel scavengers. Old machines become trellises, burrows, mineral sources, nesting towers. Human dead, when the weather allows ceremony, are not buried deep; the ground is too wet and too young. Some are placed in stone cairns on ridges. Some are given to reed plots in wrapped forms that roots can enter. Some stations keep memorial panels whose surfaces are so overgrown with lichen that names must be traced by touch.

Nothing here disappears cleanly. It is incorporated.

The dominant character of all the basin’s life is attentiveness. Not peace, not savagery, not abundance in the easy sense. Attentiveness. Every creature listens through some part of itself. Hooves feel tremors under moss. Roots taste fresh silt. Birds read reed tension. Frogs pulse into black water. Machines measure pressure in clay. Humans watch the angle of rain and the color of foam. Even the moss seems to know when to swell and when to grip tight.

The basin’s inhabitants are therefore not conquerors of a frontier. They are negotiators in a country that is still deciding its terms. They live by yielding early, anchoring deeply, moving before certainty, and trusting nothing merely because it was true yesterday.

They are wet, dark, clever, cold-resistant, half-hidden, and constantly revised.

They live in the roar.
