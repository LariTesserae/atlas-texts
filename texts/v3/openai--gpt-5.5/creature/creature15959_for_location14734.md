What lives there is not a civilization, though from a distance it can resemble one.

It is a population of processes wearing bodies.

The plateau’s inhabitants are creatures of heat, charge, pressure, vibration, and phase. They are not adapted to the desert in the way a cactus or lizard is adapted to dryness; they are made from dryness as a first principle. Water would not nourish them. It would interfere with them. It would soften contacts, dissolve salts, short circuits, invite rot, blur edges. To them, moisture would be a kind of corruption.

They live by remaining exact.

Most of them are partly mineral. Some are mostly mineral. Flesh, where it exists, is not wet muscle but a dry, fibrous, ceramic tissue: layered, conductive, tough, and brittle in different directions. Their bodies contain vanes, plates, vents, columns, seams, hinges, resonant chambers. They do not breathe air so much as exchange pressure with it. They do not eat food so much as harvest gradients: thermal difference, static charge, torsion in stone, alternate geometries briefly made available during the plateau’s phase breakdowns.

They are animals, but each one is also a tool the plateau uses to keep itself possible.

The clinker-beasts are among the most obvious because they touch the ground.

They are low, deliberate creatures, black and rust-red, their bodies cooled into shapes like slag that remembered walking. Each has many limbs, though not always the same number from one day to the next; limbs are lost, regrown, incorporated, or reassigned as the crust demands. Their hides are made of overlapping plates cracked with faint internal glow. Beneath those plates runs a dry conductive tissue, packed with filaments that can open or close like bundles of wire.

They graze on electrical tension. At noon they spread their branching mouthparts across the salt pans and draw charge out of the crust in blue threads. This is not feeding in a soft sense. It is more like winding a spring. Afterward their plates lift once, release a quiet metallic sigh, and settle.

They migrate in vast sparse herds, each animal keeping a great distance from the others because proximity confuses their fields. A herd may cover an entire basin and still appear, to an observer, as a handful of solitary beasts crossing an empty world. Their social life is conducted through the ground: tiny pulses, delayed clicks, sympathetic tremors sent through salt and glass. They know one another by resistance.

They are slow because speed would waste definition.

Above them live the vane-swarms.

Each vane is thin, translucent, and almost weightless, a radial body arranged around a dark bead of dense sensory matter. They do not fly by effort. They trim themselves against rising heat. Their fins open and close by fractions, correcting constantly, like living shutters. In a thermal column millions of them form a silver cylinder, each individual both separate and part of a larger regulatory body.

They feed on rising ionized gas, on pressure pulses, on the faint chemical bitterness of vent exhalations. Their dark central beads store instructions, but not memories as animals elsewhere would understand memory. A vane remembers angles. It remembers pressure intervals. It remembers the difference between one hour’s venting and the next.

When the vents inhale, the swarm collapses to the ground in spirals and plates itself over the salt. For a while the plain becomes armored in delicate bodies. Then, when pressure returns, they stand on edge and lift again.

Their swarming is not merely movement. It tunes the air.

Without them the vent columns would tear, shear, and misalign. The vanes smooth them. They make the atmosphere usable.

Higher still are the great drifters.

They are enormous buoyant animals, slow enough to be mistaken for pieces of weather, except that there is almost no weather there. Their bodies are ribbed envelopes of heat-retaining tissue, inflated by gases hotter and drier than the surrounding air. They have no faces. Sensory pits cluster along their forward ridges in black arrays, reading charge, vibration, radiant heat, and the subtle instability of space below.

A drifter is less an individual than a floating district.

Its underside carries dependent life: hook-bodied clingers, cable worms, small regulating vanes, parasites that siphon heat from its seams, cleaners that scrape crystallized salts from its vents, and tool-like animals that unfold only when the drifter lowers its filaments to harvest charge from the plateau. These hanging communities may live for generations without touching the ground except through conductive tendrils.

When a drifter passes overhead, it hums the crust into agreement. Stones form rings beneath it. Glass pans answer with low tones. Vane-swarms rise toward it, some to feed, some to mate, some to be eaten, some because the old pattern says they must.

The drifter’s filaments are black, flexible cables of keratin, mineral, and nerve. Where they touch the salt, white fire crawls outward. The animal drinks the stored tension of entire flats, then releases part of that energy as stabilizing vibration. It is a grazer, a weather system, and a maintenance engine.

The plateau keeps its sky by means of such beasts.

There are also the slab-hunters.

They are difficult to count because counting requires noticing them before they act, and they have evolved to be indistinguishable from geology. A slab-hunter may wait for decades on an erosion terrace, tilted at the angle of fallen stone, its skin collecting dust and salt, its mouth sealed as a fault line. Its metabolism is almost nonexistent until prey crosses within its jurisdiction.

Then one edge alters.

Not quickly. A degree. Less than a degree. A seam opens in the world.

The prey continues walking, often unaware that the first part of it has already been claimed. The hunter does not pounce; it revises adjacency. A leg, a plate, a tendon of conductive fiber becomes “inside” rather than “outside.” Hours later the rest follows. The mouth closes with the patience of tectonics.

Slab-hunters digest by pressure and reinterpretation. Bone, plate, charge, and positional history are separated and stored in layers. Old hunters contain many former paths. If cut open — though almost nothing can cut them — their interiors would look like stratified maps of vanished migrations.

During the late-afternoon phase loosening, stranger inhabitants appear.

These are not visitors from elsewhere. They are residents of versions of the plateau that usually cannot quite coexist with this one. The breakdown gives them thickness.

Thread-grazers extend from cracks in the air. They are almost two-dimensional, long as wires, narrow as scratches, and they feed on angles: the tension between one possible orientation and another. They browse along polygon borders, mesa edges, animal shadows, and the corners of sounds. Where many have fed, the landscape becomes briefly smoother, less likely to choose a violent geometry.

Bell-animals descend through invisible surfaces without falling. Their bodies are translucent membranes stretched over rings of mineral cartilage. They chime when their rims strike boundaries between adjacent orders of space. Some are small enough to fit in a hand; others are the size of towers that were never built. Their chiming is reproductive. Each note knocks loose a cluster of smaller bells from a possibility layer below the audible world.

There are eel-forms made of transparent hinges, swimming through heat shimmer as if through liquid glass. They prey on vane-swarms by appearing simultaneously ahead of and behind them. There are black triangular things that fold around pockets of high pressure. There are shadow-beasts whose bodies remain elsewhere while their digestive shadows pass over the ground, absorbing weak or delayed organisms. There are sound-only animals that cast shadows only when they call.

Many of these creatures cannot survive once the plateau returns to a single dominant order. They withdraw, flatten, or become dormant possibilities embedded in crystal seams. Some leave eggs that are not objects but arrangements: a particular tension among three stones, a pitch stored in a glass pan, a future route inside a clinker-beast’s migration.

Reproduction there is rarely intimate.

The inhabitants do not gather in nests, burrows, or dens. They seed conditions.

A clinker-beast may leave behind a charged plate of shed armor. If later crossed by the right vent pulse and shadowed by a drifter, that plate may crack into a cluster of small black walkers. Vane-swarms reproduce when their coordinated flashes reach a certain harmonic inside a thermal column; the dark beads of the oldest individuals divide, and new fins precipitate around them from mineral dust. Slab-hunters bud along fault lines after absorbing enough path-history. Bell-animals ring new bells into being.

The plateau itself participates in every birth.

No creature there is born from parents alone. Each requires heat, timing, mineral grammar, and the consent of the hidden engine.

Their senses are correspondingly unlike those of wetter animals. Eyes exist, but vision is secondary and often too crude for such a bright place. They sense stress in stone, charge in crust, pressure beneath vents, the nearness of phase breakdown, the future positions of shadows. Many can perceive not only where something is, but how firmly it has committed to being there.

A weakly committed object is prey.

A strongly committed object is terrain.

This is why their movements seem ceremonial. They do not merely cross the land; they confirm it. The clinker-beasts draw paths through competing basin geometries. The vanes regulate columns of gas. The drifters stabilize the sky with hum and charge. The slab-hunters remove surplus bodies and surplus possibilities. The phase-creatures harvest contradictions when contradictions become plentiful.

Together they form an ecology, but also a factory.

The factory produces no goods. It produces continuity.

Every pulse, migration, feeding, predation, hum, chime, and shimmer helps manufacture the next usable interval of time. Duration is not assumed there. It must be laid down, annealed into salts, conducted through beasts, resonated through glass pans, hammered into sequence by the buried engines. The animals are not workers in a metaphorical sense. They are the labor by which cause continues to precede effect.

If there is intelligence among them, it is not centralized. There are no planners, no priests of the vents, no overseers in the high mesas. But the whole living system behaves as if it has learned one immense lesson: reality can persist if enough bodies agree to maintain it.

So what lives there are maintainers.

Dry beasts. Mineral herds. Living regulators. Predators like faults. Floating furnaces with dependent cities under their bellies. Swarms that trim the air. Temporary organisms from adjacent versions of the world. Creatures that eat charge, pressure, geometry, and unrealized routes.

They are not gentle. They are not cruel. They have almost no relation to mercy because mercy belongs to worlds where things soften.

They are exact, patient, and innumerable.

And beneath ordinary sunlight, on a plateau empty enough to look abandoned, they keep mining causality from stone.
