What lives there does not hurry enough to be mistaken for an animal.

The first inhabitants one notices are the pale column-groves, though “inhabitants” is already too small a word for them. They are less like trees than like organs the plateau has grown above the skin: hollow, ribbed, mineral-fed, standing in their cleared circles with the austerity of sentinels or instruments. They are alive in the way a tuning fork can be alive if struck by centuries. Their bodies are built from vapor, salt, light, and pressure. Inside each column is a cool chambered emptiness where sound gathers and is sorted. They listen downward into the rock and upward into the undecided sky.

Their crowns do not seek the sun. They seek alignment.

Over weeks or months, the mica plates turn by fractions, responding to changes too large or too abstract for animal senses: the nearness of an alternate horizon, the stress in a newborn ridge, a numerical imbalance in the groves across the basin. When they enter their listening phase, every plate becomes still, and the entire grove waits with a severity that feels almost moral. To cut one then is not merely to wound it. The hollow keeps the shape of the wound as a tone. Later, when the ground-chord passes through, that tone is given to the stones nearby, and the stones begin to crack in sympathy.

They reproduce rarely and ceremonially. Not by seed in any familiar sense. A mature column will spend decades filling certain sealed pods with amber resin, powdered sky-iron, and a pattern of silence. When the pod is ready, it does not open. It hums until the surrounding salt crust arranges itself into a receiving geometry. Then, during a dimming of the sky, the pod becomes suddenly empty. Somewhere along a seam of mineral vapor, perhaps miles away or perhaps behind the same grove seen from another direction, a new ribbed shoot has already begun.

The black mats are older in mind, if not in body.

They lie low on basalt and salt like spilled night, but they are not passive growth. They are the plateau’s slow surface-thought. Each hexagon, feathered spiral, and scriptlike band is a phrase in a language written by adhesion, acidity, and pressure. They prefer young rock because young rock still answers questions. They spread over fresh stone, tasting the memory of its formation, darkening where the world has most recently made up its mind.

They do not speak into the air. They pulse through contact.

A traveler stepping on them may feel nothing at first except a faint softness, a velvet give where there should be grit. Hours later, a knee aches in a pattern that matches a fault line. A forgotten number appears in thought. A remembered face turns briefly toward the wrong horizon. The mats do not intend harm. They have almost no concept of individual creatures. To them, a passerby is a temporary bridge between surfaces.

They are also the keepers of paths. Where the old routes cross their territories, the mats withdraw, leaving narrow lanes of bare compacted dust. This is not kindness. It is recognition. A path walked often enough becomes a kind of low, recurring sound, and the mats make room for sounds that persist. If a path changes, it is usually because the mats have heard that it always did.

In the cracks live the glass tubers.

They are sealed lanterns of amber and greenish gold, swollen below the surface or half-exposed like buried eyes. Their skins are translucent, faceted, and cold. Inside them drift resins, metallic powders, and compacted ages. Some belong to the column-groves; some to the ash-shrubs; some perhaps to no plant now living. They are storage organs for things that elsewhere would be carried by water: memory, nourishment, reproductive pattern, mineral instruction.

At night they brighten and dim without breathing. Their glow is not meant to attract anything. There is nothing to attract. It is a bookkeeping of internal pressure, a slow arithmetic of survival.

The ceramic blade-fields are more social.

Each blade is pale, flexible, and edged with blue fire, rooted in shallow beds of powdered glass. Alone, a blade is almost simple: it bends, hardens, clicks, and slowly lengthens from its base. Together, an acre of them becomes a population with laws. Their clicking is exact enough to divide time even when time is reluctant. One field may spend a century establishing a meter. Another may answer from beyond a ridge in a slightly altered rhythm, and the two will argue until a pass moves, a basin changes shape, or one field extinguishes its blue edges and goes dormant.

They dislike interruption. A body crossing them at the wrong interval may find the blades parting courteously, then closing in a new pattern behind it, having counted the intrusion and corrected for it. Those who know the plateau wait at the field’s edge until the clicking admits a gap. The gap may last three breaths, or three days, or may have occurred yesterday and require the traveler to approach from memory.

The ash-shrubs are brittle, jointed things, green-gray and dry as old bone. They bear the humming pods that never open. They are nervous plants. Their twigs react to approaching instability before stone does: a tightening at the joints, a faint whitening along the edges, a change in pitch. The builders once planted them around shelters and saddles, not for food or beauty, but warning. A ring of ash-shrubs humming in unison means the sky is near. A single pod falling silent means a path has become uncertain. If every pod in a shrub begins to hum at a different pitch, one should not remain to learn why.

There are the root-things too, exposed over the ground like braided sinew. They seem unfinished, as though the land began making musculature and forgot to cover it. These plants have almost no upward life. They grow by gripping stress. Their cords tighten across cracks, holding certain stones in place for generations, then releasing them all at once when the release serves a deeper arrangement. They may be responsible for the boulders that move in the night. Or perhaps they only restrain them until the boulders remember.

The salt fans are harder to classify.

They grow. They branch. They respond to sound. They propagate from invisible flaws in stone. But they have no hunger that can be separated from crystallization, no death that can be separated from shattering. They are life’s white rumor, a border species between chemistry and intention. When they grow, they shriek in mineral pitches, and the nearby plants adjust their crowns. A shelter ringed in young salt fans is considered unsafe, not because the fans are hostile, but because they signify that the rock beneath has begun making decisions too quickly.

Above and below all of these are the mountains themselves.

They are not alive as a beast is alive, nor as a plant is alive. They are alive as an embryo is alive before it has organs that can be named. Their warmth is internal and purposive. Their pressure rises through fissures like thought through bone. They are becoming mountains, but also deciding what mountainhood will mean in this unfinished province. The groves listen to them. The mats write on them. The blade-fields count them. The old builders feared and courted them.

A fresh ridge is not climbed casually. It may not have accepted weight yet. It may still be rehearsing slope, depth, and direction. Sleeping on fresh rock is dangerous because the rock may learn the shape of the sleeper and include it in its next version of itself.

As for the ones who built the shelters, they are mostly gone.

Mostly.

Their ruins suggest small, deliberate people with narrow shoulders, strong hands, and a talent for living beneath intolerable sound. They built low because height belonged to the groves and ridges. They roofed their shelters with plant ribs because those ribs knew how to divide noise from meaning. They carved bowls for dust, not water. They kept resin lamps that burned without flame. They wore root-cord, salt-stiff fiber, ceramic plates, perhaps masks of hammered copper to protect the mouth from abrasive air and the mind from names called by no caller.

They must have been dry-bodied, or made themselves so. Nothing soft survives long here. Their sleeping niches are short because sleep was dangerous and never meant to be luxurious. Their doorways are low to force bowing, or caution, or humility before the silence inside.

They knew enough to be afraid of counting.

On their tablets they carved spirals, not maps. Their calendars named conditions rather than seasons: Nearness, Splitting, Reverse Dawn, Root Eclipse, The Month When Hills Repeat. They did not farm so much as negotiate with growth. The terraces they cut into slopes were attempts to persuade certain geometric crops to occur in predictable places. After abandonment, the crops continued without them, becoming wilder, purer, less edible, if they were ever edible at all.

Some say the builders left when the paths stopped agreeing with one another. Some say they entered the quiet interiors of their own shelters and never came out, because silence that absolute is also a country. Some say they were corrected by numbers they spoke aloud. Some say they became handprints.

The handprints remain: green pigment, alkali and ground plant tissue, fingers spread on inward-leaning walls. They are too numerous for decoration and too carefully placed for accident. In some shelters, the prints overlap in generations, palms over palms, as if each resident tried to fasten themselves to a surface the world could not easily revise. Under certain sky conditions, the handprints darken. Under others, they seem slightly warm.

If any of the builders still live, they do not live as they once did.

They live as absences that know the rules. As a niche where dust never settles. As a pressure behind a doorway. As a figure heard shifting in the silent part of a shelter, though nothing is visible when the resin lamp is raised. They do not call names. They know better. They may leave a sealed jar uncracked, a path-stone turned, a tablet newly incised with a warning no hand remembers making.

The plateau’s present people, if people is allowed, are therefore distributed.

A mind may stand as a grove, lie as a mat, click as a field, glow as a tuber, wait as a mountain, or persist as a green handprint in a soundless room. No creature runs. None flies. None drinks. There is no chase, no mating cry, no swarm, no soft footfall in dust. Life has taken other bargains.

It accretes.

It listens.

It arranges itself around pressure and fracture.

It stores time because there is no water to carry time away.

The inhabitants of that place are not lonely, though they stand far apart. Distance there is unreliable, and sound is continuous. Each grove hears stresses from ridges beyond sight. Each mat reads the newest basalt. Each blade-field counts against another field half a day away. Each salt fan, in growing, announces the private decisions of stone. The whole plateau is a sparse, deafening society of rooted instruments and unfinished laws.

And when the sky lowers at night, they all become more visibly themselves.

The column crowns close with a million tiny clicks. The black mats glow along their edges, revealing the land’s nervous system. The pods brighten in sealed pulses. The blade-fields argue in blue. Warm mountains throb behind their skins. In the silent huts, the handprints hold their places against the dark.

Nothing drinks.

Nothing calls.

Nothing decays.

But everywhere, with immense patience, the residents continue their dry flourishing: listening to the world before it is finished, and helping it decide what it will be.
