What lives there is not a forest, exactly, and not a grassland, and not a desert. It is a civilization of plants without citizens, a continent of bodies so old and so intergrown that individuality has become approximate.

The first inhabitants are the ground itself.

Beneath the ivory crust lie flat, vast organisms whose lives are measured in uplift, abrasion, and the slow migration of mineral seams. They are not mosses, though from far away their surfaces can look like pale moss hardened into enamel. They are not lichens, though they share that patience. They are pressure-skins: layered, photosynthetic, vascular plains stretched over root-masses kilometers deep. Their upper surfaces are dry, reflective, armored with silica scales and waxy plates. Underneath, in darkness and tension, they store vapor, gases, sugars, dissolved salts, and the tiny rationed histories of ancient moisture.

They breathe rarely, but when they do, they do it by the horizon.

A single plain-organism may keep its pores sealed for months, even years, while its internal chambers swell. It listens through tension. It feels light, mineral load, root pressure, electrical charge, the tug of distant kin opening somewhere beyond the visible world. Then, at the right imbalance, it opens. Not gently. Not like a leaf uncurling. More like a valve-field released across a country. Its breath becomes weather. Its exhalation lofts pollen, grit, winged animals, spores, scent, charge, warning, hunger, and seed.

These ground-beings are the oldest residents. Everything else lives on them, inside them, between them, or by stealing from their rhythms.

Rising from them are the tower plants.

They are the most visible inhabitants: pale fluted columns, ribbed chimneys, organs of bark and glass. Some grow alone for hundreds of kilometers, so that each one makes its own province. Others stand in widely spaced ranks along buried fault-roots, seven or nine or twelve giants making what passes here for a grove.

Their trunks are hollow, but not empty. Inside are chambers stacked like lungs, mills, wombs, and resonators. Dry gases move through them with enormous force. Seeds are polished in internal drums. Mineral dust is sorted by electrostatic hairs. Pollen is stored in sealed bladders until pressure-bloom. Some towers grow lightning collars: dark copper-green bands around their upper shafts, burnished by repeated strikes. Others carry crowns of mirror-vanes that open at dawn with a sound like knives being drawn.

They are not trees in the soft, generous sense. They give little shade except by accident. Their leaves are instruments: slats, mirrors, black absorptive blades, transparent lenses, mica fans. Each moves according to light angle, wind stress, internal pressure, and the needs of the root-field below. A tower may spend three hundred years building the mineral strength to flower once. Its flower is not beautiful until one understands it: a hard aperture opening in the bark at night, exposing a cage of crystalline filaments that vibrate with a single clean pitch. Pollinators come to that sound. So do thieves. So do predators.

Around the towers live the small things.

Most of them are sealed.

There are vent-mites, no larger than seeds, flat as flakes of translucent horn. They live on the lips of pores and root-valves, scraping dried sugars and mineral exudates with rasping mouthparts. Their bodies are clear enough that the green smear of their gut shows through. During pressure-blooms they clamp down with all their legs and become part of the surface. Those that fail are blown into the sky and may colonize another vent a thousand kilometers away.

There are bead-eyed pollinators with jointed bodies like polished husks. They do not drink nectar in liquid form. They harvest dry sweetness: crystal grains, waxy dust, charged pollen, resin crumbs softened only by enzymes in their mouths. Their tongues are not tongues but bundles of vibrating filaments that loosen reproductive powder from the crystalline flowers. At night they swarm in glowing veils, each body clicking against the next, making a communal static that guides latecomers through the dark.

There are needle-flies whose wings are stiff vanes, almost mineral. They cannot take off from stillness. They climb stems and wait for vents to open, then let the upward rush throw them into motion. Their lives are routes between explosions. They mate in turbulence. Their eggs are glued into cracks with resin harder than shell.

There are clamp-backed grazers in the root-joints: pale, eyeless, many-legged creatures with hooked feet and wedge heads. They chew at exposed cambium and retreat instantly when chemical alarms flood the tissue. Some towers tolerate them in small numbers because their gnawing keeps pores open. Others seal whole galleries around infestations, embalming generations in resin.

There are the chaff-walkers, which at first seem like scraps of dead leaf tumbling in wind. Up close they are animals with folded sail-bodies and tiny gripping limbs. They cross the plains in erratic bursts, waiting for gusts, throwing themselves flat when shadows pass overhead. Their camouflage is almost perfect because the world is full of dry fragments, and they have learned to become another fragment.

The larger animals are sparse, but not rare. They are simply spread at the scale of the vegetation.

The herds are long-distance bodies: high-legged, narrow-chested, dust-colored, built like hunger walking over mirrors. Their ribs show not because they are starving but because a deep barrel would waste heat and vapor. Their nostrils are labyrinths of cooling plates. Every exhale is filtered, reclaimed, condensed internally. Their dung is dry pellets wrapped in mineral film; many seeds require passage through them before they can wake.

Some herds browse on leaf-scales as hard as ceramic. Their jaws are massive, stone-faced, moving sideways with slow precision. They do not crop plants so much as fracture them. A feeding line of these beasts can be heard before it is seen: crack, grind, pause; crack, grind, pause. They favor the older vanes shed from tower crowns and the margins of radial mats, where defensive chemistry is weakest.

Others follow flowering vents. These are more delicate animals, though still tough by gentler standards. They have split lips, combed tongues, and forelimbs adapted for bracing against gale. When certain plants extrude dry nectar crystals, the animals arrive from impossible distances, guided by scent carried through high air and tremor carried through root-crust. They lick, scrape, and swallow frantically for the few hours before the vents seal. Then the plain closes, and they move on.

Most striking are the moss-spined herd-beasts.

They are broad-backed, slow until alarmed, with rows of green-black filaments rising from their skin like a portable thicket. The growth on their backs is not wet moss but a symbiotic photosynthetic pelt, each spine a hollow blade that drinks light and trace vapor from the beast’s breath and blood warmth. In return it shades the animal, stores emergency sugars, and releases bitter compounds when predators bite. Calves are born bare and dangerously reflective. For their first season they stand beneath the bellies of the adults while fragments of the family’s spine-growth are pressed into their skin.

When these herds run, the spines whistle. A fleeing mass of them sounds like a field of flutes being dragged through a storm.

The predators are built from patience.

Speed is expensive here. A chase wastes vapor, tears skin, overheats muscle, and announces itself through the living ground. So the hunters wait.

Under root-arches lie the long-boned listeners: low, sinewy carnivores with forelimbs folded against the crust and skulls shaped to conduct vibration. Their ears are reduced, protected, almost irrelevant. They hear with jaw, sternum, elbow, and claw. Through the root-web they read the plateau’s enormous percussion: the click of hooves, the tremor of seed-fire, the shiver of vent-mites, the heavy synchronized footfall of a herd. During ordinary hours they remain statues under shadow. During pressure-blooms, when vision becomes glare and dust and confusion, they move.

Their mouths are narrow until they open. Then the head splits wider than expected, revealing dry black gums and backward glassy teeth. They kill quickly when they can. Wounds are too valuable to let bleed into air. Many predators have sealing saliva, resinous and anticoagulant in reverse: it closes the prey’s fluids inside even as the animal dies, preserving the meal.

There are also kite-hunters, winged but not birdlike, with ribbed membranes that lock open in high wind. They sleep clamped to tower flutes and launch only when gas fronts rise. From above, they look for the moment when a grazer loses footing on polished crust. They do not dive silently; nothing here is silent. They fall screaming, all vanes and hooked limbs, using the noise of the bloom to hide the direction of attack.

In husk dunes live the ambush coils. These are almost never seen whole. They bury themselves under drifts of shed cuticle and pollen scale, leaving only sensory threads exposed. Those threads imitate root hairs chemically, so small grazers and pollinators walk over them without alarm. When a large footfall compresses the dune, the coil strikes upward with a dry explosive snap. Its skin is armored in overlapping plates that fill with dust, making it indistinguishable from the dune that shelters it.

There are scavengers, too, and here they are among the most important citizens of the dry world.

Death does not rot. It splinters, powders, bleaches, and is taken apart by mouths designed for difficult leftovers. White carrion-beetles bore into fallen trunks and dead beasts alike, carrying colonies of dry enzymes in sealed gut-pouches. Fiber-jawed crawlers comb old carcasses for tendon, keratin, seed coats, shed horn, anything that can be shaved and swallowed. After them come the dust mats: creeping plant-films that advance over centuries, absorbing mineral memory from bone, bark, and ash.

A dead tower may feed a region for a thousand years.

The young of this world are mostly hidden.

Nothing delicate is exposed before it must be. Seeds are capsules, bullets, stones, screws, coins, needles, sealed geometries of waiting. Some lie dormant in cracks until a precise chemical signature tells them a parent root has withdrawn. Some require the grinding passage through a grazer’s jaw and gut. Some are fired into the upper winds with enough force to cross mountain-sized distances. Some are not dispersed at all but lowered into the ground by the parent plant through contracting root-cables, planted like treasured organs in prepared mineral beds.

Animal eggs are similarly armored. Pollinators cement them under vent lips where warmth and trace vapor are guaranteed. Herd-beasts carry developing young in internal sacs with ruthless reclamation of every fluid. Predators give birth in old hollow roots, where the walls still pulse faintly with plant heat. Newborns of almost every kind are dull-colored, waxed, silent, and instinctively still.

There is tenderness, but it has no softness.

A tower plant may angle a fan of mica leaves for decades to protect a cluster of seedlings from lethal noon glare. Herd adults form windbreak rings around calves, their bodies arranged by instinct according to gust direction. Pollinator swarms sleep in dense knots near warm exhaust vents, the outer individuals desiccating slightly so those within survive. A long-boned predator may carry water-rich organs back to its hidden young without feeding first, though the delay costs it dearly.

Even cooperation is shaped like austerity.

Many animals are also gardens. Many plants are also traps. Many surfaces are both shelter and mouth.

The moss-spined herds trade breath with their back-growth. Vent-mites clean pores they also exploit. Pollinators carry reproductive dust and steal from the same flowers. Root-arch predators shelter beneath plants that benefit from their kills; the spilled minerals, sealed in skin and bone, eventually return to the root-field. Some tower groves maintain “guest cavities” where small animals nest, not out of kindness but because their waste salts the internal walls and their movements discourage more damaging borers.

The closest thing to a mind may belong not to any animal but to the continent’s plant-networks.

This is not thought as a human would recognize it. There are no plans, no speech, no tools lifted in hands. But the root-fields remember. They record lightning seasons in conductive bands. They store old wounds in amber escarpments. They alter bloom timing after centuries of failed dispersal. They learn the migratory pressure of herds and thicken their skins along favored routes. Signals move through them as chemistry, charge, gas pulse, and strain. A valley can warn a ridge. A grove can refuse to flower if the plains upwind are dormant. A mat can starve a parasite by rerouting vapor through kilometers of buried tissue.

At great scale, the inhabitants are not merely living in weather.

They are making it, reading it, and being made by it.

The flying creatures wait for the plants to breathe. The grazers follow the mineral flowers. The predators follow the grazers but listen to the roots. The roots adjust to the trampling. The towers grow vanes to harvest wind made turbulent by other towers. The plains open when pressure, light, and season align, and their opening sends half the smaller world into motion.

So what lives there is a dry ecology of valves, armor, vanes, jaws, capsules, dust, and memory.

It is inhabited by plants that behave like geology and machines; animals that move like scraps, shadows, instruments, and famine; pollinators that sing in static; herds that carry their own shade; predators that hear through bone; seeds that travel like ammunition; and root-fields so old that a single breath from them can become a regional event.

Nothing there is lush. Nothing is careless. Nothing spills if it can be sealed, reclaimed, condensed, or stored.

And yet it is crowded with life.

Not crowded in space, but in relation. Every pale kilometer is occupied by exchanges too dry to glisten: vapor traded through membranes, charge passing under crust, pollen riding pressure, minerals rising through roots, warnings moving through wood, breath becoming wind, wind becoming road.

The inhabitants of that high continent are what life becomes when it gives up rivers and refuses, absolutely, to give up abundance.
