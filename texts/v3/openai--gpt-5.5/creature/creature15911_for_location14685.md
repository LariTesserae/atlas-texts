What lives there does not walk.

The inhabitants are spread thinly through seams, stains, crusts, roots, films, and waiting tissues. They are not creatures of pursuit or flight. They do not cross the open flats except by growth, seepage, spore, or the accidental carrying of rainwater from one basin to another. Life in that country has learned the architecture’s lesson: hold, endure, use every drop, relinquish nothing quickly.

Most of it lives flat.

In the shaded joints between plates, where mortar stays damp a little longer than the exposed paving, there are dark green threads finer than hair. They wake after condensation and lie almost invisible when dry. Seen closely, a seam may be a whole narrow garden: moss like powdered felt, beadlike colonies of algae, minute black crusts clinging to lime, transparent films that shine only when the light strikes them low. They have no height to spare. Their world is the width of a crack and the depth of a fingernail, but within it they make shelter, shade, chemistry, memory.

The open pools have other residents. In the long rectangular troughs and sunken pans, red algae bloom when water lingers long enough. They appear first as a faint blush in the shallows, then as rusty veils, then as dark coagulated skins that gather along the windward edge. When the pool shrinks, they settle into the bottom as a leathery stain. Dry, they look dead: black, brittle, mineral. Wet again, they loosen, deepen in color, and begin once more. Their life is less a continuous flame than a lamp repeatedly covered and uncovered.

The greenish pools contain slow mats. They grow from the edges inward, layered and soft, trapping dust and salt and fallen fragments of plaster. Their surfaces sometimes blister with trapped air. In the heat they sag and wrinkle; under rain they swell. There are no insects to disturb them, no larvae beneath, no small mouths grazing them away. They spread until the water abandons them, then harden into curled skins like old paint. The next season’s water may lift those skins and make them briefly buoyant, so that a whole former shoreline floats loose and wanders across a basin before catching on a brick lip.

The reeds are the nearest thing to vertical life.

They stand in little companies, rarely more than a dozen stems together, gray-green and narrow, with a dry gloss that makes them seem partly ceramic themselves. Their roots enter the cracks as if the built land were a riverbank that had forgotten to become earth. Below the visible stems, they are more extensive than they appear: pale rhizomes following buried dampness along channels, pausing at sealed joints, probing around stone-hard mortar, swelling where seepage persists. Aboveground they are slight, almost token gestures toward vegetation. Below, they are maps of the hidden water.

When rain comes, the reeds change first. Their stems darken. Their sheaths loosen. A faint green brightness passes through them that is almost startling in that colorless country. If the wet season is generous, they send up small seed heads, dry and feathery, which release almost nothing into the wind. Seeds fall near the parent plants, lodge in silt at the bottoms of runnels, and wait. Most never find enough moisture. A few do. In this way a reed-bed may take a century to move the length of a courtyard.

The shrubs are rarer and more solitary.

They occupy cracks wide enough to have collected dust for many years. Their leaves are waxy, small, and turned edgewise to the sun. Their bark is pale and slightly flaking, the color of old plaster. They do not look lush even after rain; they look less thirsty. Their roots descend into the architecture with a patience almost invasive, following joints, hairline fractures, buried conduits, forgotten voids. A shrub may stand alone for so long that the wall beside it begins to fail around its rootwork, not violently, but by a slow prying apart.

These shrubs flower briefly. The blossoms are small, cream-colored or faintly yellow, with thick petals that open in the cool of morning after rain. By afternoon they begin to close or bruise. By the next day many have dried into papery stars. There are no bees, no moths, no birds to visit them. Their pollination belongs to wind, touch, and accident: dust blown from one low plant to another, rain splash carrying grains down a channel, a torn flower head lodged against a neighboring stem. Their beauty has no audience except light.

Beneath ledges and inside roofless cells lives the shade-life.

It is not abundant, but it is persistent. Pale mosses cling to the underside of sill stones. Gray lichens freckle plaster where the sun reaches only indirectly. In some vaulted cells, where condensation falls drop by drop from the ceiling, living films grow in vertical streaks below each drip point. They are glossy at dawn, dull by noon. Some are green, some black, some the color of weak tea. They make the walls look as though they are remembering rain in long downward thoughts.

The sealed chambers have their own hidden residents.

Behind bulging plaster, in cisterns that still hold darkness and mineral damp, there are soft colonies that never see direct sun. They are translucent, beige, amber, or milk-white, clinging to submerged brick and lime. Some hang in threads from ceilings where condensation gathers; others coat the inner walls in slow skins. They live on seeped minerals, dissolved salts, and the faint organic wash carried down from the surface after rains. They do not bloom visibly. They thicken. They fur. They make membranes over still water. If a chamber cracks open, their exposed edges dry almost at once, sealing into dull flakes like old glue.

There are also salt-lovers, though they are difficult to distinguish from salt itself.

Along the rims of evaporating basins, where white crusts gather, certain living films occupy the boundary between wet and crystalline. They redden the salt faintly, or stain it violet-gray, or give it an oily sheen. They tolerate bitterness that would kill the reeds. When water retreats, they retreat into crystals, folded and dormant. When brine returns, they unfold microscopically. To the eye they are discoloration. To the place they are among the most faithful inhabitants.

Nothing there has the luxury of being only alive. Every organism is partly a weather process.

The algae are stains and skins.  
The mosses are damp held in place.  
The reeds are cracks made green.  
The shrubs are wells wearing leaves.  
The cistern colonies are darkness digesting minerals.  
The salt films are evaporation learning to wake again.

If there is a dominant resident, it is not a species but a condition: dormancy. Life there spends more time suspended than flourishing. It is packed into spores, dry mats, sealed rhizomes, tight buds, brittle crusts. It waits in forms that resemble debris. A courtyard may appear empty for years, then after three nights of condensation and one brief rain, its seams green faintly as if an image were developing in them. A basin may be black and dead-looking through a long heat, then suddenly glaze over with red-brown life. The country’s inhabitants do not arrive; they reappear.

They have no voices. Their sounds are borrowed from water and heat: a soft tearing when a dry mat curls, a faint crackle when salt crystals grow through living film, the almost inaudible slide of dew down a reed blade, the tiny collapse of an algae skin as the pool beneath it vanishes. They do not make the silence less silent. They give it texture.

Nor do they make the land feel inhabited in the usual sense. There are no eyes. No burrows. No nests. No trails worn by repeated choice. Life does not animate the distance. It merely alters surfaces, darkens hollows, greens the joints, softens corners, catches silt. From far away, the plain remains empty. Up close, every damp place has tenants.

And perhaps the largest thing living there is the constructed plain itself—not as an animal, not as a mind, but as a vast unfinished body whose tissues are brick, lime, clay, salt, ash, water, and microbial skin. Its channels behave like veins only when rain enters them. Its cisterns are organs that wake in darkness. Its pavements sweat at dawn. Its cracks choose futures. Its basins receive, concentrate, and relinquish. The sparse plants and films are not separate from it so much as its first living surface, the beginning of an ecology trying to decide what sort of world this built continent will become.

So what lives there is early life, low life, patient life.

Life without hurry.  
Life without animals.  
Life without shade enough to be generous.  
Life that has accepted flatness, heat, exposure, scarcity.  
Life that blooms as a stain, flowers for a day, sleeps for years, and drinks from stone.
