What lives there is not one species, and not quite a society in the old sense. It is a compact among dry things.

First, there is the city itself.

Not metaphorically. The undercity is alive enough to count as an inhabitant. Its towers are not passive trunks hollowed for use, but civic organs with tempers, appetites, immunities, and slow preferences. A bridge dislikes too much weight on its western root and thickens there over several weeks. A market leaf remembers the scent of frequent customers and lowers its ribs for them before they touch it. Certain avenues become tense before chemical storms in the vents. Whole districts sleep shallowly during municipal dimming, their stomata narrowing, their walls cooling, their root-pulses falling into a coordinated hush.

It thinks, if it thinks, in pressure, mineral taste, light angle, gas balance, and traffic. It does not have opinions quickly. It has tendencies. It favors empty spaces. It resents standing solvent. It opens new passages toward useful stone. It closes around fire with the speed of fear.

The people who live in it are shaped by that patience.

They are slight, dry-bodied, pale where they are not lacquered or stained with working powders. At a distance they might pass for human descendants: upright, clothed, tool-bearing, communal, with narrow shoulders under layered fiber coats and careful hands gloved in resin. Up close the resemblance loosens. Their skin is matte and finely scaled, better at keeping vapor in than sweating heat out. Their eyes are large, dark, and heavily lidded against dust and pollen. Their noses are not prominent, but complex: folded scent slits along the cheeks and throat that flare when they read a doorway, a notice-tree, a spoiled protein cake, another person’s mood.

They do not drink.

That fact has changed everything about them.

They take in moisture only as bound chemistry, locked in cakes, gels, waxes, and bitter mineral pastes. A meal is not a bowl or cup but a packet: compressed nodule-protein, salt-balanced resin, powdered fungal rind, volatile oils trapped in edible film. Children are taught early never to open food casually. Loose vapor is waste. Spilled solvent is civic offense. Breath is managed by habit and etiquette. People speak close to intake fronds in public halls, or through small masks that catch their exhalations and return trace compounds to the body. A sigh can be rude in a poor district.

Their voices are soft and dry. Many of their languages are partly scent and partly touch: fingertip taps against bark counters, pulses sent through wrist cords, aromatic punctuation released from small glands or carried in beads at the collar. In loud places they sign. In official places they use colored powders pressed onto living plaques, where the city reads them and changes the plaque’s shape in reply.

They move with caution, but not fearfully. Every surface may be alive, and most surfaces have rights of way. One does not lean on an unmarked wall. One does not cut a tendril unless one knows whether it is ornamental, sensory, arterial, or municipal. Children learn street botany before arithmetic. They learn which blue moss means transit, which means quarantine, which means recent repair, and which means the pavement is thinking and should not be interrupted.

Their homes are grown chambers, leased from particular root-bulbs or inherited as grafting rights. A family does not simply own a house; it keeps a relationship with a dwelling-organism. They polish its inner bark, feed its mineral fins, trim its memory-fungus, negotiate expansions with civic gardeners. When a child is born, the family may ask the house to bud a sleeping alcove. If the house agrees, a blister forms in the wall and slowly hollows itself over a season.

Their furniture is light, foldable, and aromatic: bark-slat beds, waxed fiber stools, nets of cured tendon, tables with root-feet that grip the floor during pavement tightening. They keep very few useless objects. Dryness preserves things too well; without rot to erase clutter, inheritance can become geological. So there are strict customs of reduction. Broken tools are surrendered. Old clothes are shredded for insulation. Personal relics are kept in scent boxes no larger than a hand.

The most respected citizens are not rulers but maintainers.

There are gardeners, though that word is too small. They are surgeons, engineers, priests, plumbers, and interpreters of vegetable intention. They wear aprons stiff with dried sap and belts of grafting knives, pressure needles, pigment capsules, splint-clamps, spore seals. Their hands are stained green-black at the nails. They can tell from a wall’s warmth whether a growth command took properly. They can calm an overactive door. They know how to persuade a staircase to change pitch, how to convince a factory lung to rest, how to excise a predatory tendril that has developed unauthorized habits.

There are gas clerks, too, thin and formal people who work in vaulted offices full of trembling gauges and breathing leaves. They schedule exhalation harvests, assign factories according to vent chemistry, and issue warnings when a district has become too carbon-hungry or sulfur-rich. They are treated with the wary respect given elsewhere to weather prophets and tax collectors.

There are miners who travel beyond the green-lit boroughs into raw stone. They come back powdered white or blue-gray, with mica in their lashes and ceramic shields strapped across their backs. They smell of pressure, old salt, and unclaimed dark. Their work is dangerous not because the tunnels flood — nothing floods — but because the unplanted cavern has no mercy. Stone cracks dry and sharp. Gas pockets bloom invisibly. There are blind things out there that never learned civic restraint.

There are factory tenders, broad-handed and slow-moving, who understand the moods of pistons made from bundled stem-muscle. There are seed librarians who cultivate books rather than shelve them, germinating a text for a single reading and then drying its knowledge back into dormancy. There are map printers whose maps must stretch because streets migrate. There are ceiling mirror-keepers who climb root ladders into the green vault and polish municipal noon. There are animal callers, waste registrars, scent-law advocates, kiln wardens, moth netters, vent listeners.

Their government is probably not elected in any simple fashion. It is negotiated among councils, guilds, neighborhood rootmasses, and the great founding organisms beneath the central boroughs. A decision has to be voted, scented, grafted, and accepted by the architecture. A law that the city refuses to grow is only a wish. A new street requires civic need, mineral permission, gas budget, and vegetative consent.

Crime exists, but it is dry and careful. Theft of water-equivalent compounds. Illegal solvent brewing. Cutting transit moss to mislead travelers. Polluting an exhalation garden. Smuggling feral seeds from the growth fronts. Breeding unregistered ambush flowers. Burning anything without a heat writ. The worst criminals are arsonists and those who poison root speech.

Punishment is often restriction from certain chemistries. A person may be forbidden access to high-protein nodules, or barred from warm districts, or marked with a scent that makes doors slow to open. Exile is terrible: outside the civic plantwork there is no breathable courtesy, no food architecture, no surfaces that know your hand.

Their children are quieter than surface children would be. They play games of stillness, scent-guessing, moth-shadow counting. They race cleaning beetles across plazas and dare one another to touch harmless sensor blossoms. They wear little masks painted with family colors. In nurseries, they sleep in stacked pods while elder walls breathe over them, filtering dust and teaching them, perhaps, the city’s pulse before language.

They draw the sun as a mythological device: a dangerous open lamp, an ancient machine, a flower too bright to approach.

Around them lives an entire citizenry of lesser beings, though “lesser” is a civic category more than a biological one.

Lacquer-shelled grazers work the curbs like municipal livestock, scraping mineral films and leaving behind pellets already tagged by scent for collection. Pale jointed browsers climb buildings on hooked feet, trimming lichen from vents before it clogs the respiratory slits. Seed-moths drift down light wells in silent processions, pollinating notice-trees and carrying genetic messages between districts. Some are privately owned, marked with minute bands of pigment; others belong to the city and cannot legally be netted.

Cleaning beetles are nearly sacred. No one steps over their black fans when they cross a plaza. They emerge with grave timing from under-pavement hives and dismantle dust, husk, hair, shed scale, dead spore, insect wing, and crumbs of civic food. They are followed by children and by old people, both of whom like predictable rituals.

The long-backed scavengers of the factory wards are tolerated rather than loved. They have clever masked faces and too much patience. They know the schedule of waste cakes better than some workers do. Each animal carries the colors of its chosen district in pollen smears, and rival packs can be distinguished by scent. They never quite become pets, but individuals are recognized, named, insulted, and occasionally mourned.

The predators are part of the city’s sanitation.

Pipeway foxes keep grazer numbers low. Ambush flowers take the careless, the sick, and the unauthorized. Ceiling bulbs descend during dimming, trailing their snares through upper air, catching seed-moths and sometimes the gloved hand of a foolish climber. There are laws about where predatory plants may root, but living things misread maps. A good gardener knows the difference between necessary hunger and civic disobedience.

Then there are the insects that seem less like animals than like trades.

Pale carriers haul charged dust to growth fronts, each one the size of a thumb joint and burdened with a crystal grain. Chitin auditors live in the walls of counting houses and click when numbers balance. Glass mites clean lens-organs in the eye plazas. Red-jawed salt ants patrol cracks for illegal moisture blooms. Soft-bodied wax worms are raised in warm cabinets and fed obsolete documents, converting old bureaucracy into sealant.

The fungi are slower citizens.

Not the wet rot-fungi of forests, but dry, disciplined varieties: panel fungi that glow with steady administrative light; archive fungi that hold scent memories; cracking fungi that digest the dead only when licensed and supplied with proper catalysts. Some form pale commons where people gather to rest under a low, even luminescence. Others live inside machinery, translating mineral into usable chemistry. Fungal keepers speak of them with affection and suspicion, as one might speak of brilliant accountants.

And beneath all of these, below the rootfelt and the civic bulbs, something older may live.

The undercity is young, but not everything under it is. The oldest root does not belong to any visible tower. Its fibers pass beneath districts like a buried weather system. It may be the first plant, or the remnant of a failed city, or an organism that slept in the dry cavern long before people cut shafts for light. Most inhabitants do not discuss it plainly. They refer to “deep pressure,” “lower listening,” “the old appetite.” Machines at the vents pause when it moves. Sensitive children wake crying from dreams of a forest with no leaves. Gardeners in the central boroughs sometimes place both palms on the pavement and wait, faces expressionless, as if receiving instructions too large to translate.

No one knows whether it protects the city, feeds on it, remembers it, or is slowly becoming it.

The people have adapted to that uncertainty. They live among organisms that are homes, roads, utilities, employers, and neighbors. They do not ask whether the city is natural or artificial. That distinction would seem childish there. A thing is judged instead by whether it keeps pressure, whether it preserves breath, whether it grows where it has promised to grow.

They are not a lush people. They are exact. They waste little, shout rarely, and decorate with astonishing precision. A festival may consist of five minutes of gold pollen released down an avenue while everyone stands masked and still, watching the light thicken. Mourning is done with gray aromatic ash pressed into door seams. Marriage is a grafting ceremony: two households exchange living slivers and wait to see whether the tissues accept. Public celebration favors synchronized mirror shifts, shadow plays on membrane walls, moth releases, and towers blushing in agreed color sequences.

Their beauty is not softness. It is polish, tension, fragrance, geometry, restraint.

They love the cool noon of their cavern because it does not change without permission. They love dry air because it carries every scent distinctly. They love living walls because stone alone is deaf. They fear open flame, uncontrolled wetness, and places where nothing grows back.

They are inhabitants of a world where nothing flows and everything grows, so they have become a people of increments: graft by graft, pulse by pulse, breath by captured breath. Their civilization does not rush forward. It thickens. It roots. It tests the stone with fine white fingers and waits for the answer.
