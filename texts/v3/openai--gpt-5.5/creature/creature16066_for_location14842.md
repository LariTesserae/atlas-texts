The first inhabitants are not the people.

The first inhabitants are the things that keep water without admitting it.

They live in folds, skins, joints, sealed chambers, swollen stems, buried jars, animal bellies, remembered routes. Every living thing in the cleft has learned some form of secrecy. The plants hide their softness behind thorns and wax. The insects hide in the seams of masonry. The people hide their stores beneath beds and under shrines and behind walls whose plaster has been repaired so often that no one but the house remembers where the hollow places are.

The people of the cleft are dry-country people, but not sparse people. They are compact, crowded, quick to touch and quick to object to being touched. They grow up knowing how much space a shoulder needs, how to pass someone on a stair without knocking a basket from their head, how to duck under hanging cages without waking the birds, how to step over a sleeping dog without inviting its teeth. Their bodies learn the city before their minds do.

They are narrow-footed from stairs and ledges. Their calves are hard. Their hands are callused not only from labor but from stone: stone walls, stone tools, stone mortars, stone heat. Many have pale dust worked permanently into the lines of their palms. Their hair is often braided tight or wrapped in cloth to keep it from thorn-vines and cooking smoke. Children go nearly naked in the hottest months, wearing charms, beads, seed rattles, bits of polished bone, and little packets of bitter herbs against fever. Adults dress in layered wraps of woven fiber, patched hide, and cloth traded from elsewhere until it has been mended into local usefulness.

Their faces are watchful. Not suspicious exactly, though outsiders often think so. It is the look of people who must constantly measure: shade, wind, animal mood, ripeness, thirst, obligation, the weight of a jar, the sound of a roof under too many feet. A person who cannot measure does not live long there.

They do not move lavishly. Even their anger has economy. A quarrel in the market may be sharp, public, theatrical, full of insult and genealogy, but no one kicks over a vessel or strikes near a water jar. Their gestures are precise. A finger crooked toward shadow means bring the sealed jar. A palm laid over the mouth means not here, not aloud. Two knuckles tapped against a wall may summon a neighbor through a vent nobody admits is there.

They speak in a language full of textures and containers. There are many words for kinds of dryness: the dryness of clean sand, of old bone, of a throat after fever, of dung ready to burn, of a jar that has lost its seal, of a dead season that may still be survived. There are fewer direct words for water, and those are used carefully, mostly in ritual, medicine, contract, or grief. Children are taught not to beg for water by name. They ask for coolness, for weight, for the inside of the gourd.

Their humor is dry too, in both senses. They laugh at collapse narrowly avoided: a goat stuck in a shrine niche, a proud trader fainting in the first market court, a child bitten by a pigeon after boasting loudly that birds fear him. They mock greed, clumsiness, softness, and anyone who thinks an empty horizon means an empty land. They are excellent mimics of animals and of one another. A good imitation of a clan elder’s cough may circulate for years.

They are not a gentle people, but they are not cruel without purpose. Gentleness exists there as maintenance: oiling a cracked heel, resetting a bird’s broken wing because the bird will hunt again, waking in the night to shift a feverish child closer to a wall that releases heat more slowly, sharing shade with an enemy because the rule is older than the quarrel. Mercy is practical. Practicality, over generations, has become sacred.

Their households are not neat little units. A household may include grandparents alive and dead, sisters’ children, a widower from another ledge, three goats technically belonging to a cousin, a vine whose roots pass through two floors and therefore involve two lineages, a trained kite, several cages of fowl, a tuber pit, a spider no one may kill, and a sealed ancestor niche that confers rights over a stair landing. Ownership is less a line than a knot.

The dead live there in a manner more literal than memory. They are present in walls, under platforms, beside stairways, behind plastered chambers marked by beads, pigment, or small handprints. They are consulted, blamed, thanked, soothed. A household may apologize to its dead before moving a storage jar. A bride or adopted child is introduced to the wall. A man who repairs a lintel above an ancestor niche works quietly and does not spit dust.

The people believe the dead dislike waste. This is convenient, perhaps, but no less believed for that.

Their infants are watched fiercely but not fully welcomed until they have passed the first hot season. Before then they are called by temporary names: Little Shade, Moth, Pebble, Red Foot, Third Cry, Not-Yet. When they survive, the naming is not loud. The child is carried to several thresholds, touched with dust from old steps, shown to a living plant, a useful animal, and an ancestor niche. Only then does the city properly admit them.

Their old people become maps. They know which ledges crack in heat, which family once stored grain where a wall now bulges, which court catches evening wind after the longest month, which vine bears sweet fruit only after being insulted with ash, which snake in the lower granaries should be tolerated because it eats rats and which should be killed because it has learned to enter cradles. An old woman sitting in shadow may look idle to a stranger. She is often the hinge on which three households turn.

The children are half-wild because the city trains them alongside their elders. They climb before they count. They steal eggs, trade beetle shells, sleep in hammocks slung above goats, learn to whistle to dogs, learn which roofs are forbidden and which are forbidden only if one is caught. They carry messages through cracks too narrow for adults. They know the under-routes: behind ovens, through empty coops, over a granary roof, under the fig platform, across the shrine court if the keeper is asleep. They are bitten often. Their scars are part of education.

The adolescents become dangerous for a while. They test distance. They climb to the signal slits at night. They court each other across vertical space, singing from ledges, tossing knots of fiber, lowering contraband fruit in baskets. Some join caravans and return broader in gesture, with foreign cloth, lowland words, shell ornaments, and ridiculous opinions about open roads. Some do not return. The city has songs for both kinds.

There are specialists, though few professions are pure. A potter may also keep bees in clay tubes. A midwife may know snakebite medicines. A bird trainer may also be a thief, which everyone knows and uses when necessary. The flint-knappers sit in the lower court like patient predators, flaking dark glass into edges so fine they seem almost wet. The weavers work in shade, their looms creaking softly, making cloth tough enough to strain pulp, bind wounds, wrap bodies, shade seedlings, and become clothing only after all higher purposes are satisfied.

The water-keepers are not rulers, but no ruler can ignore them. They are mostly older women and exact men with quiet voices, people trusted not because they are kind but because they are incorruptible about measures. They know the sealed jars, the lineage cisterns, the emergency gourds hung in shadow, the plantings that may be cut in famine and those that must never be touched. During dry years their authority hardens. During rare rain they become frantic generals.

There are leaders, but leadership is distributed like shade. Clan elders settle root disputes and marriages. Vent-masters understand air and smoke. Gate-keepers know outsiders. Shrine-keepers know which ancestors are easily offended. Caravan speakers know trade tongues and lies. In true danger, authority passes to whoever understands the danger first.

They trade, because the cleft cannot provide everything. From the plateau and beyond come obsidian nodules, salt not from their own walls, timber, pigments, shells, metal fragments, cloth, stories, disease, news of wars too far away to matter until suddenly they do. They send out dried fruit, tough fiber rope, medicinal resins, bone tools, trained birds, preserved locust cakes, bitter seed pastes, pigments, carved charms, and knowledge of hidden routes across land that appears empty to fools.

Outsiders are received cautiously. The city does not open all at once. It admits in layers. First the gate court: noise, smell, eyes from above. Then perhaps a market passage. Then a shaded room, if someone vouches. Rarely the upper ledges. Almost never the storage chambers. Outsiders are not shown where water truly sleeps.

But the people are only one citizenship of the cleft.

The goats live there as thieves with legal standing. They are lean, angular, clever, and morally vacant. They can open simple latches, climb impossible piles, and distinguish between plants they are allowed to eat and plants whose theft will produce the most satisfying human shouting. Their milk is precious, their dung useful, their tempers foul. Children wrestle them. Old men curse them. Everyone depends on them.

The pack beasts are slower, larger, more solemn. They kneel in the courts like folded hills, chewing and watching through long lashes. Their humps and bellies carry wealth: water, trade goods, skins, fuel, salt, sleeping children. They are painted before journeys with white clan marks and protective signs. A caravan animal that has crossed the dead outer plateau more than once is treated with a respect close to that given to an elder warrior.

The dogs are rangy, scarred, yellow-eyed, and opportunistic. They belong to households, streets, children, themselves. They sleep in shade by day and become sudden law at night. They hate rats, tolerate babies, steal offal, warn of cats, fight one another over bones, and know every person who habitually drops food. Some are trained to guard roof gardens from goats. Most are only partly successful.

The birds are everywhere, and each kind holds a different treaty.

The finches are tolerated as weather signs and seed thieves. The nectar birds are too beautiful to kill except in famine. The pigeons are food, message, nuisance, sacrifice, and inheritance. The owls are respected because they hunt the granary rats; a house with an owl in an old vent may boast of it as if blessed by a severe little god. The scavenger kites are watched carefully. They take chicks, but they also clean the high stones, warn of carcasses, circle over strangers, and remember insult. The trained hunting birds belong to particular families and are fed better than some cousins.

The domesticated fowl are ugly, loud, useful, and perpetually offended. They scratch through courtyards with bare red necks and bead-bright eyes, muttering as if reciting old grievances. Their eggs are counted. Their feathers are saved. Their bones become needles. Their rage entertains children.

The rats are citizens no one admits. They know the city’s true shape better than anyone. They travel through walls, under floors, behind ancestor niches, across roof beams, into grain, out of traps, around sleeping bodies. People wage war on them continually and lose with dignity. Certain children become famous ratters. Certain dogs become valued beyond price. Certain snakes are allowed to remain because of them.

The insects are the majority.

Ants maintain roads older than some stairways. Beetles turn refuse into bodies. Moths appear in such numbers beneath awnings that a cloth shaken at dawn may release a gray breath of wings. Locusts are apocalypse with legs and dinner with wings. Children learn to scoop them into baskets while adults smoke the passages and beat metal, clay, hide, anything that makes a sound. Afterward the city smells of roasting shells and relief.

The spiders are household guardians. Large ones occupy corners above doorways, under shelves, behind ovens. They are given names but not affection. To kill one invites flies, bad luck, and criticism from grandmothers. Their webs collect the city’s drift: soot, pollen, whispers, seed fluff, hair, feather down.

The reptiles live in the heat grammar of the place. Blue-throated lizards on the ledges bob like little priests of sunlight. Geckos fatten above lamps. Sand-colored snakes slip through grain rooms and sleeping walls. Children can identify them by movement before they can read marks on pottery. The wrong snake is killed with swift seriousness. The right snake is left to work.

The night cat is not owned by anyone. It belongs to the upper ledges, to shadow, to the moment before a bird screams. It is slender, spotted, nearly soundless, and regarded with admiration, irritation, and ritual caution. If it takes too much, traps are set. If it merely reminds the city that roofs are not walls and walls are not safety, it is allowed its passage.

The plants are perhaps the oldest residents after stone.

They are not passive. They grip, invade, bargain, remember wet years in seeds that can wait longer than human grudges. The figs crouch in their pockets of soil, glossy-leaved and stubborn, feeding birds, children, insects, and lineages. The thread-vines make curtains and traps, catching fluff and dung dust and anything the air offers. The columnar gray-green plants stand along the upper shelves like sealed towers of thirst, opening their red flowers at dawn to every creature with wings or a tongue long enough to reach. The peppery groundleaf accepts trampling and repays it with scent.

Households cultivate plants the way they raise difficult relatives: with pruning, argument, protection, punishment, praise. A vine may be older than the roof supporting it. A root may be party to a legal dispute. A fruiting branch crossing a wall may create obligation between enemies. The city’s agriculture is vertical, intimate, and political.

Then there are the things no one planted. Thorn-ropes. Bitter weeds. Crevice shrubs. Dust moss. A pale fungus that appears only in sealed jar rooms. Seeds brought in dung, feathers, trade sacks, sandal cracks. Some are pulled out as soon as seen. Some are allowed to remain because a grandmother remembers their medicine. Some take a wall apart over fifty years while everyone complains and harvests them anyway.

And beneath all this are the ancestors, the sealed ones, the powdered ones, the ones drying slowly behind plaster.

They are not ghosts in the dramatic sense. They do not wail in passages or glow over roofs. They are weight. They are title. They are warning. They make a room feel occupied when no living person is there. They are invoked in disputes over jars, vines, marriages, ledges, tools, and names. A child may be told, “Do not kick that wall; your mother’s mother is listening.” A thief may fear not being caught by the living but being known by the dead whose niche he passed.

What lives in the cleft, finally, is not a species or a people alone, but a packed agreement.

The people live there, yes: sharp-eyed, dust-skinned, practical, musical, superstitious when superstition preserves good sense, generous when generosity prevents collapse, secretive because secrecy keeps water alive.

The animals live there too: stealing, hunting, feeding, warning, fouling, fertilizing, singing, biting, dying into tools and meals.

The plants live there: hoarding, flowering, piercing, binding house to cliff and roof to roof.

The dead live there: dry, close, named, still owning corners of the world.

Even the stone seems to live by participation. It stores heat, gives shade, holds chambers, breaks slowly, remembers vanished water in curves along its walls. It is not worshiped exactly, but it is never treated as inert. A person leans against it for coolness, cuts into it for storage, seals kin inside it, curses it when it cracks, thanks it when it holds.

Those who live in the cleft are therefore not inhabitants in the simple sense. They are contents under pressure. Seeds in a pod. Breath in a flute. Water in a gourd. Life folded so tightly into the old plateau that every root, claw, hand, wing, and bone has learned the shape of every other.
