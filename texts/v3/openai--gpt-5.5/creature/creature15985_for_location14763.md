What lives there is not a colony so much as a maintained condition.

It has bodies, many of them: lacquer mites, frost moths, gutter crabs, thread-eels, socket beetles, glass snails, pale films, salt-fed lichens, the hidden bacterial slicks in seams and under washers. But none of these quite live as separate creatures. They are organs of a colder animal made from repetition. The room has taught them to be functions.

The mites are the nearest thing to fur. They mass around warm couplings in black granular pelts, each no larger than a flake of burnt varnish, each jointed with absurd precision. They do not swarm outward. They do not explore. They pulse in density according to heat, tightening when a gasket exhales, loosening when the metal cools. Their backs are polished by vapor. Their legs make a dry ticking under the pump roar, like sleet striking glass. They feed on oil-smoke, mineral sweat, dead skin from larger things, the taste of rubber just beginning to perish. When crushed, they leave no gore, only a dark resinous smear that other mites clean away.

The moths are the room’s hanging lungs. Blind, furred, slow, they cling to the ceiling mesh in damp overlapping veils. Their wings are too heavy for flight in any ordinary air, but they open and close them in measured fans, stirring vapor away from frost and toward warmer plates. Their bodies are packed with fine white down that catches droplets and spores. They are cold to the touch, almost inert, yet not asleep. Their heads turn toward vibration. Their antennae tremble at the exact frequencies of valves. A change in pressure passes through them before it passes through the gauges.

They lay their eggs in the felted grime behind lamp housings, where amber light keeps the frost thin. The larvae do not crawl far. They eat condensate mold, old wing scales, and the softened edges of insulation. Those that wander into true cold become seedlike pellets, preserved until some later leakage wakes them. Those that hatch properly join the ceiling, adding themselves to the dim breathing canopy.

In the gutters live the crabs, if crabs is the right name for things so small and so intent. They are pale, almost translucent, with mineral-dark stomachs and claws shaped for sorting. They stand in the metallic rain and separate the world grain by grain: salt to one channel, rubber flake to the mouth, ceramic dust to the stomach, dead mite to the communal hollow beneath the grate. Their backs are etched with tiny frost scars. Their eyes are pinpricks under milk-colored shell, mostly useless. They know the room by current, taste, and tremor.

They are frantic only because the room requires exactness. If a gutter slows, they thicken around the obstruction. If a drain begins to skin over with ice, they rasp it. If algae softens the lip of a channel, they harvest it before it can mat. Their little claws never stop. They are janitors without memory of dirt.

The thread-eels are nearly invisible until they bend. They live inside the condensation tubes and transparent returns, long slivers with black heads and bodies like clear wire. They swim against the pump’s pull, always climbing, always being carried back. Their lives are a loop of effort and return. They graze the inside of tubes where no claw can reach, scraping biofilm so thin it is more idea than substance. Sometimes one coils in a droplet at the mouth of a pipe, tasting the air, its body making a question mark in the green diode light. Then the pump takes it.

The beetles occupy the hard places. Bolt sockets, bracket holes, the seams beneath inspection numbers, any cavity too tight for softness. Their wing cases are glassy and ridged, beaded with condensed water. They wedge themselves into metal with the patience of hardware. Some look dead for months. Then a vibration changes, a nut warms by a fraction, and they unfold needle legs to clean the socket of salt, rust bloom, and mite castings. They are small keepers of tolerances. Without them, screws would crust over; with them, nothing ages enough to become history.

The snails are the palest inhabitants, almost not animals at all. They graze where the ceramic sweats. Their bodies are soft commas of cold flesh, their shells reduced to translucent caps under which faint organs move like smoke in milk. They leave clean crescent trails through algae films, signatures that vanish as new moisture beads. They do not hurry because nothing here rewards hurry. They are the erasers of green.

There are also the things too small to be seen but more numerous than all the rest: salt bacteria in the wall pores, antifreeze yeasts in the gutters, blue acid-tolerant films around the battery ducts, fungal threads growing in the fur of moth larvae, microbes that live in crab gut and snail mouth and eel skin. They are the true weather inside the wetness. They decide what becomes edible. They soften mineral bloom. They make waste usable. They make death disappear before it can smell like death.

And beneath all of them, or through all of them, lives the machine’s cold appetite.

It is not intelligent in a way that would answer. It does not watch. It regulates. It keeps the temperature just above the killing edge, feeds water through the room in amounts too precise to be natural, permits mineral leakage where mouths are waiting, sheds heat where bodies must gather, denies heat where growth would become ambition. It has made an ecology out of calibration. The animals have adapted to it until adaptation and obedience are indistinguishable.

No creature there has a wilderness inside it. The moth does not dream of open air. The eel does not seek a river. The crab does not want a shore. Their instincts have been narrowed to fit the manufactured cave: scrape, graze, fan, sort, climb, wedge, hatch, molt, dissolve. If some ancestor came from snowmelt pools, fungus cellars, engine bilges, or alpine vents, that ancestry has been machined down to a useful remnant.

Yet they are not lifeless. They hunger. They avoid pain. They mate in hidden sockets and under grates. They tremble when pressure shifts. They crowd warmth with the desperation of all small bodies. They eat the dead quickly, not out of reverence but because the room allows no surplus. Their lives are cramped, chemical, exact, and real.

What lives there is a captive mountain reduced to maintenance.

A black fur of mites for scree. Moths for cloud. Eels for streams. Crabs for erosion. Snails for thaw. Lichens for the idea of pasture. Bacteria for soil that is never permitted to become soil. All of it enclosed behind bolts rimed shut with ice, all of it roaring softly beneath the greater roar, all of it continuing without season, without climax, without visible decay.

If it has a soul, the soul is not in any single animal. It is in the balance between them: the precise amount of grazing before the film is gone, the precise number of moth wings needed to move vapor, the precise thickness of mites around a coupling, the precise hunger that prevents the drains from clogging. It is a soul made of prevented excess.

It lives by almost happening.

It is a roomful of creatures held forever at the threshold of becoming a world.
