What lives there is not one species, though the town would say it is one household.

The people are human, or near enough that no one from the lower valleys argues about it aloud. They are small-boned, broad-footed, and narrow-eyed from generations of glare. Their skin weathers early into red, brown, grey, and split white at the knuckles. Their hair is usually bound close under hoods of goat hide or woven reed, because loose hair freezes to door-lintels and drinking rims. Their voices are low, not timid but tuned to the basin. They speak as if every word must cross wet stone without waking something.

They move with an odd, beautiful caution. Outsiders think them slow until they see one cross a glazed roofline carrying a basin of coals, or step through a room where three channels run under smoke and shadow. Their balance is learned before language. Children do not toddle so much as test surfaces. They slap stone with their palms, listen to ice with their teeth, taste condensation from walls, and learn which kind of shine will bear weight.

Their clothing is layered and practical: shaggy goat-wool, marmot fur, patched hide, reed-woven shin guards stiffened with mineral paste. Most wear smoked slits over their eyes outdoors. Around their wrists are cords knotted with household signs, spring-marks, warnings, debts, small prayers, and the names of the dead who were given to running water. They carry bone awls, stone scrapers, little dark knives inherited past remembering, and cups carved from horn for testing springs.

They do not think of themselves as poor, though nearly everything they own has been mended until it has more repair than original substance. A bowl may have belonged to eight households and be laced with rawhide like a scarred face. A door may be made from the ribs of three roofs and one old sled. A child’s toy goat may later become a peg, then a fire-lighter, then ash mixed into plaster. Nothing ends quickly there. Use is a kind of afterlife.

They are not sentimental about hardship. The basin does not reward complaint. But they are attentive in ways that look like tenderness. They set crumbs on warm stones for moths. They shift nesting platforms after bad ice years. They move slowly around sleeping frogs. They lower their voices when passing spring-mouths. They greet certain pools in the morning, not because they worship them exactly, but because it is foolish to ignore anyone who may change your house from beneath.

Their elders are listened to as weather instruments. An old woman with clouded eyes may know from the taste of the hearth-smoke that the lower channels will reverse before dawn. A man whose fingers are missing from frost may hear a hidden ice-dam forming three terraces up. The oldest inhabitants become almost architectural: seated in wall-niches wrapped in hides, faces polished by cold, murmuring corrections to children, goats, water, and careless adults alike.

Their children are bright, solemn, and feral in brief flashes. They know secret routes through gutters and roof-saddles. They keep pale moth wings in bone boxes. They dare one another to touch upward-growing ice with their tongues. They make boats from frozen leaves and send them through house-streams to see where they emerge. They are warned most sternly about cloud-reflecting pools, and so of course they watch them from a distance, holding hands, each silently hoping to see something forbidden and hoping harder not to.

Their dead remain part of town logistics. Names are spoken over water. Bones are not wasted. Some become tools, some are sealed into walls, some are placed in dry high niches where vapor beads on their brow ridges and freezes clear. The people say a well-kept ancestor helps a wall remember its shape. They say a badly named dead thing makes water forget the way home.

But the humans are only the most talkative residents.

The white jackdaws are nearly citizens. They nest in chimney crowns, cliff holes, rafters, abandoned smoke vents, and the hollow backs of old shrines. They watch everything with wet black eyes and repeat the town back to itself in fragments: drip, drip, child-laugh, goat-cough, thaw-click, grandmother’s warning, false crack of breaking ice. Some households keep a jackdaw ledge the way others keep an altar. When the birds fall silent all at once, every hand in the basin stops.

The foxes live above and among the roofs. They are long, thin creatures, pale in winter, rust-shadowed in summer, with black feet and narrow, intelligent faces. They take fish guts, lost mittens, the occasional unwatched egg. They hunt marmots and hares and the small burrowers that would otherwise loosen foundations. No one fully trusts them, which is considered a respectful relationship. A fox seen sleeping beside a smoke-hole is not disturbed. A fox that barks three times from the upper rim is answered by someone rising to check the cliff.

The goats belong to no one as completely as people pretend. They are shaggy, slope-shouldered animals with crescent horns dulled from scraping mineral salt. They stand on impossible ledges chewing lichen, staring sideways at storms. Their milk is thick and faintly bitter. Their wool smells of wet stone and smoke no matter how often it is combed. They know which stairs will hold better than humans do, and more than one child has been saved by following a goat’s refusal.

Marmots occupy the lower outer walls with the gravity of old landlords. They are fat, damp, and solemn, with whiskers crusted white from mineral seepage. Their burrows are reinforced with stolen reed, hair, wool, scraps of hide, and sometimes small household objects they seem to prize for reasons no one understands. Children leave them knots and buttons. Adults curse them and then repair their tunnels so the walls do not slump.

The hares are almost apparitions: white, grey, stone-colored, gone. They appear in alleys at dawn, pause with one black eye fixed on a doorway, and vanish through gaps no larger than a mitten. The people say hares carry news between silences. This is probably only a saying, except that hunters have learned not to kill one that crosses uphill during thaw-light.

In the marsh-edge below, life is smaller and more secretive.

The blind fish are pale as fingernails and soft as threads of fat. They slip through hand-deep channels under ice plates, appearing and disappearing where no channel seems to exist. The people catch them with antler hooks and thank them by name, because unnamed fish rot strangely and bring bad dreams.

The frogs are dark, compressed things, like wet stones given patience. They sleep deep in black mud and wake during sudden warm brightness. Their brief thaw-croaking is one of the town’s calendars. When the frogs wake too early, grain is moved higher. When they do not wake at all, the spring is watched with knives laid ready beside doorways, though no one likes to explain why.

The blue-backed crabs cling beneath ice shelves and under bridge lips. They are tiny, lacquer-bright, and irritable. Their legs wave at shadows. Children collect their shed shells and string them into charms that click softly in the windless cold.

The glass eels are treated with the greatest care. They live in wall-streams, cisterns, under-threshold channels, and the dark wet seams between houses. They are nearly invisible except for pinhead organs and the faint silver line of their movement. A family that sees eels in its floor-water feels reassured. A family whose eels vanish grows quiet. If sickness follows, no one says the eels caused it. If sickness does not follow, no one says the eels were wrong. Either way, new diversion grooves are cut for them with great precision.

There are birds in the lower pools too: long-necked, grey-throated, frost-lashed things that stand so still they seem carved into the weather. They fish without rippling the water. Sometimes one keeps its beak submerged long enough that children gather to see whether it has drowned. It never has. When it lifts its head, whatever hangs from its bill is not always a fish.

And then there are the less classifiable inhabitants.

They are not spoken of as spirits in the theatrical sense. The basin people are too practical for unnecessary categories. But there are presences in the watercourses, habits with intention, recurrences with moods.

A stair that softens every seventh thaw-week is treated like an old relative with a failing hip. No one steps on it after noon. A roof that grows ice upward is given extra stone pins and a little fat at midwinter. A spring that yields feathers is covered with a pierced bowl so it can breathe without filling the house. A pool that reflects clouds moving against the wind is avoided, fenced with reed, and watched from the corner of the eye.

What lives there, finally, is a community of negotiations.

The humans build, but the water edits.

The animals steal, warn, nest, undermine, clean, predict, and mislead.

The stone remembers old routes.

The ice makes temporary architecture.

The marsh breathes under its lid.

The birds imitate the town until the town cannot always tell which sounds are true.

No one there is sovereign. Not the elders, not the builders, not even the cold. Everything survives by yielding in very specific ways. The people are not adapted to stillness but to revision. Their genius is not conquest; it is maintenance, listening, rerouting, naming, lifting sacks before the floor-stream changes direction.

They are citizens of seepage: soft-spoken, glare-eyed, sure-footed, superstitious in the way that accurate observers become superstitious when the world refuses to separate omen from mechanism.

They live crowded together with foxes, jackdaws, goats, eels, crabs, moths, frogs, fish, hares, marmots, and the thinking water itself. They share a high white hollow where every surface is occupied, every silence is monitored, every reflection may be suspect, and every morning the town must be learned again.
