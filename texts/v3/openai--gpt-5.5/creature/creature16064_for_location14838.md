What lives there is not a single species ruling a pocket kingdom, but a whole arrangement of bodies taught by the same small laws.

They are basin-creatures: warm-stone animals, rain-minute animals, things whose lives are measured not by seasons but by the interval between one brief vertical shower and the next. They have learned scarcity without becoming sparse. They do not waste motion. They appear, feed, mate, drink, vanish. Most of them are built to be sudden.

The most visible are the glass lizards.

They are not truly transparent, not like water or air, but their skin and outer flesh admit light so generously that they seem unfinished. On sunlit stone they are almost invisible except for the fine gold branching of organs, the dark bead of the eye, the quick violet shadow of the tongue. Their bones are thin greenish lines. When they breathe, a faint smoky flutter moves under the ribs. They cling to the basin walls with soft, star-shaped toes that spread and seal against mineral dust.

They hunt where the air misbehaves.

Above the polished central runnel, where falling things hesitate a fraction too long, the lizards leap in short, precise arcs. They have learned the thickness in the air the way river fish learn currents. An insect flying across the runnel enters a pocket of delay and cannot turn quickly enough; the lizard arrives already open-mouthed. The largest of them grow no longer than a hand, but age makes them clearer, not larger. The oldest are nearly absent, visible only when rain beads on their backs and outlines them in silver.

They are solitary except during the first breaths after rain. Then they gather on the warmest exposed plates of rock and press their bellies flat to the drying stone, absorbing heat through their organs. Their courtship is almost architectural: males arrange shed skins in tiny crescent lines near cracks, and females inspect the curves by walking along them with lowered heads. A good crescent catches droplets. A poor one dries too quickly. Their eggs are laid in mineral seams and look like trapped bubbles in amber crust.

The beetles are more numerous and more industrious.

They are dark, thumb-sized, lacquer-backed things with long front legs shaped like delicate tools. They climb fern stems and leaf edges before dawn, harvesting condensation droplet by droplet. A beetle can carry a bead of water nearly half its own weight, locked between its forelimbs and mouthparts, shining like a second head. They drag these droplets downward into little pits lined with chewed lichen and powdered shell. There they store moisture for larvae, for mates, and for the hot center of the day.

Their shells are not black but layered: green, bronze, blue, and brown beneath the dark surface, visible only when they open their wing-cases. They fly reluctantly. Flight is dangerous in the basin, where echoes can become weapons and gravity is not always perfectly honest. When forced into the air, they make a dry papery clatter, like seeds shaken in a gourd.

After rain they swarm the black post.

They do not feed on the material itself. They feed on what gathers in its seams: slick luminous moisture, mineral-rich and faintly sour, charged with whatever old function still wakes inside the post when wet. The beetles become intoxicated by it. Their movements grow ceremonial. They circle the post in opposing spirals, collide, right themselves, and begin again. Females who drink deeply from those seams lay eggs that hatch into brighter larvae, pale blue and restless, with jaws strong enough to scrape mineral crust.

The millipedes are the basin’s patient workers.

They move in braided lines across the open floor, pale as root-flesh, each body made of so many segments that watching one too closely becomes unpleasant. They are not blind, exactly; they see heat. Sunlit stone is a wall to them. Shade is a road. Moisture is a summons.

Their lines divide and rejoin with the slow intelligence of poured liquid. They avoid the black post except during rain, and even then they climb it with caution, tasting with every foot. When they find a dead thing, they do not rush. They surround it, test it, and wait until the first softening begins. Then the line thickens. By morning, flesh is gone.

They leave bones.

The bones are cleaned with such care that they look handled by thought. Around the buried discs, the millipedes arrange them in radial patterns, not from reverence but from habit shaped by force. Something in the discs disturbs their sense of direction. Each millipede believes it is carrying a fragment straight away from the body; each is bent, subtly, into a spoke. Over generations this mistake has become useful. The bone-rings catch seeds. The seeds catch dust. Ferns sometimes begin in the skulls of vanished animals.

There are amphibians in the western pools, though they are easy to underestimate because they seem more like sound than flesh.

They are small, soft, dark-backed creatures with pale throats and wide, solemn mouths. In dry hours they seal themselves into cracks with a membrane of their own mucus and wait, folded almost flat. Rain unbuttons them. Within seconds they emerge slick and inflated, legs trembling, eyes rinsed bright. They call from the bronze pools in clicks, bells, knocks, and little swallowed notes.

Their calls are maps.

Each pool has its own acoustics, and each male knows the exact stone angle that will make him sound larger, wetter, nearer, farther, doubled, or impossible to locate. Some call into the shelter-ribs when they appear in rain, and their voices pass through the temporary water-walls with a glassy resonance that attracts mates from cracks several body-lengths away. Females choose not the loudest male, but the one whose call returns most cleanly from the basin wall. A distorted echo suggests a weak throat, or worse, a poor understanding of stone.

Their tadpoles hatch fast. They have to. A pool may last days, or only hours. Some tadpoles become adults while still carrying tails; others delay themselves, remaining as dark commas in the water until the next rain. In the deepest western depression live old larvae that have refused adulthood for years. They are broad-headed, speckled with mineral bronze, and eat anything that falls in, including their own younger kin.

The furred creatures with too many elbows are the basin’s nearest thing to citizens.

They are small, no heavier than a fruit, with flattened bodies, soft gray-brown fur, and limbs that seem jointed for a different geometry. Their forelegs bend twice before the wrist. Their hindlegs can fold sideways against the ribs. This lets them pass through cracks that appear too narrow for a leaf. When they run, they do not bound like mice or lizards; they collapse and unfold, collapse and unfold, pouring themselves over stone.

Their faces are triangular and delicate. They have large dark eyes, whiskers that fork near the tips, and ears that can close like seed pods. Their teeth are orange from mineral deposits. They eat beetle larvae, fern spores, fallen fruit from the wall-trees, and the eggs of glass lizards when they can find them. They drink condensation by licking stone.

They are quiet except with one another.

In the half hour after evening moisture returns, they gather under the broad leaves and exchange touches: elbow to flank, whisker to ear, nose to paw. Families are loose but recognizable. A mother may nurse her own young and two stolen from a dead neighbor. Males carry fern fibers into cracks and weave sleeping pads that are immediately invaded by mites, beetle grubs, and pale threadworms. Nothing in the basin owns anything for long.

These furred ones remember the rain.

Not in language, perhaps, but in behavior so exact it resembles custom. Three seconds before the basin quiets, they are already under cover. They freeze with heads lowered, paws spread, ears sealed. Young ones who fail to do this are cuffed, dragged, or simply lost. They use the transparent shelter when it forms, though they never enter its center. They skirt its edges, as if some old animal instinct tells them that a wall made only of rain may also be a mouth.

Sometimes they tap.

This is easy to miss beneath the louder knocking from under the stone. A furred creature will sit beside one of the floor discs and strike it with a seed, a bone chip, or its own claw: two taps, pause, one tap, pause, two. The disc gives back a sound too low to hear properly. The animal listens with its jaw against the ground. No one knows what it receives. Afterward it may groom itself, flee, mate, or dig up a buried cache it could not have smelled from where it sat.

Above them all pass the split-tailed gliders.

They nest outside the basin, along the high lips and wind-cut shelves of the old range, but the basin is their hunting chamber. They are larger than birds and not birds exactly. Their bodies are narrow and muscular, furred along the spine, feathered along the limbs with stiff overlapping vanes. Their long tails divide near the end into two ribboning blades that twist independently, steering them through the rising heat.

Their heads are severe: hooked mouths, forward-facing eyes, small plated brows. When they cry, the sound is metallic enough to seem made elsewhere. It strikes one wall, shears across another, returns sharpened, and drops small prey where it stands. Beetles fall from leaves. Amphibians stop calling. Young hinge-limbed mammals stagger in the open if they are careless.

The gliders know the basin’s acoustics better than anything except the basin itself. A hunting adult will circle silently above the lip for several minutes, measuring wind and heat. Then it cries not at its prey but at a patch of stone. The wall answers. The prey collapses. The glider falls through sunlight, wings shut, tail split wide, and takes what the echo has stunned.

They do not enter during rain. Rain confuses their weapons. It softens the edges of sound and wakes the old artifacts, and the gliders distrust both.

There are winged mammals beneath the tree bark.

They are not seen until the bark moves.

The little wall-trees carry sheets of outer bark that loosen during the hot part of the day. Under these plates, pressed flat in living layers, sleep colonies of membranous, furred, needle-mouthed creatures. They fold their wings over their faces and hook their feet into grooves. Their bodies are warm and smell sharply of milk, musk, and fermented pollen.

At evening they peel away from the trunks one by one.

They feed from flowers that open only when moisture rises. Some lap nectar. Some take pollen. Some drink from the eyes of larger sleeping animals. Their tongues are hair-thin and longer than their bodies. They pollinate the vines near the western pools, but only indirectly; the vine’s true pollen rides in droplets curved by the shelter-ribs. The winged mammals harvest the droplets from the air, miss some, carry others on their faces, and wake dusted yellow.

Their young are born transparent-eared and enormous-eyed, clinging to the mother’s belly until too heavy. If one falls, the millipedes usually find it before dawn.

The plants live like animals here.

The ferns in the wet cracks open and close with a muscular deliberateness. Their blue-green tips are not decorative; they are moisture-sensing tissue, tasting the air, deciding how much of the plant can afford to exist above ground. In drought, whole fronds seem to vanish, withdrawing into coiled fists hidden in the crack. After rain they unfurl so quickly that droplets are flung from their tips.

Their spores are sticky and bitter. Beetles carry them accidentally. The furred creatures carry them in their elbow-folds. Some spores lodge in the cleaned radial bones around the discs and grow there, feeding on mineral salts drawn out of the dead.

The wall-trees are older than they look.

None are tall, but their roots go deep into seams warmed by buried things. They lean toward the basin center and toward the black post, obeying a direction not quite light. Their leaves are broad, soft, slightly furred underneath, shaped to catch dawn condensation and guide it down thin channels to the roots. Small animals chew these channels open to drink; the tree responds by thickening new channels elsewhere. Every leaf is both cup and negotiation.

The vines are stranger.

They hang from one ledge and stop at an invisible boundary because they have learned the shape of a field that no eye can see. Their tendrils explore it constantly, curl back, test again. During dry hours their flowers remain sealed, dull and greenish. During rain, when the shelter-ribs become a transparent wall, the vine opens all at once.

Its flowers are pale inside, almost white, with throats of deep rust-red. They release pollen not as dust but as a faint paste that dissolves into falling droplets. Those droplets do not fall straight. Around the shelter-ribs they bend and sheet and gather, carrying pollen along paths no ordinary plant could use. The vine depends on architecture left by unknown builders, but it has no memory of builders. To it, the wet wall is simply weather.

There are smaller lives in every surface.

Mites live in the pores of the black post and come out only when it patterns from within. Threadworms inhabit the slick boundary between dry dust and wet crack. Red algae bloom for minutes in the runnel, bright as blood, then vanish into a brown film. Lichen colonies grow in pale maps on exposed stone, advancing so slowly that generations of animals are born, eaten, and fossilized in their shade before a patch widens by the width of a claw.

Some shells on the basin floor are not abandoned.

There are soft animals inside them, spiral-bodied and patient, sealed behind mineral doors. They wake to rain, extend translucent tasting fans, scrape films from stone, mate without moving from their chosen depressions, and close again before noon. Their shells accumulate the chemistry of the basin in bands: ocher drought, bronze rain, black years when the post was active more often, pale years when it slept.

And below everything are the knockers.

No one sees them whole.

They live beneath the stone plates, in hollows warmed by the old buried system and reached through cracks too narrow for most surface animals. Their presence is known by their sound: three knocks, silence, three knocks again. The knocks are not random. They pass from one part of the basin floor to another, sometimes answering the frogs, sometimes preceding rain, sometimes continuing through the hottest hour when nearly everything else is still.

Fragments appear after floods: a hooked pale leg, a molted plate, a head-shield like a fingernail made of horn. From these remains one can guess at a flattened, many-legged creature, perhaps crustacean, perhaps insect, perhaps something descended from neither in any clean way. They may feed on roots. They may cultivate fungi on the underside of stone. They may tend the buried artifacts, scraping mineral growth from vents and seams, not out of duty but appetite.

The surface creatures fear their cracks.

A beetle dragged halfway into such a fissure leaves no sound behind. A glass lizard will abandon its own tail rather than follow prey too near a knocker-hole. The furred elbowed animals pause before crossing certain plates, listening with their jaws. If the three knocks come from beneath them, they take another route.

Yet the knockers may be the reason the basin remains breathable. Their hidden tunnels move vapor. Their scraping keeps cracks open. Their bodies carry spores, salts, and microscopic life between the damp underside and the hot exposed world. They are the basin’s unseen lungs, or one set of them.

The old devices are inhabited too.

Not by ghosts, not exactly, and not by minds in any familiar sense. The black post hosts films, colonies, electrical hungers. Rain reveals lattices inside it: thread-cities, nested geometries, pathways along which faint charges move like cautious animals. Microbes live in those seams that cannot live anywhere else. They feed on trace metals, on warmth, on the post’s slow waking. Some have transparent membranes that shimmer with internal sparks. Beetles eat them and become briefly luminous under the shell.

The floor discs gather attention.

Animals do not worship them, but behavior collects around them until it looks like ritual. Bones are arranged there. Seeds sprout there. The furred creatures tap them. Millipedes curve around them. Frogs calling near them produce doubled notes. Dead matter disappears fastest at their edges, not because the discs consume it, but because every scavenger in the basin has learned that food left there changes quickly: softens, sweetens, becomes easier to dismantle.

The shelter-ribs support a whole wet-weather community that exists only for minutes at a time.

When rain makes the transparent wall, tiny flat-bodied insects rush to its base and cling to the suspended water. Larvae rise from cracks and filter what slides down the invisible surface. Frogs call into it. Beetles drink from it. The vine pollinates through it. Mites surf its sheeting skin. Then the rain stops, the wall disappears, and an entire habitat collapses without leaving wreckage.

The creatures that depend on it do not mourn. They are already adapted to vanishing.

The largest resident may be the basin itself.

Not conscious in the manner of a face looking outward, but coordinated. Too many things answer one another. The rain falls with suspicious precision. The animals quiet at the same instant. The post wakes only when water touches it. The knockers move vapor below. The leaves turn toward buried rules. The gliders use the walls as instruments. The frogs tune themselves to echoes. The millipedes lay bones in diagrams that catch future life.

No one part commands it. The intelligence, if that is the word, is distributed through habit, erosion, appetite, old machinery, and selection. The basin does not think; it has been thinking-shaped.

It has made its inhabitants into organs.

The glass lizards are its quick eyes on stone.  
The beetles are its water-carriers.  
The millipedes are its cleaners and arrangers.  
The frogs are its pulse after rain.  
The furred elbowed creatures are its memory of paths and timings.  
The gliders are its knives from above.  
The knockers are its hidden breath.  
The plants are its cups, tongues, and green listening surfaces.  
The artifacts are its old bones, still giving off rules.

Nothing there is innocent of the place. Every creature has been bent by the basin’s small physics, by its warm walls, its abrupt rain, its acoustics, its metal aftertaste, its alternating thirst and abundance. They live as if the world is mostly stone and then, suddenly, water. They live as if shelter may appear only when falling rain draws its outline. They live as if sound can be sharpened, gravity can thicken, bones can become seed-traps, and a black post can flower with invisible food.

They are not relics, though the place is ancient. They are current, urgent, busy. Their bodies are answers to questions the basin asks again and again:

Can you drink before noon?  
Can you hide before the cry returns from the wall?  
Can you hatch before the pool vanishes?  
Can you use the rule without needing to understand it?  
Can you disappear into stone and come back when called by rain?

Those who live there can.
