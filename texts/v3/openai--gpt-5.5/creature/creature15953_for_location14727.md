No one species lives there alone.

The town is inhabited by a compact, many-bodied citizenship: people, goats, birds, rope-plants, beetles, lichens, stored seeds, house geckos, old tools, and stones with names. The people may claim the houses, but the houses belong just as much to the vines that hold their seams, the birds that use their openings, the insects that clean their refuse, and the mountain that slowly takes every wall back into itself.

The human inhabitants are small in number and close in habit. There are perhaps a few hundred of them, though no one counts them that way. They count households, roof-rights, cistern-claims, vine-lines, goat-mothers, seed jars, useful hands, old obligations. A person is not merely an individual but a knot in a net: who may cut from which plant, who must help reseal which roof, who is owed salt, who has touched the sealed cistern stones in witness, who descended last and returned.

They are lean people, not starved, but pared down. Their bodies have the look of things made by repetition: wrists tough from grinding, shoulders rounded from carrying, calves roped by ladders and roof paths, hands crosshatched with ash, thorn scars, cord burns, lime cracks. Their skin carries the mountain’s colors: brown, ocher, gray-gold, weathered black, reddish undertones like fired clay. Their faces narrow early from squinting into pale glare, though they live in shade. Most have fine lines at the corners of the eyes from looking far down into brightness and close into dim work.

Their hair is usually kept bound, wrapped, braided, or cut short. Loose hair catches dust, seeds, beetles, and the hooks of dry plants. Children wear theirs in little knobs or cords. Adults bind theirs with strips of cloth, goat leather, dyed fiber, or rings of polished bone. Elders often let theirs go white and long, not loose but netted, as if carrying a personal cloud of dry weather behind the head.

Their clothing is layered but light: woven tunics, sleeveless overwraps, knee-cloths, hoods, dust veils, sandals with thick soles patched again and again. The best cloth is not bright. Bright cloth wastes dye and calls birds. Their colors are mineral and plant colors: rust red, smoke black, chalk white, bruised violet, faded green, old gold. A festive garment is recognized not by extravagance but by fineness of weave, by the number of repairs made invisibly, by shell beads from below, by a bronze clasp polished through generations of thumbs.

They move carefully, even when hurried. No one swings an arm wide in a narrow alley. No one turns with a basket without knowing who is behind them. They step over sleeping pigs, under ladders, around drying racks, through plant curtains, past sharp tools, beside open roof edges. Children learn balance before manners. A toddler is first praised not for speaking, but for crossing a roof without scattering seed.

Their voices are strong.

A soft-spoken lowlander finds them abrupt, even quarrelsome, because everyone there has learned to speak through stone, wind, animal noise, roof distance, and cloth screens. They call across levels and expect an answer. They argue in public because there is almost no private distance in which to argue. Their speech is full of place-markers: above the red lintel, under the old goat wall, beside the fossil step, windward of the broken kiln. Directions are not abstract. Everything is by relation to stone, shade, and use.

They are not a solemn people, though their lives are strict. Scarcity has not made them quiet. It has made them exact, and exact people laugh sharply when exactness fails. They mock clumsiness, spilled grain, romantic speeches, bad knots, over-large gestures, and anyone who claims to smell rain too early. Their humor is dry, competitive, affectionate, and sometimes cruel in the way of people who cannot avoid one another for long.

Privacy exists, but it is made rather than given. A curtain lowered means more than a wall. A turned bowl outside a doorway means do not enter. A certain pattern of stones on a roof edge means someone is mourning, bleeding, giving birth, sleeping after a night watch, or settling accounts. Children are taught these signs before they are taught the oldest stories. To violate them is worse than shouting; it is to pretend the town has empty space.

They sleep close, work close, eat close, and remember everything.

Memory is one of their chief technologies. They know which roof leaks in a south wind, which vine swells earliest, which goat can untie a gate, which child hides seedcakes in the kiln vents, which family once opened a cistern during a feud and was never fully trusted again. They keep tallies on bone and potsherds, but the deeper records live in old people, in songs, in work chants, in the names of walls.

Their elders are treated less like authorities than like reservoirs. Not reservoirs of tenderness necessarily, but of weather, error, kinship, and precedent. An elder who has seen rain is listened to differently from one who has only heard the storm channels in stories. A very old person who remembers two rains becomes almost dangerous with importance. They are asked to describe the smell until the listeners accuse them of embellishment.

Children belong to their parents, but also to roofs, animals, and tasks. A child may be sent away from one courtyard for being underfoot and caught by another household before reaching the alley. They are raised by interruption: grind this, hold that, fetch ash, do not touch, climb here, listen, taste this seed and remember when it is bad. Their games imitate survival. They build miniature cisterns and seal them with mud. They race beetles through chalk mazes. They dare one another to stand beneath cliff-bird nests. They play at rain by throwing dust from roofs until adults cuff them for tempting waste.

The people’s senses are tuned differently from lowland senses.

They can smell metal on a trader’s pack beneath leather and salt. They can tell goat sickness from ordinary goat stink. They hear a loosened roof stone in the night chimes. They know by the weight of a seed jar whether it has been opened too often. They can distinguish useful dust from dead dust, edible bitterness from poison bitterness, a thirsting vine from a resting one. They do not believe someone who says, “It all looks the same.” Nothing there looks the same to them.

Their chief virtues are restraint, repair, and return.

A good person covers what should be covered. A good person mends before being asked. A good person knows how much to take from a plant, a neighbor, a story, a carcass, a silence. A good person who descends the mountain comes back when expected, or sends word, or is mourned as lost to a world too large to hold obligations properly.

Descent is a profession as much as a journey. The rope-road families are wiry, long-limbed, scarred around the hips and palms, with a particular way of looking at knots as if judging character. They trade with the lower places and return smelling strange: wet clay, green leaves, river fish, strangers’ smoke. For a day after their return people crowd them, not only for goods but for news. Then the town slowly rubs the lowland smell off them.

There are plant-keepers, though no one calls them gardeners in the lowland way. They are more like negotiators. They know the rope-plants by lineage and temperament: which will thicken a wall, which will pry it apart, which can be cut, which is storing, which is sulking after insect damage, which flowers only after a wind from the salt flats. They prune with bone knives and speak while doing it, not because they think the plants understand words, but because attention has a sound.

The barrel shrubs have household status. Some are older than anyone living. They sit in courtyards like squat ancestors, fenced, dusted, fed with ground shell and ash, guarded from goats and careless children. When one flowers, even slightly, people visit it. When one dies, its stored flesh is divided under rules so old that no one remembers whether they began as gratitude, fear, or rationing.

The shrine-keepers tend not gods exactly, but arrangements. Wind direction, old stone, fossil, bird path, cistern mouth, human oath. Their work is to keep relations from becoming casual. They renew pigment in pecked marks, replace offering stones, sweep dust from thresholds where shells are set into the floor, and recite the names of storms no one now alive has seen. They are practical people. They know how to set a broken limb, seal a jar, judge a lie by how it avoids detail. Their holiness is indistinguishable from maintenance.

The potters are half-blackened by smoke and ash, with pale eyes from squinting into kiln glow. The weavers carry patterns in their fingers. The bone-workers produce hinges, needles, combs, hooks, and little bird-shaped amulets so delicate they look impossible in that dry place. The metal-workers are treated with a mixture of envy and caution, because metal is rare, useful, and morally troublesome: it comes from elsewhere, remembers other hands, and always wants to be something else.

The cooks are among the most powerful people in the town.

To make food there is not merely to prepare it; it is to decide how bitterness will be borne, how dryness will be softened, how little can become enough. They know which herbs wake goat milk, which insects make a meal nutty instead of sour, how to roast thorn pods until the teeth forgive them, how to stretch grain without insulting it. A bad cook wastes. A good cook consoles without abundance.

Water, when present, is not owned in the ordinary sense. It is witnessed.

The water-keepers are chosen for dull virtues: patience, steadiness, lack of theatrical grief, lack of gambling, lack of beautiful excuses. No one wants a brilliant water-keeper. Brilliant people open sealed things for reasons. The water-keepers remember measures, seals, family claims, emergency laws. They can say no to a mother, a chief, a lover, a dying man. For this they are respected and disliked.

Birth in the town is a public secret. Everyone knows when it is happening; everyone pretends not to listen. The birth room is dark, warm, vine-shaded, thick with herb steam when enough moisture can be spared, or herb smoke when it cannot. Newborns are salted lightly at the feet, not as superstition alone but as recognition: you have come into a place where the body must keep what it can.

The dead do not go far.

There is too little soil for graves in the lowland sense. Bodies are dried, wrapped, reduced, divided by custom. Some bones become family bundles placed in wall niches. Some are taken to cliff ledges where birds finish the work and are thanked with offal and argument. Some small bones are made into useful things if the person consented before death: awls, toggles, beads, loom parts. This is not considered desecration. Waste would be desecration. Use is a kind of continuation.

Their beliefs are dry and literal, but not shallow.

They do not imagine paradise as gold or clouds. They imagine it as enough shade for everyone, cisterns that may be opened without a council, seeds that do not need counting, children who have heard rain with their own ears. They tell stories of a time when stone was soft, when shells lived where the summit now stands, when the mountain rose through water and kept some memory of it hidden in its bones. Fossils are not curiosities to them. They are proof that the world can become unrecognizable and still leave instructions.

They fear waste more than death, but they fear certain deaths especially: falling beyond retrieval, thirst in sight of home, being caught in a storm channel when the impossible water finally comes, dying below and being buried in wet ground where the body cannot properly dry.

The animals are not background.

The goats are angular, clever, and entitled. They know household boundaries better than strangers do and test them more often. Some are descended from famous milkers or famous escape artists. Their horns are marked with notches, cords, or stains. A goat that finds a new way onto a roof is cursed, admired, and bred if its milk is good.

The pack animals are tolerated like difficult relatives. They smell of dust, old rope, and offended dignity. They bite when overloaded and remember mistreatment longer than people expect. The descent families sing to them on dangerous paths, not sweetly, but in low repetitive phrases that keep hooves and human fear in rhythm.

The little pigs are communal criminals. They eat scraps, dung beetles, spoiled mash, dropped eggs, and anything left too low. They sleep in warm piles under platforms and scream as if murdered when moved. Children adore them. Adults pretend not to.

The chickens are ragged prophets of immediate inconvenience. They warn of snakes, strangers, falling baskets, and sometimes nothing at all. Their eggs are valuable enough that theft from a hen is still theft, even if the hen herself is foolish.

The cliff birds are another order of being.

Ravens know individual people and repay grudges with astonishing patience. Kites steal shining things, strips of drying meat, and once in a while a baby lizard from a child’s hand. Vultures sit like old judges on the outer stones, waiting without embarrassment. Swifts inhabit speed itself, cutting the air above a town where everything else conserves motion. The people read them constantly. A change in circling means carcass, weather, stranger, predator, or rumor from the cliffs.

Some birds are fed deliberately. Some are pelted with pebbles. Some receive both treatments on the same day. Sacredness there does not mean gentleness. It means inclusion in the rules.

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous and possibly the most important.

Beetles turn waste into usable matter. Ants map the town beneath the town. Moths pollinate night flowers no human ever sees fully open. Geckos patrol lamp halos like household spirits with blunt toes and lidless patience. Lichens write slow black weather on the shaded walls. Mushrooms sleep in cellars until dung, straw, warmth, and luck wake them. Seeds wait in jars, in cracks, in swept dust, in the bellies of birds. Much of the town is waiting to live more visibly.

The rope-plants may be the true old families.

They have outlived rooflines, marriages, feuds, and names. Their joints swell with seasons no person remembers. They bridge walls built by enemies now indistinguishable in descent. They hold houses together and pull them apart. A vine may shade a cradle, feed a goat, strangle a doorway, anchor a ladder, and mark a property dispute for eighty years. To cut one without permission is not merely damage. It is assault on a living piece of the town’s structure.

So the inhabitants are not “people in a settlement” so much as a dense arrangement of mutual permissions.

The humans permit birds to nest because birds warn, clean, steal, and sanctify. They permit goats to browse because milk becomes cheese and dung becomes fuel. They permit vines to invade because shade is life, even when roots loosen stone. The plants permit the town only because cracks, ash, bone meal, and human patience make habitat. The beetles permit cleanliness. The lichens permit walls to darken and breathe. The mountain permits all of them temporarily.

A stranger arriving at sunset would first notice the crowding: not a crowd of bodies alone, but a crowd of claims. Eyes in doorways. Birds on lintels. Goats chewing with sideways contempt. Children above, old women below, lizards on warm stone, vines brushing the neck, chimes speaking for the wind. Someone would give the stranger salt before asking questions. Someone else would already be judging their sandals, their water habits, their usefulness, their lies.

If the stranger stayed, they would learn that the town’s people are not romantic survivors gazing nobly over the empty world. They are busy, irritable, generous by law and by necessity, suspicious of excess, tender in indirect ways, and bound to one another by the simple fact that no one can afford to disappear from consequence. They do not think of their home as harsh. Harshness is what happens when relations fail. The town, for all its dryness, is relation layered upon relation until even dust has a category.

What lives there is a society shaped like a root system gripping stone.

It is human, but not only human. It is domestic and wild, sacred and thieving, brittle and stubbornly green. It lives by saving, binding, drying, naming, repairing, and waiting. It has learned to make abundance not from plenty, but from attention.
