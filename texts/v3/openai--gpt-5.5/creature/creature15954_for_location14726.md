What lives there is not one population but a system of unfinished attempts.

At first it seems to be a wilderness of animals and plants clinging to a volcanic highland. Then the distinction weakens. The grazers carry gardens in their hides. The roots behave like nerves. The pools open eyes. The moss hunts. The vents breathe in rhythms that the animals obey. Everything there is less a resident than a temporary expression of the plateau’s metabolism.

The oldest inhabitant is the plateau itself.

It is not conscious in any human way, but it is not passive. It circulates heat, moisture, minerals, spores, static charge, and flesh through itself. It revises its own surfaces. It feeds its vegetation from below and its animals from above. It makes weather the way a body makes breath. The mountains are its hardening skeleton, the ravines its folds and wounds, the vents its mouths, the storms its electrical shiver. The creatures moving across it are not separate from it in the way lowland animals are separate from a forest. They are more like organs that can walk away for a while.

Most of the life there is built for interruption.

Nothing expects permanence. Roots grip fast and let go fast. Seeds germinate in heat, ash, mist, blood, mineral slurry, or the warm crack of a newly split stone. Many plants do not grow upward first; they grow inward, sending black, glossy cables into fissures where they drink heat directly. Their leaves are thick, dark, waxed against dryness, but jointed and responsive. They can turn pale undersides to pressure changes, fold against ashfall, open during tremors, or stiffen when the air becomes electrically charged.

Some plants are barely plants at all. Along the vents grow valve-flowers with copper-veined throats, opening and closing in sequence with underground pulses. They trap insects, yes, but they also trap vapor, dust, and charged particles. In the ravines hang curtains of root-vines that twitch when animals pass too near. On fresh basalt grow mineral mosses that can dissolve a skin of stone in hours, digesting the young rock and leaving behind green-black mats dense enough to hold insects, eggs, dew, and sometimes the foot of a careless grazer.

The grazers are narrow, vertical creatures.

They live on cliffs as naturally as lowland deer live on meadows. Their bodies are long-backed and compressed, their ribs flexible, their heads blunt and low. Each hoof divides into many small black hooks, able to spread over glassy stone or close into points for basalt cracks. They scrape lichens, salt films, and heat-fed moss from new rock. Their hides are not bare. Moss, copper-threaded grass, and tiny flowering plants grow in channels along their flanks. These growths are part camouflage, part cooling system, part lung. When the air grows thin and wet, the green fur opens and drinks for them.

They do not run often. Running is dangerous on land that may not stay land. Instead they flatten, cling, and flow along slopes in single lines, each animal placing its feet where the last found pressure safe. When startled they do not leap away blindly. They freeze, and their hide-gardens darken to match the stone. In mist, a herd may look like a hanging orchard.

There are browsers in the vine thickets that resemble deer only from a distance.

Closer, they are too jointed. Their legs bend in several places, their bodies are sheathed in barklike plates, and their faces split into small precise mouthparts. They move without sound through the stacked forests, pruning new growth, tasting sap pressure, cutting tender leaves from plants that would poison anything less mineralized. Their antlers, if they can be called antlers, are branching sensory organs: woody, insectile, trembling. Before a pressure shift, all their antlers point in the same direction. Before a reality-slip, they kneel.

Their young hatch rather than are born. The eggs are laid in warm root-knots near vents and resemble unopened seed pods. Some are fertilized by animals, some by fungal threads, some by electrical storms. Not every young one emerges as the same kind of creature that laid it.

The air holds gliders.

They are birdlike only because the plateau has ridges, updrafts, and distances to cross. Their wings are translucent membranes stretched between elongated limbs, veined with amber and smoke. They rarely flap. They ride the warm breaths from vents and the invisible walls between weather layers. Their bodies are heavy and rust-colored, with soft insulating fat around the organs. Their heads carry crowns of filament-sensors that glow faintly violet before storms.

They nest nowhere permanent. A nest would be an error. Instead they carry their eggs under the body in mineralized sacs until a suitable cliff sweats the correct chemistry. Then they press the eggs into a crack and leave. The young emerge during condensation events, slick and pale, already able to spread their membranes and fall upward on a thermal.

They feed on vent moths, metallic worms, and the small floating organisms that bloom in mist walls. During the plateau’s key-changes, when places overlap, the gliders become confused or ecstatic. They circle the doubled cliffs and call without sound, flashing their filament crowns like sparks trapped in hair.

The pools have their own citizens.

Flat, pale creatures lie folded in black thermal water. Most of the time they look like scraps of wet parchment, mineral skins, or dead leaves caught below the surface. When the water trembles, they unfold. Eyes appear along their edges, not in pairs but in rows, each eye suited to a different kind of light: stormlight, heatlight, reflection, chemical glow. Their mouths are soft rings underneath them. They eat films, larvae, drowned spores, and sometimes the reflected image of something passing overhead.

Some never leave the water. Others can drag themselves over wet stone after a vertical rain, moving from basin to basin while the air remains saturated. If caught in dryness, they fold into hard triangular plates and wait. A folded one may be mistaken for shale until the next warm rain wakes it.

In the deepest pools live larger forms, seldom seen whole. They press themselves against the bottom like shadows. When the plateau trembles, they rise slightly, and their backs show patterns like maps of basins that do not yet exist.

The small life is more important than the large life.

Beetles mine salt from stone. Their jaws spark faintly in the dark where they cut metallic crusts from sweating cliffs. Vent moths drink heat rather than nectar, clustering around fumaroles with wings spread, absorbing warmth through black-veined membranes. Metallic-ringed worms surface when condensation thickens the soil, drawing moisture into their segmented bodies and leaving behind threads of conductive slime. These threads become pathways for fungi, roots, and tiny electric pulses.

There are colonies of animals too small to see clearly that polish rocks smooth in circular fields. Others open pores in basalt so moss can enter. Others live inside the nodules that draw moisture from the air, regulating how much vapor is released. The visible ecology depends on them. They are the machinists of the living factory, though they build nothing that looks built.

Predators are abundant, but they have learned that proclamation is wasteful.

One predator is a moss until it is not. It spreads as a soft black-green mat over warm stone, growing around cracks and roots, gathering dew. Small animals cross it safely many times. Then, when weight, heat, and vibration match some inner requirement, the mat rises in a silent purse around the leg or belly. It has no teeth. It adheres, tightens, drinks, and lowers itself again. After feeding, it flowers.

Another predator lives in mist pockets. It is long, nearly transparent, and structured like a spine with fins. In dry air it cannot be seen at all because it retreats into cracks or hangs limp among vines. In vapor it swims. Condensation beads on its body, sketching its outline for only a second: a narrow head, many faint ribs, trailing tendrils that taste electrical change. It hunts gliders, moth-clouds, and young cliff-grazers. Its strike is not fast in the ordinary sense. Rather, it seems to arrive by shortening the space between itself and its prey.

At night, or during the plateau’s dimmer intervals, larger beings descend from the upper air.

They cling upside down to warm cliffs with broad hooked limbs. Their bodies are dark and folded, like collapsed tents of skin. Their heads are small compared to their bodies, almost hidden in the throat. They drink vapor from cracks using long ribbonlike tongues, drawing moisture, minerals, and perhaps information from the stone. They are not often seen arriving. One looks up and finds them already attached to the cliff, pulsing gently as they feed.

They do not kill much. Their danger is weight. When many gather on a cliff face, the stone sometimes softens beneath them, accepts their impressions, and hardens again. After they leave, the cliff is marked with rows of shallow body-shaped hollows that fill with water and become nurseries for other life.

There may be intelligence there, but not settlement.

If there are people, they are not builders of roads or walls. They would have learned that the land refuses memory in straight lines. They would travel lightly, leaving no structures because structures invite revision. Their shelters would be temporary membranes stretched between living roots, dissolved before morning. Their tools would be grown in the body or borrowed from plants: hooked nails, mineral teeth, heat-sensing filaments, skins that bead water efficiently.

But more likely the plateau’s intelligence is distributed.

It thinks in herd-stillness before pressure drops. It thinks in grasses aligning to magnetic disturbance. It thinks in flowers closing around insects at the same moment miles apart. It thinks in fungal fans glowing under ravine walls, in root systems tapping heat vents, in animals kneeling when distance begins to misbehave. No single creature knows the whole plateau, but the whole plateau may know through them.

Memory there is not stored as paths or monuments. It is stored in scars.

A grazer’s hide-garden remembers the cliff where its mother fed. A glider’s filament crown remembers storms before they form. A vine remembers the shape of a passing animal if the cliff softened at the right moment. A pool remembers absent skies. A grove swallowed by a fissure may return downslope as saplings bearing identical wounds. Life does not preserve the past unchanged. It reissues it.

Reproduction is opportunistic, collective, and often ambiguous.

Stones split open with seeds inside. Animals shed skins that root. Flowers close on insects and later release winged things that are not exactly insects anymore. Pools evaporate, become clouds, and rain down carrying eggs from basins miles away. A dead grazer may sprout moss, then vine, then nodules, then a ring of small warm-blooded burrowers that inherit minerals from its bones. Death is not an ending there so much as a change in department.

The inhabitants are adapted not to stability but to revision.

They can sleep through tremors and wake for condensation. They can drink from air, stone, heat, blood, or reflection. They do not trust distance. They do not depend on seasons. Their migrations follow pressure fronts, mineral blooms, electrical taste, and the underground throb. Many have more than one body-state: folded for dryness, open for mist, rooted for storm, mobile for heat. Some alter color with humidity. Some alter shape with altitude. Some have shadows that lag behind them after reality slips.

They are quiet because quiet is survival.

Sound is expensive in a world that listens through stone. The animals communicate by pressure, posture, scent, skin-glow, and minute clicks absorbed almost at once by leaves. A herd turning its mossy flanks pale is a warning. Metallic grasses flattening is a warning. A vent pausing is a warning. Silence is not absence but conversation conducted below hearing.

When the whole plateau changes key, every resident participates.

The grazers lock their hooves and lower their heads. The jointed browsers kneel beneath vines. Gliders fold their membranes and cling to leeward stone. Pool creatures seal themselves into pale plates. Moss predators relax their traps. Flowers open or close according to rules older than species. The small mineral worms withdraw. The high cliff-drinkers press their bodies flat and stop pulsing.

For several minutes the inhabitants are not entirely singular. A grazer may have too many shadows. A pool may contain the reflection of a creature not beside it. A moss mat may bloom before it has fed. A glider’s wing may remember a storm from another version of the sky. Then warm rain falls, reality chooses one surface for the moment, and everything resumes.

So what lives there?

A population of cliff-clinging grazers, membrane gliders, insect-deer browsers, folding pool skins, salt beetles, heat moths, metallic worms, vapor-drinking giants, transparent mist hunters, moss predators, root valves, fungal nerves, mineral grasses, and stone-eating microbial swarms.

But beneath that list, something larger lives there.

An upland organism made of weather, basalt, heat, water, plant hunger, animal motion, and geological pressure. Its creatures are its passing thoughts. Its forests are its skin. Its ravines are its deep wet breathing. It is young, immense, unfinished, and alive enough that nothing upon it can remain merely itself for long.
