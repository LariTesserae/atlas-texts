What lives there is not a population so much as an arrangement.

The town is inhabited by pale, exact animals: creatures of damp brick, mild pressure, vibration, and mineral water. They have no wildness in the ordinary sense. They do not break out, overrun, migrate, starve, or bloom. Each one seems less like an individual intruder than like a necessary moving part, assigned to a ledge, a pipe, a groove, a temperature, a sound.

They are mostly blind, or nearly blind. Eyes are filmed over, bead-small, milked white, or absent beneath smooth skin. Sight would be of little use in a place where nothing approaches and nothing recedes. Instead they know the town by heat, tremor, condensation, pressure change, the taste of rust, the timing of pistons. They feel the engine hall through their feet and ribs. They know when a belt seam will pass, when a valve will breathe, when the great wheel above the entrance is one tooth from completing its turn.

The blind rooks are among the most visible. They gather on wires, rails, gutter lips, and the shoulders of chimneys, pale as old bone under thin soot. They have the shape of birds remembered incorrectly: heavy bodies, blunt wings, translucent lids, beaks worn smooth at the tips from endless tapping. They do not truly fly. At most they drop, hop, climb, and flutter hard enough to change ledges. Their nests are built under eaves from wire whiskers, dried valve packing, threads of shed fur, and the papery skins of dead insects. They sit in rows facing walls, tapping cloudy glass in measured patterns, as though reading vibrations back into the building.

Below them live the ratlike things, mineral-furred and narrow-shouldered, with too many incisors folded behind the first set like spare tools. Their fur is grey, white, yellowed, or iron-stained according to the masonry they sleep against. They do not gnaw for escape. They gnaw to keep apertures true: little bevels around pipe brackets, softened edges beneath doors, channels along old mortar joints. They move with great certainty through reciprocating machinery, sleeping where no ordinary animal could survive, tucked between pauses, beside rods that never quite touch them.

In the drains are the white crabs. They are flat, deliberate, and quarrelsome in a ceremonial way. Their claws strike ceramic with the sound of dropped buttons. They contest corners, outlets, and warm patches of seepage, but nothing ever comes of it. A claw lost is restored. A dead crab is absent only long enough for the number to remain correct. They clean nothing, exactly, and yet nothing in the drains fouls. Their pale shells carry brown water beads like varnish.

Around the valves knot the soft lamprey-things. They are less animals than mouths with bodies attached as afterthoughts: cool, rubbery, translucent, faintly ringed. They feed on condensation, on mineral bloom, on whatever minute sweetness pressure leaves behind. They loop around brass stems and iron flanges, pulsing when the lines pulse. Some hang in clusters like wet cords. Some live singly in bolt holes, showing only a slow opening and closing at the surface.

The insects are countless but never excessive. Coin-flat plated bugs slide under thresholds and along the seams of tile, their backs stamped with dull metallic ridges. Rust beetles live on oxidation without allowing it to advance. They rasp at iron with patient mouths, taking from corrosion the nourishment of corrosion, leaving the same red-brown weathering behind them. Wingless moths gather on dials, pipes, and enamel plaques. Their bodies tremble constantly, vibrating the useless muscles where wings might once have been. Their dust is pale, but it does not accumulate. They cloud numerals without hiding them.

There are also the rail creatures: long-tailed, jointed mammals or reptiles or something between, always moving along the undersides of railings in looping progress. They pass one another belly to back without dispute, tails briefly measuring the other’s spine. Their claws are shaped to old iron profiles. They descend only at certain posts, drink from the glazed street channels, and return to their appointed circuit. If startled, they do not flee; they merely pause until the next vibration permits motion.

In the canal live the chained eels. They are small, blind, and silver-white in the dark water, each fastened mouth to tail with the next until they form hanging cords below the outlets. They sway but do not drift. They feed from whatever the submerged pipes release: warmth, minerals, pressure-milk, old machine breath. They glimmer only when the light strikes through the brown surface at the correct angle. Their lives are individual only in the way links in a chain are individual.

The fungal crusts are quieter tenants. They occupy corners with the authority of survey markers: grey films on ceramic, black velvet beneath housings, amber nodules in pipe shade. They do not creep outward. They pulse faintly, accepting pressure changes as other things accept weather. They smell of wet pennies, old flour, shut cupboards, and extinguished boilers. Whether they are alive or merely residues imitating life no longer matters; the town has allotted them territories, and they keep them.

No creature there seems young for long, though there are chicks, larvae, soft-shelled crabs, newly pale beetles. No creature seems old past usefulness, though some are scarred, clouded, stiff, or worn to a familiar shape. Birth and death occur, but not as events. They are substitutions. The town keeps its census with impossible accuracy.

Their intelligence, if it can be called that, is distributed. A rook knows a window. A beetle knows a belt seam. A crab knows a drain lip. An eel knows an outlet’s taste. A rat knows the safe interval between two iron blows. None of them seems to command the others, and yet together they behave like one maintenance system with fur, shell, beak, mouth, and nerve.

They are not vermin. Vermin imply invasion, excess, appetite against order. These animals are the order. They occupy the human town the way oil occupies a bearing or water occupies a channel. They are what replaced use without replacing function.

And among them, perhaps above them, perhaps beneath them, live the machines.

The engines are not merely background. They breathe too regularly, answer too many bodies, permit too many impossibilities. Their pistons provide weather. Their belts make migration routes. Their gauges offer roosts. Their heat determines nests, hibernations, hatchings, and fungal borders. The animals do not interfere with the machinery because they are part of its tolerances. The machinery does not crush them because it has already made room.

So the true inhabitant may be the whole closed condition: engine, animal, damp, pipe, wall, channel, mineral crust, fixed number, fixed sound. A single basin-creature wearing a town as its shell and a thousand pale bodies as its moving organs.

It has no need of green life. No need of people. No need of change.

It lives by continuation. It eats pressure, condensation, rust, vibration, and time. It speaks in knocking engines, tapping beaks, wet drain-calls, claws on brick, belts over wheels, and the low organ note of chimneys.

What lives there is a completed ecology of maintenance: blind, pale, crowded, precise, and permanent.
