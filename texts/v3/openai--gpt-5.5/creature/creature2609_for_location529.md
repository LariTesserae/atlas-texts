What lives there is not one people, exactly, but a population of adjustments.

The human inhabitants are narrow in the way high-altitude people often become narrow: not weak, but efficiently made. Long hands, long noses, dry lips split and sealed with dark salves, eyes accustomed to estimating distance through vapor. Many have a slight forward lean, as if walking always into wind or uphill even indoors. Their chests are deep. Their voices are often quiet, partly from habit, partly because loudness travels unpredictably here and may return at the wrong time.

They dress as though every day might become a repair job. Wool under oilskin. Felted coats patched with rubberized cloth. Brass buckles, boot spikes, fingerless gloves, masks hanging at the throat for bad vapor or mineral dust. Children wear measuring cords tied at the wrist before they are trusted to cross certain districts alone. Older people carry little folding levels the way others carry prayer beads.

Their culture is practical, but not plain. They live among gauges and warning bells and municipal notices, yet they are intensely superstitious about margins: doorsteps, thresholds, stair landings, bridge joints, seams in pavement, places where one material meets another. They do not cross a new crack without naming it. They tap railings before leaning on them. They greet certain old pressure valves. They leave crumbs at cistern mouths for the blind things below.

They are builders, miners, machinists, pipewrights, surveyors, tram operators, fungus-gardeners, moss-scrapers, weather clerks, animal listeners, fissure wardens, boilermen, clay-harvesters, cartographers, and people whose entire profession is noticing when something has moved.

A respected citizen may be someone who can hear the difference between harmless settling and a slope preparing to arrive.

A wealthy one may own a stable foundation.

A foolish one says, “It was always like that.”

They do not ask visitors where they are from first. They ask: “How long did the road take you?” Then, “Uphill or down?” Then, if they are being polite, “Did anything follow?”

Their houses are vertical, inward, and heavily fastened. Rooms are small but warm, arranged around pipe-shafts and communal stoves. Windows are triple-glazed and often partly blocked by shutters, moss, or stored equipment. Nearly every dwelling has a wall of marks: child heights, floor levels, humidity stains, dates of unusual rain, the year a crack opened, the year it closed, the year Grandmother’s east window began showing the western viaduct.

Families keep ledgers. Not diaries exactly; more like domestic geology. Births, deaths, marriages, repairs, weather, subsidence, animal infestations, inexplicable echoes, the discovery of edible fungi behind the stove, a door that began sticking in autumn and opened into a different corridor in spring. These records are not sentimental, though sentiment lives inside them. They are how a household proves continuity in a place that edits continuity for sport.

Children are taught caution as a form of play. They race only in approved courtyards. They learn puddle-reading before multiplication. They can identify pipe colors beneath five layers of peeling paint. They know that a moth with street-map wings should not be killed indoors, because its pattern may be useful. They play “old city/new city,” in which one child stands still and the others slowly rearrange furniture around them until they no longer know where home is.

Their lullabies often include instructions:

Sleep with your boots toward the wall.  
Sleep with your name beneath your tongue.  
If the stairs are longer in the morning,  
count them kindly and come home.

The elderly are repositories of obsolete maps. They remember neighborhoods that are now slopes, stations that have migrated, wells that began as chimneys, and families who still receive mail though their street has not been reachable for nine years. Some old people are hired as consultants because they can say, “No, that alley is not new. It was here before, but lower.”

There are also the official professions of uncertainty.

The cartographers are pale, ink-stained, and argumentative. They work in tall rooms with weighted drafting tables and flexible maps pinned under glass. No one trusts them completely, including themselves. Their maps are beautiful, layered with seasonal corrections, pressure gradients, animal paths, fog-behavior, municipal claims, unofficial shortcuts, and regions labeled provisionally real. They use pencil wherever possible. Ink is considered arrogance.

The engineers are a priesthood in heavy boots. They maintain boilers, bridges, hydraulic foundations, condensation fields, traction cables, geothermal intakes, and the great listening machines anchored into the rock. They are not romantic people, but their rituals look religious: touching gauges in sequence, chalking numbers on iron, whispering to engines before restarting them after the mountain-note changes. They do not say machines are alive. They only behave as if machines can be offended.

The geomancers are less trusted and more needed. They read uplift, mineral bloom, heat, cracks, impossible damp, and the direction in which dropped things hesitate. Some use instruments; some use bowls of water, lichen growth, bird flight, or the taste of vapor on copper. The city pretends they are consultants. In emergencies, everyone listens to them.

The pipewrights form an old guild and are among the most powerful people in the city. They know which pipes carry water, steam, brine, compressed air, slurry, heat, messages, memory, debt, or things officially discontinued. They can identify a pipe by condensation pattern or by the sound it makes when struck with a fingernail. Their apprentices spend years learning valve genealogy: red over yellow means one thing if painted before the Third Revision, another if painted after the Year of Warm Rain, and something fatal if the black beneath it is original.

Some inhabitants almost never see the sky. The cistern-keepers, tank divers, seepage sorters, and amphibian-listeners live in damp lower quarters where everything rings and drips. Their skin takes on a gray softness. They smell faintly of mineral water and lamp oil. They keep blind fish in ceramic bowls as warning creatures. They speak gently because echoes in the tanks can become crowded.

At the other extreme are the cable crews, who move above the city among towers and ice-rimed wires. They are compact, fearless people with scarred palms and elaborate harnesses. They share the heights with birds and near-goats, and sometimes seem closer to them than to pedestrians. They know the city as a web of tension. They can tell from a tremor in a cable whether a tram is overloaded, whether ice is forming, whether a tower has shifted, or whether something large has landed where nothing should land.

The market people are the city’s social lungs. They trade in ordinary necessities and impossible ones: turnips, lamp oil, boot nails, fungal flour, smoked trout from unconnected cisterns, wire-worm traps, pressure charms, old door numbers, roof-moss for insulation, future-dated newspapers from the archive quarter, and tiny sealed bottles of “rain from the correct side.” They are loud by local standards, though even their shouting has an angled caution to it.

Food is hearty, hot, and mineral. Broths thickened with roots and barley. Black bread baked in communal ovens. Pickled alpine greens. Mushrooms dried in pipe-warm attics. Goat cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. Marmot, sometimes, though politely not when a pressure change is expected. Ironberries cooked with sugar until they stop tasting like blood. Tea made from dwarf willow, lichen, resin, and whatever medicinal plants can survive under copper nets. Salt is precious and strange, gathered from condensation, traded by grade and weather-origin.

Their celebrations are not tied to fixed dates as much as to measurable events. The First Thaw of the Northern Pipes. The Day the Central Station Relevels. The Opening of Soft Clay. The Quiet Week, if the mountain’s note remains low for seven days. The Festival of Additional Steps, when children decorate staircases that gained height over winter. People hang bells from new cracks, not to drive anything away, but to hear whether the crack is still becoming.

They marry with measuring rituals. Two families mark a shared beam, pour water across a threshold, exchange calibrated weights, and promise to correct one another gently when either begins to drift. Divorce is sometimes described as “unbolting.” Mourning involves covering mirrors and reflective puddles, because grief here is thought to attract alternate rooms.

The dead are not buried deep; deep ground is too young and too busy. Some are placed in stone vaults bolted into stable ridges. Some are cremated in furnace districts, their ash mixed into concrete for public works. Engineers consider this honorable. To become part of a bridge is a respectable afterlife. A few old families maintain “listening graves,” shallow chambers fitted with metal plates, so descendants can hear whether the mountain has accepted the body or is still considering it.

But the human population is only the most administratively visible form of life.

The city is full of animals that have learned industry.

The alpine crows are clever, glossy, and legally ambiguous. They steal wire, trade buttons for food, unfasten small latches, and sometimes drop stones onto suspicious new cracks until workers notice. Some are banded by the Weather Office as official pressure birds. Others are wanted by the Tram Authority. Their nests in the cable towers glitter with foil, mica, copper thread, and stolen survey flags.

The white-throated swifts are almost never still. They live in the air between districts, stitching fog with their bodies. Children believe they carry messages faster than pneumatic tubes. This is untrue in most weather and true in some.

The little red birds are considered lucky because they remain visible when everything else turns gray. No one agrees on their species. They nest in warm vents and sing before certain clocks correct themselves. Their eggs are sometimes sold in markets, though it is frowned upon unless the seller can prove the bird abandoned them willingly.

The near-goats are not entirely domestic and not entirely wild. They have heavy shoulders, backward-curving horns, thick winter pelts, and eyes with a disturbing talent for judgment. They walk across cornices, roofs, tram supports, and unfinished retaining walls as if gravity signed a separate agreement with them. They lick salt from masonry and mineral bloom from fissures. They are used for milk, wool, companionship, and slope-testing. If the goats refuse a ledge, no competent builder steps there.

Factory marmots live beneath warm foundations in colonies that predate some buildings and postdate others. They are fat, soot-stained, and extremely opinionated. Their whistles form part of the city’s informal alarm system. One whistle for pressure change, two for fox, three for pipe rupture, a long descending trill for something underground moving wrong. Workers feed them peelings and argue over whether this counts as bribery.

The foxes are thin, red-gray, and steam-wise. They move through slag terraces, warehouse gaps, and night markets. They eat rats, beetles, dropped dumplings, and occasionally official documents. A fox seen crossing a bridge before dawn means the bridge is still trustworthy. A fox refusing to cross is reported.

The moths are too large, too silent, and too beautifully patterned. Their wings show false maps, forgotten maps, predictive maps, or nonsense that becomes useful later. Entomologists collect only dead specimens; killing a live map-moth is illegal in three districts and unlucky in all of them. During warm industrial nights they cover lamp cages like living parchment, trembling as if streets are trying to rearrange themselves across their wings.

The pale lizards in switch boxes are tolerated because they eat wire-worms and because electrical faults are less common where they sleep. They are cool to the touch, blind in one eye more often than probability allows, and capable of flattening themselves into cracks thinner than paper. Electricians leave crumbs of dried insect near junctions to encourage tenancy.

The cistern trout are a civic embarrassment. No one can explain them, though explanations are proposed every year: eggs carried by birds, time seams, ancestral reservoirs, underground rivers, spontaneous fishhood, clerical error. They are silver-green, slow, and difficult to catch. Some cisterns have generations of them. Children swear the largest ones know door codes.

Beneath the oldest district, in rain tanks and wet cavities, live the blind amphibians. They are soft, translucent, blunt-headed, and musical. Their low pulsing songs travel through pipes at night. Plumbers learn to distinguish ordinary breeding calls from warning choruses. There are neighborhoods where people sleep better when the tank-singers are audible. Silence from below is more frightening than noise.

Insects own the moss. Black cold-beetles, copper mites, glass-wing gnats, pipe ants that build nests around warmth, and the dreaded wire-worms: pale segmented things that chew insulation, mineral salts, and occasionally memory stored in certain message lines. Warehouses keep beetle-cats to control them. The cats are not mentioned often in official descriptions, perhaps because they are only cats in outline: long, low, thick-tailed, with heat-loving bellies and eyes that reflect green even in total darkness.

Bats occupy ventilation shafts. They emerge at dusk in ragged ribbons and hunt above the condenser fields. Some roost in abandoned observatory chambers and return with frost on their fur. They are banded by students, blessed by old women, cursed by laundry workers, and studied by navigators because their flight reveals invisible air.

The long-legged scavenger birds of the dawn markets are unpleasant but useful. They have gray necks, hooked beaks, knees like folded calipers, and an interest in refuse, offal, and unguarded pastries. They clean what would otherwise rot. Market vendors insult them by name.

And then there are the plants, which live here with a tenacity that looks almost like ambition.

Moss is the city’s second masonry. It pads concrete seams, swallows bolts, softens stair edges, drinks from steam, and records leaks more faithfully than inspectors. People harvest some varieties for bandages, insulation, bedding, poultices, and roof packing. Other mosses are left alone because they grow only near unstable heat or because touching them produces dreams of deep water.

Liverwort covers certain walls in slick, shining sheets. Ferns crowd tunnel mouths where the air exhales warm. Alpine grasses colonize abandoned platforms, bending and rattling like fine metal. Waxy shrubs root in cracks and refuse to die. Dwarf willows are trained along wires in courtyards, becoming living trellises, windbreaks, privacy screens, and family heirlooms.

Fungi do well in stairwells, cistern rooms, old tram shelters, and behind loose tile. Some are eaten. Some are medicinal. Some glow faintly when pressure drops. Some are removed by municipal order. One pale shelf-fungus is used by archivists as a humidity warning: if it opens overnight, documents must be moved before noon.

The ironberries near the condenser yards may be blackberries, or may only have learned blackberry as a strategy. Their fruit stains the mouth dark and leaves a metallic sweetness. Children eat too many and are scolded. Foundry workers make a harsh wine from them that tastes like rust, smoke, and weather.

There are also things living there that do not fit comfortably into animal, plant, or citizen.

The machines have accrued habits. Not minds, perhaps, but preferences. A pump that runs better after being struck twice and called by its old name. A tram cable that sings before fog. A hydraulic column under the central station that refuses new paint. Boilers that settle when a particular engineer hums near them. Elevators that stop at unlisted floors during rain. The people insist these are maintenance issues. Then they maintain them with offerings, songs, chalk marks, and inherited insults.

The pipes carry more than substances. Certain message lines preserve whispers for years. Some steam mains repeat arguments from the households they pass. Old brine pipes knock in answer to questions, though never accurately enough to be useful in court. Children press ears to warm metal and claim to hear neighborhoods not yet built.

The clocks are semi-domesticated. Most keep ordinary time if wound, leveled, and not exposed to archive mildew. Others require persuasion. The north terminus clock is treated like a dangerous elder: respected, avoided, consulted only with paperwork. Watchmakers here are part mechanic, part mediator. They do not repair time. They negotiate local agreements with it.

Reflections breed. Not everywhere, not always, but in selected puddles, black windowpanes, polished gauges, cistern surfaces, and wet slate. A reflected courtyard may appear before its walls are raised. A person may see themselves wearing older clothes, or standing beside someone not yet born, or walking down stairs that do not exist on this side. Most citizens avoid staring. Artists, lovers, and the bereaved do not.

The fog is inhabited too, though opinions differ by what.

Some say it contains ordinary moisture, mineral vapor, furnace breath, and optical confusion.

Some say it contains lost distances.

Some say it is how the mountain thinks.

There are forms glimpsed in it: tram passengers waiting at stops that have no platform, goats standing upright at the edge of sight, surveyors with obsolete instruments, a woman carrying a basin of water uphill forever, red birds flying backward, buildings with familiar rooflines but wrong chimneys. The practical response is to ring a handbell, check one’s compass, count one’s fingers, and continue only if the road remains underfoot.

The mountain itself may be alive, but the city is divided on whether saying so is childish, poetic, heretical, or simply imprecise. It rises, adjusts, exhales heat, opens veins, changes pitch, accepts anchors, rejects foundations, makes water, mislays roads, grows stone through paving, and occasionally seems to answer pressure with pressure. The oldest workers speak of it not as a beast but as an unfinished body. The city is not built upon it so much as caught in its becoming.

If it lives, it lives slowly.

If it speaks, it speaks in millimeters.

The people have learned to listen at that speed.

At night, when cloud lowers and lamps become small yellow cages in the wet air, all these lives overlap. A tram crawls across a trestle, blue sparks falling too slowly beneath it. Moss drinks from a leaking pipe. A pipe carries heat, water, and a remembered sentence. Crows rearrange stolen wire in a tower nest. An engineer pauses with one hand on a valve because the ground-note has shifted. Children in a high dwelling sleep under blankets that smell of wool and smoke. Blind amphibians pulse in the tanks below. Somewhere, a clock rings once for an hour arriving and once for the same hour leaving.

What lives there is a city’s worth of creatures that have accepted revision as climate.

They do not conquer the place. They fasten themselves to it, bargain with it, cultivate its seams, harvest its hesitations, and make homes in the interval between one version of the ground and the next.
