What lives there is mostly edge-life.

Not forest life, not field life, not the life of burrows in earth, but the life of seams, lips, brackets, drains, handrails, pipe-shadow, underside, warm gap. Everything that endures there has learned that the center belongs to pressure, weather, and machines. The inhabitants keep to the margins with the plants, occupying the same narrow treaties: a ledge under a duct, a nest inside a broken horn, a dry flange above a condensation run, the hollow behind a gauge face where warmth comes through the wall.

They are small because the place is small, but they are not timid. Timidity would be useless in a world that never lowers its voice.

The birds are the most visible. They are compact, bright-eyed things with heavy blunt beaks and feet that close around railings like clamps. Their feathers are dark at the roots and iridescent at the tips, dusted often with gray metallic powder, so that in strong sun they flash blue, green, and oil-black. They do not sing in any ordinary sense. Their throats pulse, their crests flare, their wings snap open to show panels of warning yellow and rust red. Courtship is semaphore. Alarm is posture. Rivalry is a dance performed on vibrating bronze.

They nest where the old loudspeakers once faced outward over the drop. Generations of them have packed the speaker horns with vine fiber, filter lint, shredded gasket, hair, shed lizard skin, and pale moss. Their young hatch to vibration before light. They know the compressor boom as other birds know dawn. When the great wheel turns and spills water into the upper trough, they arrive in a flickering line to pick larvae from the wet edges.

The frogs belong to the drains.

They are almost colorless until seen against metal, where their translucence becomes a kind of camouflage: the green threads of their organs echo algae, their bones look like pipe-scale, their pulsing throats like trapped bubbles. They do not call above the machinery. Instead they press themselves to grates and send tremors through their bellies into the wet iron. Mates answer from channels several body-lengths away. A whole breeding chorus may pass through the floor without making a sound recognizable as sound.

Their tadpoles live in tea-colored puddles, in cistern lips, in the water caught beneath sagging plates. Many die when a drain opens or a hot pipe wakes. Many more survive because the adults choose water by taste, by tremor, by the faint electrical prickle of contamination. They know not to breed in the silver-skinned pools. They leave those for beetles with armored mouths and for stranger things that do not mind poison.

There are lizards too, copper-skinned and flat-ribbed, living between heat and height. They bask on exchanger housings where no mammal would rest long. Their scales are patterned in fine dark chevrons like machine knurling. Their toes can hold to painted metal, oxidized bronze, cable sheath, wet ceramic. They feed on moths, algae beetles, and the pale flies that emerge from digesters after rain. When the turbine rhythm changes, they vanish before anything else seems to have noticed.

The moths are broad and soft and improbable. Their wings bear markings like old signage: diagonal hazard bands, false screw-heads, red-ringed eyes like emergency shutoff labels. They sleep on condensation screens, so still they resemble scraps of peeled instruction decal. At night they move heavily through amber lamp-glow, visiting valve-flowers that open in humidity. Their caterpillars eat the engineered leaves and incorporate flecks of metal into their skins, making them bitter, glittering, and difficult to chew.

The snail-like scavengers are older inhabitants, or seem older because they move as if time has granted them special permission.

They cling to vertical metal with magnetite shells: dull black, striped by rust, worn smooth where they pass through tight seams. Their bodies are gray and velvet-soft, their mouths patient rasps. They eat corrosion bloom, lichen, mineral crust, dead algae, softened paint, pollen sludge, and the first film of decay on fallen leaves before the drains take it. They leave behind clean arcs on pipes and bright trails on old warning plates. Maintenance drones tolerate them. The vines seem to tolerate them too. In some places the snails have cleaned contact points that would otherwise have failed years ago.

Below the obvious animals are countless smaller citizens: mites in moss, blind white isopods under rubber walkways, wire-thin worms in root-vats, translucent shrimp in puddles no wider than a hand, colonies of algae arranged by leak temperature, fungal mats that bloom only on warm insulation, bacteria that taste of oil and mineral scale. The garden is crowded with lives that never cross the open deck except as spores, dust, eggs, or scent.

But the true residents—the ones who make the place seem watched—are the keepers.

They are not many. Perhaps nine, perhaps twelve, depending on whether the very old and the newly born are counted separately. They are hard to see at first because they have the color and habits of the margin. They live in the service alcoves behind the basin wall, in warm compartments between cisterns, in chambers originally meant for pumps or diagnostic cabinets. Their doors are not doors so much as hinged screens hidden behind fern and cable. They emerge when mist thickens, when lamps buzz on, when the wheel reaches certain positions, when a drain coughs wrong.

They are small-bodied and long-limbed, built for climbing rather than walking distances. Standing straight, the tallest would not reach far above the bronze rail. Their feet are narrow and prehensile, with dark pads toughened by hot grating. Their hands are clever, calloused, stained green-black by bronze and plant sap. Some have five fingers, some six; the extra digit, when present, is small and jointed sideways, useful for hooking mesh and testing live cable from a careful distance.

Their skin is smooth in some places, fine-furred in others, usually the color of damp ash, old rubber, or the underside of leaves. Along the sides of the throat and chest lie faintly luminous patches—not bright, not decorative, but capable of flushing pale yellow, blue-white, or rust red. They speak with these, and with hands, and with taps carried through rail and deck. Their mouths can make sounds, but the sounds are mostly for close comfort: clicks, hums, soft glottal notes felt more than heard. In the garden’s roar, language has become visual, tactile, rhythmic.

Their ears are small and often folded tight against the head. They are not deaf, exactly. They hear differently. Sudden high shrieks pain them; deep machinery they read through bone. A keeper standing barefoot on a plate can tell whether the lower compressor is approaching its boom, whether the wheel trough is running thin, whether an elevator shaft three levels below is in use, whether rain is gathering in cloud before it touches the rail. Children learn these things before they learn the names of plants.

Their eyes are large, dark, and filmed with a second lid for mist. They blink sideways in warm rain. They can see inspection blue and sodium amber, but also the tiny changes of sheen that mark unsafe water, hot metal, fresh oil, active current. The youngest are fascinated by stars because stars make no vibration. They will sit in the rail-shadow after a clear nightfall, staring upward at that silent brightness, while the adults work around them.

They do not own the garden. Ownership would be too large and too rigid a concept for such a place. They belong to it as much as the frogs do, though with more obligations.

They clear drains with hooked rods and long-fingered patience. They prune the red vines without cutting the filament cores. They smear mineral paste on pipe-joints that sweat too freely. They move frog eggs from doomed puddles to safer channels. They knock beetle colonies away from active relay boxes but leave them on dead hazard stripes. They harvest moss in squares from the old sensor grids and press it into sleeping pads. They polish the rail not out of reverence but because hands constantly need it.

Their tools are made from what the tower gives: wire twisted into needles, ceramic shards hafted with root fiber, old gauge glass shaped into scrapers, drone limbs repurposed as tongs, bird bone whistles used only at very close range, insulated gloves patched until they resemble bark. They carry small spools of colored thread to mark danger: blue for hot, red for awake, yellow for not-yet-known, black for do-not-touch-even-if-it-looks-dead.

The keepers eat from the garden, but carefully. They cultivate fungus in warm housings and grow bitter greens in troughs fed by clean condensation. They collect moth eggs, beetle grubs, and pale shrimp from tested pools. They ferment leaves in sealed ceramic jars warmed by digesters. On feast days—if days can be called that where the tower’s cycles matter more than calendars—they tap the valve-flowers before they fully open and mix the peppery nectar with algae meal. Their food tastes of steam, iron, leaf, and sourness.

They raise children communally because no ledge is safe enough for private parenthood. Infants are carried in sling-nets against the chest, where they feel the adult heartbeat through the tower’s constant shaking. Older children are tethered until they learn the rail, the drains, the horn, the silver water, the waking pipes. Their first rite is not a name but a crossing: from one edge of the basin to another without stepping into the central plate, without startling nesting birds, without touching the red vines, and without losing count of the wheel’s groan.

The oldest keeper lives near the north edge, behind the slow wheel. Whether they are male, female, both, or something age has made irrelevant is not easy to say. Their throat patches are dim. Their hands are swollen at the joints. Fern spores grow in the seams of their outer wrap. They know which leaks are ancestral and which are new. They remember when the inspection pedestal still turned, when one maintenance arm moved smoothly, when a particular tree-tank bore leaves with clearer numbers. They can place a palm on the deck and listen for changes no one else believes are there until a week later a pipe bursts or a hatch seals itself shut.

The keepers have no written law in the open, but they leave memory everywhere.

Notches under the rail.

Knotted root-cords hanging beside drains.

Shells arranged near unsafe puddles.

Painted dots on the backs of access panels.

Bundles of old feathers tied to service ribs where someone fell or was lifted by pressure or went over the edge in cloud.

They do not make monuments in the center. Nothing lasts there. Their dead are not buried, for there is no earth deep enough and no stillness suitable. Some are given to the digesters with ceremony, becoming warmth and vapor and leaf. Some are wrapped in moss and lowered into service shafts whose depths belong to older agreements. A few, the wheel-tenders and drain-mothers and those who died in repair, have their names tapped into bronze where hands pass daily, polished gradually into touch rather than sight.

They fear the open sky less than they fear silence.

Silence, to them, means failure below. A stopped fan. A dead pump. A pressure loss. A break in the body that holds their little lung aloft. When a machine pauses unexpectedly, every visible creature seems to freeze: birds flatten, lizards drop into cracks, frogs sink, keepers crouch with fingers spread on metal. Only when the rhythm resumes does life resume around it.

They are not the masters of the tower. They may not even know what the tower was built to do. Their myths are practical. The tower is the Below-Body. The garden is the Wet Crown. The wheel is the Slow Throat. The central plate is the Breath Door. The red vines are Nerves. The drains are Memory Holes. The rail is the Last Circle. Beyond it is not death exactly, but Too Much Air.

Sometimes drones still come up from lower levels.

Old maintenance units, patched by unknown hands or by their own routines, rising along exterior tracks or unfolding from hatches with insect caution. The keepers greet them by staying out of their way. Some drones are blind and harmless, polishing lamps that no longer function. Some are dangerous with obedience, clearing anything from a marked path whether living or not. A few have entered the garden’s society in their own limited fashion. One trims only moss from pressure gauges. One spends every dawn testing the rail with a soft brass probe. One small drone, no larger than a bird, follows children and flashes warning lights when they approach hot seams.

The keepers decorate these useful drones with vine fiber and shells, not because the drones care, but because decorated things are easier to remember and less likely to be mistaken for threats.

At cloud-passage, all inhabitants become partial.

A keeper’s hand appears on a rail, then vanishes.

A bird’s wing opens like a colored sign in gray vapor.

The wheel groans somewhere near and enormous.

Frogs pulse beneath the grates.

Water runs down faces, feathers, leaves, shells, wires.

In those moments the garden’s life seems less like a collection of species than a single distributed creature: roots drinking, pipes sweating, snails scraping, children blinking mist from their eyes, birds balancing on the slow wheel, drones ticking, frogs trembling messages into the deck. All of it held inside the roar. All of it adapted to the fact that the world is made, failing, alive, and high.

What lives there, finally, is a people of maintenance and mist, surrounded by animals that have forgotten any quieter ancestry.

They are not primitive, though their tools are simple.

They are not wild, though they are not tame.

They are not separate from the machinery, though they are flesh.

They live as every successful thing there lives: by reading vibration, respecting edges, using leaks, distrusting open space, and giving every surface more than one purpose. They are the garden’s memory with hands. They are the tower’s small, damp conscience. They are what happens when repair continues for so many generations that it becomes culture, and when a manufactured summit grows inhabitants who no longer ask whether it is natural enough to be home.
