The first and oldest inhabitants are not animals at all, but the floor.

The plateau is lived in by a many-bodied skin: plant-colonies so old and continuous that no one creature can say where one life ends and the next begins. They are not trees reduced by hardship, but something that has forgotten the ambition of trees. They do not reach. They occupy.

They live by being broad, low, difficult, and patient.

Some are mat-plants with no central stem, only thousands of interlocked pads, each pad able to root if torn away. A grazing mouth may rip a hole in them, a flood may peel a sheet from stone, fire may blacken them down to ash-colored crust, but the surviving edges begin leaning inward almost at once. In warm wet hours they visibly work, hairs uncurling, pores opening, beads of water sliding into little mouths along their seams. In dry weeks they darken and tighten until they resemble dead hide. Then rain touches them and they soften, green returning from underneath like a thought remembered.

There are bladder-plants in the basins, slick and swollen after storms, dull and wrinkled between them. There are thorn-felts that grow not upward but inward, making a springy armor over hollows, so that a hoof or foot may find solid ground one moment and a mouth of woven stems the next. There are red-black lichens that eat stone slowly and make a gritty dust that other plants use as soil. There are root curtains under ledges, pale as old hair, drinking fog, pollen, insect bodies, the breath of animals sheltering there. There are ring-plants in the basins, each species waking at a different depth of water, so that the memory of every flood is written afterward in colored circles.

The plants are not clever in the manner of animals. They do not plan. But they answer everything. Pressure, shade, blood, dung, ash, hoof, corpse, footprint, tool, bone: all of it is information to them. They braid over it. They dissolve it. They make it useful. Anything left still becomes plant-shaped.

Among them lives the second great population: the small hard nations.

The insects are the real weather of the plateau. They have more kinds than anyone has names for. Oil-dark stem-borers with shovel heads. Glass-winged gnats that rise from wet basins so densely that animals breathe them in. Red-jawed ants that build low, temporary fortresses after rain and abandon them before they dry. Hoppers with hind legs like bent reeds, striped to match the vine fields. Beetles that roll pellets of grazers’ dung into the seams of the mat, where their young feed in green darkness. Pale larvae that sleep for years inside dry plant bladders and wake only when the wall around them fills with water.

Many of them live on interruption. They wait as eggs, scales, husks, cysts, seeds of animal life. Then moisture arrives and the plateau opens for them.

For a day or two they are everywhere: breeding in hoofprints, singing from puddles, tunneling into softened stems, rising in silver curtains from the mats. Their lives are brief but not wasted. They feed birds, frogs, lizards, small hunters, plants. They pollinate the thunder-flowers and chew them to shreds. They fill the air with sound until silence seems like something that belonged to another world.

There are amphibious things too, though no permanent lake is large enough to promise them safety. They are squat, flat-bodied creatures that spend most of the year sealed in mudless chambers under the plant-skin, wrapped in their own dried mucus. When rain comes, they break out already calling. Their voices are clicks, knocks, wooden rattles, little barking pulses that travel through the wet mat. They mate in water no deeper than a palm. Their young grow fast, feeding on algae, drowned insects, and one another. If the basin lasts, they leave it on soft legs. If it fails, the quickest burrow down; the rest become food.

The grazers are built for distance and for lowering themselves to the world.

Many are narrow-backed, light-footed animals with small branching horns or antlers, their bodies tawny, gray, reddish, or moss-dark depending on the part of the plateau they favor. Their muzzles are tough and mobile, able to tease apart edible strands from thorn-felt and crop the slick margins of bladder-plants without losing too much blood. Their teeth grow continuously. Their tongues are often dark, stained by mineral-rich leaves and the pigments of wet-season flowers.

They move in loose herds that seem scattered until danger touches them. Then they become one body, all heads lifting, ears shifting, flank-muscles trembling. They know the safe ribs of buried stone, the basins that hold water longest, the mats that will carry them, the mats that will not. They calve in the open because there is no closed place to hide. A newborn must stand quickly here. The plateau does not forgive softness for long.

The larger grazers are lower and heavier. They kneel to feed. Their forelimbs are thick, their shoulders humped, their faces blunt and armored, with lips and teeth made for tearing whole sections of plant-skin from stone. They leave wounds behind them: raw oval patches of mineral soil, steaming with exposed dampness and full of insects within minutes. Plants follow them almost lovingly. Their dung is rich. Their damage opens space. In a place where everything closes, they are makers of gaps.

There are browsers as well, though they are fewer: long-necked animals that travel between the rare upright thickets and spring growths. Their height makes them visible from impossible distances. They look exposed, almost foolish, walking across a land that prefers low things. But they are watchful and quick, and their young hide by lying flat with their necks stretched along the ground, becoming another line in the vegetation.

The predators have learned that there is no forest to leap from, no deep shadow to own. So they become ground, fold, patience, or speed.

Some are flat-bodied ambushers, mottled in green-brown and violet-black, with wide paws or splayed claws that distribute their weight over the mats. They press themselves so low that insects crawl across their backs. Their eyes are set high, their breath slow. They wait beside game paths, puddles, torn feeding patches, and the narrow places where stone ribs force animals into lines. When they strike, they seem to come not from hiding but from the land deciding to move.

Others hunt in families: lean, low-running pack animals with long jaws, deep chests, and tails held straight for balance over uneven plant-skin. They do not need trees or caves. They use distance. They run an animal until the heat and altitude enter its blood. They cut it away from the herd, turn it from safe ground, drive it toward hollow mats or slick basins. Their calls are thin whistles by day and yelping pulses at night. They are hated by herders and followed by scavengers.

The aerial hunters rule the empty height. Broad-winged birds and perhaps things not quite birds circle for hours without flapping, using the warm breaths rising from stone bowls and sunlit flats. Some take insects by the thousands, opening their mouths as they skim the swarms. Some drop on frogs and lizards at water’s edge. The largest strike young grazers, sick adults, and careless people. Their nests are not in trees but on exposed rock knobs, cliff lips, abandoned shelter roofs, or towers of hardened plant-fiber stolen from insects.

Scavengers are constant and unsentimental. Bare-headed birds with soft feet. Beetles with jaws like black shears. Bone-cleaning ants. Fox-sized night things that laugh and dig. Pale worms in carcasses. Fungi that arrive as powder and bloom in the damp underside of ribs. Death here does not remain an object for long. It becomes a place of assembly, then a stain, then a greener patch.

The people are fewer than all of these, and they know it.

They are small in the landscape and have arranged their lives around that fact. They do not imagine themselves masters of the plateau. No one who has watched a wall disappear under roots in three seasons, or a path seal itself after one wet month, can believe too strongly in ownership.

They are probably compact, tough-footed people, with strong calves, narrow hips, and the habitual forward lean of those who walk against wind. Their skin is weathered by clean hard sun and constant reflected light from pale stone and wet plant. Their eyes crease early. Their hands are scarred by fibers, thorns, insect bites, and stone work. Children learn very young where to place a foot and where never to place one. They are carried at first in slings high against the body, away from grasping mats and biting ground-life. Later they are taught to test the surface with a pole before trusting it.

They live on knobs, ribs, and edges: wherever stone still refuses the plants for a little while. Their shelters crouch. Nothing about them invites the wind. Roofs are lashed down, walls are low, entrances narrow. A village from far away looks like a scatter of dark rocks until smoke rises or someone moves.

They do not clear land unless they must. Clearing is labor, and the plants consider it temporary. Instead they scrape paths repeatedly, harvest in rotation, burn small patches at the correct dryness, and let grazers open places for them. They know which mats are edible after rain and poisonous before it. They know which bladders hold sweet water and which hold bitterness that twists the gut. They know the basins where frog-things wake, the ledges where root curtains can be combed for moisture, the thickets that should not be cut because they mark old springs.

Their tools are intimate with the plateau. Digging sticks polished by years of use. Broad bone scrapers for lifting plant-sheets. Nets woven from cured vine-fiber, weighted with pebbles and bone. Rain cloaks made from stitched skins or flattened waterproof leaves. Containers of gourd, bladder, shell, and resin-sealed stem. Stone knives often hafted short, because long blades are a liability in close work near the ground. Needles of bone, and rare metal ones kept wrapped and named.

They eat what the plateau allows: seeds shaken from dry pods; tubers pried from beneath mat edges; sour translucent plant-bladders after storms; insects roasted, pounded, fermented, or eaten alive in season; frogs during the brief calling time; eggs from ground nests; meat from hunted grazers; marrow; blood; bitter herbs; stored cakes of seedmeal and dried insect paste. They may keep small tamed animals—perhaps not true livestock, but semi-claimed companions that return to salt, shelter, and hands that pull parasites from their hides. Herding on this plateau would never be absolute. Animals go where water speaks.

Their settlements move without seeming nomadic in a simple sense. Some shelters are seasonal. Some are ancestral but occupied only when nearby basins fill. Some are left deliberately to be taken by growth, because a swallowed house can mark a promise: this place was used, will be used, but must rest. They build as if in conversation with decay.

Their language, if heard by an outsider, would likely be full of words for surfaces.

Not just wet and dry, but wet-on-top, wet-under, dry-crusted, hollow-dry, safe-soft, swelling, sealing, newly torn, root-held, insect-loosened, grazer-opened, flood-slick. They would have many names for wind according to height: the ankle wind that carries spores, the face wind that dries the eyes, the bird wind that means storms are forming beyond sight. They would not ask simply where water is, but when it is.

Their stories probably do not begin with mountains. Mountains are too young and dramatic a shape for them. Their oldest tales begin with things lying down.

The first stone lay down and became the plateau.  
The first tree lay down and became the mat.  
The first animal lay down and became food.  
The first person learned to sleep lightly.

They may regard height as dangerous pride. Things that stand too tall are struck by lightning, seen by hunters, dried by wind, broken by weather. Wisdom is in spreading, bending, returning, repairing. A good house crouches. A good path follows. A good hunter waits lower than his prey’s gaze. A good elder knows when not to cross an open place at noon.

They fear certain basins, not because monsters visibly dwell there, but because the living floor behaves differently. A ringing mat may announce a footstep to insects, predators, or something older. Noon ridges may vent heat, scent, or invisible vapors that confuse animals. Hexagonal pavements that shine after rain may be places where the plant-skin is thin and the stone beneath remembers lightning. Their taboos would sound like superstition until one ignored them.

Children are probably taught through songs whose rhythms match walking poles striking ground. The songs name routes between basins, safe ledges, old cairns, dangerous mats, springs that taste of copper, thickets where large birds nest. Memory matters because markers erode, paths close, and the same landscape changes its permissions with every storm.

The dead may not be buried deeply; there is little soil, and the plants are eager. Perhaps bodies are laid on prepared mats with stones around them, given back openly while relatives keep watch until the first green hairs touch the skin. Perhaps bones are retrieved later, cleaned by insects, painted with mineral red and stored in low houses. Perhaps some dead are placed in root curtains, where fog, plants, and beetles take them slowly. To be kept from the living ground may be considered a kind of exile.

The people’s religion, if it can be separated from practical knowledge, belongs to moisture and return. Rain is not merely blessing; it is command, danger, birth, hunger, noise. Thunder opens flowers. Flood wakes eggs. Dampness erases tracks and also saves lives. Their holy moments are brief: the first condensation on a dry morning, the first call from a basin, the first swelling of a plant thought dead, the silence just after hard rain when every surface shines and every creature is deciding what to do next.

But the most characteristic inhabitants may be neither the people nor the great herds. They are the boundary creatures—the ones that make use of the plateau’s rule that nothing remains separate.

There are lizards with skin patterned like cracked lichens, sleeping half-buried in plant felt. There are little mammals or mammal-like things that tunnel not in soil but between layers of root and mat, harvesting seeds and beetle grubs. There are birds that nest inside abandoned insect towers. There are mites that live only in the hair-rims of condensation leaves. There are fungi that fruit from old hoofprints. There are plants whose seeds require passage through the gut of a particular grazer, then the scraping of a beetle, then one night of standing water. There are predators that follow flowering storms because they know prey will follow. There are flowers that mimic the smell of fresh dung, carrion, frog skin, wet stone, female insects, lightning-split stems.

Everything here is entangled, but not lush in the easy way of a jungle. It is abundant under constraint. Life does not tower. It persists laterally. It waits in sealed chambers, in dry curls, in eggs like dust, in roots like threads, in memory paths between basins. Then water passes over the land and the entire plateau answers as if a nerve has been touched.

So the beings that live there are low, quick, tough, musical, opportunistic, and hard to separate from the surface beneath them.

They are mat and mandible, hoof and bladder, wing and thorn, crouched house and closing path.

They live in a world where the ground is alive enough to heal, hungry enough to reclaim, and old enough that every creature has learned humility from it.
