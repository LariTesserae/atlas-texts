What lives there is not quite a people, not quite an absence of people.

The city is inhabited by keepers of an interval.

They are human enough at first imagining: small, highland-built, brown-skinned or ash-pale according to house and lineage, with compact bodies made for stairs, cold air, and patient labor. Their hands are broad, dry at the knuckles, darkened by stone dust, lime, soot, plant dyes, and the tannins of wet wood. They wear layered garments of felted wool, bark cloth, beaten fiber, hide, and woven reed, fastened with bone toggles, antler pins, copper where a family is wealthy or old enough to possess it. Their hair is bound close to the skull or wrapped in cloth against the moisture. Their sandals are thick-soled, repaired many times, always placed neatly at thresholds.

But they are difficult to encounter as bodies.

You find them by their interruptions: the space left between a stool and a loom, the handprint in damp clay, the dip in a sleeping mat, the knife set down beside a half-cut root. A cup waits where a mouth might return. A shawl hangs from a peg with one sleeve turned inside out. A child’s carved animal lies under a bench, polished by fingers that may have last touched it yesterday or five generations ago.

They live slowly enough that stillness is their native motion.

A woman may stand at the mouth of a cistern court, one hand resting on a stone jar, her eyes lowered as if counting the drops forming beneath a gutter. If watched directly, she seems carved from damp wood and bone. Only after a long while does one notice that her breath has changed the air before her lips, that one finger has tightened against the jar rim, that her gaze has moved from the channel to the pool below. She is not frozen. She is spending motion carefully.

A potter sits with a damp bowl beneath both hands. He has been drawing its wall upward for a very long time. The clay does not dry. His thumbs have left shallow crescents in it. Perhaps each day he raises it by the width of a fingernail. Perhaps each year. The bowl, when finished, will be carried to a household that has been waiting without impatience.

Children exist there, but childhood does not rush through them. They sit in doorways with knees drawn up, watching lizards on the steps, learning the first law of the city before they learn speech: do not take more change than the day can carry. Their games are games of arrangement, balance, and exact return. Pebbles placed in rings. Moss touched but not torn. A feather dropped into a basin and retrieved before it sinks. They laugh, but softly, and their laughter does not echo far.

The old are nearly indistinguishable from shrines.

They sit wrapped in pale blankets near hearths, in alcoves, under the lean shelter of roofed terraces. Their faces are creased like dry ravines, their eyes black and wet. They remember weather from before the holding, or claim to. They remember rivers with moods, snow that either deepened or vanished, animals that fled when startled, fires that consumed. The young listen politely, as one listens to myth. To them, the world has always been sufficient and restrained.

No one in the city appears hungry in the ordinary way. Hunger exists, but it has edges. It comes when it is time to eat and withdraws when enough has been taken. The meal in the courtyard is not abandoned; it is paused between need and satisfaction. Bread remains bread. Greens remain bitter. Stew remains pale and unspoiled. A hand will eventually lift the ladle. Someone will chew, swallow, set the bowl down, and the meal will have diminished by exactly as much as the city permits.

Their speech is sparse because speech is also an alteration.

When they speak, they use few words, often naming quantities, states, and relations rather than desires.

“Full enough.”

“Green to the mark.”

“Stone newly broken.”

“Three drops owed.”

“Hoof crossed here.”

“Do not hurry the smoke.”

They have songs, but most are hummed with closed mouths. They have stories, but these are recited in fragments over years, each telling advancing a little farther along a fixed path. They do not argue loudly. Their disputes are matters of excess: a vine that has crossed a line, a household that drew too much from a cistern, a bridge repaired too heavily, a shrine neglected long enough for lichen to touch a carved handprint. Justice is restoration, not punishment. The offender returns the thing to balance, or gives labor until balance is restored.

Their rulers, if rulers remain, are not kings.

The highest office belongs to those who read levels.

They are water-measurers, moss-cutters, channel-clearers, smoke-watchers, animal-spacing wardens, keepers of the pool. They carry staffs marked with shallow notches. They wear cords tied in patterns at the wrist and throat. Their work is to notice the almost-imperceptible: a cistern one breath too high, a fern leaning beyond its accustomed shadow, a roof beam swelling unevenly, a family speaking too often of leaving.

They do not command the city. They negotiate with its rule.

At the center, beside the black pool, they kneel on the cold polished stone and lower bone rods into the water. The rods always come up wet to the same mark. They know this will happen. They measure anyway. The act is not for discovering change. It is for reaffirming that change has not taken more than its share.

The pool is the oldest inhabitant.

Not a god exactly. Not a spirit with a face. It does not desire offerings in the way hungry powers desire them. It does not bless or punish with drama. It holds.

Everything in the city is related to it as veins are related to a heart that never beats. Channels descend toward it, though none feed it. Fog nets, spouts, drains, cistern lips, household basins, shrine cups, dew stones, roof moss: all belong to one quiet circulation. The pool receives without rising and gives without lowering. Its depth is black because it contains not darkness, but withheld consequence.

The people may have made an agreement with it once. Or their ancestors may have discovered the agreement already present in the mountain and built themselves into it.

The agreement is simple: life may continue if it consents not to become too much.

So the inhabitants are artisans of sufficiency.

They weave cloth that does not fray beyond repair. They build walls that crack but do not collapse. They train fruit trees to bear enough small tart fruit for the households assigned to them, never branches heavy enough to break. They keep goats, deer, ground birds, and burrowing animals at the edge of domestication, not owning them so much as sharing routes with them. They know which roofs the finches may nest on, which storage alcoves belong now to feathered things, which planted terraces must be left to the pale-muzzled deer.

Animals live there as citizens of a quieter law.

The deer graze without stripping stems. The lizards take warmth but do not scatter. The moths cling like bits of moonlit cloth. The marmot-creatures sit upright at burrow mouths, neither tame nor afraid, their whiskers holding the exact shape of alertness. Foxes pass through alleys with their paws set carefully between moss patches. Cliff swifts hang beneath eaves and bridges, wings folded, blue-black eyes open, as if waiting for a wind that has not yet been released.

Predation exists, but politely. A fox may take a bird. A hawk may stoop. A snake may swallow an egg. But such events are rare, contained, almost ceremonial. Blood does not call more blood. Panic does not spread. The taken creature vanishes into the balance, and the city closes silently around the absence.

Plants, too, live by boundary.

Moss knows the edges of its appointed stones. Ferns fill cracks but do not split the stair. Vines descend in graceful lengths and stop before smothering a lintel. Fruit trees twist against sun-warmed walls, old and young at once, bearing leaves with the gloss of perpetual after-rain. The gardens are not cultivated into abundance. They are persuaded into continuity.

The inhabitants understand this better than outsiders do. They do not pray for plenty. Plenty would be dangerous. Plenty would rot, burst, crowd, flood, attract noise, require conquest. They pray, if prayer is the word, for exactness.

A full jar, but not spilling.  
A strong child, but not restless.  
A bridge that holds, but does not tempt traffic.  
A harvest that feeds, but does not invite strangers.  
A memory that remains, but does not become longing.

There are absences among them.

Some houses are occupied only by arrangements. Some families may have stepped too far into stillness and become nearly indistinguishable from their possessions. A sleeping figure under blankets may be a person, a mound of folded cloth, or a person who has taken forty years to sleep through one night. In the shrines cut into cliff faces, the bone beads and feathers are joined by small human tokens: teeth, hair cords, baby sandals, finger bones polished smooth, names marked as circles and lines.

Death in the city is not decay. It is incorporation.

The dead are not buried deep. They are set into walls, cairns, benches, threshold stones, garden borders. Their ashes temper plaster. Their bones hang in shrine niches. Their tools pass to hands that may not lift them for a century. An ancestor is not gone; an ancestor becomes another fixed point by which change may be measured.

And yet the people are not miserable.

They know affection, though it is quiet. A mother warms her hands around a child’s cold ears. Two old friends sit beneath a roof while the same thread of smoke hangs above them. Lovers meet on bridges and lean close enough that their breath mingles visibly in the air between their mouths, lingering like a shared thought. Families mark births with beads added to cords. They mark unions by joining water from two household basins and pouring it, drop by drop, into the central channels. They mark grief by leaving a cup unfilled.

Their joy is not exuberance. It is recognition.

The right sound of a ladle against clay.  
The first bead of condensation on a new fog net.  
A repaired stair lying flush with the old stone.  
A blue finch returning to the same lintel.  
A child placing a pebble exactly where an elder once placed one.

What lives there, then, is a civilization reduced not downward but inward. It has not failed to become grand; it has refused grandeur. It has taken all the ambitions of cities—water, storage, procession, craft, memory, law, worship—and narrowed them to the scale at which they can endure forever without spilling over.

There may be another inhabitant as well: the mountain’s own first thought, still thinking.

The city’s terraces follow fracture lines too faithfully for mere convenience. Its plazas occupy joints in the stone like deliberate pauses in a sentence. Its bridges do not conquer ravines; they complete them. Its houses leave gaps not because land is scarce, but because emptiness must be housed too. Perhaps the mountain, young and sharp and not yet eroded into gentleness, dreamed of remaining exactly itself. Perhaps the people arrived and, instead of cutting a kingdom from it, learned to participate in that dream.

In that case, the people are not trapped.

They are the mountain’s way of keeping still without being dead.

They are the hands by which basalt remembers its edges.  
They are the breath by which mist stays lifted.  
They are the appetite by which gardens remain green but uneaten.  
They are the footsteps that prove a stair exists without wearing it away.

An outsider passing through might think: Where are they?

But the city would answer, if it answered at all:

Here.  
At the rim of the cup.  
At the lip of the spout.  
In the held smoke.  
Behind the curtain that moved once and has not yet finished moving.  
In the meal before the first spoonful and after the last hunger.  
In the black pool whose level does not change.  
In every life permitted to continue because it has agreed not to become history.
