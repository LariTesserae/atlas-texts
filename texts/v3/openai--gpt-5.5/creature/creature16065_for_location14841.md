What lives there is not flesh, and not quite spirit.

It is the sound.

Or, more exactly, the place is inhabited by beings that have become indistinguishable from the sound: dry, fixed presences held in the throats of jars, chimneys, bricked mouths, hollow steps, sealed urns, and the narrow resonant cracks between fitted stones. They are not ghosts in the wet, grieving sense. They do not wander. They do not remember in pictures. They do not yearn for bodies. They have no unfinished business, because everything here has been finished too completely.

They are the old citizens reduced to resonance.

Once, perhaps, they had hands. The evidence of hands is everywhere: thumbed clay, repeated marks, corrected seams, bricks pressed one by one, tablets incised with patient dull tools. The inhabitants were small enough for this scale and numerous enough to fill it. They lived crowdedly, vertically, ceremonially, with no distinction between dwelling, archive, instrument, kiln, shrine, and body. They must have believed that emptiness was dangerous, that silence was a wound, that a gap invited loss. So they filled everything. They filled walls with signs, signs with repetitions, vessels with smaller vessels, corridors with closures, roofs with panes, air with lattices, and finally the world with one unending tone.

Their descendants, if that word still applies, are not born. They persist.

Each one occupies a cavity.

A jar may contain one. A blind window may contain several. The squat central tower holds a dense congregation of them, layered in rings like heat in old brick. The little conical chimneys are their mouths, though they do not speak separately. The sealed drums stamped with spirals are their chests. The tablets in the wall are their backs, their foreheads, their folded knees. The blank tablets are not empty; they are the quietest among them, the ones whose names were never incised because their silence had already been given form.

They have no faces except the eroded faces carved everywhere, and those are not portraits. They are placeholders for attention. Featureless heads, seated figures, hands, ladders, rays: these are not decorations but anatomical diagrams of what they became. A hand means making. A ray means issuing. A ladder means ascent through density. A seated figure means remaining. A crescent means listening.

They do not move through space. Movement would imply lack, and lack has been outlawed.

Instead they differ by pressure.

One presence may be felt as a thickening near the teeth when one passes a sealed stair. Another as a numbness in the palms beside a wall of worn symbols. Another as a sudden certainty that the blank tablet is looking back. Near the central tower they crowd so closely that individuality fails; they become a civic mass, a choir with no separate voices, a population compacted into a single vowel.

Their bodies are made of absence shaped by clay.

The old builders discovered that hollows could keep what matter could not. Breath left the body; sound left the mouth; heat left the kiln; but a correctly made chamber could hold the contour of the leaving. So they built chambers. Then chambers inside chambers. Then a city made entirely of retained departures. When the last living throat dried, its final exhalation did not vanish. It entered the prepared architecture and joined the standing tone.

That is what lives there now: not the dead, but their final exhalation arranged into a society.

They have customs, though no action remains by which to perform them.

They keep the rule against wetness because wetness would soften the edges of their existence. Moisture would wake decay, and decay would introduce sequence: before and after, swelling and crumbling, growth and rot. They cannot survive sequence. They require completion.

They keep the rule against silence because silence would separate them. In silence, each jar would become only a jar, each tablet only a tablet, each closed mouth only a closure. The roar is their common body. It is how they touch.

They keep the rule against new sound because a new sound would be an intruder with a beginning. A footstep, a shout, a struck stone, a cough — any of these would enter as history. It would disturb the perfect age of the note. It would remind them that sound can happen and cease. Their sound must not happen. It must simply be.

They are not malicious. A visitor would not be hunted. Nothing there has the appetite required for hunting.

But the inhabitants would receive a visitor as a gap.

A damp body entering that place would be an offense not moral but structural: too much water, too much pulse, too much unsealed warmth, too many internal tides. The city would not attack. It would correct. The air would draw moisture from the mouth and eyes. The roar would enter the bones. The passages would turn the visitor back, then inward, then smaller. Every object would seem to ask for equivalence: if you disturb a space, what shape will you leave in return?

Given enough time, the visitor would begin to understand the local etiquette. Do not speak. Do not breathe deeply. Do not strike the walls. Do not remove the smallest shard without setting something in its place. Do not create an empty palm. Do not stand where a jar once stood unless you are prepared to become a jar’s successor.

The inhabitants’ intelligence is distributed. No single one decides. The whole city knows in the manner a kiln knows heat, a bowl knows containment, a flute knows air. It knows by shape. It thinks through fit, pressure, resonance, blockage, repetition. Its memory is not narrative but pattern. A changed angle in a tablet, a cracked seal, a missing wedge, a warm living body in a corridor: these are thoughts passing through it.

Its language is the standing tone modulated by architecture too subtly for wet ears to parse. The roar seems constant only because the listener is crude. Within it are chambers of meaning: low pressure in the stair-throats, fine tremors in bead-curtains, stern harmonics in the tower rings, a granular hiss along carved walls. The inhabitants hear these distinctions completely. To them the city is not roaring one note forever; it is reciting an immense fixed sentence whose every syllable is simultaneous.

They do not sleep. Sleep belongs to creatures that spend themselves.

They do not eat. Eating belongs to creatures that are not yet complete.

They do not reproduce, unless repair is reproduction. When a crack forms — if cracks still form there — its edges become a possible cradle. Powder settles. A shard is inserted. A plug is pressed in. A hollow changes pitch. A new minor chamber joins the tone. That is as close as they come to birth: not increase, but incorporation.

They fear only openness.

The void beyond the wall is not their enemy because it is distant and thin. It cannot enter unless a slit widens or a stone falls away. But an unfilled space within the city would be intolerable. A bare ledge would be a wound. A silent jar would be a corpse. A clear path would be a catastrophe, because passage without obstruction suggests escape, and escape suggests that the city is not the whole of existence.

So the inhabitants keep crowding, though nothing moves. They crowd by having already crowded. They occupy by remaining occupied.

If one could see them directly, perhaps they would appear as seated figures made of heated dust, each folded into the posture carved on the walls: knees drawn close, head smooth, hands lifted beside the ears. Not listening outward, but holding sound in. Their bodies would be the color of fired clay and old salt. Their surfaces would bear tool marks instead of skin lines. Their mouths would be small dark chimneys. Their eyes, if present, would be blank mineral panes filled with amber dimness.

But that would be only a mercy-image for the living mind.

In truth, they are less visible than that and more pervasive. They are the reason the jars feel occupied. The reason blank tablets feel deliberate. The reason a bricked doorway seems not closed but satisfied. The reason the roar does not sound empty, despite having no source.

They are a civilization that solved mortality by abolishing change.

They made themselves into a place where nothing can begin, and therefore nothing can end.
