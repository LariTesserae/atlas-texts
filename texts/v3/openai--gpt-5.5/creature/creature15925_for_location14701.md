What lives there is not an animal.

Nothing there has a face to turn toward you, no pupil to widen in the pulse-light, no jaw, no flank, no blood darkening in the cold. The drowned highland has refused that kind of life. Anything that once tried to swim with will rather than current is gone, sifted out of the place as if by a law older than hunger.

But it is not empty.

It is inhabited by rooted listeners.

They live in cracks, basins, roof seams, cairns, drowned paths, and the sockets of dead trees. They are plants, mostly, though some are nearer to lichen, fungus, mineral film, or patient chemical habit than to any dry-world plant. They do not grow toward the sun. They grow toward pressure, toward dissolved metal, toward slow-moving cold, toward the faint tremor of a surge miles away.

Their bodies are made for being pressed.

Some are black-green mats that seal themselves flat against stone when the current strengthens, then lift in thousands of tiny mouths when richer water returns. Some are ribboned and muscular, long as banners, anchored by root-beards that tighten like fists in rock fissures. Some have translucent leaves, horn-thin and veined with silver, which fold shut before pressure storms and open afterward with the gravity of hands unfolding from prayer.

There are pale cup-plants under ledges, always facing sideways or downward, never upward. They drink from currents the way flowers once drank light. Their rims tremble when mineral weather passes, and their interiors glow faintly blue for a few moments afterward, as if remembering lightning.

There are red-black mosses that cannot bear exposed flow. They live only on undersides: beneath slabs, under fallen roof stones, inside the shadow of old terrace walls. They make velvet colonies in places no direct current can reach. Over centuries they thicken into cushions that preserve whatever they cover. A carved mark beneath them may remain sharper than the stone around it. A woven basket may survive in their dark keeping, fibers swollen but unbroken.

There are silver veils, among the most delicate things in the highland. They hang from overhangs and dead branches in curtains finer than hair. They comb the water. Each strand gathers minerals too small to see. In calm months the veils lengthen until they join ledge to ledge, tree to wall, cairn to pole. In harsh passes they are torn away in one white streaming moment, and their fragments drift for days through ravines like shed skin.

There are the old pines, though they are no longer trees in the living sense. Their wood has become black and hard, almost metallic. Yet each trunk supports a community so slow and interdependent that the dead forest has become a reef. Root-filaments descend from branch scars. Weed-gardens hang where needles once grew. Pale lichens bloom in rings around knots. The drowned pines do not live, but life uses their shape as memory. They are vertical ruins, and the inhabitants of the highland climb them without moving quickly enough to be seen.

Some of the growth migrates.

Not by walking. Not by drifting as creatures drift. A colony dies at its trailing edge and thickens at its leading one. Over decades it crosses a wall. Over centuries it travels from a spring vent to a hut roof, from a hut roof to a carved pole, from a carved pole to the floor of a ravine. If you returned after a human lifetime, you might think the green stain had merely spread. If you returned after ten, you would understand it had gone somewhere.

Other plants build land.

They net ravines with roots and ribbons. They catch silt, bark, pollen, powdered stone, old ash. Slowly a suspended field forms between cliffs, bowed downward by its own weight, shaking when the current passes through it. In time pale mosses colonize the caught sediment. Then cup-plants. Then stiff kelp-shrubs. Eventually a hanging garden spans the gap where once there was only black water. It may last a thousand years. It may fail in one pressure season and descend in a roaring cloud, feeding the basin below.

The closest thing to a forest animal is the current itself, though it is not alive.

The rooted things behave as if it were. They lean into its moods. They brace before its violence. They open to its mineral offerings. Their colonies are arranged not by shade and sun, but by flow and shelter. A pass has its own vegetation: low, dark, tough, cropped by ice crystals and kept close to stone. A hollow has another: pale, loose, ceremonial, growing from jars, basins, hut floors, the mouths of sealed rooms. A terrace wall has another: layered mats, silt-catchers, mineral skins, patient root systems worrying stone apart.

There are blooms.

For most of the year, reproduction is almost invisible. Spores sleep in silt. Seeds lodge in cracks. Fine dust gathers in the hair of the silver veils. Then some alignment of pressure, mineral, and distant cold arrives, and the water fills with green-gold flecks.

During a bloom, the drowned highland seems suddenly crowded. Every ruined doorway wears a halo. Every bell-frame gathers a glowing rim. The black branches of the pine dead glitter as though frost had learned to drift. The flecks do not swim; they submit. Yet the currents carry them with enough pattern that the bloom appears purposeful, like a migration without bodies. Some settle. Some dissolve. Some pass into other basins and become a future forest on a wall no eye has seen.

There are also inhabitants that are almost stone.

Water-lichens plate the boulders in blue-black crusts. They grow where currents polish hardest, thriving where softer things are stripped away. They leave rings showing old water pressures, old chemical seasons, old surges from the high basins. Break one open and its interior is pale green, wet, and faintly granular, like crushed glass mixed with leaf.

In the fluted ravines live mineral fungi, if fungi is the right word. They form knobs and shelves along grooves where sound gathers. Their bodies are dense and chalky outside, fibrous within. They feed on the meeting of stone and water, on trace metals, on gases rising through fractures. When pressure pulses pass, they release threads so fine they are visible only as a softening of the dark.

The bells host their own small nations.

Slate bells, hollowed logs, stone shells: all are furred with growth specialized for vibration. Around the lips of the bells are tiny gray fronds that open when sound passes through them. They seem to feed on disturbance itself, or on the fresh water pulled across their surfaces when a bell speaks. The louder the pass, the thicker these colonies grow. Some bells have been muted by them entirely, their voices swallowed into moss. Others are kept clean along one vibrating edge, as if the plant and the bell have reached an agreement.

The carved poles are inhabited differently.

Around many of them grows a pale, tight vegetation found nowhere else in such rings. It follows the notches and spirals, filling cuts with white film, tracing hand-shapes with tiny folded leaves. The plants seem to prefer worked wood and old intention. Perhaps the tools that shaped the poles changed the fibers. Perhaps hands left salts. Perhaps the markings alter the current at a scale too fine to see. Whatever the reason, the poles remain legible because the life around them keeps reading them.

The ruins are full of such readers.

In low doorways, dark moss grows in thresholds but not beyond them. In collapsed storage cells, pale weeds root among old baskets as if the woven pattern still instructs them. Clay lamps hold small colonies of cup-leaves, each lamp a cold flowerpot for a flame that will never return. Rope bridges, hanging useless and heavy, trail long gardens from each fiber. The old houses are not occupied, but they are inhabited. The difference matters less here than it would above.

The strangest known living thing grows in the basin of the high ridge house.

It is almost white.

Not the white of bone, because there is no bone here. Not the white of snow, because nothing falls. It is the white of something grown in darkness without becoming blind.

Its leaves are small and folded around a bending stem. They do not stream like ribbons or flatten like mats. They hold themselves with a fragile deliberateness, each leaf clasped until the pressure pulse comes. Then the plant opens toward the scratched wall.

It does not open toward light.

It opens toward marks.

Rows, spirals, counts, warnings, harvests, deaths — whatever the scratches once meant, the plant responds to them. During distant surges its translucent roots brighten inside the carved stone basin. The silt around it stays cleared, though no visible current enters. Other plants do not invade the house. Even the red-black moss stops at the threshold stones.

Perhaps the basin plant feeds on mineral traces released by the old carving hand. Perhaps the wall’s scratches shape tiny eddies. Perhaps the stone remembers pressure, and the plant has learned to read that memory. In the drowned highland, these are not separate explanations.

It is not intelligent in the animal way. It does not think. It does not watch.

But it attends.

That may be the deepest form of life there: attention without eyes.

The highland is full of attention. Root-beards attend to pull. Cup-plants attend to pressure. Moss attends to shade. Silver veils attend to trace metals. Lichens attend to stone. Dead pines attend by holding still. Bells attend by sounding. Ruins attend by remaining. Even the old paths, softened under silt, seem to guide the currents that cross them.

There are also things that may not be alive, but behave too consequentially to be called mere absence.

The vertical bands of absolute dark are among them.

No plant colonizes them. No silt clouds them. They pass slowly through ravines and groves over years, touching surfaces without current. Where they cross carvings, the marks sharpen. Where they cross vegetation, the plants become glassy and colorless, still shaped like life but emptied of its green patience. The old inhabitants feared or honored these bands enough to mark them with crossed poles and pale stones.

The living things do not flee them. They cannot. But colonies near the old markers grow in defensive forms: tight rings, low crusts, roots withdrawn deep into cracks. Around dark bands the ecology becomes wary, if a landscape can be wary.

So what lives in the drowned highland?

A cold vegetal civilization without citizens.

A country of listeners, anchorers, combers, sealers, splitters, bloomers, and slow migrants.

Life there has no mouths, but it feeds. It has no feet, but it crosses distances. It has no eyes, but it knows when the pressure changes. It has no voices, but it makes the bells speak. It has no memory as animals know memory, yet it preserves carvings, paths, huts, poles, cairns, and the shapes of trees long after the makers and birds are gone.

It lives by yielding and holding at once.

It lets the flood pass through it. It grips stone. It accepts darkness. It grows slowly enough to seem like weather, and weather there is nearly a body. The drowned highland is inhabited not by creatures moving through a place, but by the place learning, over ages, how to remain alive under water.
