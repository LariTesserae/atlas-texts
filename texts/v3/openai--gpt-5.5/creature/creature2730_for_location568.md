What lives there is not one creature, but an arrangement.

At the center of it are people: a small, close-numbered population with the habits of masons, animal-keepers, water-priests, scavengers, and cooks all mixed into one way of being. They are not pale in the manner of things that never see light; the shafts give them enough sun to mark them faintly, unevenly, on cheekbones, forearms, the backs of necks. Their skin tends to be dusted with lime, smoke, clay, flour, dung powder, crushed herbs. Their hair is often bound up in cloth or netting because loose hair catches soot, insects, and condensation. Many wear sandals with quiet soles, or go barefoot where the brick is known and warm.

They are a listening people.

Their ears are better trained than their eyes. A child learns early to tell the difference between a clean drip and a dirty one, between a goat worrying its tether and a goat about to break it, between birds alarmed by a cat and birds alarmed by bad air. They know the warning creak of a damp brick, the hollow cough of a pipe that has swallowed too much silt, the small granular hiss of grain being raided. They speak close, not because they are secretive, but because the place already speaks so much. A conversation across a room would have to fight water, wings, tools, hooves, and plaster. So their talk is intimate by necessity: words given into the side of a face, into a cupped hand, under the shelter of a hanging mat.

They are shaped by maintenance. Nobody belongs to only one trade. The same woman who trims lamp wicks may know how to geld a pig, splice a reed rope, reset a sweating tile, and judge by smell whether a storage jar has gone sour. The old men and old women are not retired; they become keepers of sequences. Which shutter to open first in the hot months. Which channel must be scraped with bone rather than copper. Which herb bed takes bird droppings and which one will burn from it. Which cracks are sacred because useful things live inside them.

Their religion, if called religion, is mostly obedience to consequences. Water is not holy in an abstract way. It is holy because spilling it wrongly may mean fever, hunger, mold, or fighting. Spiders are protected over grain because the granary remembers every year the moths won. Lizards are left their egg-holes. Bats are counted after dusk. A dead cat is mourned practically and deeply; not as a pet alone, but as a broken treaty with the rats and winged thieves.

Their bodies carry the architecture in them. Many have one shoulder slightly higher from bearing jars along the same routes. Their palms are tough at the base of the fingers from rope and brick. Their knees know how to fold under low arches without thought. Their feet can find the dry ridges between channels in the dark. They move with small economies, turning sideways before a narrowing, lifting a hanging cage without looking, stepping over a sleeping animal, ducking beneath a line of wet cloth. A stranger would bruise themselves raw in an hour. A child of the place can run laughing through it with a bowl of water and not spill a mouthful.

They eat carefully, variously, and without romance. Grain softened into mash. Bitter greens. Cress clipped from troughs. Pale fish from the storage pools when the keepers allow it. Eggs from quail and finches. Goat milk, sometimes sour, sometimes made thick. Pig meat on rare occasions, its slaughter a noisy civic event. Mushrooms from the encouraged damp. Herbs so strong they can make a little food seem larger than it is. Insects too, though not always admitted to outsiders: roasted crickets, larvae rinsed and fried, the nutty beetles found in certain bins. Nothing that eats is entirely separate from what is eaten.

They have children everywhere. Thin, quick, solemn when carrying water and wild when not. Children are the settlement’s messengers because they fit the passages best and because they learn the map before they learn sums. They climb the safer shafts to adjust plates of tin and shell. They collect condensation pots. They shoo quail, tease goats, steal dried figs, fall asleep in reed baskets, and are told stories about the first builders, who are imagined as giants not because they were large, but because they made things before the world became so crowded with use.

Their elders sit by vents and become almost architectural. They know the warm walls by temperament. They can predict rain by aches, by insect emergence, by the souring of a particular corner, by how the north flames lean. They twist rope from reed fiber, hair, and salvaged threads, and while they work they keep account: births, leaks, debts, promises, forbidden hatchings, old repairs due to fail again. They are libraries with cracked voices.

There are families, but they are not private in the way surface houses might understand. Privacy is made of curtains, timing, courtesy, and selective deafness. A room belongs to a lineage until a leak changes the arrangement. A sleeping niche may be ceded to a nursing goat for three nights if the animal is valuable. Marriage is less a joining of households than a negotiation between water rights, animal shares, work obligations, and sleeping warmth. Love exists, certainly, but it learns to express itself through repaired sandals, saved fruit, a dry blanket hung in the right place, a cup of first-caught rain pressed silently into another hand.

They are not ignorant of the world above. They trade with it, fear it, depend on it, resent it. The sun-struck city above is a source of heat, refuse, metal scraps, seeds, news, disease, taxes, cast-off cloth, broken pottery, and occasionally lost people. Some residents go up regularly through legitimate doors or half-remembered service ways. They return squinting, sunburned, carrying sacks, gossip, and a faint contempt for open spaces. To them the surface is wasteful. Rain runs away there. Walls stand naked and unused. People throw out bones with marrow still in them.

But the underground folk are not merely survivors beneath a city. They are cultivators of a built ecology, and much of what lives there lives because they permit, encourage, or tolerate it.

The birds are nearly citizens. Finches in cages mark air quality and mood; their sudden silence can empty a room faster than a shouted alarm. Swallows are allowed the upper brackets and repay the privilege in insects. Quail are stupid, beloved, edible, and constantly underfoot. Children name them despite being told not to. There are rules about which birds may be eaten, which eggs may be taken, which nests must be left because their placement predicts the damp.

The cats, or catlike animals, are less citizens than minor nobility. Long-backed, narrow-faced, soot-footed, they travel where humans cannot: roof beams, shelf edges, the backs of cages, the shadow between rafters and plaster. They accept food but do not owe gratitude. Some wear bead collars to show they belong to a room or family. Others belong only to the hunt. Their eyes hold lamplight like wet copper. They sleep in warm vents, vanish before scoldings, and appear with perfect timing when grain is being measured. To kill one deliberately would be more than cruelty; it would be sabotage.

The goats are patient, argumentative engines. Their hooves are wrapped. Their horns are blunted or capped. They know every person likely to feed them and every latch badly set. Their milk is wealth. Their dung is gathered. Their breath thickens the lower rooms. They are cursed daily and blessed at births.

The pigs are quieter than expected, narrow-bodied and clever, raised in pens where scraps become meat and heat. The long-eared rodents are kept in stacked hutches, breeding fast, warm to the touch, with trembling noses and soft bones. They feed the people, the cats when one is unlucky, and occasionally the stories children tell each other about things escaping into the old drains and growing large.

The insects are the true multitude. Some are enemies, some livestock, some weather signs, some cleaners, some unavoidable facts. Roaches pale as old wax in the drain bricks. Crickets conducting the night. Moths like scraps of ash. Beetles with polished backs. Water-skimmers that appear after rain as if poured in with it. The people do not think of “vermin” as a single category. Every small life has an appetite, and every appetite has a possible use or danger.

The plants live like treasured invalids and stubborn saints. Silver herbs, bitter cresses, trained vines, water-storing bulbs, dwarfed figs with twisted roots. They are pinched, pruned, rationed, sung to by some and insulted by others. A green leaf is never just decoration. It is medicine, scent, bait, flavor, omen, or memory of the surface. Doorway sprigs announce cleanliness. Thorn bundles announce occupation by something with teeth, eggs, or rights.

There are also molds and fungi, some scraped away with disgust, others cultivated with near tenderness. A wall may be enemy on one side and garden on the other. The people know colors: harmless gray, useful white, dangerous yellow, fever-black, sweet-smelling green, the orange bloom that means a pipe is breathing wrong. Children are taught not to touch beauty unless they know who planted it.

And then there are the less classifiable inhabitants.

The hum in empty jars beside the central basin. The sideways flame in the north passage. The fish-mouthed drain that clicks during storms. The west pipe that answers when whistled into.

The residents do not worship these things exactly. They accommodate them. A jar that hums may be used to settle a crying infant, or avoided during a fever. The clicking drain is sometimes given a thread bracelet, not because anyone admits it is alive, but because after the bracelet is tied, the clicking seems less sharp. The west pipe is treated like a bad-tempered neighbor behind a wall: not seen, not explained, but not provoked.

If something singular lives in the place, it is distributed. It is not a monster under the cistern or a spirit in the vents. It is the whole compact intelligence made by hands, habits, animals, damp, hunger, and repair. The people are part of it, but not masters of it. The goats, birds, cats, lizards, insects, roots, molds, pipes, bricks, and rules all participate. The settlement thinks through maintenance. It remembers through wear. It speaks in leaks, echoes, smells, and taboos.

What lives there, finally, is a community that has become an organ.

Warm, noisy, damp in patches, dry in patches, always feeding and being fed. Human hands keep it from collapsing, but nonhuman hungers keep it from going still. Every resident is both caretaker and kept thing. Every creature has a niche, every niche a history, every history a rule. It is crowded, fragile, practical, and strangely content: a handmade underworld where survival has been polished smooth by generations of touch.
