The ones who live there are not a single people, though outsiders give them one name.

They are plateau people, cistern people, wall people, people of the upper stone. They live in districts so far apart that a marriage song in one basin may be unintelligible three ridges away, yet all of them know the same first lessons: how to breathe while climbing, how to carry water without spilling, how to read clouds by their bellies, how to listen for stone moving under snow.

They are generally compact rather than tall, not because no tall people are born there, but because the country favors those who keep heat well and waste little. Their faces are browned and weather-cut, with cracked lips, reddened cheeks, and eyes narrowed by glare. Many have the permanent squint of people who live between snowlight and smoke. Their hands are broad, scarred, and dry. Fingernails break often. Knuckles swell early from cold work. Old people become light and rope-like, all tendon and memory, able to climb stairs that leave visitors gasping.

They move slowly when they can and quickly only when they must. No one born there wastes breath on unnecessary haste. A child may seem solemn to a lowlander, not from sadness but from learning early that shouting into thin air is foolish, that running uphill is paid for dearly, that a dropped cup of water matters. Their gestures are economical. They point with the chin, call with whistles, answer from rooftops with lifted cloths or the flash of a polished shard.

Their clothing is layered, patched, and dense with use. Wool is the true skin of the place. Goat hair, cattle hair, felted fleece, hide, sinew stitching, bone toggles, horn buttons, woven belts, ankle wraps, hooded cloaks stiff with lanolin and smoke: these are more common than ornament. Color exists, but it is precious. Red thread at a cuff. Blue beads traded from far below. Yellow lichen dye on a child’s festival hood. Black bands at the hem to hide soot and mud. People wear their wealth in warmth, in waterproof seams, in boots whose soles have been replaced five times and still hold.

They sleep close together.

Rooms are low and dark, not from poverty alone but from wisdom. A high ceiling is a place for heat to escape. Families share platforms above animals or beside storage bins. Children sleep between adults and old people. Dogs sleep against doors. New lambs and sick kids are brought indoors during storms and wrapped in old cloaks. In winter, a house breathes as one creature: humans, animals, embers, damp wool, stored grain, drying dung, fermenting milk, sleeping dogs, all held inside stone.

The people have a deep intimacy with weight. They know what a jar weighs empty, what it weighs when full, what it weighs when ice has begun to form at the lip. They know how many blocks a pair of cousins can shift before noon, how much dung a child can carry, how much salt a pack animal can bear over a ridge in bad weather. Their architecture is not drawn so much as accumulated through remembered loads: this wall leans but has leaned for forty years; that roof can hold snow but not three people; that bridge sings under hoof but does not crack.

Their houses are inherited in layers. A household may include rooms built by a grandmother’s grandfather, a pen roofed last summer, a blocked doorway from when the street ran lower, a cistern mouth older than any living lineage, and a new wind wall raised after one terrible winter. No one entirely owns such a place in the lowland sense. They belong to it as much as it belongs to them. They repair it because their dead repaired it, because their children will curse them if they do not, because stone remembers neglect.

Water shapes their manners more than any king could.

They do not pour casually. They do not wash in display. They do not spit near channels. A guest is offered water with gravity, not abundance: a small cup, both hands, watched but not begrudged. To refuse it is strange. To waste it is nearly unforgivable. Children are corrected sharply for splashing. Lovers may give each other meltwater caught from a first thaw. Mourners may wet the lips of the dead before burial or exposure, a final act of household tenderness.

Their laws are often less about guilt than consequence. If you damage a roof channel, you help repair every cistern that went dry below it. If your wall creates a dangerous drift, you dismantle and rebuild it with those harmed by it. If your animals foul a shared spring, your household loses drawing priority until the next moon or thaw. The worst crimes are those that endanger storage: poisoning a cistern, burning a dung stack, opening a grain room in thaw damp, stealing winter fodder, cutting a bridge rope and leaving no warning.

They are not humorless. Severe places do not necessarily make severe people; they make people alert to absurdity. They laugh at slipped boots, stubborn goats, pompous ravens, young men who overestimate their lungs, traders who bring delicate lowland shoes. Their jokes are dry and small, often delivered without smiling until everyone else has understood. They tease by comparison to animals: a slow uncle is “a stone cow in fog,” a vain girl “a snow finch admiring ash,” a greedy boy “a dog with two doorways.”

Their children are everywhere, though not loudly everywhere. They run along parapets in games that would terrify visitors. They know which walls may be climbed and which stones are false-faced. They learn knots before letters, weather before numbers, animals before formal prayer. They scrape frost into cups, collect beetles from warm stones, carry chips of dung, chase finches, memorize the turns between districts by smell and slope. Many are given small tasks almost as soon as they can walk: hold this cord, watch that kid, cover this jar, bring three stones, listen if the water begins to run.

Old people are maps.

They sit in sun pockets against south walls, wrapped in dark wool, and tell where a spring used to rise, where a glacier once stood, which bridge fell in whose father’s time, which neighborhood moved because shadow swallowed the barley terraces. Their memories are not decorative. They are infrastructure. A district may consult an elder before cutting a new channel, not out of sentiment but because she remembers the year when water came from a crack everyone else has forgotten.

They bury, expose, stack, or entomb their dead depending on district, slope, season, and means. Where soil is too thin, stone chambers take them. Where cliffs are clean and birds reliable, some dead are given to height. Bones, after time and rite, may be gathered into family niches or built into memorial walls behind sealed slabs. Names are spoken at cisterns, terraces, and thresholds. A person is remembered by the things that still function because of them: “She deepened the west tank.” “He set the lower stair.” “They brought the red goat line through the winter of no thaw.”

Their religion, if called that, is practical before it is doctrinal. They honor sources: springs, fog, snowfields, animals that yield milk, walls that hold, fires that catch, ancestors who stored wisely. Shrines are small because excess stone has other uses. A stack of river-smoothed stones beside a seep. Horns tied with red thread above a passage. A niche holding a blackened bowl, a feather, a carved bone, a pinch of salt. Prayers are often indistinguishable from maintenance. Clearing ice from a channel may be an act of devotion. Covering a cistern before dust rises may be a blessing.

Their music is made for air that steals sound. Long flutes are rare; breath is too dear. More common are low horns, skin drums struck indoors, clappers, rattles of bone and seed, throat songs sung in close rooms where bodies make the air warm. Songs tend to move by repetition and small shifts, like footpaths becoming roads. Work songs mark lifting, hauling, threshing, pounding grain, setting roof slabs. Lullabies imitate wind, goat bells that do not exist, ravens, dripping water.

They do not have many bells, but they have signals. A horn from a gate means travelers. Three clapper bursts from a roof means animals loose. Smoke in two columns means water running. A red cloth held still means danger on a path. A black cloth snapped twice warns of bad ice. Whole ridges can speak this way in fragments, not quickly, but reliably enough.

Their markets are not roaring places. They are dense, negotiated, smoky, and watchful. Goods are laid on cloths or flat stones: salt cakes, dried cheese, barley, wool cord, horn spoons, obsidian flakes, pumice scrubbers, medicinal roots, smoked meat, tanned hides, carved bone needles, old metal fragments, lamp fat, pigments, feathers, river shells from impossible distances. People bargain with memory as much as coin. Who helped whose herd in snow. Whose son married into which cistern-right. Which caravan arrived short last year and why.

Metal is treated almost as a living elder. A knife may have a name. A copper pin may pass through six generations of cloaks. Broken iron is not discarded; it is reset, hafted, traded, drilled, wrapped, invoked in disputes as proof of continuity. A bronze blade worn down to a sliver may still be brought out for births, deaths, and the cutting of first harvest sheaves. Children are taught not to touch metal tools without permission, the way they are taught not to lean over cistern mouths.

Their animals are not possessions only. They are co-inhabitants, economies on legs, warmth with tempers.

The goats are the cleverest citizens and the least trustworthy. They know gates, weak latches, kitchen smells, the sound of grain being measured. They climb stairs meant for people and roofs not meant for goats. Their herders curse them with affection and suspicion. Bloodlines are remembered carefully: milk-rich, sure-footed, good mothers, wall-rubbers, fence-breakers, winter survivors. A good goat is praised as if it has moral character. A bad goat is still valuable, which makes its wickedness more personal.

The shaggy cattle are slower, darker, less numerous, and more solemn. They provide dung, traction, milk, hide, and a sense of wealth that can be seen steaming in the morning. Children are warned not to stand near their horns in narrow lanes. Their breath freezes on their muzzles. Their bells, where bells exist at all, are made of wood or bone and sound like hollow knocking.

The pack animals are bred close to the mountains. Long-haired, narrow-chested, steady-eyed, with split hooves or padded feet depending on lineage, they kneel to be loaded and rise with a kind of resigned dignity. They know routes better than young drivers. In fog, people trust them. On bad scree, people listen to the hesitation of their feet. A pack animal that refuses a bridge may save a whole caravan.

Dogs live half in households and half in their own society. They are not pampered, but they are respected. They guard food, sleep against children, warn of foxes, find lost goats, trail blood on snow, and know which strangers are harmless. Their coats are thick and often the color of ash, lichen, or old stone. They do not bark without reason; unnecessary noise is disliked even in dogs. A good dog may be fed before a careless uncle.

Then there are the unofficial residents.

Ravens know everything. They know slaughter days, weak roof covers, which children throw scraps, which old women pretend not to. They walk along parapets with bureaucratic importance, heads tilted, eyes bright. People insult them, consult them, chase them, feed them accidentally, and sometimes deliberately. A raven tapping at a smoke cowl may be an omen in one district and merely a nuisance in another.

Rock doves soften the city’s hard edges. They nest in holes left by scaffolding, old drainage gaps, cracked towers, abandoned wall niches. Their droppings are scraped and used. Their eggs are taken carefully, not all at once. Their cooing fills covered alleys during still afternoons, a warm sound in a mineral place.

Foxes are tolerated until they become too bold. Pale, narrow-faced, light-footed, they pass through twilight like blown ash. They raid drying racks, steal eggs, and leave prints across roof snow. Children tell stories about foxes that know secret cisterns or lead travelers off paths. Adults pretend not to believe these stories while still watching fox tracks closely.

Hares live where gardens meet rubble. Marmots claim outer stone piles and whistle insults at anyone approaching. Snow finches pour through courtyards in sudden flurries. Beetles survive under warm slabs near hearth vents. Moths haunt wool stores and are fought with smoke, herbs, and patient fingers. Frogs appear after thaw as if the stone itself has remembered how to sing.

Above all are the great birds: eagles, lammergeiers, high circling scavengers whose shadows pass over terraces and make chickens freeze. People look up when they pass. Not always reverently, but always with attention. These birds know the city’s deaths, its slaughter courts, its exposed bones, its cliff paths. They are part of the sanitation of the sky.

The people’s bodies belong to the city, but so do their minds. They think in gradients: upper, lower, windward, lee, thaw-facing, shadowed, cistern-fed, drift-dangerous. Distance is not measured by length alone but by breath, load, slope, and weather. A place may be “near” if downhill and deadly if uphill in fog. A neighbor across a ravine may be socially close and physically half a day away.

They are suspicious of abundance because abundance often rots, spills, attracts, freezes, or brings thieves. They prefer sufficiency well stored. A lowlander’s feast may impress them less than a sealed granary, a full dung wall, a cistern with a clean cover, and boots drying properly.

Yet they are capable of extravagance in labor. They will spend years moving stones for a terrace that yields a little barley. They will carve a channel across a stubborn face of basalt so meltwater can be persuaded into a tank. They will rebuild a bridge exactly where the last one failed because the path matters and because failure teaches the next set of hands. They will carry a beam from a valley over three passes and speak of it for generations.

What lives there, finally, is not only a people but a compact between breath and stone.

Humans live there, yes: herders, quarry workers, cistern keepers, roof menders, dung stackers, weavers, bone carvers, bridge builders, salt traders, fog catchers, children with frost-reddened fingers, elders who remember vanished springs.

Animals live there: goats with criminal intelligence, cattle like warm boulders, dogs like gray shadows, pack beasts with mountain patience, doves, ravens, foxes, hares, marmots, finches, beetles, moths, frogs, eagles.

And habits live there too, as durable as masonry: saving, patching, watching, sharing only by rule, mourning through repair, celebrating thaw, distrusting waste, building upward one stone at a time.

The inhabitants are not grand in the imperial sense. They do not need to be. They are fitted to a hard place so closely that from far away they nearly disappear into it. But close by, they are everywhere: a hand closing a cistern lid, a child laughing behind a wool curtain, a goat testing a gate, an old woman reading clouds from a roof, a raven waiting beside a butcher’s stone, a line of people passing blocks from palm to palm while the young mountains stand around them, unfinished and cold.
