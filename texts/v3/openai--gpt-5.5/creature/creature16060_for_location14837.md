What lives there is not a single species, though the place has taught all of them the same manners.

They are dry things, careful things, things that have learned to take nourishment from what other worlds would call lack.

The smallest residents are everywhere first: beetles, lizards, moths, air-slugs, stem-coils, six-footed foxlets with brittle ears. They are not pests. Nothing here is incidental enough to be a pest. Each has a route, a season, a tolerated appetite. The lacquer beetles polish the grooves between tiles as they pass in black processions, wearing paths finer than script. The pale lizards keep to the plaster faces and blink one eye at one wall, one eye at another, especially in the western corner where a single gaze is unreliable. Ash-moths sleep inside mineral flowers until the heat reaches the correct thickness, then wake without fluttering, opening and closing their wings as though folding messages back into themselves.

The air-slugs are almost never seen directly. One notices them by what bends around them: pollen parting, a hanging seed nudged aside, a thin crescent of shadow crawling where no body should cast it. They move along invisible surfaces, feeding on vapors and the faint residue left when a room has nearly existed. The stem-coils are stranger still. They live inside hollow reeds and ceramic pipes, nested in spirals, tasting duration. When a corridor becomes longer, they grow fat. When an afternoon repeats part of itself, they sing very quietly, though the sound is usually eaten before anyone hears it.

The fox-faced animals are the boldest. They nest high in the shelves among dry leaves and broken lattice. Their six feet are delicate, with hooked toes made for climbing fired clay. Their eyes are dark and overlarge, their noses always dusted gold with pollen. They steal curled leaves from baskets, sleep in masks, chew the corners of clay tablets if not watched, and make no cry louder than the creak of a seedpod. When frightened, they do not run. They become intensely still, folding their papery ears flat, waiting for reality to misplace them.

Above, below, and between these animals lives the planted life: not wild growth, but not obedient growth either. It has the demeanor of something trained from infancy and still secretly older than its trainers.

The plants grip rather than root. They clasp knobs, hooks, ribs, pegs, shelves. They drink oil, salt, heat, shadow, powdered shell, forgotten acoustics. Their leaves hold light like lacquered shields. Their flowers open by joints, hinges, ratchets, tiny decisions. Some bear fruits that remember celestial events they have never seen. Some release pollen only when the air has been silenced sufficiently. Some keep count of footsteps. The braided copper vine is almost an animal in its patience; it tightens around trellises, ladders, and disused handles with the slow authority of a thought returning again and again to the same conclusion.

But there are caretakers too.

They are easy to miss because they have made themselves compatible with the garden’s quiet. They do not announce themselves with footsteps or speech. They arrive as a shifting of a reed screen, a hand appearing between black leaves, a ladder moved from one alcove to another, a hook lifting a shutter at the exact hour when warm air must be persuaded to turn.

They are people, or near enough to people that the distinction matters only to strangers.

Their bodies are narrow, compact, and sun-browned to the color of fired clay. Their skin has the faint matte dryness of unglazed ceramic. At the joints and along the throat it gathers into fine creases where pale dust settles. They do not sweat. Their eyes are protected by inner lids that draw sideways when pollen falls or when the fountain’s absence bends too sharply. Their lips are often cracked, but not from thirst. Thirst, in the ordinary sense, is something they know about mostly through diagrams, prohibitions, and inherited grief.

They wear layered cloth, reed-fiber, softened hide, and aprons stiff with resin. Around their wrists are loops of copper wire, measuring strings, pruning hooks, bone awls, folded masks, little tablets of instruction. Their feet are bare or bound in thin sandals that know the tile better than sight does. The soles of the oldest keepers are polished hard from walking the same ringing mosaic for decades.

They speak rarely, and when they do, they speak close to the listener’s ear or into cupped hands. It is not secrecy. It is thrift. Sound is food here, and a careless sentence can fatten the wrong nest. Children learn early that laughter must be done into cloth, that names should not be spoken near hollow stems, and that songs belong only in sealed jars unless one intends to feed the moths.

Their language has many gestures. A crooked finger means the fifth stair has changed. Two knuckles touched to the brow mean the western wall should be looked at with one eye only. A palm held downward over the empty fountain means do not ask today. A thumb pressed to the tongue and then to a plant’s stem means this one is remembering; wait.

They tend the place as one might tend a stove, a patient animal, a wound, and a calendar all at once.

At dawn, if dawn enters from above that day, they open the warm vents with hooked poles. They check the suspended seeds and sweep down those that have grown tired of hovering. They scrape dried sap from the undersides of shelves. They turn mineral cakes so that root-grips can feed from an unbitten face. They dust flowers with ash, bind swelling pods with thread, loosen vines before the frames crack, and tighten other vines when a wall begins to forget its shape.

At midwarm, they avoid the blue vine. They do not cross the short stair unless carrying a weight in the left hand. They listen at the jars that contain echoes, tapping them lightly with a fingernail. If a jar answers with more room than it should possess, they chalk a warning mark beside it and turn its mouth toward shade.

In the long still part of the day, the keepers sit in alcoves and mend. They repair baskets, knot fibers, sharpen chipped stone knives, repaint pictographs, oil the bellows, copy old warnings onto new tablets. Often they work beside sleeping animals. A foxlet may curl in the crook of an elbow. A lizard may cling to the back of a shoulder for warmth. Beetles cross their ankles in processions and are not disturbed.

The oldest among them are called by titles rather than names: the Hook-Keeper, the Counter of Alcoves, the One Who Breathes for the Eastern Flue, the Rememberer of Wetness. Whether any of them has actually seen water is unclear. They know rituals for it. They know the shape of a cup made for it. They know the blue glass in the fountain is meant to resemble it. They know certain old words that must be spoken only when all sound-eaters are asleep.

But they do not need water.

Or perhaps they have been taught not to.

They eat waxy leaves shaved thin, salt-fruit cured in ash, cakes of crushed seed and mineral gel, translucent slivers cut from swollen plants and allowed to smoke in the mouth. They drink oils in drops from ceramic spoons. They inhale vapors from cracked pods. The children are given warm stones to hold against their bellies after meals, and these seem to nourish them as much as food.

They sleep in niches along the walls, on mats that can be rolled away quickly if a ladder begins remembering its treehood or a doorway starts reconsidering its destination. Their beds are never placed in straight rows. Straightness attracts certain errors. Instead, they sleep staggered, tucked between shelves and flues, under hanging screens of dry grass. Infants are wrapped in cloth dusted with powdered shell. Their cradles are hung from hooks that have never held anything else, because reused hooks teach babies old directions.

They are not prisoners. Yet few leave.

The garden is small, but it contains enough nearness to occupy a life. Outside may exist, but outside is not trusted to have the proper rules. Outside may have rain. Outside may have open distance. Outside may permit a voice to travel too far without being eaten, and that would feel indecent.

New caretakers are born rarely, or perhaps found, or perhaps grown by arrangements nobody explains to visitors. The children are solemn early, though not joyless. They play quiet games: catching upward-bouncing seeds in woven cups, racing beetle shadows without touching the beetles, guessing which alcove will appear next, balancing on tiles that ring at different pitches. They are taught to count days by leaf angles, not by sun. They learn which flowers may be watched with both eyes and which must be passed as if unaware.

There is also something else living there.

The caretakers do not call it a god. That would be too simple and too loud.

It lives at the dry fountain, or through it, or as the shape of what the fountain lacks.

It has no body except the bending of air above the reed-shaped spout. No face except the slow circling of dust motes. No voice except the knock far inside the wall every few hours, like someone patient behind a door that may not yet have been built. The animals feed from it. The plants lean toward it. The keepers maintain its basin with a devotion more practical than worship. They sweep the blue glass. They replace cracked tiles only when the cracks stop forming maps. They leave rings of salt and polished seeds around the rim. They bring dying leaves there to finish becoming useful.

The absence in the fountain is fertile. That is the secret everyone knows and no one says plainly.

It is the spring of no water. The mouth of the missing thing. A hollow from which life has learned to draw instruction.

Sometimes a keeper will sit alone beside it and extend two fingers into the distortion above the spout. Not touching. Not quite. The air around the fingers tightens, and the keeper’s face becomes very still. Perhaps they are receiving moisture of a kind no skin can confess. Perhaps they are feeding it memory. Perhaps they are asking whether the world has changed its mind.

The fountain never answers in words.

Instead, a flower opens somewhere after many days of hesitation. A shadow becomes ripe enough to harvest. A new alcove appears behind a screen that was flat yesterday. A child dreams of rain and wakes with blue dust on their tongue.

So the inhabitants are gardeners, yes, but also products of the garden’s handmade law. They are dry-bodied, soft-stepping people who cultivate absence as carefully as fruit. Around them live animals that eat sound, plants that feed on salt and heat, vines with the patience of metal, moths that wake to warmth, slugs that crawl on unseen surfaces, and a central lack so nourishing that everything leans toward it.

Nothing there is wild.

Nothing there is fully tame.

They live inside a rule built by hands, and over time they have become part of its grammar.
