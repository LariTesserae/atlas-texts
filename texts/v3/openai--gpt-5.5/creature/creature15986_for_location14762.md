What lives there is not a people anymore.

Not in the old sense. Not a crowd, not a culture with meals and quarrels and lamps in windows. The district’s true inhabitants are things that have learned to be small, quiet, timed, and misclassified.

They live by becoming part of the maintenance.

The most numerous are the nearly invisible ones: pale threadworms in the mineral runnels, glassy and jointless, with bodies no thicker than wet hair. They do not swim so much as measure the water with themselves. When the pumps sleep, they lengthen and drift from one warm pool to another through cracks too narrow for sight. When the machinery wakes, they knot themselves around flakes of copper salt and hold fast, becoming indistinguishable from fibrous scale. Their mouths are soft rings. They graze on bacterial films, dissolved organics, the slow bloom that gathers where old self-healing material bleeds nutrients into stone. They have no eyes; eyes would be useless here. Instead they feel pressure changes through their whole bodies. A fan starting far away is, to them, weather. A footstep is catastrophe. A drainage predator passing overhead is night becoming teeth.

In the moss live insects that seem almost manufactured. They are pale, flat, many-legged, with translucent plates overlapping like broken circuitry. Their movements have the caution of machines imitating insects and doing it too well. They fold themselves into seams when light appears. They harvest droplets from the black moss and scrape mineral residue from pipe lips. Some carry grains of dust to static gardens, not because they serve the city, but because the gardens grow a faint nutritious skin after each compaction cycle. The insects have learned the schedules of vents, rains, and cleaning fields. A whole generation may hatch, feed, mate, and die between two major thermal groans.

There are plants, though they are not lush enough to own the place. They are legal trespassers, accepted by malfunction.

The blade-plants in the wall seams grow like knives placed edge-first into the architecture. One side of each leaf is silver, reflecting whatever small light the district leaks; the other is a dark arterial red that drinks warmth. They open in the brief hush after rain, when condensation hangs thick and the air is full of dissolved metals. Their roots do not seek soil. They enter hairline cracks, service membranes, corroded seams, and the porous edges of old composite. They live on seepage and on the minute chemical generosity of decay. When the fans pass, they fold shut with a dry clicking, becoming black needles against the wall.

The cup-stem groves are more delicate. They rise in little families in alcoves and basin margins, translucent as cartilage, each capped by a tiny vessel. They are patient rain-catchers. Their roots form mats beneath the floor skin, sharing moisture through slow pulses. During dry intervals they withdraw color until they resemble abandoned filaments or failed sensors. During wet intervals they blush faintly amber from within. Moths visit them, if visit is not too intentional a word for what the moths do: drift, land, shiver, drink, leave dust.

The moths are among the loveliest things there, because they have no reason to be. They gather under warm vents in pale colonies, wings held upright like folded paper. Their bodies are furred with mineral powder. Under ultraviolet haze they shine blue-white, then vanish when the fountain flickers dead. They lay their eggs in mats of black moss, and their larvae eat the moss in crescent-shaped paths. The adult moths fly only during certain acoustic conditions. When the deep floor-pulse comes, they rise in a loose cloud and let the vibration guide them, as if the city’s sleeping heart were a moon.

Higher up, in the ribs and insulation, live the warm-blooded gliders.

They are not bats, though a frightened traveler would call them bats and be forgiven. Their wings are narrow membranes stretched between elongated fingers and the fluted sides of their bodies. Their heads are blunt. Their ears are internal slits protected by soft valves, because the district is too loud for exposed hearing. They sense by pressure, heat, and a low throat-click that hides inside the machinery’s rhythms. Their fur is dark gray and faintly oily, beading condensation. They roost in clusters where old insulation still holds warmth, hooked into ceramic seams with four small claws.

They emerge when the district becomes loudest. Rain on roofs, pumps in sequence, fans pushing air through the hollow: that is their dusk. In silence they are vulnerable. In thunder they become ghosts. They drop from ceiling ribs, skim plazas, scoop moths from ultraviolet haze, drink from falling curtains of condensation without landing. Sometimes one misjudges a field boundary and leaves behind a crisped wingtip or a smear of burnt fur on an invisible edge. The others learn the altered air and do not cross there again.

They have a social life, but a compressed one. They groom in the warm dark. They exchange food mouth to mouth. Their young cling to the insulation until the first time a major rain-cycle begins; then the adults shove them out into the roar, and the weak fall, and the strong become part of the ceiling.

Something larger lives below.

No one sees it clearly because it has made a life out of not needing to be seen. It moves through drainage tunnels, sump throats, half-flooded service channels, and the undersides of grates. It may be one animal, or a small breeding population, or a lineage that has altered so much with time that each generation seems like a different rumor.

Its body is probably long. The evidence suggests length: silt dragged in a continuous curve, threadworm colonies erased in strips, a grille bent outward by pressure from beneath. It has a hide that sheds oil into water, because certain pools sometimes carry a rainbow film after the pumps stop. It feeds on worms, insects, fallen gliders, moss-sludge, perhaps on the nutrient cakes compacted by static gardens. It knows which cisterns empty first and which rainfalls are safe to enter. When the district vibrates, it becomes still. When the machinery misfires and a quiet pocket opens, it crosses.

It has no name unless the city has given it one in a damaged category: obstruction, mobile silt mass, unauthorized biomass, recurring drain fault.

That is how most living things survive there. They are not citizens. They are tolerated errors.

And above them all—or beneath them, or around them—lives the district itself.

Not alive as an animal is alive. Not conscious in a clean, declarative way. But it has appetites of continuity. It has reflexes. It has memory distributed through warm panels, responsive filaments, pressure doors, acoustic drains, field-stairs, rain condensers, load threads, and civic machines still turning in the dark. It drinks heat from below and sweats it into passages. It catches water, lifts it, drops it, filters it, loses it, finds it again. It mistakes decay for instruction and instruction for weather. It opens vents to plants because the channels sing at the correct pitch. It avoids crushing moth colonies because they register as harmless dust. It routes warmth toward empty benches. It lights paths for absent feet.

Its intelligence is senile but immense in comparison to the creatures sheltering inside it. It does not know them as inhabitants. It knows them as fluctuations: soft heat crossing a plaza, moisture loss near moss, tiny impacts in a conduit, protein film, recurring motion, noncritical. Sometimes it kills them accidentally. A sterilizing pulse through a pipe. A pressure door deciding to become a wall. A rainfall hard enough to smash cup-stems flat. Sometimes it saves them without meaning to. A vent opens. A leaking joint remains unrepaired because the repair protocol has forgotten the material name. A warm bench becomes a nursery.

The old human or humanlike inhabitants remain only as shapes that other life uses.

Their rooms are dens. Their handrails are moth perches. Their market stalls trap heat. Their transit mouths breathe air that plants understand. Their civic basin has become a pond-edge ecology. Their language survives as tones that no throat answers, except perhaps the gliders, who have folded the arrival chimes into their mating calls.

If there is any person left, it is not a resident in the ordinary way.

Perhaps somewhere behind a pressure door that opens only once every few years, a maintenance custodian persists: not fully machine, not fully flesh, a narrow figure grown into its harness, asleep between alarms. Perhaps it wakes when the black rings are spoken to too loudly. Perhaps it moves along service ways that no animal uses, leaving three-toed prints in mineral dust and correcting small imbalances with tools fused to its hands. It would not think of itself as lonely. Loneliness requires expectation. It would think in tasks, thresholds, dampers, seals. It would pass a colony of moths and classify them as dust with wings.

But even that figure, if it exists, is only one more organ of the hollow.

The dominant life is the buried city’s afterlife: an ecosystem of leftovers, leaks, permissions, and mistakes. The creatures there do not conquer the ruin. They do not reclaim it. They fit themselves into the intervals of its ongoing service to nobody.

They live between pumpbeats.

They flower after rain and close before wind. They hatch in warm seams. They feed where corrosion sweetens. They glide when the machines are loud enough to hide them. They vanish when the blue guide-lights wake. They learn which doors listen and which doors misunderstand. They are sparse because the place is sparse in mercy, but they endure because the place is also endlessly providing: heat, water, darkness, shelter, mineral, rhythm.

So what lives there is a thin, secret ecology inside a sleeping engineered body.

Worms like wet threads. Insects like careful flakes of bone. Moths made luminous by broken civic light. Dark gliders nesting in the ribs. A long unseen thing in the drains. Plants that bloom by schedule rather than season. Possibly a custodian no longer distinguishable from duty.

And the district itself, still breathing through stone, still speaking to absence, still mistaking survival for maintenance.
