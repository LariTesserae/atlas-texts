What lives there is mostly not animal.

The true inhabitants are the forests: slow, buoyant, warm-bloodless forests rooted in new mountain stone and spread through water as if through dream. They are not trees, not kelp, not coral, though they borrow from all three. Their bodies begin in cracks where mineral heat seeps upward. First come black films and velvet mats, then pale root-fans, then hollow stems that rise in spirals until they find the higher light. Their leaves are broad, translucent, and faintly veined with gold. They open and close over hours, sometimes days, taking in light, minerals, warmth, and the slow drift of dissolved life.

They are the dominant creatures of the drowned heights. Everything else lives among them, on them, from them, or in the shelter of their patience.

Some of the forest-forms grow as single towering bodies, anchored deep in basalt fissures and lifting a crown of floating pads to the water’s upper skin. Others are colonies: thousands of small cups, fronds, bladders, hairs, and root-mouths joined by dark cords hidden in silt. These colonies creep across ridges by fractions of an inch, softening the raw mountain as they go. A ledge that looks merely furred with green may be one organism centuries old, feeling the mineral taste of stone through a million fine mouths.

They do not think quickly, but they answer one another. A change in light moves through them as a faint tightening. A tremor under the mountain becomes a slow bloom of pale leaves. When a new vent opens, the nearest mats darken first, then the ribbon-stems lean, then whole submerged groves adjust over the following season, turning not toward the sun but toward warmth rising from below. Their communication is pressure, chemistry, shadow, and touch. A torn vine can warn an entire ravine before the animal that grazed it has moved ten body-lengths away.

Around the vents live the oldest colonies.

They are low and dark at first glance: black mats, red fans, white nodules closed like sleeping flowers. But they are dense with life. Mineral-feeding threads weave through them. Tiny transparent chambers fill and empty. Heat-loving films shimmer silver when disturbed. These vent gardens do not depend entirely on daylight, and so they persist in hollows where the water is cloudy and green-black. They are the nearest thing the place has to hearths. Many other beings come there, not to gather socially, exactly, but because warmth and mineral breath loosen food from the stone.

The smallest animals are almost indistinguishable from drifting debris. There are soft-bodied grazers no longer than a finger, pale as peeled seeds, with mouths made of combs. They move along leaves and rock faces, scraping films so gently that the plants tolerate them. Some trail mucous threads that catch spores and powdered mineral. Others carry bright specks inside them, symbiotic grains of green or amber that continue to drink light through their translucent skins.

There are winged swimmers too, though they have no wings in the air-breathing sense. Their bodies are narrow, ribbed, and nearly boneless, with four wide membranes that undulate in slow opposing waves. They look fragile until they turn. Then the whole animal becomes a blade of intention, slipping sideways between fronds without stirring them. Most are grazers or silt-sifters. They feed by passing water through feathery folds along their throats, taking pollen, spores, soft algae, and the invisible young of other things.

Their eyes, if they can be called eyes, are wide cloudy lenses set low in the head. They see brightness more than shape. They know the world as gradients: warmer, darker, denser, bitter, open, rooted, moving.

In the suspended meadows, where fallen leaves stop halfway between stone and surface, another kind of life gathers. These midwater rafts become nurseries. Eggs cling to their undersides in pearly strings. Rootlets descend and ascend from the same rotting leaf, making tangled chambers where small swimmers hide. Some creatures spend their entire lives there, never touching the mountain below or the upper water-skin above. They are little hinged things, like seeds with legs, opening and closing as they filter the slow rain of organic matter. When disturbed, they fold flat and become indistinguishable from leaf scars.

There are also thread-mouths: long, narrow animals that move rarely and feed almost constantly. They wedge themselves in cracks or among root-fans and release dozens of hair-fine lines from their mouths. These threads drift outward, sticky with invisible beads. Spores, larvae, and powdered plant flesh collect on them. Every few hours the animal reels the threads back in and swallows the water’s memory. In clear places one can see whole slopes delicately strung with these feeding lines, as if the valley has been stitched together by patient hunger.

The larger animals are few.

One of them resembles a leaf only from far away. It is broad, thin, and slow, with a body shaped like a dark green shield and edges that ripple in muscular waves. Young ones graze openly in the blue corridors, browsing on suspended pollen and the tender rims of floating pads. Older ones rise higher and travel alone. Their backs grow gardens: mossy films, red tassels, sometimes small rooted plants that ride them for years. Seen from below, crossing the light, they appear as shadows with softened margins — leaves that have learned to choose their direction.

They are not often predators, but they can be. The underside of the body bears a circular mouth usually kept closed and hidden among folds. When opened, it is pale and muscular, capable of drawing in a cloud of small animals, silt, spores, and water all at once. After such feeding, the creature may not eat again for many days. It settles beneath an overhang, dims its skin, and becomes almost landscape.

There are rarer things deeper in the drowned valleys, where light comes green and late. They are long-bodied and nearly colorless, with trailing sensory filaments that brush stone, root, and current. Their bones, if they have bones, are soft rods of mineralized cartilage. They move with deliberate contractions, as though each decision must travel a great distance through the body before action follows. They feed on vent colonies, dead wood, and sometimes the sleeping young of the broad leaf-swimmers. Their mouths are small but persistent. Given time, they can remove a body from inside its own skin.

Nothing here wastes speed unless it must.

Even fear is slow. A grazing animal does not bolt at the first shadow. It folds, dims, settles, waits to learn whether the shadow is hunger or merely passing shade. Plants do not snap shut. They sour their surfaces, thicken their skins, withdraw sweetness from exposed leaves, call bitter minerals into their outer tissues. A predator does not chase far. It positions itself where drifting, curiosity, warmth, or the confusion of light will eventually bring food near enough.

The creatures of this place live by accepting delay.

Many of them have double orientations. Roots grow both down and up. Fins push against water that is sometimes layered in density like invisible ground. Larvae hatch with no fixed sense of fall. Some spend their early lives clinging to flakes of stone that hang beside cliffs instead of dropping. Others attach to bubbles and ride them upward until the bubble breaks against the water-ceiling; then they unfurl tiny membranes and glide down through shafts of light, feeding as they descend.

Reproduction is quiet and extravagant. At certain times the forests release pale dust that turns entire basins opalescent. The dust is not all seed; some is food, some signal, some decoy, some the shed skin of growth. Animals gather in it, feeding until their transparent bellies glow. The broad shadow-swimmers pass through these clouds with their mouths open, becoming briefly golden inside. Vent mats answer with red threads. Floating pads bead their undersides with embryos. For a few days the whole drowned mountain seems to be thinking in pollen.

There may be intelligence there, but if so it does not gather into faces or tools.

The closest thing to a mind belongs to the old root-colonies that span whole bowls and saddles. They remember through growth. A rockslide is recorded in the bend of stems. A century of mineral seep becomes a white grove. Repeated grazing shapes bitter corridors where animals no longer feed. If one part of the colony is shaded for too long, another may open leaves higher up, lending sugar through buried cords. It is not planning as animals plan. It is adjustment so slow and complete that it resembles wisdom from a distance.

The rare animals know these colonies the way weather-bound creatures know seasons. They follow the flowering of vent fans, the souring of young leaves, the loosening of silt after tremors. They migrate not across continents but from basin to basin, from warmth to light to mineral bloom. Some return to the same submerged saddle to breed for generations, guided by the taste of stone. Some never leave the valley where they hatched.

At night the inhabitants change without becoming loud. Leaves retain a faint green glow. Vent cracks pulse softly. Small grazers rise from the undersides of pads. Thread-mouths cast longer lines. The broad swimmers descend, their upper skins no longer leaf-dark but faintly speckled, like pieces of night carrying old algae. Transparent larvae flicker in mineral light. The forests close some leaves and open others, turning their attention from sun to heat.

What lives there, finally, is a society of slowness.

Rooted bodies, drifting mouths, winged swimmers, patient mats, mineral-fed colonies, animals shaped like thoughts passing through green water — all of them adapted to a world where stone is young, water is sky, and urgency is a costly mistake. They do not conquer the drowned mountains. They soften them. They taste them. They veil them in leaf and filament. They wait while cliffs blur, while vents cloud and clear, while suspended meadows thicken in midwater.

The place is inhabited by life that has learned not to hurry because nothing here is truly still.
