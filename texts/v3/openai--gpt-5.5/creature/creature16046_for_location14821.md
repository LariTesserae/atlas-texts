What lives there is not large in the way a bear is large, nor visible in the way a tree is visible. It is a resident made of pauses.

At first you would think the hollow belongs only to moss, frogs, shrews, midges, and the small birds that spark through the willows. That would be true enough. They are there in their proper hunger: the shrews fierce as wet needles, the frogs gathered at the breathing mud, the voles stitching secret roads under the roots, the beetles working the stone with blind purpose. But none of them quite explains the feeling that the basin is occupied.

Something else lives in the seams between them.

It is easiest to notice by its absences. A patch of sedge flattened in a perfect crescent. A pool that was clouded yesterday and clear today, its silt combed into delicate ribs. A bright moss island shifted half a body-length across black water with no wind strong enough to move it. A row of bubbles rising in sequence, too steady to be chance, then stopping when footsteps approach.

The thing is low. Everything here must be low.

It has no upright posture, no antlers, no lifted head made for being seen against the sky. Its life is pressed into the same grammar as the hollow: mat, slick, root, fold, pocket, breath. When it moves above ground it does so as a dark length under vapor, something between otter, salamander, and loosened peat. Its back is basalt-black when wet, but not smooth; it is roughened with mineral flecks and green-brown growth, as though moss and stone dust have chosen to remain on it. Along its sides are pale seams that open and close faintly, not quite gills, not quite vents. In the cold air they smoke.

Its eyes, if seen, are disappointing at first: small, clouded, set low and wide. They do not shine like a predator’s eyes. They look more like droplets caught in old bark. It does not hunt by looking.

It listens through its whole body.

The pounding beneath the marsh is not only background to it. It is weather, speech, map, clock. It feels pressure changes through its belly. It knows which crusts will hold and which will tear. It knows when a vent is choking before steam finds a new outlet. It knows the difference between a vole’s tunnel and a root loosening in warm mud, between frost cracking slate and a fox placing one careful paw on stone.

Its body is built for uncertainty. It has limbs, but they are seldom seen as limbs. They slide from under it when needed: short, jointed, splayed, with long pale toes that distribute weight over floating moss. The toes are soft underneath and darkly clawed at the tips, suitable for gripping wet rock, parting sedge, anchoring in clay. When it crosses open mud, it leaves tracks that do not settle into one familiar animal: a drag mark, then five little stars, then a smooth place, then nothing where it has taken to water.

It does not break the surface of a pool so much as edit it. One moment the pool is whole. The next, a dark lens opens under the ice skin, and something has passed beneath.

Its den is not a burrow in the ordinary sense. The hollow is too wet, too mutable, too given to collapse. Instead it keeps several chambers: warm pockets under root mats, hollows between fallen basalt slabs, clay domes reinforced with willow fibers and its own hardened secretions. These chambers are connected by water, by cracks, by mud channels no wider than an arm. Some are nurseries. Some are listening places. Some are merely shelters used once, then abandoned when the ground changes its mind.

Inside them the walls are smooth and faintly waxed. Mineral bloom gathers there in pale blue-white crusts. Shed skins, thin as wet paper, cling to stone edges. The skins show the shape more clearly than the animal itself ever does: long torso, broad flat skull, ribbed throat, many delicate side-slits, limbs folded tight as if the body prefers not to admit it has them.

It feeds on what the hollow makes.

Not only meat, though it will take meat. It eats frog eggs, midge larvae, drowned voles, beetles stunned by steam, pale worms from the heated mud. It presses its mouth to mineral films and rasps them away. It grazes on bacterial slicks around vents, leaving clean black crescents on stone. It noses through moss for eggs and mites. In winter, when most hungers narrow, it lives close to the warm vents and feeds in the dark on the small lives that wake there out of season.

Its mouth is almost never visible. When opened, it is wider than expected and soft-edged, lined not with obvious teeth but with translucent ridges like the underside of a fungus. It does not bite so much as hold, rasp, strain. A frog taken by it vanishes with very little drama: a tightening of water, a muffled click, a brief widening of steam.

It is not the master of the hollow. Nothing here is master. The place would not permit that.

Herons spear its young if they find them in shallow pools. Stoats raid its warm chambers. Foxes know its scent and avoid the brightest moss after dusk. Hard freezes kill the small ones. Rockfall crushes adults. A vent can scald it; a mud skin can close over it. It survives not by dominance but by agreement with instability. It expects loss. It keeps copies of itself in several places.

There are usually more than one, though rarely together.

The young are pale, almost translucent, with dark organs visible through the skin and little crowns of external filaments trembling behind their heads. They cling to the undersides of moss mats where warm water slides through roots. At that age they look vegetal, like pieces of living liverwort given a pulse. They feed on films, larvae, and the soft rot of drowned leaves. If disturbed, they stiffen and become indistinguishable from mineral slime.

As they grow, they darken. Their filaments withdraw into side-slits. Their backs roughen. Moss spores catch in the texture of their skin and sometimes root there for a season. Older ones carry whole miniature gardens: green stains, orange lichen scales, threadlike fungi, tiny mites traveling the ridges. An elder lying still beside a weeping rock can disappear almost completely, becoming one more wet, dark, breathing surface among many.

Their courtship happens in pressure weather.

When the hollow exhales more strongly, when warm vapor rises hard enough to set the fern fronds shaking, two or three adults may come close to the same trembling pool. They do not call loudly. The frogs are louder. The vents are louder. Instead they pulse their throats against the mud and answer the engine under the ground with smaller engines of their own. The sound is felt before heard: a damp tapping, a subterranean purr, a rhythm almost but not quite matching the basin’s hidden pound.

They circle without touching. They stir the silt into spirals. They open the pale seams along their flanks and release threads into the warm water—scent, seed, memory, something between. By morning there may be a gelatinous lace anchored under a moss shelf, each bead of it holding a dark point. By noon it is hidden under mineral cloud. By the next week, if frost has not lifted the mat or a shrew has not found it, the beads tremble.

They are careful makers, but not builders. They place nothing that looks like a tool. Still, the hollow bears the marks of their maintenance.

They clear some trickles and clog others. They drag fallen sedge over exposed eggs. They press weak mud down with their bodies until it seals a chamber. They open tiny side-channels when a vent grows too hot. They move dead things into warm places where rot will draw insects. They do not farm exactly, but their habits encourage abundance. A pool used by them becomes richer. A moss mat slept under by them grows thicker at the edges. Where they rasp mineral film, new slicks bloom brighter.

So they are not separate from the hollow in the clean way animals often seem separate from landscape. They are one of its processes given a spine.

The birds know them as sudden silence beneath water.

The frogs know them as warmth that sometimes eats.

The voles know them as the reason some tunnels end in black openings and steam.

The fox knows them by smell: iron, peat, cold skin, boiled stone.

If a person came often enough and carefully enough, they might never see one entire. A shoulder slipping through vapor. A line of toes withdrawing under sedge. A low back humped with moss. A throat pulsing beside a vent. An eye open in what had seemed a lump of mud. The mind would keep trying to make it into known animals and failing: otter, newt, eel, root, seal, fungus, stone.

That failure is close to its truth.

It lives there because the hollow is unfinished. It requires a place where categories have not hardened: water that is not pond, ground that is not ground, heat inside cold, weather made below the sky. In a settled valley it would be exposed. In a forest it would be crowded out by roots and shade. In an ordinary marsh it would lack the mineral breath that feeds its small hidden gardens. It belongs only where the earth is still revising itself.

At dusk, when the basin darkens from the walls inward, it becomes most present.

The birds withdraw. The midges remain suspended like faint ash. Steam thickens over the vents. The pounding below seems to slow, or perhaps the ear gives itself over to it. Then one of the moss islands may flex against the current. A black pool may dimple though nothing has fallen. Something long and low may cross from stone to water without splash, bearing on its back a few bright beads of moss like green stars.

It is not a monster in the hollow.

It is the hollow’s resident thought.

A wet, listening animal.

A keeper of vents and intervals.

A creature made to live where the world is cold, breathing heat, and not yet done becoming land.
