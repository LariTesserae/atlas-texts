What lives there is not a people first.

At first it seems to be only weather: a trapped, muscular weather with no horizon, no direction except down and up again. The main inhabitant of the hollow is the falling itself. It occupies the basin the way a beast occupies a den: pressing against every wall, breathing through every crack, worrying at every edge with white teeth. It has moods, habits, hungers. It strikes certain stones more often than others. It keeps some ledges bare and allows others to green. It lifts things from the deep and returns them altered. It does not sleep.

If it has a body, the body is distributed: cloud-ceiling, vertical rain, rebound spray, central plunge, under-mountain throat. If it has a voice, the voice is too large to be called speech. It says one thing continuously until meaning is crushed out of it. Those who come there learn not to listen for words, but for changes in pressure: a thickening, a hollowing, a sudden low hum beneath the roar.

The small plants live there as tolerated witnesses.

They are not gentle alpine flowers. Nothing delicate survives long enough to become beautiful in the ordinary way. The mosses grow like sealed wounds, dark and tight against stone. The lichens spread in ashy maps over the safer faces of rock, so pale they seem less alive than remembered. The grasses are wires. Their roots enter cracks too narrow for fingers and cling with an almost animal stubbornness.

The translucent stems in the sheltered recess are the strangest of the green things. They grow where the falling water cannot quite kill them but where silence never reaches. Each stem is nearly clear, faintly bluish at its base, with a cupped tip that gathers droplets until it bows. When the weight becomes too much, the cup spills and springs upright again.

They appear to be listening.

No one who has watched them for long believes they are merely plants. They tremble differently when the basin hums. Their cupped tips all turn, not toward light, but toward the deep green center. In the clear pauses they stand very still, as if receiving instruction. The people who used to mark the alcove may have called them ears, or mouths, or children of the rain. They are probably none of these, but the place encourages such mistakes.

The insects are white because color would be wasteful there.

They are narrow, jointed, almost transparent, made for surfaces that do not stay still. They skate the films of water near the walls on feet so fine they leave no disturbance. Their bodies are flat and light; their wings, if they have them, are folded close and useless most of the time. A gust of spray can erase them. Yet they return after every violence, emerging from seams in the rock and from the wet undersides of slate.

They feed on mineral bloom, on drowned spores, on the softened skins of larvae washed up from below. Some are born in the falling water itself, or seem to be: egg-pale threads attached to the undersides of stones, pulsing faintly as the current combs over them. They live quickly. A generation may pass between one clear pause and the next.

The birds know this.

The black birds are not residents, not exactly. They are visitors with inherited maps. Their kind has known the notch from before the shelters, perhaps from before the hollow had its present shape. They come singly or in pairs, always silent, always thin from altitude. Their claws are curved for wet stone. Their beaks are narrow and hard, good for prying pale insects from mineral crust and probing the soft clay seams where larvae lodge.

They do not sing because song would be pointless there. Instead they communicate with posture: a tilt of the head, a shiver of wing, a brief opening of the beak that vanishes into the thunder unheard. They know the safe minutes. They know which overhang will shed a blade of ice, which ledge will be under water by evening, which returned stones should not be touched. If the birds abandon the rim all at once, any human who sees it should leave.

There is something in the central plunge.

It is not always there in the same way. Sometimes it is only a pallor moving against the dark green, too slow for a fish, too deliberate for a root. Sometimes it shows a curve like a knuckle or a shoulder. Sometimes a length of it uncoils under the hammered surface and folds back into the throat beneath the basin.

It may be one creature. It may be several. It may be the mountain’s unfinished idea of an animal.

Its flesh, if flesh is the word, is the color of bone soaked in milk. Not white, not pink, but the tender pallor of things that have never needed sun. Its surface looks smooth until the water clears; then faint seams show along it, joint after joint, as if it is assembled from many similar segments. No eyes have been seen. There are openings, perhaps, along the sides: small dark slits that open and close when the water thins. They may be gills. They may be wounds. They may be mouths.

It does not rise fully. That may be wisdom, or size, or incompleteness. The basin is too small for anything truly vast, yet the depth below it suggests the seen portion may be only a limb, a feeler, a pale exploratory part sent upward from chambers farther down. When stones fall into the plunge, it sometimes gathers them. When they return days later, higher than they fell, they carry a slick mineral coating and tiny crescent scratches.

The people who built the shelters knew enough not to name it plainly.

They were not a village, though they may have come from villages below. They were a returning few: water-keepers, weather-herders, oath-breakers, mothers of difficult children, old climbers who had learned the mountain’s bad moods, young ones sent upward to become quiet. They did not live there all year. No human body could bear that saturation for long. They came in seasons when the path opened, when the cloud-lid held, when the birds appeared on the rim facing inward.

Their dwellings were not meant for comfort. They were listening frames, drying racks that never dried, ribs built against impact. A person inside one of those structures would sit wrapped in greased hide and wool, knees drawn up, tools tied to the wrist, hair plastered against the skull. Fire would be a hidden ember in a clay pot. Food would be hard cakes, smoked meat gone wet at the edges, bitter roots, perhaps pale grubs pried from the rock and eaten without ceremony.

They spoke very little in the hollow, not only because speech was useless. Speech there became suspect. Words thrown into the roar did not feel destroyed; they felt taken. So the visitors used hands, shell-ropes, knots, cut marks, touches on the forearm. They had signs for the water thickening, for the eastward twist of the hanging cords, for the sweet taste, for the hum, for a stone returned from below, for a clear pause beginning.

Their faces would have been raw from spray. Their eyes narrowed constantly. Their hands were strong, cracked, often bleeding around the nails. They smelled of wet hide, old smoke, fat, cold stone. They carried little, and everything they carried had a loop, a binding, a hole for cord. A lost tool did not merely vanish there; it might come back changed, and changed tools were dangerous.

They made offerings, though perhaps they would not have used that word.

An obsidian flake left where the water could find it.

A feather bound with thread and wedged above the reach of spray.

A bowl placed empty and later found full of dark grit.

An antler tine laid point-inward toward the plunge.

A fishlike bone too large for any known fish, kept not as a trophy but as a warning.

The cuts in the alcove are not a history in the usual sense. They are attempts to keep count in a place that resists counting. Days of heavy fall. Pauses witnessed. Births below. Deaths above. Names not spoken. Changes in the shape of the basin. The repeated cup-mouth symbol may mean “opening,” or “do not answer,” or “the mountain drinks.” It may mean all three.

If any of those people remain, they are fewer now.

Perhaps they live lower on the slopes, in a settlement hidden among black pines and moraine, and climb only when the water calls through the stone. Perhaps only one family remembers the route. Perhaps the last keeper is old, half-deaf from the roar, with shell weights hanging in her house so she can read their motion in dreams. Perhaps children are told not to go above the stunted shrubs, then are shown the first handhold when they become old enough to keep fear quiet.

The hollow itself has begun to mark them. Those who return often develop a stillness that seems less like calm than saturation. They pause before answering, as if waiting for the noise inside them to settle. Their hearing may be poor, but they sense vibration through their feet and teeth. They dislike dry rooms. They do not speak important names near running water. When it rains in the valleys, they look up at the ridgeline with an expression that is not reverence exactly, but recognition.

There are other residents more difficult to separate from the place.

The returned stones, for instance.

They are not alive by any valley definition, but they behave like things with history. A stone dropped carelessly into the plunge may reappear on a high ledge with its sharpest edges softened not by erosion but by a glassy skin. Some bear holes bored clean through them. Some contain trapped bubbles that shift against gravity. A few are found warm. No keeper takes them home. They are set aside on shelves or built into the bases of shelters, where they knock softly when the basin hums.

The ropes are residents too, after a fashion. Braided fiber, sinew, hair, hide-strip: all altered by years of mineral spray until they stiffen and relax according to weather no outsider can read. The shell weights clatter without being heard. The pierced shells are from far below, from ancient riverbeds or sea-trade or a time when the mountain was not yet high. Their presence in that wet, high basin is an argument with altitude. They belong and do not belong, which is true of everything there.

And under all of it, beneath plant, insect, bird, keeper, pale coil, rope, mark, and stone, there is the youngest resident: the mountain feature becoming itself.

It is not a lake yet. Not a shrine. Not a cave. Not a spring. Not a mouth. It is trying on forms. It borrows inhabitants to test them. It gives plants a ledge, then removes the ledge. It lets people build three shelters, then shifts the water one handspan closer. It raises stones from the plunge and watches what refuses them. It grows a crack and fills it immediately. It makes a pool too deep for its size and places something pale inside it, as if to see whether depth requires a guardian.

So what lives there?

A few hard green things.

A race of white insects thin as spray.

Silent black birds that visit like omens with appetites.

A seasonal human remnant, if they have not vanished, carrying rules instead of maps.

A pale jointed presence in the plunge, unfinished or ancient or both.

And the hollow itself: bright, deafening, newborn, careless, not yet landscape, not yet ruin, alive in the way a wound is alive while it is closing or opening further.
