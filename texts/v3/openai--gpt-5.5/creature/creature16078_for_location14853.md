What lives there is mostly not shaped like a citizen.

The first inhabitants are films.

They lie over the drowned city in skins so thin they seem less like organisms than decisions made by the water. Orange where the heat is rich. White where calcium seeps. Black-green where metals bleed from pipe seams. They gather in the lee of bolts, inside valve mouths, along cracked insulation, on the warm undersides of conduits. They do not wave or flower. They do not make the place gentle. They make it annotated.

They are patient chemistries with appetite. They eat heat, sulfur, iron, electrical leakage, whatever dissolved bitterness the old machines exhale. Around vents they form soft collars and wrinkled lips, trembling constantly in the flow. Around failed cables they thicken into greasy halos, tolerant of current that would stun larger bodies. In the Furnace Sump they are brightest, not beautiful exactly, but emphatic: rust-red veils, yellow crusts, chalk-white lace, each color marking a different bargain with poison.

They are the true natives. Everything else is a visitor, a scavenger, or a mistake.

Small animals live there by being almost absent.

There are pale crustacean things no longer than a fingernail, jointed and translucent, their organs visible as faint threads inside them. They cling to rough concrete and move only between pulses of current. When the pumps begin, they fold flat and vanish into seams. Their claws pick at bacterial mats, softened rubber, drowned paper, the gelatinous edges of decay. They have no color because color is useless here. Their eyes, where they have them, are only cloudy dimples that feel vibration and heat.

There are threadlike worms in the sediment, so fine they look like scratches in the silt until they recoil. They live in the sheltered drifts beneath stairs and inside control booths where the water hardly moves. Their world is a few centimeters wide: mineral dust, bacterial breath, the slow fall of particles from above. A pump surge can erase generations of them and scatter the rest into new boroughs.

There are blind fish, but they do not seem to belong to the city so much as to its accidents. They slip in through broken conduits and stay only if the pressure allows. Their bodies are narrow, silver-gray, underfed, with fins like torn membrane. They travel along heat gradients, tracing invisible walls in the water. Their skulls are soft-looking and blunt, their mouths always testing. They feed on the small crustaceans, on mats lifted by turbulence, on each other when hunger makes the water smaller.

They fear open spaces. They fear the Throat. They fear sudden silence more than noise.

When a pressure gate slams far away, they scatter before the sound arrives, reading the blow through their bones. When a lamp flickers into green electrical bloom, they avoid it, unless starvation has made them careless. Then one may twitch once in the lit water and drift backward, stiffening, before the current takes it into the dark.

There is also the eel.

Not one, perhaps, but the place gives the impression of a single eel because no one sees more than one at a time. Long as a hanging cable, translucent at the fins, dark along the spine, it lives in dead ducts and inspection sleeves, coiling where the water is warm but not scalding. Its head is narrow, almost tool-like. Its mouth opens sideways in a pale seam. It eats fish when it can catch them and bacterial mats when it cannot. It scrapes surfaces with small backward teeth, leaving clean gray streaks through orange growth.

It knows the city better than the fish. It uses suction fields as doors. It waits beside grates when old cycles flush debris from upper tunnels. It can hold itself motionless in a vibration that would exhaust any surface creature, body flexed in exactly the rhythm of the machinery around it. Sometimes it drifts across a lit corridor and becomes indistinguishable from torn insulation until it bends against the current.

It is not intelligent in the human sense, but it has learned the city’s moods: the cough before the valve, the metallic inhale before the gate, the change in warmth that means a plume will turn lethal. It is a memory made of muscle.

The largest thing that enters does not live there. It trespasses.

It comes rarely, from the outer drowned districts, forcing itself through bars wide enough only because rust has made them weak. Nothing sees it clearly. It is known by displacement: silt lifting before it arrives, fish flattening themselves into cracks, crustaceans sealing into their joints, the eel abandoning its duct. Then comes the slow pressure of mass passing through water, a darkness that has weight, a scrape along the grates, a dull bell-sound as something armored or boned knocks metal in the black.

It does not feed delicately. It pushes its head into chambers and vacuums what it can. It tears mats from vents, swallows fish, disturbs sediments that have not moved for years. Then it leaves, or is driven away by pressure cycles, and for days afterward the hidden city tastes of panic: loosened minerals, torn slime, blood so diluted it becomes only information.

But the most numerous inhabitants are not animals.

They are the machines.

Not alive, not dead, not properly separate from the place. They behave like a civic species whose instincts were written long ago and never revised. Pumps breathe. Valves blink and clench. Robotic arms dip and rise in blind feeding gestures. Elevators tremble in their shafts like trapped vertical animals. Warning lights rotate their red attention over empty galleries. Switchboards tick, hum, and fail by degrees.

They do not know they are underwater. Or if they know, they regard it as a condition to be managed: equalize, drain, cool, vent, seal, flush, repeat. Their obedience has become metabolism. Their errors have become weather. The currents that feed the fish and tear away the mats are made by their routines. The warm plumes that nourish the films come from their leaks. The deadly suction fields, the scalding bursts, the electrical halos, the sediment storms—all are symptoms of mechanical life continuing without purpose.

The bacterial films grow on them like thought.

The crustaceans graze their wounds.

The fish use their shadows.

The eel nests in their discarded throats.

In the Assembly Basin, the robotic arms are perhaps the closest thing to priests. They perform the same workless rite over empty tables: descend, clamp, turn, release, rise. The water around them never rests. Their joints are white with mineral scale. Tiny creatures live in the pits of their housings and are shaken loose whenever an arm completes its ancient gesture. The machines do not notice. Their devotion is perfect because it contains no understanding.

In the Throat, life thins almost to nothing. Only the most stubborn films cling to sheltered bolts and the undersides of galleries. Fish avoid the spiral pull unless trapped. Crustaceans live in narrow cracks where the current passes above them like a sky too violent to enter. The Throat’s resident is motion itself: the periodic downward hunger, the invisible turning that makes particles orbit like citizens around a civic center. Anything living there is temporary. Anything that cannot hold fast becomes part of the descent.

In sealed rooms, other inhabitants persist in quieter forms.

Spores sleep in sediment on desks. Bacteria live inside the soaked foam of old chair cushions, inside the grain of swollen doors, under the glaze of ceramic mugs. Faint colonies trace the edges of keyboards and instrument panels where skin oils once dried. These rooms are museums with digestion. Nothing large moves there. The life is archival, reading the last human residues molecule by molecule.

If there are ghosts, they are not figures.

They are routines.

A cafeteria where spoons still gather in one eddy and cups in another. A clinic where sealed cabinets exhale preservatives into the water. A dormitory where dissolved mattresses make dark lint clouds under the ceiling. The animals do not understand these arrangements, but they inherit them. A crustacean colony lives behind the face of a stopped clock. A fish hides in a locker. An eel sleeps where a ventilation duct once breathed air for workers.

The drowned city’s inhabitants are therefore a hierarchy of endurance.

At the bottom: chemistry, patient and nearly immortal.

Above it: films, mats, beads, stains that eat the city’s slow decay.

Above them: small grazers, transparent and furtive.

Above them: blind fish, nervous as sparks.

Above them: the eel, long, remembering, almost architectural.

Beyond them: occasional larger hunger from outside.

And surrounding all of them: the machines, whose broken labor makes the conditions for every other life and destroys those lives with equal indifference.

Nothing there thrives in the cheerful sense. Nothing expands into abundance. To live there is to cling, flatten, wait, read pressure, avoid light, love warmth cautiously, and never trust calm water. The creatures are not masters of the drowned city. They are annotations in its margins.

The city itself is the dominant organism: warm, hollow, deafening, mineral-veined, full of involuntary circulation. Its heart is a pump that does not know for whom it beats. Its breath is bubbles. Its nerves are live cables leaking green into the dark. Its skin is concrete, steel, ceramic, silt. Its smallest thoughts are bacterial. Its dreams are old commands.

What lives there lives because the city has not finished dying.
