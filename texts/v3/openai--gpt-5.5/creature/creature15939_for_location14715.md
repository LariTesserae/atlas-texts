What lives there is not one creature so much as an arrangement of hunger around heat.

The first residents are the smallest: films, veils, dull skins, gray-white fuzzes on black stone. They are bacteria and archaea that do not care for sunlight. They take the vent’s harsh breath—sulfide, hydrogen, methane, dissolved iron—and make body from it. They are the pasture before there is pasture, the invisible crop on which the visible animals depend. They lie over basalt in greasy maps, thicken on mineral lips, bloom inside cracks, and coat the inner throats of chimneys where no grazing mouth can reach.

Everything larger lives either by carrying these microbes, scraping them, stealing those who carry them, or dying into their custody.

The tubeworms are among the most solemn inhabitants. They grow in crowds from seams and ledges, white tubes packed so tightly they seem extruded by the rock itself. They have no ordinary face. No eyes, no mouth for browsing, no patience for the open sea. Their red plumes lift into the flow like exposed blood made ceremonial. Inside them live dense colonies of bacteria, and the worms feed by keeping those inner citizens supplied: sulfide from the vent, oxygen from the sea, carbon from the water. They are animals built around a private furnace. They stand where poison and breath mingle in just the right proportion, each plume trembling as if reading scripture written in temperature.

The shrimp are more restless. Translucent, nervous, almost glass, they hang in the layered water and vanish when the plume thickens. Some have pale backs dusted with microbial growth; some seem to tend themselves, combing and scraping their own bodies for food. Their eyes are reduced or strange, less suited for images than for brightness, heat, and chemical change. They turn as one when the vent coughs. They scatter from sudden heat, then drift back immediately, unable to abandon the mouth that could kill them because it is also the mouth that feeds them.

The crabs are the visible custodians of the place. White, gray, faintly blue at the joints, they stand over the basalt like old workers in a flooded foundry. Their legs touch. Their claws rise into the current. They pick at films, eggs, soft bodies, dead shells, the weak, the unlucky, the newly fallen. They are not cruel; cruelty would be too grand a word. They are precise. A shrimp stunned by heat becomes food before it has quite finished being alive. A dead worm plume is trimmed clean. A soft larva settling in the wrong inch of stone is removed. The crabs make the hollow less messy by eating everything that can be eaten.

The snails are darker, armored things, their shells blackened with iron and sulfide until they look forged rather than grown. They cling along gradients so narrow that a hand laid beside them would cover several climates. Too close to the vent and their tissues cook; too far and the chemistry thins into hunger. They live on the edge of a blade and carry the blade as law. Their shells rasp faintly against mineral crust. Their soft bodies are pale and vulnerable beneath the slag-colored armor, but the armor is convincing. They resemble discarded pieces of the mountain that have learned to move.

There are limpets too, flatter and more patient, arranged like punctuation marks on the stone. Each occupies a chosen temperature, an acidity, a current. They scrape with deliberate, almost mathematical persistence. If the chemistry shifts, they shift by fractions. If the vent dies, they endure a little while on fading films, then thin, loosen, and become part of the floor.

In the cracks live pale eels and eel-like fish, though they are not kings of the hollow. Nothing here is king for long. They coil in basalt clefts where the water is cooler and dart out when the current delivers flesh. Their skins are colorless or faintly pink, their eyes reduced, their mouths always opening and closing in the roar. They know the hollow by vibration. A falling shard, a crab fight, a vent pulse, the panicked beat of shrimp—these are their weather signs.

There are also animals that barely admit to being animals. Clear bells pulsing between chimneys. Gelatinous tongues folded against rock until they unfold and take something. Threadlike hunters stretched across currents, nearly invisible except when mineral snow catches on them. Soft sacks with ciliated rims. Ribbed ribbons that turn in the flow and are gone. They do not dominate the place, but they complicate it, making every open space secretly occupied.

The true resident, though, is the whole congregation.

It is a living border around a wound.

It has no single body, but it behaves like one. When a vent strengthens, the community leans inward. When a chimney clogs, life withdraws from it in rings. When a new crack opens warm and sulfide-rich, larvae arrive with impossible speed, as if the hollow had been expecting the birth. Microbes bloom first. Then grazers. Then tubeworms. Then crabs. Then predators. A bare fracture becomes a neighborhood. A neighborhood becomes a crowded province. A province collapses when the chemistry fails.

Its senses are distributed among thousands of bodies. The worms taste the balance of poison and oxygen. The shrimp feel light, warmth, and metal. The crabs read current through their legs. The snails know the exact border between feeding and burning. The microbes know no landscape except reaction. Together they make an intelligence without thought: a constant adjustment, a turning toward the planet’s breath.

They do not live in the hollow as animals live in a forest or on a reef. They live on terms set by stone before stone has finished becoming stone. Their home can seal shut. It can split open beneath them. It can become too hot, too cold, too acid, too clean. It gives lavishly and then withdraws without warning. Every resident is therefore temporary, but none behave as if temporary. They attach, feed, molt, mate, brood, fight, scrape, settle, and die with the full seriousness of creatures for whom this small bowl is the center of existence.

Their young come from elsewhere, from the dark water beyond the ridge. Larvae drift invisibly over the abyssal plain, small as dust, carrying within them the ability to recognize a particular wound in the earth. Most never find one. They are taken by cold, distance, mouths, emptiness. But some enter the bright column above the drowned summit and feel the chemical signal rising. They descend through blue water into noise. They pass over bare black slopes, then into the crowded hollow where every surface is contested. If they settle correctly, they become citizens of the vent. If they settle poorly, they become food.

There is no peace among them, but there is order.

Not moral order. Chemical order.

The tubeworms cannot move away from their bargains. The snails cannot afford curiosity. The shrimp must keep returning to danger. The crabs prosper by being where others fail. The eels wait because waiting is efficient. The microbes inherit everything.

They are pale because sunlight is not their maker. They are red where blood or plume or inner chemistry must announce itself. They are black where metal has entered shell. They are clear where a body can afford to be nearly water. Their colors are not decorative; they are consequences.

And beneath them, within them, over them, the microbes continue their quiet founding work. They turn poison into flesh. They make the vent edible. They are the hidden mouth of the whole community, eating the mountain’s exhalation and being eaten in turn.

So what lives there is a crowded, mineral-fed society of specialists: worms with internal gardens, shrimp like glass sparks, crabs like pale mechanics, iron-armored snails, blind fish in cracks, soft transparent hunters, and microbial nations spread over fresh basalt.

But more than that, what lives there is a single temporary answer to an opening in the earth.

A brief, dense, cold-water city gathered around heat.

A factory’s congregation.

A chapel’s worth of creatures attending a wound, each one alive because the mountain has not yet closed its mouth.
