What lives there is not a people, not in the usual sense. The plateau has no builders, no fire-carriers, no voices that carry names across open ground. Its inhabitants belong to seepage, compression, patience. They are made to be pressed, wetted, muffled, concealed. They do not dominate the place. They fit into it so completely that most of them seem less like animals than specialized motions of the land.

The first inhabitants are the ones that do not move quickly enough to be recognized as creatures.

Moss lives there in sovereign abundance: gray, copper, bottle-green, almost black; moss like velvet, moss like sponge, moss like drowned fur. Some forms grow in tight hummocks that can bear the weight of a hoof. Others are treacherous, spreading in glossy skins over water and giving way under anything heavier than a bird. Certain mosses glow faintly where mineral seepage feeds them, not with true light, but with a pale metallic saturation, as if they have absorbed moonlight and iron together. They climb stone, bury fallen wood, close wounds in the ground. They are the plateau’s first language, written over everything.

The shrubs and dwarf trees are old beyond their size. They stand knee-high or shoulder-high where, in another country, their kind might have become forests. Their trunks twist upon themselves in black, wet cords. Their needles are small, waxy, blue-green, resinous enough to resist the endless damp. Broad-leaved thickets crouch beside them, glossy and close-packed, holding rain in their cupped leaves until the weight bends them and they pour suddenly onto whatever waits below. Their roots are not deep taproots but gripping hands, braided through peat, lichen, stone cracks, old bones, each root finding some seam of warmth or mineral taste.

The reed-tubes near the vents are stranger. They stand in colonies, jointed and hollow, their stems dark at the base and translucent near the tips. When the underground pressure-beat passes through them, their membranes tremble. Whole stands quiver at once without wind, shivering in rings around a hidden pulse. Midges shelter inside their segments. Small amphibians lay eggs in the warm condensation gathered along their inner walls. When the vents rise toward a flush, the reeds stiffen before anything else reacts, and the grazing animals read them like weather signs.

The flowers are mostly traps or instruments.

Some are small and white, closing at the first touch of hard rain. Some are wax-red cups half-filled with sour water, where drowned insects soften and dissolve. Some are pale, low stars that open only to the piston rhythm below, exposing damp inner surfaces furred with pollen. There are mats of yellow-lipped blossoms that seem like firm ground until they flex underfoot; beneath them is black water, and the flowers are rooted in a floating net of their own dead stems. Their scent is faintly sweet, faintly meatlike, and strongest when fog descends.

Fungi inhabit everything that has stopped resisting.

They grow under bark, in the chambers of fallen trunks, along the damp undersides of rock shelves, and inside the mineral-warmed mouths of vents. Some are broad as shields, tiered in shelves of umber and cream. Some are soft, gray, and ear-shaped, trembling with each drop that strikes them. Others send up slender black fingers through the moss after long rain. A dead animal here does not rot openly; it is received. Beetles enter first, then white threads, then green, then roots. The fungi are not scavengers so much as archivists, translating muscle, hide, antler, feather, and bone into peat-thickness and brighter shrubs.

Among this vegetable crowd live countless small animals.

The amphibians are everywhere. They are the plateau’s nearest thing to a chorus, though even they are subdued. Their bodies are dark and lacquered, brown-black, green-black, sometimes marked with pale mineral flecks along the spine. Some fit inside a leaf-cup. Some are long as a hand and live under wet stones. Their feet are spread wide and soft, their throats glassy. They do not croak in loud air-filling notes; they click, chirp, pulse, or give a single clear tone from deep in moss. After warm vent-rain, they appear in improbable numbers, sitting on stones, on fungal shelves, on the backs of sleeping grazers, their eyes like beads of old amber.

In the flooded root channels are fish without any need for open water. They move through submerged tunnels under mats of sedge, slipping between roots and stones in dim brown light. Their backs imitate drowned bark. Their bellies are pale. Some have small barbels that taste mineral currents; others have flattened heads for pushing through peat-softened passages. During vent flushes they gather in the warm outflows, hanging almost motionless, mouths opening and closing in the red-dark water.

The spring crustaceans are almost ghostly. They live where mineral water comes up from below, blind or nearly blind, white as peeled stems, jointed and delicate. Most of the time they cling to the inside of black stone throats and are never seen. When pressure rises, they are expelled into open pools in brief pale scatterings. Birds take some. Fish take others. Many hook themselves to moss or sink backward into cracks before the cold surface water can still them.

The insects are numerous but restrained by damp. They do not fill the air in swarms for long. They fold themselves under leaves, into reed joints, beneath bark scales, inside spiral cases made of mud and silk. Beetles with sealed wing-covers patrol fallen wood. Thin-legged flies stand on pool surfaces, tasting the tremor of anything beneath. Midges hatch in the warm circles around vents, rising in low smoke-like columns that never climb far before mist presses them down again. There are shell-winged insects that click when they open and close, producing one of the plateau’s quietest but most constant sounds: a dry little mechanism hidden in wet green.

The hoofed animals are the most visible of the larger life.

They are compact, low, and shaggy, built as though the plateau has pressed them downward over generations. Their coats are dense and rain-shedding, dark brown or soot-black, often greened along the flanks by harmless algae and rubbed moss. Their legs are short but immensely careful. The hooves spread slightly when weight comes down, allowing them to cross trembling bog-shelves and saturated peat without sinking too deeply. Calves learn foot placement before they learn speed.

Their horns are not high spears but flattened ridged tools, sweeping back along the skull or flaring beside the face. They use them to scrape mineral crust from rocks, pry wet shrubs aside, and test the firmness of moss before stepping. In winter, they gather around warm reed colonies and steam faintly in the cold air. In fog, a herd may pass close without being seen, announced only by the soft suck of hooves, the scrape of horn on stone, the damp tearing sound of shrub leaves being eaten.

They do not panic easily. Panic kills here. A running animal breaks through surfaces, misjudges pools, plunges into silt. So they freeze, listen, angle their ears toward the reed-tremors and mud-sighs, then move in narrow files along paths remembered by many generations of hooves.

The plateau’s predators have learned the same discipline.

One of them is catlike, though heavier in the shoulder and longer in the tail than a true cat of lower forests. Its coat is water-dark, close, and almost lightless, patterned only where wetness catches the guard hairs. Its paws are broad, furred between the pads, made for moss and mud. It can crouch for hours beside a reed tunnel while rain beads on its whiskers. Those whiskers are long, pale, and sensitive; they read the little air movements under leaves, the breath of frogs, the tremor of fish in flooded channels.

Its ears are small to preserve heat and avoid snagging. Its eyes are large but not bright. It hunts by nearness, by patience, by understanding where an animal must place its next foot. It rarely gives chase. It waits beside certainty.

There are smaller hunters too: eel-bodied mustelids slipping through root water, taking fish and amphibians; blunt-snouted insectivores with spade claws that tunnel through moss cushions; long-legged waders that stalk the shallows in absolute silence, their bills probing not mud but vibration.

The birds are pale because the plateau is dim.

They nest in cavities eroded into cliffs and ravine walls, where the stone is dry only by comparison. Their feathers are gray-white, ash, lichen-cream, sometimes barred with charcoal along the wings. They seldom call. Sound carries strangely here, and needless sound gives too much away. Instead they communicate with posture, with wing-flicks, with the brief exposure of pale throat patches in the fog.

Some are small and mothlike, dropping from cliff hollows to take insects in short silent flights. Others are heavier, round-winged, able to fold themselves through stunted forest and vanish against wet bark. Their talons are narrow and hooked for plucking frogs from moss. A few hunt the hoofed calves, but only when mist and terrain have already separated the weak from the herd.

At the cliff crowns lives a larger pale bird with a face like worn stone. It waits during rain with its back to the weather, eyes half-lidded, receiving the entire plateau as movement: reed tremors, midge-lift, hoof lines, the shudder of a pool over something passing below.

And below, there are the great concealed inhabitants.

No one sees them whole. They are known by the way the landscape yields.

They live in the deepest bogs and under the root mats, in the black cold-water layers warmed here and there by mineral vents. Their bodies are long, broad, and flexible, not fish and not worm, with hides the color of peat water. Some may have limbs reduced to paddles or digging hooks. Some may move by slow muscular waves, pressing through slurry, water, root, and silt. They do not surface unless forced. The plateau above them is their roof, their hunting ground, their camouflage.

When one passes beneath a pool, the surface rises in a smooth dome, holding a bent reflection of gray sky. Sedge parts without snapping. Moss mats lift and settle. Channels open behind it and then close slowly, as though the earth has breathed in. Small animals go still; grazers raise their heads; reed-tubes tremble in the wrong rhythm.

These underbog creatures are not monsters in the theatrical sense. They are too integrated for that. They are old pressures with appetites. They take fish, drowned grazers, amphibian masses, perhaps the softened remains of anything that breaks through floating mats. They may also feed on mineral films and fungal tissue in the warm dark, moving between vents as other animals move between pastures. Their lives are measured in submerged routes and pressure changes. Their young, if they have young, may be mistaken for root-knots or lengths of black waterlogged wood.

The plateau also houses beings too small or too slow to fit ordinary categories.

There are microbial skins on pools, oil-dark and rainbowed, feeding on iron and warmth. There are mineral-loving colonies in the vent throats that stain stone red, green, and silver-white. There are lichens older than herds, growing in coin-sized patches for centuries, accepting rain, fog, bird droppings, and dust. There are root-fungi networks passing signals through the saturated ground, not thoughts but chemical urgencies: warmth here, rot here, salt here, death here, flowering now.

In a sense, the true resident is the exchange between all of them.

The hoofed grazers carry seeds in their wet coats and mud-packed hooves. The catlike hunters keep the herds from overusing the safe paths. The pale birds transfer cliff minerals to forest floors in their droppings. The amphibians eat the insects that breed in flower-cups, then are eaten by birds, fish, and root-water hunters. The underbog giants open channels that later become streams. The vents warm the reed colonies; the reeds shelter eggs; the eggs feed beetles; beetles open carcasses; fungi inherit them; moss closes over the work.

Nothing here lives apart. Every creature is partially habitat for another creature. Backs grow algae. Horns carry lichen in their grooves. Bird nests sprout fern spores. Dead wood becomes fungal architecture before it becomes soil. Even the predators, when they sleep in root hollows, warm the dens where insects and amphibians later gather.

If there is an intelligence here, it is distributed and damp. It does not plan. It responds. It thickens where water lingers, retreats where vents whiten the stone, blooms after death, seals after disturbance, listens through reed membranes and hoof pauses and whisker-touches. The inhabitants do not conquer the plateau because conquest would require separation from it, and nothing here remains separate for long.

They are a wet, quiet, pressure-taught population: moss-cities, vent reeds, mineral fungi, glass-throated amphibians, bark-backed fish, ghost crustaceans, shaggy careful grazers, silent pale birds, dark patient hunters, and the great unseen bodies moving under the bog.

They live as the plateau lives: slowly, densely, with no wasted motion, manufacturing themselves out of cloud, stone, cold water, and time.
