What lives there has learned to be narrow.

The people of the crack are long-boned, compact in the chest, economical in gesture. They move as if every motion has been measured against a railing, a cable, a ledge without a guard. Even in sleep their hands curl slightly, ready to grip. Outsiders call them thin, but that is not quite right. They are dense people, all tendon and habit, bodies made for stairs, chains, ladders, hot grates, and sudden drafts.

Their faces are usually half-hidden. They wear wrapped mouth-cloths stiffened with salt and fine metal dust, goggles smoked amber or blue, caps with flaps that seal the ears. When uncovered, their skin has the matte look of fired clay. It cracks at the knuckles and around the lips. Their eyelashes are pale with powder. Many have permanent gray crescents beneath the eyes from years of squinting through mineral haze.

They speak quietly. Not timidly—nothing about them is timid—but with the discipline of people who know a careless sound can travel farther than intended. Their voices are dry, close, often roughened by alkali. In conversation they lean near, not from affection necessarily, but because that is how speech survives in the pocket. A sentence spoken too openly may come back from the upper walls changed in tone, as if mocked by the mountain.

They are called the cleftborn, though not all were born there. Some came from the salt flats, from failed caravans, from ruined mines, from debtor ports beyond the desert. But after a few seasons inside the spire, everyone becomes alike in certain ways. They begin to check cables with their fingertips before trusting their eyes. They learn to taste a leak in the air. They stop expecting horizons. They look upward before stepping into any open place.

Children of the crack learn height before they learn weather. They can name every lift-chain by its sound, every bridge by the rhythm of its flex. They play on maintenance platforms, sliding polished slag marbles through etched grooves while dust falls past them into darkness. Their games are full of rules about pressure, load, and silence. One child closes their eyes and listens through a pipe; another drops a pebble into a chute and must tell, from the delay and the echo, whether the bin below is empty, half-full, or jammed.

They are fearless in the way of creatures raised among danger that has been made familiar. A child may scamper across a cable-rib above two hundred feet of haze, but will freeze in terror if someone uncaps a water flask in the open.

Water is not merely precious there. It is indecent.

To expose it is to invite corrosion, swelling, rot, electrical failure, fungal bloom, chemical betrayal. Water belongs in sealed bulbs, ceramic arteries, locked infirmary drawers, emergency boilers, and the bodies of the living. It is not poured. It is not splashed. It is not used for washing where vapor might escape. The cleftborn clean themselves with powdered salts, oil-scrapers, abrasive cloths, and slow patience. Tears are wiped away quickly. Spilled blood is smothered with white mineral dust before it can steam.

They do not say “wet” casually. They say softened, compromised, opened.

Their meals are dense and dry: salt-fat cakes, pressed meal, smoked root imported from lower climates and desiccated until it snaps, mineral broth served so hot and thick that little vapor escapes. The rich inhale stimulant vapors through brass masks in rooms sealed by rubber gaskets. The poor chew bitter pellets that stain the tongue blue and keep hunger folded small for a shift.

Their homes cling to the walls like tool nests. A family room may be no wider than a freight pallet, curtained with patched fabric and insulated by slag-wool panels. Beds are hammocks or shelves with raised lips, because a sleeper must not roll. Pots lock into brackets. Cups have lids and chains. Every object has a hook, peg, loop, clasp, or magnet. Nothing is trusted simply to remain where it is placed.

They decorate sparingly, but not without tenderness. A polished copper vein left exposed in a wall. A row of cracked pressure gauges arranged around a tiny lamp. A child’s first worn-out breathing cloth, folded and lacquered flat. Bells from dead courier beasts. A piece of exterior sky-glass, too bright to look at directly, kept wrapped in black felt and shown to guests as if it were a saint’s bone.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is mostly maintenance.

They pray by tightening bolts. They mourn by keeping a dead person’s tools in working order. They bless a newborn by touching its brow with salt, soot, and warm machine oil. Their shrines are made from failed instruments: gauges whose needles point forever to impossible pressure, cracked voltmeters, fused switches, broken regulators. These are not symbols of defeat. To them a failed instrument has touched the true conditions of the mountain and been unable to lie about it.

They believe the spire listens through load-bearing members.

Old workers sit with their backs to warm ducts and interpret tremors. Some are merely skilled mechanics. Some are something stranger. The best duct-listeners can hear a bearing souring three levels below, a cable taking too much strain, a pocket of gas opening behind fresh-cut stone. They are treated with the respect given elsewhere to physicians or judges. Their palms are broad and scarred from years of touching hot metal. They speak in brief diagnoses.

“Hungry.”

“Too tight.”

“Salt behind it.”

“Shut her before she sings.”

The guilds rule most things.

There are the Cutters, who expand the city by biting farther into the mountain. They are pale with stone dust and carry wedge charges, ceramic drills, and maps no one else can read. They know which veins are safe, which are profitable, and which should be walled over with no questions asked.

There are the Chainwrights, whose authority extends over lifts, hoists, cranes, and suspension bridges. They dress in blackened leather, carry testing hammers, and can halt traffic across half the city by hanging a red weight from a cable.

There are the Lungkeepers, who tend the soft-bodied filter creatures near the exhaust vents. They are gentle in a place not famous for gentleness. Their charges hang in cages of brass mesh: pale, folded animals like enormous sleeping throats, slowly pulsing as factory breath passes through them. The Lungkeepers feed them soot, alkali mist, trace oils, and powdered compounds. They stroke them with hooked brushes, clean their membranes, and sing under their breath in tones too low to carry.

There are the Foundry Houses below, whose members wear glass faceplates and never answer direct questions. They return from pressure-sealed shifts smelling of ozone, hot salt, and something sharp enough to make the teeth ache. Their eyebrows are often gone. Their gloves are thick and stiff, crusted with residues that glow faintly in the dark. Everyone depends on them. Everyone distrusts them.

There are the Water Wardens, few and unpopular, who inspect seals, ration emergency reserves, and punish open moisture. Their symbol is a closed circle crossed by three downward lines. Their ledgers are more feared than knives.

And there are the Signalers, who keep lamps, flags, shutters, bells, and coded sparks from descending into confusion. They hate the black-winged scavengers with a personal bitterness, because the birds crowd warm lights, steal bright wire, and learn patterns faster than they ought to.

The animals are not pets exactly, though many are loved.

The little pale pipe-creatures live in the margins of every level. They have triangular heads, sealed nostrils, and feet split into four gripping pads. Their tails are flat and flexible, useful for balance along pipes. Their eyes are bead-small and milky, but they smell heat, salt, and insulation with extraordinary precision. They are pests when hungry, omens when absent. If they flee a wall all at once, people follow.

The black-winged scavengers are cleverer and worse. Their wings look like folded burnt paper, thin and veined, nearly silent except for a dry clicking at the joints. They nest in transformer housings and steal shreds of cloth, wire, hair, and foil. They remember faces. They can be bribed with fat-salt pellets but not trusted afterward. Their long tails act like rudders among cables, and they pass through the city vertically as easily as smoke.

Blind soot beetles move in disciplined streams along heated rails. Their backs are dull iron-gray, their undersides white. They graze on mineral films and combustion residue, polishing paths as they go. Children sometimes divert them with warm stones, making temporary living circuits. Engineers encourage them in some ducts and exterminate them in others.

The burden beasts are solemn, indispensable, and almost holy. Low to the ground, plated across the shoulders, they have wide splayed hooves that grip metal stairs and stone ledges. Their skulls slope like shovel heads. Their eyes are set deep behind bony ridges, protected from grit. They can carry loads where machines cannot fit: ore baskets, sealed cylinders, ceramic pipe sections, sleeping children, the injured. They do not bray or bellow. When distressed, they grind their teeth in a slow rhythm like a failing mill.

Their keepers know each beast’s internal economy with precision: how much salt-cake, how much fat, how long between sealed solvent draughts, how warm the stall must be to keep the joints from stiffening. A burden beast dying of fluid loss is treated with more urgency than a broken crane.

The courier animals are another matter: hook-clawed, narrow, restless, bred for span-crossing and vertical routes no human should attempt in high wind. They wear little brass bells and message tubes under the throat. Their bells hardly ring in the thin air; they tremble instead, a faint metallic shiver. They are vain creatures. They enjoy being watched. They will take a dangerous route if it gives them an audience.

But beneath the people, beneath the guilds and beasts and scavengers, something else lives there too.

It is not the gray crusts, though those may be alive. They spread around heat leaks and old electrical fields in scalloped skins, ash-colored and faintly iridescent when scraped. They grow where no ordinary life should bother trying. Some catalyst-scrapers swear the crusts retreat from certain tools. Others insist this is superstition caused by fatigue and bad goggles.

It is not the thread-mats in abandoned ducts, though those tremble when charged air passes and sometimes hum in weather shifts. They hang like translucent nerves. No one admits to hearing words in them.

The larger life is the city itself.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. The crack behaves like an organism that has recruited people, animals, machines, salts, winds, and stone into one dry body. Its bridges are ribs. Its cables are tendons. Its ducts are intestines and throats. Its lamps are dull eyes opening in bands. It feeds on ore and heat and labor. It exhales sorted gases. It sheds powder. It grows new scaffolds over old wounds. It seals off infected chambers. It keeps what is useful and drops the rest into bins below.

The cleftborn know this, though they rarely say it plainly. They do not believe they own the mountain. They believe they are permitted to remain useful inside it.

So what lives there is a people becoming mineral, animals becoming mechanical, machines becoming half-domestic, and a mountain-spire learning the habits of a city.

They are dry, quiet, industrious, superstitious in practical ways, tender toward pressure seals, merciless toward waste, and intimate with height. They fear softness more than darkness. They trust vibration more than speech. They raise children on catwalks, court one another beside furnace vents, bury their dead as clean salts and named tools, and listen always for the moment when the crack shifts in its sleep.
