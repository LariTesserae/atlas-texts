What lives there is small, because the room permits nothing large for long.

The chief inhabitants are not the fish or the eels, though those are numerous; not the shrimp, though they cover the warm vents in trembling colonies; not even the orange-flowered plants that open by the clock of the pumps. The true residents are a kind of amphibious creature that has become almost indistinguishable from the chamber’s rhythms.

They are no longer than a human hand.

At first they look like scraps of wet machinery come loose from the wall: pale gray, copper-stained, narrow-bodied, with flattened limbs and long toes splayed for gripping slick enamel. Their backs are mottled in the exact colors of the room—green algae, old bronze, white mineral crust, blackened gasket-rubber. When still, they vanish against ladders and valve wheels. Only their throats give them away, pulsing quickly in the wet air.

Their skin is thin and glassy over the belly. In bright water you can see the slow movement of organs inside them, dark threads and soft golden blots. Their bones have taken on a faint blue tint from the copper in the water. Along their flanks run little pressure-sensitive hairs, finer than moss filaments, which shiver whenever the pumps change pace. They do not hear the room so much as feel it arriving through their entire bodies.

They live in the ledges between water and air.

During the nine-minute surge they flatten themselves against grates and let the current comb over them. During the thirty-two-minute rain they climb higher, spreading their toes on the wet beams, blinking silver membranes across their eyes. When the chamber partially drains, they become frantic and purposeful, running along exposed terraces to gather stranded eggs, insect husks, and trapped shrimp before the crabs find them.

They are hunters, but not cruel ones. They wait under orange flowers when the light shifts west, mouths slightly open, catching moths that fall drunk with pollen. They hang upside down beneath pipe lips and snatch larvae from the current. They pry coin-sized crabs out of rivet holes with delicate fingers. They follow eels at a respectful distance, stealing scraps from the disturbance an eel leaves behind.

They also garden, though not by intention as a person would understand it.

Their droppings seed the moss towers. Their wet bodies carry spores from one grate-island to another. They scrape away suffocating algae from the bases of certain plants because those plants attract insects. They nibble dead fern tissue but leave the new curls. Over generations, the plants that tolerated them prospered, and the plants that did not vanished. So the chamber’s careful spacing, its strange refusal to become a choking mass of green, is partly their work: a maintenance habit evolved into appetite.

Their nests are built in old gauge housings, in hollow handles, in the mouths of pipes that no longer draw strongly. They line them with softened gasket fiber, shed fern stems, moth wings, and strands of translucent algae. The eggs are laid in clusters of twelve or twenty, each egg clear as a bubble and stuck to metal by a tough amber glue. The young hatch as bright, tadpole-like things with large heads and black eyes. For their first weeks they live upside down under floating leaves, feeding on bacterial films and the smaller glowing larvae. Later their limbs unfold, their tails shorten, and they climb for the first time into the hot, thin air under the ceiling.

They are most active when the turbine begins to whine.

That sound excites them. Before it fully rises, they already know it is coming. One will lift its head, then another, until the rails and ledges are dotted with small alert bodies. When the vibration reaches its peak, they begin to call.

The call is not loud. The chamber is too loud for that to matter. It is a clicking felt more than heard: tooth against tooth, throat sac against palate, toe bones tapping enamel. In the water it becomes a silver tick through the rails and grates. They answer one another across the room by vibration. A colony spread over many islands of metal can speak through the old structure itself.

Their intelligence is local and bodily. They know the pump cycles with absolute precision. They know which pipe warms first, which flowers open late, which vent kills, which drain takes the unwary. They know the chamber in the way a hand knows its own palm. If removed from it, they would seem stupid, panicked, incomplete. Inside it, they are exquisitely competent.

They remember without stories.

A creature born near the western wall will avoid a certain pale drain, though it has never seen anything taken by it. A mother will move her eggs higher a day before the long draining begins. Males competing for a nesting gauge do not fight during the rain-overload; they pause, cling side by side, and resume when the downpour slackens. Their lives are tuned to instructions no longer written anywhere except in water pressure, mineral taste, and inherited fear.

The oldest among them grow dark and heavy-headed. Their backs become furred with harmless green film. Snails graze on them while they sleep, and they permit it. These elders stop hunting insects and instead haunt the warmest vents, eating biofilm and soft dead matter. When they die, they usually crawl deep into pipe shadows. Their bodies feed the orange bacteria, then the shrimp, then the fish, then the plants rooted in powdered metal. Nothing leaves the room.

They have no concept of outside.

Thin bright air beyond the tower, clouds below, the impossible drop, the dead world of human scale—none of it exists for them. Their universe is bounded by enamel, condensation, warmth, light column, current. The sky is a ceiling that rains. Weather is machinery. Stone is unknown. Soil is a kind of rust.

And yet they are not primitive in any simple sense. They are perfectly modern to their world: citizens of a built ecology so old it has become native. Their bodies are answers to pipes, pumps, grates, and forgotten maintenance schedules. Their souls, if they have them, are shaped like little rooms full of warm water.

At rest, one may sit on a valve wheel with its toes folded neatly beneath it, orange pollen dusting its snout, throat flickering in the wet light. Below it fish flash through ladder rungs. Above it moths cling to sweating beams. Around it the ancient chamber knocks, rains, exhales, refills.

It blinks once.

The pump-cycle changes.

It moves before anything else does.
