What lives there is not a kingdom of plants and animals, but a population of pressures given bodies.

The oldest inhabitants are the films.

They are spread so thinly over stone that at first they seem like weathering, oil, tarnish, or the faint discoloration left by vanished water. They are gray, pewter, black-blue, sometimes with a color like bruised silver when the dim light lies across them. They do not grow upward. They thicken by degrees, molecule by molecule, where seepage brings iron, salts, sulfur, and heat. They are neither moss nor mold nor algae, but a flatter, older kind of life: patient chemistry with an appetite. They taste the stone continuously. They remember the paths of water. They can survive centuries sealed under ice as a dull stain, then awaken when a single warm crack breathes beneath them.

In places where the stone hums, the films arrange themselves in rings and branching circuits, like frost patterns or the diagrams of unknown machines. They may be the closest thing the basin has to vegetation, except they do not soften anything. They make the rock slicker, darker, more articulate. They are pasture, archive, skin.

Upon them feed the small scrapers.

These move in herds low enough to be mistaken for loose plates of shell sliding over the flats. Each has a long, flattened body armored in overlapping black or translucent scales, with many short legs tucked beneath it. Their feet strike the basalt in exact, ticking rhythms, and a herd crossing stone sounds like rain made of bone. They have no heads in the expected sense. The front of each body is a blunt grinding mouth, a wet rasp of mineral teeth, constantly planing the living tarnish from the rock.

They are nearly blind, but they do not need sight. They read temperature with their bellies, pressure with their legs, metal with their mouths. When the ground knocks below them, they turn as one, not because they hear it, but because the knock passes through their shells and briefly makes them the same animal. Their translucent young travel inside the adults’ armor plates for a time, visible as dim beads, like trapped bubbles in ice.

They migrate across distances that cannot be trusted. A herd may begin crossing a shelf at dawn and arrive yesterday in a neighboring pool. They accept this without alarm. Their tracks often precede them.

Beneath the scrapers live the seam-things.

They occupy cracks too narrow for a finger, old fractures, the undersides of mineral shelves, the interfaces between black ice and black stone. Some are wormlike, but jointed; some are clusters of hinged needles; some resemble pale hands without wrists, gripping and ungripping in the dark. They are the basin’s hidden population, more numerous than anything visible above. When pressure changes, they pour out briefly in pale streams, drinking mist, mating, shedding skins, then withdrawing before the freezing drift takes them.

Many of them filter the air itself. They lift feathery fans from fissures, not to catch spores or pollen—there is none—but to strain condensation, mineral dust, and sound. Their tissues vibrate with the buried engines. A deep clank passes through them and becomes heat. A hiss from a vent becomes stored motion. Some species feed only during the great metallic screams, opening their bodies wide and shuddering as if in ecstasy or terror. Afterward they leave behind strings of clear eggs glued to the cold stone, each egg containing a speck that pulses in time with distant machinery.

There are hunters made for this crowding.

The common low hunters are slick-backed, long-jawed creatures that travel half in water and half in reflection. Their hides are dark, seal-like, but plated under the skin, with raised knots of frost along the spine. They carry their skulls close to the ground, the lower jaw spread wide and sensitive. Through that jaw they listen to footfalls, cracking ice, the rasp of herds, the convulsions of buried chambers. Their eyes are small and often filmed over; some have none at all.

When they strike, they do not chase. They calculate the nearest weakness in the moment and enter it.

A scraper herd may scatter into three possible directions, and the hunter lunges into the one that will become real. Sometimes it misjudges and bites only the reflection of prey; sometimes this is enough. Their mouths are built with backward-hooked cartilage plates that close like a trapdoor. They swallow by folding the front of the body inward, a violent mechanical motion that seems less like eating than like a part of the landscape shutting.

Older hunters grow broad and patient. They lie in brine pools until mineral crust forms over their backs and small animals begin grazing upon them. Their nostril slits open and close just above the water. Their hearts beat slowly enough that ice sometimes forms between beats. When prey comes near, the whole pool may leap.

There are also the tall walkers.

They are solemn, stilt-legged beings with bodies suspended high above the freezing wet, though “high” here means only the height of a child or a crouched adult. Their legs are pale and many-jointed, delicate-looking but immensely strong, ending in wide, sensitive pads that distribute weight across treacherous skins of water. Their torsos are small, dark, and lantern-shaped, armored with translucent ribs. Beneath them hang clusters of bellows sacs, gray-white and veined, inflating and collapsing with a tired wheeze.

They gather around vents in orderly lines, each waiting its turn to stand over warmth. No one teaches them this. They arrive already knowing the etiquette of exhalations. A walker that cuts the queue may be ignored for years, which among them is a serious punishment: warmth is not merely comfort, but digestion, fertility, memory. Their bellows sacs draw mineral vapor through internal lattices where dissolved metals condense into bright grains. These grains are later deposited in cracks as offerings, waste, or eggs; the distinction is uncertain.

They are not social in the familiar sense, yet they acknowledge one another through stance. A raised rear leg means warning. Lowered bellows mean mourning. To turn all legs inward and become briefly cage-shaped means that something in the sky is wrong. When one loses its reflection to a hungry seam or mirror-pool, the others make space around it thereafter. The survivor walks lighter, less decisive, as if part of its weight has been assigned elsewhere.

Some inhabitants live almost entirely in reflection.

They are seen in puddles at the edges of sight: thin-backed animals crossing impossible shorelines, pale swimmers moving under stone that is dry to the touch, small black birds that have no bodies above the water. They are not illusions, because they leave consequences. A reflection-grazer may eat the mirrored version of a mineral film, and the actual film will pale. A reflected predator may strike, and a real animal will stumble with a wound on the side no tooth reached.

These mirror-fauna obey angles rather than distances. They approach when watched indirectly. They retreat when named by attention. Their young develop in shining surfaces: ice lenses, polished basalt, the eyes of dying animals. During the freezing drift, many are trapped in layers of transparency, swimming through different moments stacked one atop another. When the thaw comes, some emerge on the wrong side of the surface, gasping in the air, rapidly becoming stone.

There are sound-eaters.

They look less like animals than like discarded instruments: ribbed fans, folded membranes, hollow cones anchored to rock by muscular roots. During quiet periods they remain shut, resembling mineral growths or broken shells. When the buried machinery begins its cycle, they open. Their membranes tremble visibly. Low booms travel through them in waves; metallic clanks strike sparks in their nerves. They turn vibration into warmth, movement, mating color, and a slow communal awareness.

A colony of sound-eaters near a loud vent may think, but not continuously. Its mind exists only during certain rhythms: boom, hiss, clank, groan. Between pulses it dissolves into separate appetites. While thinking, it may influence nearby creatures, encouraging scrapers to graze in spirals or predators to pause before striking. Not from kindness. It arranges traffic so that sound travels cleanly through the bodies around it.

Their offspring are released as nearly invisible tremors. These pass through stone until they find a suitable hollow, where they condense into flesh.

In deeper pools live the pressure-bodies.

They are rarely seen whole. A rim may be only ankle-high, but the center descends into vertical blackness, and from that depth come limbs, mouths, pale feelers, the slow turning of something larger than the pool should contain. These creatures are adapted to impossible interiors. They are compressed, folded, perhaps distributed through multiple basins at once. One may raise a single white appendage from a shallow puddle while the rest of it turns miles below, listening to itself through the crust.

They feed on those who break the surface tension unwisely, but also on drowned sound, mineral heat, and the downward fall of cold. When the basin screams and all the pools lift convex above their rims, the pressure-bodies rise closest to the world. Their outlines press against the water from beneath like faces against dark glass. Smaller animals freeze at such times not merely from fear, but because movement gives the pressure-bodies a shape to follow.

The dead do not vanish here, so the dead also live in a way.

Bodies become mineral husks, articulated deposits, polished shells threaded with salts and metal. The weather cleans them instead of rotting them. Chemistry enters where warmth cannot. A fallen scraper becomes a row of bright plates fused to stone. A hunter’s jaw remains open for centuries, collecting rime until tiny seam-things nest in the hinge. The legs of dead walkers stand long after the suspended body has been eaten away, pale tripods marking vent-lines like abandoned measuring instruments.

Some mineral husks continue to move during pressure changes. Not resurrected—nothing so simple—but reanimated by swelling ice, expanding vapor, or the tiny industry of creatures inside them. A field of old shells may clatter in chorus when the ground booms below, answering like bones remembering muscles.

There are eggs everywhere.

In cracks. Under ice. Inside mineral frost. Glued to the undersides of stones. Suspended in droplets of condensation that never quite fall. Some are soft and clear; others are armored nodules indistinguishable from pebbles. Many do not hatch after a duration, but after a condition: a certain sequence of knocks, a reversal of shallow water, the appearance of two skies in one pool, the loss of a reflection nearby. Embryos wait without impatience. Development may pause for centuries, accelerate backward, or complete itself before fertilization has occurred.

Life here is inherited from waiting.

Nothing seems new because birth is not an arrival but a re-entry into an old agreement. The creatures come prepared for cold, pressure, paradox, abrasion, and dimness. They do not explore the basin as a frontier. They occupy it as if remembering it.

And beneath all of them, perhaps, lives the largest resident: the laboring substructure itself.

Not a machine, not an animal, not a mind in any clean category. It turns, knocks, vents, floods, withdraws. It opens valves of stone and closes them. It circulates trapped sea, heat, pressure, mineral hunger. The fauna listen to it as weather, ancestor, predator, clock, and god. Their migrations answer its rhythms. Their eggs wait for its signals. Their bodies echo its mechanics: jaws like clamps, bellows like pistons, legs like calipers, shells like plates from some drowned factory.

If it is alive, it is too deep and too old to need a face.

So the basin is inhabited by a cold, crowded parliament of flattened things: grazers of iron skin, listeners with membrane-ribs, hunters that strike through angles, stilted vent-pilgrims, larvae in the seams, pressure-beasts in shallow abysses, reflections with teeth, and patient films spread over stone like the first draft of life.

They do not green the world. They do not soften it. They make it busier, stranger, more articulate.

They live low. They live slick. They live by pressure, mineral, vibration, and the unreliable consent of reality.
