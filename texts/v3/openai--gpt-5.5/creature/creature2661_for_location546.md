What lives there is not a creature in the ordinary sense. Not at first.

At first it seems there are only the small survivals: the ribbon-growths, the bladder-plants, the blind folding things, the soft black snails under the pale arches. But after long watching, after the mind has been battered loose from ordinary scale by the sound, it becomes difficult to believe these are separate lives. They feel more like attempts. Test marks. The hollow practicing the idea of inhabitants.

The true resident is the basin itself, or something being born through it.

It is young enough that it has not yet decided where its body ends. Sometimes its body is the water. Sometimes it is the basalt ribs and the glowing cracks. Sometimes it is the circular rain-wall, flexing like a pupil. Sometimes it is the pale fragments of impossible houses humming under pressure, as if bone has been taught to remember architecture before flesh has been invented.

It does not think quickly. It thinks in surges, tremors, rearrangements. A thought may appear as a new ledge of black rock steaming in the dim water. A hesitation may become a passage that narrows and vanishes. Curiosity may arrive as a blind cartilage-creature unfolding from between stones, tasting the thunder with its filament crown. Memory may come as a doorway crusted with barnacles older than the mountain, set down where no doorway was a moment before.

The hollow’s life is distributed among its failures.

The dark ribbon-plants are among its simplest impulses. They cling wherever the current gives them permission, hairlike and stubborn, streaming sideways in the warm flood. They do not grow toward light so much as toward rhythm. Their roots grip the new basalt with a delicacy that seems impossible on such sharp stone. When the basin shudders, they flatten themselves and listen. When mineral milk pours from a vent, they darken, drink, and lose whole lengths of themselves without complaint.

The bladder-plants are more nervous. Each one holds a small fever of gas in its translucent leaves. Disturb one and it glows with a faint bruised green, a pulse of irritation, a private lantern quickly regretted. They anchor in cracks near warmth. They seem to feed on the mountain’s remembered fire. In the wrong current they tear loose and drift upward, downward, or otherwise, shining weakly until they are taken into some throat in the rock.

The copper moss in the air-bubble chamber is different. It is the nearest thing in the hollow to patience. It grows where water becomes almost air and sound becomes round enough to endure. It spreads in seams and islands across the central fang, then stops at invisible borders. It knows laws the rest of the place has not finished making. If anything here keeps a record, it is the moss: tremor by tremor, tide by impossible tide, it oxidizes the basin’s confusion into a low metallic green.

The blind flat animals are not fish. They have no allegiance to water as fish do. They are folded motions, pale or gray or cartilage-clear, shaped like shards shaved from some larger organism. Their bodies bend around force rather than resisting it. They pass through cracks too narrow for them and emerge wider than before. Their filament crowns tremble constantly, reading the huge architecture of noise. They know the waterfall as others might know sunlight. They know basalt by its pitch, vents by their fever, arches by their buried note.

They eat what the water loosens: mineral films, soft new growth, the skins of vents, perhaps the first fragile forms of other creatures before those forms become sure of themselves. They are preyed upon mostly by accident. A pressure change folds them too completely. A fissure inhales them. A corridor revises itself while they are inside. Yet they return, or versions of them return, slipping between stones with the resigned grace of recurring doubts.

The black snails are older in manner, if not in fact. They adhere to the undersides of the pale ruin-fragments, clustering where the hum is deepest. Their shells look soft but are not; they are faceted, layered, volcanic, each one like a tiny cooled catastrophe. They rasp at the white films that grow and peel from the arches. Their bodies are almost all foot and listening. They move so slowly that the place changes around them faster than they can cross it, and perhaps this is why they survive. The hollow forgets to kill what does not insist on being present.

Sometimes there are eggs.

They appear without parents, gathered in cups of broken stair or nested in the elbow of a basalt ridge. Pale, trembling, opalescent. They quiver as if something inside is deciding between pulse and mineral. Most become pebbles. A few become snails. A few dissolve into warm threads that root themselves as plants. Once in a long while, one opens into a mouth-shaped bubble of air, which rises through the water against no clear direction and vanishes into the rain-wall.

The eggs are not reproduction exactly. They are proposals.

The pale thing beyond the waterfall is one such proposal that has lasted.

It circles, or seems to. It is only ever seen through the descending wall: a flank without scale, a length without visible head, a jointless passing whiteness that bends too slowly for any current. Sometimes it appears outside the basin. Sometimes, impossibly, its shadow crosses the rocks within. The smaller creatures still when it passes, though whether from fear or recognition is unclear. It may be an animal. It may be the rain-wall’s own muscle. It may be the future shoreline of the basin, swimming around itself until the world agrees to hold it.

If it feeds, it feeds on boundary. Its body presses against the membrane of falling sky and leaves brief thinning places behind it, through which other weathers show: desert, snowfield, red-star night, the empty smoking hollow before drowning, the green version full of trees. It noses at these possibilities like a calf at milk. It does not enter because entrance is too simple a motion for it. It arrives only as a partial consequence.

And then there are the structures.

They may be alive in the slowest and most embarrassed way.

The pale lintels, thresholds, stair-stubs, and ribbed arches are not ruins of a people, or not only that. They hum when struck by current. Their notes differ, but they are tuned to one another beneath the roar, a chord too deep for ears and too intimate for stone. Mineral films grow on them in hours. Snails graze them. The water abrades them, buries them, exposes them, moves them elsewhere. Still they persist in resembling intention.

They are the hollow’s bones dreaming of builders.

A doorway with no wall does not open anywhere, but creatures avoid crossing it unless driven. A stairway of six steps ending in open water gathers eggs more often than any natural shelf. A three-sided chamber can hold a pocket of colder current for days, then release it in a single exhalation that changes the color of nearby moss. The arches collect sound. The thresholds collect arrivals. Sometimes a fragment vanishes during a tremor and returns wearing age it did not have before.

They may be larvae of habitation: not houses made by inhabitants, but inhabitants trying to become houses.

The central fang is the nearest thing the resident has to a head.

Around its tip, in the inverted chamber of vapor and stone, the hollow pauses just enough to perceive itself. The copper moss there, the trembling liquid ceiling, the rounded thunder, the view of the pale fragments below like an unfinished drowned village—all of it forms a kind of organ. Not an eye, because sight is unreliable here. Not an ear, because everything is hearing. Perhaps a lung. Perhaps a thought-bubble.

Anything that reaches that chamber feels watched, though there may be nothing watching. The sensation is less like being seen than being included in a calculation the place has only just learned to make. A body standing there becomes one more unstable fact: weight, warmth, salt, breath, fear, memory. The basin takes these in without malice. It may answer with a shudder. It may answer by aligning the ruins into a street beneath the water. It may answer by making the rain fall sideways for three breaths.

The resident does not understand guests as separate from weather.

It does not hunt. It incorporates.

A fallen stone becomes punctuation. A plant becomes a green attempt. A snail becomes a black thought moving slowly under bone. A visiting animal would become pressure, disturbance, residue, perhaps eventually architecture. Speech would become bubbles and be torn apart, but the torn bubbles might seed some new form: a ring of white film, a clutch of pebbled eggs, a brief corridor where a voice-shaped current moves against the rest.

What lives there, then, is a larval world.

A mountain-born organism too large to be called an animal and too immediate to be called a spirit. A flooded intelligence without intention. A newborn law-field clotting around heat, water, thunder, and broken forms of shelter. Its creatures are not citizens but symptoms. Its plants are not vegetation but first handwriting. Its ruins are not remnants but anticipations. Its pale outer swimmer is perhaps the first dream it has had of a body.

It is learning permanence by failing to keep anything.

It is learning life by making things that almost live.

And above all it is learning itself through sound: the endless crown-roar, the basalt flutes, the humming arches, the grinding stone, the bubble detonations, the deep fissure-note descending into the mountain. The inhabitant of the drowned hollow is that thunder given warmth and shape, still unfinished, still revising its own anatomy, announcing over and over into the shaken water:

I am here.

I am here.

I am not yet done.
