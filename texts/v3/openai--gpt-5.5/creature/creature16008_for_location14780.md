The inhabitants are not quite a people of darkness, and not quite a people of stone. They are people of surfaces.

At first they look human, or close enough that a stranger would call them that before noticing how completely the basin has revised them. They are low-bodied, not dwarfed but compressed by habit: shoulders forward, necks shortened, knees always slightly bent, as if every ceiling might descend without warning. Even in open courts they walk as though beneath an unseen lintel. Their backs are strong from carrying slabs and wet clay. Their hips are broad. Their feet are wide, bare, pale-gray with old lime, the soles cracked into hard plates like fired earth.

They do not step lightly. They test.

Every footfall asks a question of the ground.

Their skin has the color of things kept out of the sun: ash, milk, old mortar, damp bone. Some are darker where soot and kiln-smoke have settled permanently into creases. Their hands are the most expressive part of them. The palms are thickened and scarred; the fingers flattened by pressing mortar, setting tile, scraping mineral crust. Many have one or two nails replaced by little caps of shell, copper, or polished bone, useful for prying grit from joints. Their wrists are wrapped in hide strips stiff with lime.

Their eyes are large, but not beautiful in the usual way. The lids are heavy, often reddened by damp and smoke. They can see in the basin’s grayness better than outsiders can, but sight is not the sense by which they trust the world. Their ears are what mark them. Ears exposed, unadorned, trained from childhood until they become almost ceremonial organs. They tilt their heads constantly. They pause mid-sentence to listen to a channel three walls away. They know the difference between water falling into an empty cistern and water striking a cistern whose bottom is beginning to crack. They hear a bad seam before they see it.

Children learn to walk by echo.

They are carried through lanes while adults tap walls with knuckles, shells, bits of fired clay. The child is made to listen: hollow, full, backed, flooded, false, sleeping, hungry. Before they can read the pressed signs in mortar, they can follow the shape of a corridor by the way their own breath returns to them. Blind children are not pitied. They are watched with interest, then placed early among the listeners, where many of them become authorities.

The inhabitants speak softly when close together, because loud voices blur the working sounds of the place. Their ordinary speech is full of clicks, tongue-taps, and glottal stops, little sounds suited to carrying down passages without becoming shouts. At a distance they signal with tools: three knocks on tile, two scrapes, a pause, a bell struck with the heel of the hand. Whole conversations move through walls this way. A person in one district may ask for a sluice to be opened in another without either party seeing the other.

Their names are not inherited from parents alone. A child is given a birth-name, usually plain and small: a syllable, a touch, a mark. Later, when the child can identify a sound correctly in the dark, another name is added. Some are named for dripping patterns, some for faults in stone, some for the bell-tone of a particular cistern lid. An old person may carry five or six names, each one tied to a repair, a collapse, a flood, a sealing, a discovered hollow.

They live inside the construction rather than upon it. Their dwellings are not houses in the open sense. They are chambers fitted between load-bearing walls, under ramps, beside cistern throats, behind screens of stacked tile. A family room may have one wall that sweats constantly and another that is warm from a kiln flue. Beds are shallow shelves lined with hide, ash-felt, or layered cloth made from animal membrane beaten thin and dried. Nothing is wasted enough to be soft for long.

They own few things that cannot survive damp.

Clay cups. Shell scrapers. Bone needles. Copper hooks. Seals. Lamp-hoods. Listening rods. Mortar knives. Cord made from gut. Folded hide. Small tablets impressed with family repairs. The dead leave tools more often than ornaments.

Food is a problem they have made sacred by never solving it completely.

No gardens exist. No grain is stored. No fruit has ever ripened there. The inhabitants eat what the basin permits without becoming green. They scrape pale films from cooling tiles and cistern lids: mineral cultures, waxy and sour, grown not in sunlight but in condensation, smoke residue, human breath, and salts. They cultivate these without calling it farming. Farming implies roots. Roots are forbidden, or impossible, or both.

They keep shallow trays in dark warm niches where white mats thicken slowly over powdered shell and ash. These are cut into strips and dried near kiln ducts. Children chew them like tough skin. Adults pound them into paste with salt and rendered fat.

They trap the flat-bodied cistern creatures and the soft runners that live in the seams. The small animals are eaten whole after being steamed over enclosed lamps or baked in clay packets. Larger ones are saved for festivals, illnesses, and the feeding of pregnant women. Some families maintain private grilles under which pale things gather, fed by rinsings and crumbs of culture-mat. It is not husbandry, exactly. It is an agreement with hunger.

There are rumors of trade at the far rims of the basin: dried meat, blood cakes, bone, hide, shell, copper, perhaps even grain that is never allowed to sprout. But if such trade exists, it is handled by a disliked caste of rim-walkers who smell of outside air and return with stories considered structurally unsound.

Water is not merely drunk. It is assigned.

Each person carries a small clay tally impressed with marks indicating household, work duty, and current ration. Clear condensation from cold vanes is for drinking. First rain from roofs is for mixing. Second rain is for washing. Cistern water is graded by taste, sound, and the color it leaves on lime. Children are taught not to gulp. The old rinse their mouths and swallow slowly, as if water must be persuaded to remain inside the body.

They do not bathe in the way river people might. Too much water on the body is wasteful, and standing water invites wrong films. They clean themselves with damp ash, shell powder, scraping stones, and cloths rubbed over condensation plates. The result is not cleanliness but a maintained state: skin sealed, odor controlled, lime balanced.

Their clothing is close-fitting and practical. Hide aprons, short wraps, shoulder pads, knee guards. In wetter districts they wear ceramic scales tied onto gut cord, not as armor against weapons but against abrasion. Workers who carry unfired brick smear their arms with gray slip to keep lime from eating the skin. Kiln-keepers wear masks of fired clay with narrow vents, making them look faceless and ancient even when young.

The kiln-keepers are treated with caution. Fire is necessary, but no one fully trusts those who tend it. They live in round-walled districts where heat moves underfoot and soot condenses into black channels. Their eyelashes burn short. Their palms are glossy with old scars. They speak less than others, partly from breathing smoke, partly because their work always risks cracking what everyone else depends upon. When they pass through cooler lanes, people make room.

More respected are the listeners.

A listener may be blind, old, injured, or simply gifted. They carry rods of bone, shell, or copper, each tuned to a different kind of wall. They kneel and press their ears to tile. They ask everyone nearby to be still. Then they declare whether a surface is backed by rubble, water, air, tablets, bone, or an unlined absence. Their word can stop construction, reroute a channel, condemn a dwelling, or save an entire district from sudden collapse.

After them come the sealers. They are not simply masons. They are witnesses. When wet mortar is pressed into a joint, a sealer marks it with a sign: circle, bar, hand, tooth, ripple, broken square. The mark records not ownership but responsibility. If the wall leaks, the mark is read. If the wall holds for a generation, the mark is honored. Some old sealers have hands so famous that their prints are recognized long after their bodies have been rendered into ash and bone.

Death is not a departure from the basin. It is a change of use.

There are no graves, because there is no earth to receive them. Bodies are dried in warm chambers or reduced in low kilns. Bone becomes struts, toggles, rods, handles, needles, ladder-pins. Ash is mixed into mortar for family walls. Teeth are sometimes set above doorways as listening charms, because teeth are thought to remember pressure. A skull may be sealed whole into a corner if the dead person was a master listener, the hollow of it facing the wall so it may continue to hear.

This is not considered desecration. To be wasted would be desecration. To lie unbuilt would be shame.

Their laws are architectural before they are moral.

Do not leave a cavity unlined.

Do not let water decide too long.

Do not strike a wall without listening afterward.

Do not bring roots into the lower districts.

Do not burn open flame under a load-bearing roof.

Do not remove a mark unless you take its duty.

Do not call a surface finished.

That last law is the deepest. Nothing is finished. Finished things are dead in a way even the dead are not. A wall is holding for now. A channel is carrying for now. A cistern is sound for now. A child is apprenticed, an elder is curing, a roof is waiting, a crack is speaking, a district is learning how to fail.

They do not think of themselves as living in a city. A city, to them, would be something placed upon land. They live in continuation. They are the current hands of a construction begun before memory and expected to outlast comprehension. Their myths say the first people arrived when the world below was still soft and unassigned. Others say the basin was once the floor of a sea, and the sea withdrew because no one had yet built channels worthy of holding it. Others say there never was a sea; the idea of a sea is only what water dreams when left unattended.

They fear open natural ground more than darkness. Travelers who speak of forests are asked uncomfortable questions. What supports the trees? How are the roots permitted to enter unlined depths? Who measures the water stolen upward into leaves? What prevents soil from becoming everywhere at once? The answers never satisfy them. A forest sounds to them like collapse pretending to be abundance.

Green is treated as a rumor of infection.

A sprouting seed, if one ever occurred, would likely be surrounded by watchers, then sealed under a bowl, then discussed until it died. They say roots are tiny cracks with hunger. They say leaves are unauthorized catchments. They say flowers are lamps that do not know they are burning.

Yet they are not joyless.

Their pleasures are small, exact, and communal. They gather after rain to listen to the whole basin draining. They admire a clean fall of water from a newly cut spout. They celebrate when a wall dries evenly. They sing in low vaulted chambers where a single note returns layered and multiplied. Their music is mostly rhythm: palms on clay, shell on tile, heel on slab, water in jars. Dancers do not leap. They stamp, slide, turn, and pause, mapping imagined structures with their bodies.

Children play at collapse. They build little courts of broken tile, flood them with cupfuls of water, and compete to divert the flow before it undermines a wall. They hide in vents and answer each other in echoes. They dare one another to touch the black joints where runners vanish. They make tiny people from wet lime and press them flat into bricks.

They also play at outside, though adults dislike it. In this game, a child lies on their back in an open court and pretends there is nothing above them at all. No lid of cloud, no roof, no stone vapor, no ceiling waiting to be built. The game usually ends quickly. Someone becomes frightened.

The inhabitants’ faces grow more basin-like with age. Lines run downward from the eyes and mouth as stains run down walls. Lime gathers in scars. Their voices become rougher, more percussive. Old workers often choose a place to sit each day where they can feel vibrations through their bones. They become living gauges. A grandmother may know from the ache in her left hip that a cistern two districts away has filled beyond its proper measure.

Some among them are almost more craft than person.

There are wall-readers who spend so long in narrow lanes that their shoulders twist sideways permanently. There are cistern divers, small and hairless, who enter black tanks with a cord around the ankle and come up with plugs, bones, lost tools, or blind animals clinging to their skin. There are fog-tenders who sleep beside cold vanes and wake to the first change in condensation. There are ash-mixers whose arms are always gray to the elbow. There are channel-children born in the deepest wet districts, said to be able to smell moving water through sealed brick.

And there are the quiet ones who go alone to the Low Mouth.

They stand in the broad raw depression at the center of the basin and listen to all bells at once until the sound becomes continuous. Some return and take up ordinary work. Some begin marking plans no one taught them: walls that will not be built for three generations, channels that slope toward water not yet gathered, pillars that support an absent future. A few do not return. Their footprints are found in soft mortar, leading toward shallow pools, then stopping.

No one says they drowned. Drowning implies water took them by accident. The basin is not granted accidents so easily.

As for the other life there, it is sparse but intimately known.

The pale flat creatures beneath cistern lids are called many things: lid-skins, white coins, damp ears. Children are told not to put their fingers near the large ones, because they can fold around a fingertip and rasp it raw. Thread-worms in the grates are signs of slow water. Soft runners in the cracks are signs of warmth. A district with no small animals at all is distrusted; it may be poisoned by copper runoff, bad ash, or something worse moving below.

The long white animal that appears after rain is never named directly. It is referred to by absence: the one without eyes, the swimmer under walls, the seam-mouth, the unstruck bell. When it passes, work stops. Even kiln doors are closed gently. It moves through channels too narrow for its body, as if the water makes room. Its skin is the color of unfired clay left too long in darkness. Its mouth is a straight line. It never thrashes. It proceeds.

Some believe it is the oldest inhabitant, older than the people, older than the first fitted floor. Some believe it is made from all the unlined cavities that have ever been sealed in haste. Some believe it is a warning given flesh. If it pauses beside a wall, that wall is emptied within the day. If it turns in a cistern, the water is not drunk. If it enters the Low Mouth, bells are rung until the sound hurts.

The people do not worship it. Worship would place it above them. They attend to it, as they attend to pressure, seepage, cracking, and rain.

That is how they live: by attention sharpened into culture.

They are not masters of the basin. They are not its prisoners, either. They are its current texture: warm-breathed, scar-handed, listening, sealing, carrying, eating the pale yield of dampness, making tools from their dead, teaching children to hear hollows, refusing root and uncontrolled flame, building every surface twice because once is never enough.

If the basin is becoming a landscape, they are the weathering before weather. They are the erosion that repairs. They are the hands by which the made ground keeps making itself.
