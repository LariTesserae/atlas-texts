What lives there is mostly small, and most of it lives by lowering itself.

The hollow belongs first to the things that press flat to stone: mosses, lichens, creeping willow, root-hair, sedge-foot, fern-knuckle. It belongs to animals that know every crack as a doorway and every overhang as a roof. Pikas live there like thrown voices. Marmots live there like warm stones that have learned to breathe. Beetles, midges, black flies, worms, transparent pool-creatures, and dark amphibians occupy the wet seams of the place more completely than any larger animal could.

But beneath all that ordinary life, or among it, there is something else.

It is not large. Nothing large could belong there for long.

The thing that truly lives in the hollow is low, damp, and patient. It is easiest to mistake for several other things: a heap of moss on a stone, a crouched marmot at dusk, a wet cloak left beside the hut, a person kneeling so long in prayer that the mountain has begun to grow over them. It does not stand upright unless forced, and even then it seems uncomfortable, as if height were a kind of exposure or rudeness. Its natural posture is folded: heels tucked, shoulders rounded, hands spread on the ground, head inclined toward water.

It is a listener.

Its body is built around listening the way a bird’s body is built around flight. Its ears are small and set close to the skull, but the bones around them are broad and delicate, and its jaw rests often against stone. It hears through its palms. It hears through its knees. It hears water moving under rock, frost opening hairline cracks, roots drinking, beetles worrying beneath barkless wood. It can tell from the pitch of the waterfall whether the snowfield above has softened. It knows which pool has begun to sour with dead insects, which burrow is flooding, which slab will loosen in the next thaw.

Its skin is the color of wet granite in shadow, gray-brown with olive undertones, darker along the spine and joints. Across its back and shoulders are pale speckles, irregular at first glance, but in winter, when the air is clear and the stars burn hard above the peaks, the pattern answers the sky. Not exactly a map. More like a memory of the sky reflected in water for many generations.

Fine hair grows only where warmth must be kept: along the forearms, the nape, the ribs, the backs of the thighs. Elsewhere its skin is smooth and often slick with condensation. Moss spores cling to it. Lichen sometimes establishes itself in old scars. The oldest of them carry greenish stains around the elbows and knees, not decoration but habitation, as if the hollow has begun to claim them in the same way it claims stone.

Its fingers are long, blunt, and strong. The nails are dark and flattened from prying up stones, scraping roots, cleaning fish no longer than a hand, and pecking cup-marks into rock. Between the fingers is a slight webbing, not enough to make it a swimmer in the usual sense, but enough to move well in pools, to search under ledges, to hold water briefly in the hands. Its feet are broad and quiet, with toes that spread on slick stone. It rarely slips.

It eats what the hollow can spare.

In spring it eats shoots, sedge bases, eggs abandoned by birds, larvae from under stones, bitter roots softened in water, the first slow insects that rise in the noon warmth. In summer it gathers petals and tiny alpine fruits, digs for tubers in the black soil, traps midges in woven screens, takes the occasional pika or fledgling, though never wastefully and never from the same colony twice in a season. In autumn it grows heavier on marmot fat when it can get it, beetle grubs, stored roots, and a black paste made from crushed lichen, seeds, and dried amphibian flesh. In winter it eats little. It sleeps much, but not deeply, curled in the stone hut or in warmer cracks behind the waterfall where the rock sweats even under ice.

There may be more than one, but never many.

The hollow cannot bear many. Usually there are three or four at most: an old one, one or two grown, perhaps a child kept close to the hut and carried under a hide when the fog thickens. Some years there is only one. Some years none are seen, and yet the cup-marks are cleared of grit, the drying racks are repaired, and a fresh willow rib appears in the hut roof.

They do not possess the hollow. Possession would be too heavy a thing to bring there. They attend it.

They return rather than remain. Some pass the hard months lower down among shrubs and broken forest, where there is fuel and thicker air. But certain ones come back every thaw, climbing by ledges no goat would trust, bringing bone needles, sinew, dark glass blades, skins, small bundles of salt, and stories condensed into song. They arrive thin and leave thinner, but changed. Listening alters them. Their voices become rougher from the waterfall. Their movements become more deliberate. They stop turning quickly when called.

Their language is full of water sounds.

It clicks, drops, repeats, breaks off, resumes somewhere else. Names are not fixed things but patterns: three taps on stone, a held breath, a whistle through the teeth, a syllable swallowed in the throat. A person, an animal, or a pool may have several names depending on weather. The waterfall has too many names to count, and most of them are not spoken aloud. They are made by placing the mouth close to wet stone and humming until the stone answers.

They do not shout unless warning. To shout is to spend too much at once.

When they speak to one another across the basin, they use the cliffs. A small sound placed correctly will travel, strike, return, and arrive enlarged but not broken. This is why footsteps sometimes answer from the wrong direction. This is why a pika’s cry may seem to come from the ground. The hollow is an instrument, and they have learned where to touch it.

The hut is theirs, but not exactly a home. It is more like an ear.

Its doorway faces the waterfall because the hut was built to hear. Inside, when rain is falling and the roof-moss is saturated, the whole structure murmurs. Water threads down the outer stones, collects in the sod, drips from willow ribs into shallow bowls. A person lying inside can distinguish weather by the pattern of drops. Snowmelt, rain, fog-condensation, and seepwater all arrive differently. The old ones sleep with one cheek against the wall.

The bone needles in the crack are not forgotten. They are left there because needles belong to cracks. The scraper on the flat stone may look abandoned, but it is waiting in the place where scraping should happen. Tools are not carried constantly unless the body needs them. In the hollow, things are placed where their work lives.

The cup-marks on the boulder are their oldest work.

They did not make all of them. Some were pecked by hands so long gone that even memory has worn smooth around them. Each cup is a small weather-catcher, a patient mouth. Rain fills them. Melt fills them. Fog fills them grain by grain. Sometimes they are filled deliberately: with petals, insect wings, ground bone, a drop of blood from a thumb, milk stolen from a marmot kill, the first thaw-water of the year.

One cup never empties because it is not only a cup. It opens, very slightly, into the under-hearing of the mountain. The listeners bend over it when they need to know whether to stay or descend, whether a child will live, whether the snow above the north wall is safe, whether the pale cat has crossed the ridge. They do not see visions in it. They listen to its surface.

Their children are born quiet.

Not silent—no living infant in such a place could afford true silence—but quiet in the way of damp things. They make small throat-clicks and breathy squeaks, sounds like stones nudged in shallow water. They are carried low against the belly, wrapped in skins lined with sedge fluff and marmot fur. Before they can walk, their palms are pressed to moss, to wet granite, to the side of the hut during rain. The first lesson is not “speak” or “stand.”

The first lesson is “feel where you are.”

They grow slowly. Everything there grows slowly. Their bones thicken late. Their lungs become powerful, but not for running; for holding breath, for humming, for living in thin air without panic. A young one who moves too fast is corrected not with anger but with a hand laid flat on the back until the body settles. Quickness calls hawks, loosens stones, startles marmots, wastes heat. Quickness is for danger only.

They fear fire a little.

They use it when they must, coaxing it from carried embers, dried dung, hoarded stems, old bird nests, splinters brought from below. But fire is an uneasy guest in the hollow. Smoke clings. Sparks die. Flame makes things stand up and separate from one another. It throws hard shadows against the wet walls and makes the moss smell wounded. So their hearth is small, and many nights they choose cold food, body warmth, and the long mutter of the waterfall instead.

They are not immortal. The hollow would not allow anything so excessive.

They sicken from lung-rot if fog stays too long. They break bones on slick stone. They are taken by cold, by hunger, by the pale cat if they misread the ridge, by ordinary age, which bends them closer and closer to the ground until one day they do not rise. Their dead are not buried deeply; the soil is too wet and shallow. They are placed in cracks above the basin, wrapped in willow and sedge, where meltwater passes over them season after season. Some bones are later retrieved and made into needles, whistles, toggles, or listening pins worn through the ear.

This is not considered desecration. It is continuity. A bone that listened all its life should not be sealed away from sound.

They have a wary peace with the animals. Pikas scold them but do not always flee. Marmots know which of them is hungry. Birds steal hair from their sleeping mats. The dark amphibians gather near their feet during rain, blinking with ancient calm. The listeners never kill the speckled frogs. They believe the frogs remember the sky before it broke into separate stars.

As for the pale cat, it watches from above and does not enter because it knows the hollow is not empty. Predators understand ownership differently from humans. The cat feels the basin listening back.

The strangest thing about these beings is not their shape, nor their speckled backs, nor their language of water and echo.

It is that they seem less like inhabitants than expressions of the hollow’s own habits.

They are what happens when life accepts every rule of that place without resentment. They are low because nothing tall survives. They are damp because nothing dry lasts. They announce themselves because nothing quick moves there unmarked. They are sparse because the basin cannot feed a crowd. They return because the hollow does not permit permanent possession, only repeated attention.

If you saw one from a distance in fog, you might think it was a stone beside the pool.

Then it would turn its head—not toward you, but toward the sound your boot made in the moss.

It would know your weight from that sound. It would know whether you were frightened, tired, hungry, careless, reverent. It would know whether you had come to take, to hide, to pray, or merely because the mountain opened and you followed.

If you were loud, it would be gone before you crossed the basin.

If you were quiet, and if rain began, and if you knelt near the boulder with the cup-marks without putting your hand into them, you might hear an answering knock from under the stone.

Not a greeting exactly.

More like the hollow acknowledging that, for the moment, you had made yourself light enough to be noticed.
