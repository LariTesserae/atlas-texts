The ones who live there are a small people, though the city makes them seem smaller still.

They are not hidden because they are afraid, exactly. They are hidden because secrecy is one of the conditions of survival, like shade, like airflow, like the careful distance between a root and a wall. They have the manner of people who have inherited a fragile instrument and must pass through it without striking the wrong note.

They are narrow-bodied, dry-skinned, and deliberate in all movement. Outsiders often think them old at first sight, even the young ones, because their faces are veiled and powdered, their gestures economical, their eyes narrowed against the light. Their skin ranges from clay-brown to ash-pale to deep umber, but everyone is dusted by the same mineral whiteness after an hour in the streets. Salt gathers at their brows, in the seams of their knuckles, along the edges of their lashes. They brush it away with little combs of horn.

Their clothing is layered but light: loose wrappings of beaten plant fiber, stiffened collars, glare-veils, fingerless gloves, soft masks for the pollen hours. Nothing hangs freely if it can snap in the wind. Cloth is tied close with resin beads and copper pins. Children wear padded soles until their feet harden; adults go nearly silent-footed over salt, brick, ladder, and roof-rib. Their shoes are made to grip rather than cushion. Everyone climbs. Even the elderly climb, though slowly, with hooked staffs and practiced pauses.

They do not speak much aloud.

Their throats can make sound, and in the sheltered inner rooms they sometimes murmur, sing, argue, laugh. But public speech is mostly useless, and useless habits do not survive long there. Their language lives in the hands, shoulders, mouth, brow, and in taps passed through walls or floors. A greeting can be made by touching two fingers to the veil and then to the nearest surface, acknowledging both person and city. Formal speech is carved, carried, displayed: polished tokens strung at the belt, each marked with a sign too old to be merely practical.

Children learn three alphabets. One is for the hand. One is for incising salt and clay. One is for vibration.

Before they can write their own names, they learn to feel a warning pulse through their heels: blocked vent, pollen release, root crack, roof-shift, stranger above. Their games are full of this. They chase one another through alleys by reflected sound. They hide behind wind fins and identify who approaches by the tremor of the ladder. A child of five can tell the difference between a seed-rattle, a loose hinge, and a beetle swarm under a leaf-plate.

The people’s hearing is strange. Not sharper, exactly, but redistributed. Their ears are often plugged or veiled against the screaming vents, yet their hands and feet are attentive. They lean against pillars to listen. They sleep with one palm touching the wall. Lovers lie back to back against the same stone, reading each other’s breathing through plaster.

Their bodies are the only wet things permitted.

This is treated with a mixture of reverence and embarrassment. Blood, saliva, tears, birth-fluid: all are dangerous, powerful, private. They do not weep openly if they can help it. At funerals, mourners press ash-cloths beneath the eyes, and any dampness is folded away, dried, powdered, and placed in the wall niche with the dead. A cut finger is not simply bandaged; it is dusted, sealed, apologized for. Children are taught early that the body contains a weather the city must never be asked to bear.

They eat dry foods that crack, flake, dissolve, or spark faintly on the tongue.

There are pollen cakes, bitter mineral wafers, roasted beetle meal, scraped resin, salt-fruits from pale columns, curled sheets of sun-hardened leaf, and dark pastes made from ground root-husk and ash. Some foods must be breathed as much as eaten: fragrant powders placed beneath the veil and inhaled slowly, nourishing through the mouth and nose. Meals are quiet, spaced apart on mats, with each dish covered until the moment of use. To spill food is unfortunate. To spill liquid would be scandalous.

They do have sweetness, though not the wet sweetness of fruit. Their sweets are brittle amber resins, crystallized exudates, toasted seed-dust, and moth-powder mixed with edible mineral bloom. Festival candies are cast into tiny shapes: vents, ladders, leaf-fans, sleeping foxes, ancestor masks.

They are not numerous. The city could not bear multitudes, and they know this. Families are small, households porous, kinship practical and complex. A child may belong to a birth-house, a root-house, and a vent-line all at once. Parentage matters less than placement: who taught your hands, whose wall fed you, whose air you slept in, whose dead share your garden.

They count descent through maintenance.

A person may say, in effect: I am of the west hum, third lower turning, under the black-root that holds the broken arch. Or: I am of the mica gardens and the old market vault, where the floor remembers my grandmother’s drum. Names are not merely personal. A full name includes a location, a tone, and a duty.

Their trades are ancient and exact.

There are ventwrights, who know the invisible architecture of moving air. They wear masks with long reed feelers and carry boxes of powders to test currents. A ventwright can place a pinch of violet dust on a sill and, from the way it lifts, tell which chamber below is choking.

There are root-minders, who speak of the black-roots as though they are slow animals with legal rights. They map them, feed them, prune them, plead with them, and sometimes order a house abandoned because a root has chosen that direction. Root-minders are patient even by the standards of the city. Many go half their lives without making a dramatic decision, then save an entire district by cutting one strand at the correct hour.

There are dust-sorters, usually older women and men whose senses have become almost botanical. They sit beneath mica awnings with trays of colored powder and separate food from dye, medicine from irritant, fertile pollen from dead grit. They can smell poison through a sealed cloth. Their fingertips are stained permanently green, black, yellow, and rust-red.

There are glare-readers, who study the salt roof. They track stress-lines, inclusions, bright faults, and the subtle changes in the noon blaze. A good glare-reader can predict a roof-flake fall days before it happens. They often go nearly blind by middle age and are honored for it.

There are scribes, but their records are not expected to last forever. This changes their idea of history. They carve knowing that the tablet will soften, chip, be ground into plaster, enter a wall, feed a plant, become dust on someone’s sleeve. For them, preservation is not resistance to decay. Preservation is correct circulation.

Their artisans make things that can endure abrasion: ceramic knives, horn scrapers, salt-glass lenses, stiff mats, little bells, root-fiber ropes, carved hand-signs, masks with replaceable filters, wind toys for children, mourning combs, ancestor beads. Metalworkers are rare and slightly feared. Copper is too precious to treat casually, and the sound of metal on stone carries an authority almost like a voice.

Their priests, if that is the word, are keepers of silence.

They do not command temples. They tend moments. They mark the rare failures of wind, record their duration, location, taste, and omen. When silence falls, they walk quickly through the city touching doorways, plants, sleeping screens, the brows of infants. No one is supposed to waste a silence by speaking. Silence is allowed to arrive entire.

The white court has its own attendants: judges of endurance, witnesses with weighted veils, medics carrying lime and ash, scribes who record decisions in marks broad enough to be read through trembling vision. Disputes are taken there when ordinary gesture has failed. In the roar, anger loses its ornament. Lies become difficult to maintain, not because the wind reveals truth, but because it strips a person down to breath, stance, and will.

They are affectionate people, though not in expansive ways.

Affection is shown by making space, by repairing another’s ladder rung before being asked, by turning a shade screen so someone’s working hand falls out of glare, by leaving edible dust at a threshold, by polishing a name-token while its owner sleeps. To crowd someone unnecessarily is rude. To interrupt airflow around another’s body is almost aggressive. Even lovers maintain a careful geometry, touching wrist to wrist, brow to brow, palm to wall.

Their humor is dry in every sense. They make jokes about outsiders trying to whisper over the wind, about children who confuse poison dust with dye, about roots with better property claims than families. They laugh silently in public, shoulders shaking, eyes bright above the veil.

The plants are not merely crops to them.

They are residents, infrastructure, ancestors, hazards, tools, witnesses. A vine may be older than a bloodline. A fan of green-black mica above a lintel may be greeted each morning as grandmother, not metaphorically but without sentimentality. The city’s people do not imagine a hard boundary between architecture and life. A room can ripen. A wall can become hungry. A garden can remember where the dead were placed.

Some plants are treated almost as civic officials. The black-roots determine load and passage. The tube-plants regulate breath. The brass-spoon leaves measure light. The powder-columns store famine. The filament mats clean air and catch heat. When one of the oldest plants dies, its husk is not discarded. It is dismantled with ceremony, its salts divided, its fiber twisted into communal rope, its dust mixed into new beds.

The animals live at the edge of notice.

Glass-backed beetles are tolerated, sometimes cultivated. Children keep them in little ceramic cages and feed them mineral pollen, then race them across warm tiles. Transparent lizards are considered lucky if seen at dawn and unlucky if found indoors at night. Moths belong to the roof and the dead; their silver dust is gathered for paint used on mourning tablets. The blind foxlike visitors are watched with wary tenderness. They are hunger given legs, and the city understands hunger.

There are also things that live there which are not quite animals and not quite plants: slow crusts that bloom on shaded plaster, listening mats of root-hair that recoil from touch, dust colonies that gather around copper and change color before a pressure shift. The people classify these carefully, not because classification makes them safe, but because unnamed life is harder to negotiate with.

Outsiders rarely stay.

The brightness hurts them. The noise unmans them. The dryness enters their thoughts. They ask for water too often, speak too loudly, step where air should pass, lean against living walls, mistake an ancestor-vine for a handrail. The city’s people are not cruel to them, but hospitality has limits. A stranger is given shade, powder food, a mask, a slate, and rules. If they cannot learn quickly, they are guided back up through the seam before they damage something irreplaceable.

Those who do stay become quiet. They lose the habit of calling across rooms. They learn to read dust on ledges. They begin to dream in tones.

The inhabitants do not think of themselves as poor, though their possessions are few. Wealth is airflow, shade-right, root-trust, a durable ladder, a clean pollen yield, a chamber where old people can sleep without toothache from vibration. A family with a good cool-air cistern is comfortable. A district with a clear warning tone is blessed. A house whose dead have fed strong growth is fortunate.

They fear water, collapse, crowding, and wrong silence.

They fear a roof seam widening too fast. They fear a vent changing pitch in the night. They fear a child born during a pollen storm, because such children are said to belong partly to the gardens. They fear greed most of all, because greed takes the form of blockage: hoarded space, hoarded shade, hoarded air, hoarded dust. In a city like this, selfishness is not immoral in the abstract. It is mechanically dangerous.

Yet they are not grim. Their lives are full of small luxuries: the warmth of a heat-storing leaf at evening, the taste of fresh gold pollen, the blue shadow under a gypsum arch, a well-tuned stairwell humming in the soles, the silver flash of moth-dust on a child’s cheek, the first deep lowering of the wind after a brutal noon.

They are, above all, custodians of continuation.

They do not build monuments to conquest. They do not dream of spreading over the salt plain. Their ambition is inward, intricate, patient. To open a new chamber without killing an old root. To tune a vent so two districts can hear one another. To turn a dead wall into a nursery. To keep the city bright but not blinded, loud but not broken, dry but not dead.

What lives there is a people shaped by abstinence and abundance at once: spare in number, rich in attention, nearly voiceless, never silent. A people who have made citizenship out of listening, architecture out of breath, ancestry out of mineral hunger.

And around them, with them, through them, the city lives too: a hidden organ of salt, root, dust, glare, bone, and wind.
