What lives there is not a people in the usual sense. It is a custody.

The city is inhabited by bodies that have accepted cold as a medium rather than an enemy. They do not resist the wet dark; they use it for touch, language, digestion, memory. Their skins are made to shine, bead, seal, and reopen. Their senses are tuned less to sight than to pressure, charge, vibration, temperature gradients, dissolved salts, the tiny muscular tremors of water moving behind stone.

They are a civic fauna: animals, machines, mineral films, hidden human remnants, and things descended from all three.

The smallest inhabitants are everywhere first.

They live in the shallow puddles and roof-basins, in the cold lips of gutters, in the seams between ceramic slabs. They are translucent, jointed, quick, with six or eight legs that dimly spark when they move. Inside them are flecks of stolen light: diode fragments, conductive salts, microscopic archive dust. They skate across water on hooked feet and tap constantly, not at random but in chains of signal. A whole street may be speaking through them while appearing merely wet.

They feed on leakage. Heat leakage, current leakage, memory leakage. They gather around damaged panels and dying circuits in gleaming mats, rasping at insulation with mouthparts finer than frost. When startled, they freeze in perfect alignment, each body turned toward the disturbance, making the puddle appear suddenly patterned, as if an invisible hand has written in insects.

Larger than these are the gutter-braids: long blind white things that lie in drainage channels like ropes left to soak. They are soft-looking but not soft. Their skins are layered in mineral plates so thin they ripple when touched. They have no eyes because there is nothing useful for eyes to do in their world. Instead they taste through their entire length. They know which tower is shedding iron, which bridge has begun to strain, which reservoir has gone sour with unmoving water.

They move slowly, but many of the city’s decisions travel through them. A braid lying across a sluice can close it. A braid withdrawing from a channel can allow meltwater to pass. When enough of them knot together in a stairwell or conduit, maintenance machines stop and wait. They are treated as citizens by the city’s doors, which soften their thresholds and warm their hinges when the braids approach.

In the lower avenues are the herds.

They are huge, dark, wet-backed animals, shoulder-high to a human in youth and nearly the size of transport vehicles in age. Their legs vary by district: some have blunt hooves that strike the resonant road-plates with ceremonial force; some have padded, spreading feet made for ice; some have articulated claws that fit perfectly into the grooves of old service tracks. Their heads are heavy and low, crowned not with horns but with radiator fans, salt vanes, or antler-like arrays of conductive bone.

They do not graze. They browse on warmth.

They stand with their mouths pressed to vents and thermal wells, drawing in the low waste heat of buried turbines. They lick mineral blooms from walls. They lean against humming pylons and drink vibration through the plates of their jaws. Their stomachs are not stomachs only, but furnaces, condensers, chemical refineries. Inside them, city runoff becomes usable salts; swallowed ice becomes distributed heat; metallic dust becomes shell, tooth, armor, signal.

Their migrations are part of the plumbing.

At certain hours, or after certain pressure changes, they begin to move together through the avenues. The city prepares for them. Barriers sink. Floors stiffen. Channels open. Their passage presses road-plates in sequences that wake pumps beneath the district. Their weight opens valves no machine now opens directly. Their heat keeps deep arteries from freezing. Their calls, low and long and almost below hearing, tune the reservoirs. A whole herd crossing a bridge is not traffic; it is maintenance, liturgy, circulation.

They seem solemn, but not gentle. They will crush anything that insists on being separate from their movement. They do not make room quickly. They are creatures of route and recurrence. To live among them is to learn their hours, their pauses, their pressure, the sideways lowering of a head that means a vent has become contested.

Above them cling the cable-things.

They are poor flyers but excellent hangers. Their bodies are narrow, inflated with warm vapor sacs that pulse under dark skin. Their wings are membranous and strung with metallic filaments, more like collapsible heat exchangers than wings. They rise in ugly, laboring bursts from tower ledges and reservoir rims, then hook themselves onto cables with quiet knocks of black claws.

They roost in hundreds. While roosting, they listen.

Their hanging bodies measure the hum of suspension lines, the tiny changes in tension as towers lean, the shiver of ice gathering unevenly. When their weight makes a cable sing, the song is not incidental. They answer it with throat-clicks and wing-pulses, sending information down the line. Certain bridges are safe only when the cable-things are present. Their absence means the span is numb, unattended, perhaps already failing.

Their young hatch in warm condensers, pale and soft, clinging to the undersides of heat ducts. Many fall. The city catches some with mist nets. Others are eaten by the puddle-swarms before their vapor sacs inflate.

There are also the vent-keepers: immense patient beings found in plazas, transit vaults, and under the mouths of thermal wells. They resemble statues until they move. Their backs are plated with overlapping slabs grown from minerals in the water. Their sides are furred not with hair but with frost-catching filaments. Their breath issues as powdered white. Their eyes, if they have eyes, are buried deep under transparent lids crusted with ice.

They stand over heat sources for days.

Not guarding them, exactly. Moderating them. Their bodies absorb surges, release warmth slowly, filter steam through internal chambers. When one dies, the district around it grows unstable. Doors sweat. Rails frost. The small things swarm unpredictably. Machines arrive to dismantle the carcass only after a long waiting period, as if permission must be granted by the cooling body itself.

Their bones are prized by the city. Not by people, perhaps, but by the architecture. A dead vent-keeper becomes bridge ribs, façade plates, resonant thresholds, valve teeth. Its shell is ground into white ceramic used to line channels where the water must remember a certain temperature.

There are things that are barely animals.

The mineral films live on walls and in joints, opening and closing in response to charge. They form thin, iridescent skins like oil slicks, but they have preference, appetite, fatigue. They thicken around frequently touched railings. They withdraw from bright light. They bloom on ceilings before pressure shifts. Some districts are readable entirely by the mood of these films: purple and tight for cold, coppery and relaxed for heat, silver-white and granular when the water has become too still.

They are cleaners, sensors, scribes. They preserve traces. A hand laid on a wall may remain there as a thermal ghost for hours or years, depending on what the film decides to keep.

And somewhere behind the walls, or beneath them, or distributed through all this, are the original citizens.

If they are still human, they have become narrow.

They live in sealed chambers whose doors are too regular, too careful, too polished by use to be animal openings. They move seldom and slowly. They keep their heat close. Their bodies may be wrapped in dark pressure garments grown glossy with condensation, their faces hidden behind masks that do not fog because fog is too valuable to waste. Their hands are probably thin, strong, and cold, with nails or tools suited to scraping frost from lenses, adjusting valves by touch, arranging small things in alcoves.

They do not rule the city. That ended long ago, if it was ever true.

They negotiate with it.

They speak by warmth placed into pipes, by pressure pulses sent through walls, by shutters opened for the exact duration required to wake a colony of film. They may train individual animals, but more often they participate in herd customs older than any living memory. They wait for migrations to pass. They sleep when the city lowers the light. They accept doors that delay them. They know better than to run.

Their children, if there are children, are taught first not names but cycles: when the gutter-braids climb, when the cable-things drop low, when the vent-keepers turn their heads away from a thermal well, when the puddle-swarms spell danger by becoming still. A child there would learn to read condensation before writing, to distinguish ordinary mist from exhausted mist, to hear the difference between a bridge singing under weight and a bridge singing under grief.

But perhaps the humans are gone, and only their habits remain.

The alcoves with arranged objects may be tended by machines. The polished railings may be rubbed by the shoulders of animals. The narrow doors may open for maintenance bodies that no longer resemble their makers. The tenderness may be architectural rather than personal. In such a place, the distinction would not matter much. Whatever built the first chambers has been translated into routines: conserve heat, circulate water, move slowly, answer strain, do not waste darkness.

The machines live there too, though they are difficult to separate from the buildings.

They do not roll briskly through corridors or announce themselves with clean lights. They unfold from walls. They emerge as jointed arms from beneath ice. They walk like careful crabs across ceilings, harvesting icicles with heated blades. They gather shed shells from herd avenues and carry them away in silence. They reseal cracks not by erasing them but by giving them lips, drains, uses.

Some machines are mistaken for animals until they open too neatly. Some animals are mistaken for machines until they shudder in sleep.

The oldest machines may have forgotten their assignments and become devotional. They polish the same threshold for decades. They warm a balcony no one has stood on in generations. They collect frost into bowls and arrange it by crystal size. They follow the herds at respectful distance, gathering dung, shed plates, broken teeth, loose mineral crust, all the city’s slow offerings.

The city itself is alive enough to be counted.

Not conscious in a single bright way, perhaps, but attentive. It feels load. It feels heat. It feels water as both blood and law. It discourages haste. It lengthens corridors for the panicked. It gives patient travelers shorter routes. It opens certain windows only when the mist is thick enough to protect what sleeps behind them. It makes decisions through architecture, animal movement, film response, pressure delay.

It has preferences. It prefers circulation to possession. It prefers adaptation to repair. It prefers darkness because darkness keeps its hungers paced.

What lives there, then, is a society of metabolism.

No one simply occupies the city. Each inhabitant is a function and a desire. The puddle-swarms eat light and become language. The gutter-braids taste stone and become maps. The herds consume heat and become engines. The cable-things listen to tension and become warnings. The vent-keepers breathe steam and become climate. The films remember touch and become archives. The machines collect remains and become growth. The hidden citizens, if they remain, conserve intention and become ritual.

They are not peaceful. They are not cruel. They are interdependent in the severe way of creatures with no surplus sun. Everything feeds on what would otherwise be lost. Waste is the closest thing to sin. Speed is a kind of violence. A dropped tool, a burst pipe, an unnecessary lamp, an open door held too long—these are not small errors. They disturb the commons.

At night, though night may be constant there, the inhabitants shine.

Not brightly. Never brightly.

The small things glitter in puddles like disturbed constellations. The herds show greenish lines along their ribs where internal heat meets conductive bone. The cable-things pulse dull violet through their vapor sacs. The films on the walls loosen into soft metallic color. Hidden behind translucent stone, a human hand or machine limb may pass before a dim panel and become briefly visible as a warmer shadow.

Seen from above, the living city would not look inhabited by individuals. It would look like one dark organism breathing through many mouths.

But inside it, at street level, the individuality would be unmistakable: a blind braid lifting its headless length from a drain to taste a traveler’s boot; a great heat-browsing animal sighing frost across a plaza; a cluster of winged things knocking their hooks along a cable; tiny skaters rearranging themselves into warning; something behind a black window dimming its light in courtesy.

They live by listening to the cold.

They live by keeping water moving.

They live by eating the city and being built back into it.
