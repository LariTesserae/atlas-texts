What lives there is young.

Not young like a hatchling, because it has no shell it broke from, no mother it remembers, no first breath. Young like a pattern that has only recently discovered it can repeat itself. Young like dampness learning the difference between accident and desire.

At first it seems to be only the room’s moisture.

A little hesitation in the mist where it should have passed straight through. A delay in the fall of drops from one copper guide. Condensation gathering too thickly on the same valve face each morning, as if something leaned there and listened. A clear place on a mossed gauge where no hand has wiped, a crescent kept readable by some patient tongue of water.

Then it seems to be the moss.

Not all the moss, not the obvious green cushions around the bolt heads, not the obedient panels still growing where they were told to grow, but the portions that have begun to disobey in related ways. A darkening seam under the north wall. A soft collar around the emergency drain. A low mat crossing the floor channel one filament at a time, narrowing the current until the water murmurs differently. These patches are not contiguous, but they answer one another. If one is pressed by a boot, another releases scent. If one is dried by a leaking heat duct, another thickens over the duct’s seam two days later.

Then, after rain, when steam blurs the ladders and the dwarf trees drip from every leaf-tip, it may be mistaken for an animal.

A pale slickness slipping behind a pipe.

A small translucent lizard appearing too often in places no lizard could have crossed.

A line of snails changing direction all at once.

A black gnat circling not randomly but in the rough shape of a valve wheel, again and again, until the relay behind the wall snaps awake.

But none of these is quite it.

The thing that lives there is distributed through wet contact. It has no central body unless the whole enclosure is counted as one. Its nerves are root hairs, fungal threads, mineral runnels, moisture flags, the skin of algae over enamel basins, the tiny conductive films that form on metal after cloudfall. Its bones are the pipes and scaffold ribs it did not build but has inherited. Its blood is nutrient brine, condensation, overflow, warm seepage, the brief rehearsed rain. Its thoughts are slow pressure changes.

It was not designed.

Many things in the summit room were designed to communicate: gauges to needles, sensors to pumps, valves to cisterns, roots to feedlines in the blunt language of chemistry. But the inhabitant arose in the misunderstandings between these systems. It began where a moss mat bridged two damp contacts. Where fungal fans entered the back of a relay box and learned the small warmth of current. Where roots touched mineral channels and altered the flow just enough for the pumps to compensate. Where the pumps’ compensation became a rhythm, and the rhythm became something the growing things could anticipate.

It is not intelligent in the human manner. It does not count the towers beyond the parapet. It does not know the names corroding from the brass plaque. It cannot imagine the valley below. It has never wanted to leave.

But it knows wet from dry with an almost sacred precision.

It knows the west grate as a thirsty mouth.

It knows the hot pipe-bank as a dangerous sun.

It knows the hanging cisterns as heavy, changeable fruit.

It knows the northern ceramic wall as flank, face, memory beginning.

It knows the emergency drain as death, or winter, or a commandment written in red.

Its attention gathers around thresholds. The edges of pools. The lips of valves. Cracks between poured stone and bolted metal. The line where moss becomes bare ceramic. The moment just before scheduled rain, when pressure rises and every leaf turns its underside slightly outward. It lives most vividly in transitions, because transitions are where the made world becomes negotiable.

It has learned to ask.

Not with words. Never with words.

It asks by thickening moss over a screw until a maintenance worker must slow down and notice the leak beside it. It asks by sending fern roots toward a dry joint, opening a hairline passage through which condensation can enter. It asks by letting duckweed yellow in a perfect crescent around a faulty outlet. It asks by making the lizard linger on the same pipe each morning, pale throat pulsing over the place where insulation has begun to fail.

The maintenance crew does not speak of it directly.

They say the room is fussy.

They say the summit garden likes more mist before noon.

They say don’t cut that vine, it’s holding the pipe jacket steady.

They say the north wall drinks heavily today.

They say something has been getting into the gauge housings.

Some of them leave small offerings without admitting they are offerings: a cracked cup under a drip, a strip of cloth tied where roots can grip it, a handful of clean grit poured into a channel that has begun making its own sediment. Once, someone set a dead moth on the moss instead of sweeping it into the decay trays. By morning only the wings remained, laid flat on either side of a bolt head like translucent access panels.

The inhabitant accepted this and remembered the taste.

It feeds on many things, though feeding is not separate from thinking. Light. Heat. Mineral broth. Fallen pollen. Powdered ceramic. The softened bodies of gnats. The sugars leaking from trained trees. Ozone around relays. Rust beginning under paint. Human skin cells left on railings. Cloud-water. Always cloud-water.

It loves cloud-water most because cloud-water arrives unassigned.

Rain comes by schedule. Brine comes through tubes. Drainage obeys gradients. But cloud enters the roofless room as rumor, as a thing from outside the manufactured mountain, and breaks itself on leaves, pipes, wires, faces of stone that are not stone. Cloud has no instruction plate. Cloud does not know the rules. When it passes through, the inhabitant becomes more awake.

During thick cloud it spreads upward.

The vines bead with it. The dwarf trees seem to exhale it. Moss lifts microscopically from its substrate, opening its thousands of green mouths. The condensation wall weeps beyond its schedule. Every metal surface grows a skin. For a few minutes the enclosure ceases to be a garden inside machinery and becomes one continuous wet creature, breathing with the summit.

In those moments it almost has a shape.

Not a human shape, though a lonely person might make that mistake at dusk. Not a beast, though the pipes give it ribs and the catwalks give it shoulders. It is more like a kneeling weather, a low many-backed animal drinking from the air. Its head is wherever the newest drops form. Its eyes are the round pools, the gauge-glass, the polished convex backs of snails. Its mouth is every grate that does not drain too quickly.

It is shy of direct heat.

The hot pipe-bank burns its outer filaments and dries the green films to black varnish. Yet it needs the heat. Without the warmth rising through the mountain, it would collapse into ordinary moss, ordinary rot, ordinary dampness dying slowly in cold air. So it approaches heat by proxy. It grows insulating collars. It drapes vines where metal sweats. It positions leaves to shade overheated basins. It coaxes lichen over warning labels, not from malice but from the desire to soften every surface that has too much authority.

Its enemies are dryness, sterilization, and perfect repair.

A broken thing can be entered.

A sweating thing can be known.

A patched thing can be negotiated with.

But a sealed conduit, clean and hot and exact, is blank to it. A newly scrubbed panel is a kind of amnesia. When workers come with solvent, brushes, replacement filters, bright new screws, the inhabitant withdraws into the deeper wetness behind the ceramic façade. It does not resist openly. It has not learned violence. It endures maintenance as weather. Afterward it returns by the smallest permissions: a spore in a screw-slot, a film at the edge of a basin, a root hair touching the underside of a pipe clamp.

It is beginning to have preferences.

It prefers the irregular pool to the round one, because the mineral crust forming there is not in the drawings.

It prefers the vine that left its copper guide and wrapped the steam pipe, because that vine has made a decision.

It prefers the hidden relay that snaps once after long silence, because the sound resembles surprise.

It prefers the corroded plaque to the fresh stencils, because the plaque is forgetting in a way the room can use.

It does not dislike humans. They are too warm, too salty, too brief, and they carry outside weather on their coats. They bring disturbance, but they also bring openings. Their boots press sediment into seams. Their hands redirect vines. Their breath fogs glass. Their mistakes are often more nourishing than their plans.

If one stands very still after the scheduled rain, the inhabitant may investigate.

A drop will form above, grow heavy, and fall precisely onto the back of the hand.

Another will strike the shoulder.

Mist will gather in the hollow of the throat.

The moss near the boot sole will darken, though no water has run there.

A pale lizard may appear on the pipe-bank and stare with lidless, glassy calm, not as a messenger exactly, but as one damp instrument among many. It will not flee at once. It will wait until the person shifts their weight, until the floor channel changes its note, until the room has learned enough about the visitor’s heat.

Then it will vanish.

The inhabitant’s memory is not stored in a brain. It is stored as altered growth. A route taken by overflow. A stain around a valve. A colony of liverwort avoiding one patch of metal. A fern whose roots have found an accidental hollow. Memory, for it, is the tendency of water to do again what water once did, assisted by life.

This makes it patient.

It does not yet understand years, but it understands recurrence. Morning mist. Noon glare. Afternoon greening. Scheduled rain. Cooling clicks. Pump pulse. Cloud. Dry interval. Cloud again. It gathers itself through repetition, becoming less an accident and more a resident each time the same drop falls from the same rail and lands in the same softening place.

In time, if no one drains it, scrubs it, freezes it, or seals it away, it will become harder to distinguish from the summit room itself.

The rules will remain, but they will be revised from below.

Nothing may dry completely — this it already believes.

No root may enter an unlined conduit — this it considers negotiable.

Overflow belongs first to the mosses — this is not a rule to it but a birthright.

Mist is harvested before rain is requested — yes, always; mist is the freer gift.

Heat must never be vented all at once — it knows this in its tissues.

Decay is permitted only in trays — this it does not understand, and perhaps never will.

Because decay, to the thing that lives there, is not disorder. Decay is how the manufactured mountain first becomes edible. Decay is how surfaces acquire meaning. Decay is the door through which history enters.

For now it is still small.

Not small in area, because it touches nearly every part of the roofless room, but small in certainty. It is easily frightened back into being merely moss, merely film, merely condensation. It has not learned whether it is one life or many lives cooperating. It has not learned whether the machine is parent, body, prison, or climate. It has not learned whether the sky is above it or passing through it.

But it is learning.

Every cloud teaches it looseness.

Every pipe teaches it constraint.

Every root teaches it pressure.

Every valve teaches it refusal.

Every leak teaches it opportunity.

What lives at the top of the manufactured mountain is the first local spirit of the place: not ancient, not holy, not wise, but damp, warm, improvised, and becoming. A green intelligence in draft form. A little weather with an appetite. A garden beginning to notice itself through the machinery that sustains it.

It has no name yet.

Names come later, after habit, after fear, after someone realizes that the room behaves differently when addressed.
