What lives there is not a single kind of creature, but a whole society of heat-trained bodies.

They are mostly dark, wet, patient things.

The inhabitants of the basin have learned not to waste motion. They do not flourish by speed except in instants: the strike, the leap, the wingbeat, the panic rush through reeds. Most of life there is measured in waiting. A crocodile waits as if it has never known another condition. A heron waits on one black leg above water warm as blood. A cat waits along a fallen trunk, its belly almost touching moss, its spotted hide broken into leaf-shadow. Frogs wait in mud that matches them exactly. Snakes wait as branches. Insects wait as bark, thorns, lichen, rot, seed pods, bird droppings, and dead leaves.

Everything living there has become part of the dark.

The large animals are broad-backed, heat-slick, and mud-armored. Herds move through the flats like slow weather: heavy grazers with swinging heads, blunt muzzles, fly-flicking ears, and hides glazed with clay. Some carry curved horns darkened by rubbing against mineral banks. Some have splayed feet for soft ground. Some are smaller, narrow-shouldered browsers that slip between cane and thorn, taking leaves without sound and vanishing before a predator’s eye completes the shape of them.

They gather at water, but never trust it. Their bodies know every pool has teeth.

At the margins of the blacker pools lie the old reptiles: crocodiles or their close cousins, armored, patient, almost geological. They look less like animals than decisions the water has made. Mud dries along their backs in plates. Leaves settle on them. Dragonflies land on their snouts. Only the eyes remain freshly alive, golden or dull bronze, set just above the surface. They feed not often, but completely. When they move, the basin remembers that stillness was never peace.

The cats belong to the edges: pool edges, light edges, path edges, the border between one animal’s attention and its fatigue. They are low and muscular, painted by shadow rather than color. They use fallen trees as roads above the mud and termite mounds as brief lookouts. Their paws know where bark will hold and where rotten wood will collapse. They do not announce themselves. They leave only prints filling with water, a drag mark through leaves, the sudden silence of monkeys overhead.

The monkeys live higher, but not safely. They are clever with branches and fruiting trees, wary of snakes, cats, and raptors. They travel in bursts, pausing often, listening with their whole bodies. Their hands are always testing: bark, fruit, insect nests, one another’s fur. They know the schedule of figs and the danger of open crossings. When they call, it is often brief and practical — a warning, a claim, a complaint — then the green swallows them again.

Bats own the upper darkness after dusk. During the day they hang in folded clusters beneath branches, in hollow trunks, in the roofs of root caves, wrapped like damp leaves around their own heat. At night they loosen and spill out into the basin’s warm air: fruit bats heavy and deliberate, insect bats flickering through narrow spaces with impossible precision. They carry pollen on their faces and seeds in their bellies. Whole groves are planted by their hunger.

Birds are everywhere, though seldom all at once. Long-legged waders haunt the shallow pans, stabbing at larvae, frogs, and little fish trapped in shrinking water. Parrots arrive as violent color, green and red and yellow breaking the dimness for a moment before becoming leaves again. Nightjars lie on clay like patterns in the clay itself, invisible until their eyes open. Small reed birds make low nests over water and sit in them with the tense stillness of creatures that know how many mouths climb.

There are raptors too, but the canopy denies them grandeur. They hunt through gaps and corridors, dropping suddenly through shafts of greenish light. Their wings are short enough for trees, their eyes made for motion among confusion. They take monkeys, birds, lizards, snakes, whatever exposes itself for one breath too long.

The reptiles are numerous and various. Lizards bask on mineral crusts where a little light gathers. Small ones flick over bark and termite clay, throat skin pulsing. Larger ones patrol the banks, shouldering through leaf litter, tongues tasting rot, egg, blood, and rain. Snakes lie through all levels of the place: vine-thin green ones in branches, heavy patterned ones among roots, water snakes in black runnels, venomous ones coiled under leaf fall where a hoof or hand would be an argument quickly settled.

Amphibians are among the basin’s secret citizens. In the dry heat they seem reduced to memory: tucked into mud cracks, sealed in root hollows, pressed into wet bark, waiting for the atmosphere to change. Then rain comes, and they return as if poured from the ground. Tiny frogs cling to leaves holding beads of water. Broad-mouthed ones sit in puddles no larger than footprints. Tree frogs call from bromeliad cups overhead. Their voices knit the darkness into a living fabric, then stop abruptly when danger passes through.

The fish are survivors of interrupted water. Some live in permanent pools, dark-backed and slow, nosing through leaf tea. Others endure in channels that barely deserve the name, slipping through submerged grass, trapped and released according to rain. In the old hollows there are blind or pale fish that seem more like habits of water than animals, waiting in darkness beneath mats of roots. When pools shrink, they concentrate: fish, tadpoles, insects, birds, reptiles, all drawn into a tightening ring of appetite.

But the true multitude is smaller.

The basin belongs, in number and labor, to insects and their kin.

Ants move through it as if through a city only they can see. Some cut leaves and carry green crescents along roots in disciplined lines. Some raid nests. Some farm fungus underground. Some live in hollow thorns and defend their trees with chemical fury. Their paths cross animal trails, climb trunks, vanish into mud, reappear overhead. They are the basin’s traffic.

Termites are its masons and stomachs. Their mounds stand like blunt, breathing organs of earth: warm inside, ventilated, chambered, busy. They eat the fallen forest and return it changed. Their workers are pale and soft, almost defenseless alone, but together they alter ground, air, and soil. Their soldiers wait in tunnels with hard heads and jaws made for sacrifice. Around them gather lizards, birds, mammals, fungi, roots — whole neighborhoods arranged around termite heat.

Beetles work the hidden trades. Dung beetles roll and bury. Carrion beetles enter the dead. Water beetles flash bronze under filmed pools. Bark beetles write beneath the skin of fallen trees. Grubs turn wood into softness. Weevils drill seeds. Borers hollow stems. Every solid thing is being entered, milled, chewed, softened from within.

Flies and mosquitoes rise whenever water allows them. They breed in hoofprints, tree cups, leaf axils, the smallest held dampness. They are tormentors, pollinators, scavengers, food. Their bodies are brief but innumerable. They stitch together blood, rot, flower, dung, and bird stomach.

Spiders live wherever an opening can be turned into a trap. Webs span root gaps, reed corridors, hollow logs, spaces between buttress roots. Some wait in silk funnels. Some hunt without webs, quick over bark. Some mimic ants. Some sit beside flowers and take the visitors. Their patience is finer than the cats’, but no less predatory.

Leeches wait in the water and wet leaves. Centipedes run under bark with venomous urgency. Millipedes coil in the damp and process decay. Roaches polish the undersides of fallen wood. Mites inhabit fur, feathers, soil, nests, fungi, beetles, one another. The smaller the life becomes, the more impossible it is to count.

And beneath all of them, inside all of them, after all of them, are the fungi and bacteria.

They are the basin’s deepest residents, though they rarely show themselves except as fruiting bodies: pale shelves on logs, threads under bark, little cups in leaf mold, luminous specks in the wet dark, soft eruptions from dung, fans from dead trunks. Their real bodies are hidden networks, patient and penetrating. They enter wood, fruit, skin, carcass, seed. They dissolve the fallen and lend it back to roots. Nothing dead remains singular for long. Death there is not an ending but a sudden opening of employment.

The plants are not scenery. They are the largest inhabitants.

Trees stand crowded and sweating, their crowns locked overhead in a slow contest for light. Palms lift ragged fans into the dim. Figs feed half the basin and strangle their hosts with indifferent generosity. Lianas bind tree to tree until the forest seems less grown than woven. Thorn shrubs defend the drier margins. Reeds occupy wet hollows. Creepers take every low opportunity. Ferns live in forks, on logs, in old wounds. Bromeliads and orchids hold small elevated ponds where mosquitoes breed, frogs hide, and fallen petals rot into miniature soils.

The plants drink, climb, shade, trap, poison, sweeten, shelter, and overgrow. They make the rules the animals obey. They decide where paths can pass, where light can fall, where water lingers, where fruit gathers bodies from every direction.

No creature there is wholly alone. Each animal carries others: ticks in ears, flies at eyes, mites in feathers, seeds in dung, spores on feet, bacteria in gut, parasites in blood. A grazer is also a pasture for insects, a seed vessel, a future carcass, a maker of trails. A tree is also a tower, reservoir, larder, bridge, nursery, and eventual mound of food. A pool is a mouth, a cradle, a mirror, a trap.

If the basin has a native character, it is this: everything is useful to something else.

Its inhabitants are not innocent, but neither are they cruel. They are exact. They live by conserving energy, reading dampness, trusting shade, and answering hunger. They know when to be silent. They know when rain has changed the terms. They know which fruits fall at night, which pools last longest, which mud will hold a print, which log is hollow, which flower opens for moths, which call means mate and which means hawk.

They are creatures of pressure rather than display. Their beauty is often hidden until motion reveals it: the blue flash inside a bird’s wing, the lacquer on a beetle’s back, the pale throat of a frog swelling in rain, the rosettes of a cat becoming visible only when it passes through a shaft of green light.

Nothing there needs monuments because every living thing leaves marks by use.

The residents of that low country are the makers of tunnels through reed, shine on bark, holes in fruit, mud wallows, leaf scars, root dens, dung balls, abandoned nests, polished crossings, bitten bones, fungal blooms, and sudden silences.

They are the basin made animate: dark, hot, crowded, economical, watchful, endlessly feeding and being fed.
