Nothing lives there that would answer to a footstep.

There are no quick, warm bodies in the cracks, no faces turning behind windows, no private animal panic in the lanes. The place has no rats, no gulls, no insects, no fish accidentally promoted into citizens by flooding. Its motion is not the motion of appetite. Its noises are not calls. If it has residents, they are residents by persistence rather than by flesh.

The smallest inhabitants are films.

They live where the city gives them permission: along the lee side of a warm pipe, inside the roughness of a fresh mineral seam, beneath the lip of a filtration rack where current brings food but not enough violence to tear them loose. They are pale, olive, gray-green, almost colorless. Some are algae engineered to photosynthesize under diluted civic light. Some are microbial mats that eat what the filters miss: metal salts, lubricants, shed polymers, the faint organic residue of visitors. Some are more like chemical habits than organisms, self-renewing stains that thicken when the pumps slow and retreat when the water sharpens.

They do not spread lushly. They are disciplined by design and pressure. The city allows them as one allows moss on a stone wall: tolerated, useful in places, watched everywhere. When they overgrow a sensor, a black seam opens and a comb of soft machinery passes through them. When they help bind a crack, they are fed minerals and warmth. They are the closest thing the district has to weeds, and even they seem municipal.

Above them, in the water itself, live the drifters.

Not animals. Not plankton in the old sea sense. They are maintenance motes, transparent and almost weightless, each smaller than a grain of dust, moving in coordinated hazes through streets and rooms. They test salinity. They count corrosion. They taste stress in the membranes. In clear light they are nearly invisible; in shadow they appear as a silver hesitation, a brief thickening of the water, as if the city were thinking too hard in one place.

Some travel alone and fast, carried by signal threads. Some gather in pale clouds around damaged surfaces, where they secrete binder, polish mineral, or call larger mechanisms from the walls. Others are archivists of a kind, carrying tiny fragments of chemical information from reservoir to reservoir. They are not alive by any old definition, but they reproduce their patterns, repair their losses, respond to weather, and avoid the compression seams with something very like caution.

The larger inhabitants are built into the architecture.

They are valves with temperaments. Membranes with memory. Doors that know the density of a body before they open. Handrails that warm by a fraction when gripped. Walls that breathe water through themselves, taking in rainwater on one side and exhaling tuned brine on the other. Pressure ribs flex in slow increments, too small to see unless one watches a hairline crack for hours. Transparent bulkheads cloud and clear like eyes sleeping open.

These are the city’s sessile creatures: rooted, patient, half-asleep until needed. They do not move through streets because they are streets. They feed on gradients, runoff, heat differentials, civic obligation. They shed scale, grow mineral skin, seal wounds, and trade data through faint glints in the liquid. A person from above might call them infrastructure. Down here, infrastructure is the native body plan.

There are also walkers, though they do not walk.

They are maintenance forms that emerge from housings under stairs, from the backs of kiosks, from vertical slots in intake towers. Most are not shaped like people. A few are jointed frames with gripping pads and ballast sacs, able to pull themselves hand over hand along rails. Others are flexible black loops, eel-like only in silhouette, made to thread conduits and scrape growth from pressure seams. Some resemble collapsed umbrellas or folded cages until the current lifts them open and their vanes catch the flow.

They do not hurry unless a seal fails.

They cross lanes by letting currents take them. They anchor themselves to pipes during rain reversals. They hang beneath bridges and clean the undersides with patient rotary mouths. They gather in small, silent knots near the central rain column after storms, sampling grit from the upper world. If damaged, they do not limp away; they attach to a wall, become still, and wait for motes to dismantle or rebuild them.

They are the city’s visible servants, but not its masters.

The mind of the place is distributed.

It lives in the membranes, the water, the signal threads, the municipal clock nobody trusts, the sealed kiosks, the old shopfronts, the ballast engines below. It is not one intelligence sitting in a central chamber. It is a population of overlapping custodies. One watches pressure. One remembers rainfall. One tends the young stone. One listens to structural pain. One regulates what may grow. One keeps maps of corridors that change length in the folded edges. One predicts when a stair will need to become a cascade.

They speak through current and chime.

Their language is partly hydraulic: a pulse in a conduit, a reversed flow, a pressure drop too precise to be accidental. It is partly optical: angled flashes along data threads, pale inscriptions crawling over the insides of glass. It is partly material: a wall thickening where the city expects future stress, a door refusing to open because the water beyond has not yet become safe. To them, weather is news. Sediment is testimony. Corrosion is injury. A human visitor is a temporary irregularity to be buoyed, warmed, guided, and prevented from dying.

If there are locals, they are not numerous.

They would not live there the way people once lived in dry apartments above streets. They would arrive sealed, altered, or assisted: workers in pressure skins, divers whose lungs are optional only because machines make them so, custodians who know how to read a chime through their bones. They would have slow gestures, practical hands, and an instinctive respect for currents. They would pause before entering a corridor, not from superstition but because permission here is physical. They would know which rail hum means wait, which white turbulence means climb, which sudden warmth means a purge is coming.

They would not own rooms in the old sense. They would keep stations: niches with tools locked to the walls, sleep bladders behind density gates, lockers full of dry relics that can no longer be safely opened below. Their domestic life would be brief and ritualized. A meal would be taken in a sealed bulb. A conversation would happen palm-to-palm through gloves, or by bone conduction, or by text glimmering across a visor. They would know the central rain column as others know a town square, a church bell, a window.

But even they would feel like visitors.

The true citizen is the drowned district itself: not a metaphorical organism, not exactly, but a working ecology of water, stone, computation, and repair. Its life is maintenance. Its hunger is for balance. It consumes rain, pressure, heat, grit, salt, and failure. It excretes clean water, stored memory, mineral skin, and regulated motion. It sleeps only in sections. It dreams in maps of stress.

It is young, and that youth shows in everything that lives there.

The stones have not been softened into forgiveness. The algae have not learned abundance. The machines have not acquired the theatrical rust of old industry. Even the city’s intelligence feels newly tasked, exacting and watchful, still learning which corners collect grief and which seams misbehave under storm load. Nothing has had time to become picturesque. Survival here is crisp-edged, engineered, almost shy.

So the inhabitants are these:

thin green threads clinging close to warmth;

microbial films doing quiet chemistry in the dark;

silver motes tasting the water for change;

maintenance bodies folding and unfolding in the lanes;

walls that heal, pipes that listen, stairs that become rivers;

distributed civic minds keeping pressure from becoming catastrophe;

and, rarely, human figures at the cold rain pillar, hands extended into falling weather, less like residents than petitioners.

The place is not empty.

It is simply inhabited by things that do not look back.
