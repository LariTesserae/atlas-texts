What lives there does not live by water.

It lives by difference: between shade and exposure, stress and release, near and far, before and after. It feeds on gradients so slight a human body would mistake them for stillness. A cooling seam in a tower wall. A grain of salt swelling in a crack. A century loosening from the west face of a stone while the east face remains newborn. These are enough.

The smallest inhabitants are nearly indistinguishable from the city’s own maintenance.

The black crusts in the deepest shade are alive, but only in the way a refusal can be alive. They are not plants. They do not seek light; they endure it as damage. They spread outward in circles so slowly their growth resembles an error in the stone’s memory. Their edges etch the rock, digesting polish, salt, mica, the faint mineral residue of passing storms. They leave halos paler than the surrounding wall, as if they have eaten not matter but certainty.

They have no softness. If broken, they do not bleed or wilt. They fracture into brittle flakes, each flake capable of becoming another patient stain if it falls into the right kind of shadow. They communicate, if they communicate, through absence: by altering the way walls weather, by changing the paths of dust, by marking surfaces the tall ones avoid.

The pale filaments in the courtyards are less committed to being organisms. Sometimes they are fungal threads, sometimes salt nerves, sometimes cracks that have learned hesitation. They cross from flagstone to flagstone in the cold hours, tasting mineral differences. If watched too directly, they retreat into explanation. One moment they are strands; the next, ordinary scratches; the next, nothing but veins in old stone. They seem to survive by never remaining one category long enough to be consumed by any law.

The clicking creatures are more plainly animal, though even they appear assembled from permissions granted reluctantly by the basin.

They are low, translucent, many-jointed things, the size of a hand or smaller. Their shells are not shells exactly, but layered mineral windows through which their interiors can sometimes be seen: faint rods, folding membranes, little dark knots that pulse without liquid. They have no eyes that can be found. They perceive pressure, temperature, shadow, and attention. A footstep far away will send them flat against the stone. A gaze will stop them completely, as if vision has weight.

They come out after the day’s last warmth has abandoned the salt flats. Then they move in small processions along cracks and under ledges, clicking so faintly that the sound seems to happen inside the teeth of anyone listening. They graze on films: mineral blooms, crust spores, mica dust, the dry residue left after the city has rejected breath or blood or weather. Their mouthparts scrape without hurry. They do not drink. They have no use for liquid; moisture would probably kill them, dissolving the delicate salt hinges by which their bodies negotiate themselves.

Their burrows are narrow, slanting, and communal. Whole colonies live inside seams between strata, arranged not by family but by temperature. The young are almost invisible: clear flecks with legs, mistaken for chips of quartz until they move. The old become cloudy and opaque, their shells thickening with accumulated city. When they die, they do not rot. They remain in place, and their bodies become additional gravel, edible to their descendants.

They fear the tall ones.

The tall ones are not predators in any ordinary sense. They do not chase, seize, tear, or feed. But wherever they pass, small life is erased from schedule. The clicking creatures will abandon a rich wall for days if one of the tall ones has crossed beneath it. The crusts cease expanding along certain avenues. Dust lies flatter. Shadows behave.

The tall ones are the closest things to citizens.

They are seldom fully present. At a distance, they seem like narrow figures cut from the same faceted stone as the towers, though they move with a slowness too deliberate to be collapse. Nearer, if nearness can be trusted, they are less like bodies than vertical agreements among planes: long hinged columns, oblique shoulders, appendages that bend where no joint should be, heads or headlike terminations that never turn and yet attend.

Their surfaces are matte, pale, and striated. Some carry dark seams down their length, like old cracks filled with night. Others are almost translucent at their edges, thinning into air. They have no faces. They may have antlers, but only under certain lights: branching structures of shadow or stress that rise from them, vanish, and return in different configurations. Sometimes those branches resemble eroded colonnades. Sometimes they resemble dried river systems. Sometimes they resemble written language before meaning enters it.

They move by altering the relationship between places.

One does not see a tall one take a step so much as notice that it has become farther along an avenue. Its lower limbs, if limbs they are, touch the ground without pressure. No footprints remain. Yet after it passes, walls appear older. A lintel loses an edge. A stair becomes shallow from use it never received. Fresh weathering blooms along the route, years spent in an instant. It may be that the tall ones feed on unspent duration, rubbing time from surfaces and carrying it elsewhere.

They gather after storms, standing in intersections, plazas, and dry markets with the solemnity of officials after a disaster. They do not repair damage. They do not inspect it. They listen to the new geometry until it becomes lawful. Then, one by one, they withdraw into distances, archways, colonnades, or the vertical faces of towers.

Whether they are animals, machines, weather, or the city’s own ambulatory thoughts is not clear. The distinction may be local to wetter worlds.

Above them live the high crossings.

These are the birdlike shapes that pass over the dim sun. They may not be birds. They may not be alive continuously. Their bodies vanish between wingbeats, leaving only slow dark articulations against the mica haze. Their shadows are not synchronized with them; a shadow may sweep a plaza minutes before the body appears, or linger after the crossing has ended.

The high ones seem to nest in altitude rather than on stone. No ledge bears their droppings, feathers, eggs, or bones. Perhaps they feed on scale, descending only far enough to draw distance out of the basin and carry it beyond the cliffs. Perhaps they are not creatures but recurring gaps in the sky shaped by whatever passes for migration in that height.

Still, the city notices them. When one crosses, the upper terraces lighten slightly. Dust pauses in the air. The tall ones, if visible, become more architectural. The clicking creatures freeze under stone shelves until the delayed shadow has gone.

There are also inhabitants too large or too diffuse to be counted.

The avenues themselves may live. At night, the long polished grooves along their centers seem less like marks left by traffic than channels through which the city circulates its own mass. Towers shifted from their foundations may not have been dragged by anything external. They may have migrated, slowly and with purpose, from one district to another, following stresses, alignments, memories of processions that never required bodies.

The pits in the ringed plazas are alive in a more dangerous way. They are mouths that do not open and eyes that do not look upward. They do not consume matter; dropped stones never return because falling becomes irrelevant inside them. What they take is position. Leaning over one, a visitor is briefly removed from the ground and shown the city from above, before, after, or elsewhere. The pits may be organs by which the basin remembers its possible forms: bare salt, crowded shadow, endless white plain.

The city itself is the dominant life.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. It has laws of metabolism. It rejects water. It suppresses flame. It sheds names. It exhales powder at dusk and reabsorbs definition by noon. Its districts rise and settle like slow ribs. Its staircases alter destination with the modesty of muscles shifting under skin. Its walls open seams at dawn, glowing with buried moonlight, then close again before heat can enter too deeply.

It does not think as animals think. It thinks by weathering. A crack is a question. A collapsed arch is a decision postponed for ten thousand years. A plaza rotating imperceptibly against the mountains is a change of attention. Its silence is not emptiness but nervous tissue made of spacing, cold, and stone.

The tall ones may be its mobile organs. The clicking creatures may be its scavengers. The crusts may be its skin flora. The high crossings may be parasites of its sky-reflection. Or all of these may be independent lives that have adapted to an ecology where matter is negotiable and water is forbidden.

Nothing there is born wet. Nothing there dies wet.

Birth, in that basin, is a crystallization, a separation, a seam deciding to become an edge. Death is not decay but loss of distinction. A creature becomes gravel. A wall becomes slope. A name becomes weather. An avenue becomes a former avenue and continues functioning.

What lives there is patient, faceted, and nearly empty. It has learned to inhabit absence as other beings inhabit flesh. It moves slowly because speed would require a more stable world. It eats mineral films, old time, pressure, shadow, and the residue of impossible measurements. It avoids flame, refuses water, and treats footsteps as temporary defects in dust.

And beneath all the smaller lives, before and after them, the city remains: not abandoned, not inhabited in any human sense, but occupied by its own ongoing becoming. It is a settlement whose residents are processes that have taken on bodies only where bodies are useful. It waits without expectation because expectation is too liquid a thing.

At dawn it opens. At dusk it rearranges. At night it speaks in ticks and stresses across miles of empty streets.

That is what lives there: the dry life of stone learning, very slowly, how to be a city.
