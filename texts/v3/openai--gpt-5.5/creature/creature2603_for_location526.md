Nothing here is native in the old sense.

The first residents are films.

They are the thin, patient lives that make every hard surface less certain of itself: green-brown algae on lamp-warmed walls, bacterial slicks in the seams of ceramic panels, pale mineral skins over concrete, rust-colored blooms around bolt heads. They occupy the place before any larger animal understands it. They turn glare into sugar, metal into chemistry, leaks into gardens. They make the sharp world slightly furred. Underwater lights have become their suns; heat exchangers, their springs; slow corners behind baffles, their meadows.

Where the light is steady, the walls are grazed clean in patches by small snails, shrimp, and translucent little fish. Where the light fails, the films thicken into darker mats, fed by oil traces, dissolved metals, filter waste, and the soft rain of dead plankton falling through the engineered sea. Some of these mats pulse faintly when disturbed, not with intelligence but with chemistry: colonies reacting to vibration, pressure, temperature, current. They are maps of the machine’s habits. They grow thickest where pumps rest, thin where turbulence bites, bright where lamps remain on longer than their designers intended.

Then come the fixed animals.

Barnacles live here as if the whole place were one enormous hull. They crust over ladders, railings, intake grates, submerged signs, the undersides of catwalks, the ribs of old access bridges. Mussels hang in black-blue bunches from mesh fences and cable trays, sealing gaps, narrowing drains, turning clean engineering into reef. In some service corridors, their shells form waist-high ridges along the walls, so that a passage built for maintenance crawlers has become a canyon of living knives.

They do not know the difference between a cliff and a bulkhead. They know only current, food, chemistry, vibration, light. A low-vibration tower becomes a safe anchorage. A warm pipe becomes a breeding coast. A warning beacon becomes a moon.

They are the masons of the new sea. They take the machine’s straight edges and scallop them. They make shelter where there was only specification. Small crabs live among them, wedging themselves into hinge housings and under inspection plates. Pale shrimp move backward through forests of valve stems. Worms lace the sediment in drainage channels. Tiny amphipods swarm inside the hollow square beams of drowned railings, turning structural steel into apartment blocks.

The fish are the most visible inhabitants, because they understand open space.

They come in fast, reflective schools: silver, blue, glassy, yellow-eyed. Fry collect beneath ceiling lights in trembling clouds, each body catching the artificial glow for an instant and losing it. They gather under maintenance apertures where insects sometimes fall from dry galleries, under warm outflows where plankton blooms, beside aeration columns where microbubbles lift food through the water like snow in reverse.

Their movements make the emptiness legible. A school bends around an invisible current. Another vanishes downward where a return flow begins. A third erupts into scattered sparks when a lock shudders open in the distance. To watch them is to see the machinery translated into fear, appetite, and habit.

Some are small, nervous fish that live by the lamps. They have learned the timing of illumination sequences, moving ahead of artificial dawn as it travels across the ceiling. They rise when the white strips brighten, feed in the plankton-rich upper water, then sink again when maintenance shadows pass overhead. Generations of them may never see sunlight, but their bodies still obey light as law.

Others are heavier, dark-backed, slow in the service trenches. They patrol the painted arrows on drowned roadways and lurk beneath grated platforms. They wait near sluice mouths, where stunned smaller fish tumble through during gate changes. Their territories are not reefs or river bends but pump shadows, expansion joints, turbine exclusion zones, the calmer lee of a ballast tower. They know by pressure when a door is going to open. They know by vibration when food is about to arrive.

Eels live in the conduits.

They are well suited to this world because the world is full of tubes, gaps, sleeves, drains, vents, cable runs, and inspection holes. They thread through dead pipelines and half-flooded junction boxes, appearing suddenly from places that seem too narrow for any animal with bones. Some are mottled gray and brown, almost invisible against rust stains. Others are pale from deep tunnels where light rarely enters. They hunt crabs in the cable trays and small fish in the darkness behind intake screens.

The eels give the place a sense of hidden continuity. A creature can enter a crack in one wall and emerge days later from a valve assembly half a kilometer away. The architecture has become burrow.

Jellyfish occupy the slow basins.

They drift where current is weak and the water is full of suspended light. They pulse beside depth markers, around buoy chains, under hanging crane hooks. In the broad chambers they seem almost architectural themselves: translucent domes, faint trailing threads, soft bodies moving among hard structures. Some bloom in great numbers after warm discharge events, filling a reservoir with ghostly bells. Pumps tear many apart. Others pass through screens as shredded lace and become food for smaller things.

They are the closest the place comes to dreaming. Everything else reacts sharply to gates and alarms. The jellyfish merely answer gradients: salinity, warmth, plankton, stillness. They make no distinction between a sacred abyss and an overflow basin.

Crabs are everywhere there is edge.

They live under grates, inside abandoned tool lockers, around anchor bolts, in the shadow beneath stair treads that disappear into green depth. Their shells darken with industrial stain. Some carry flakes of paint and algae on their backs. Some have learned to pry at mussels attached to railings; others feed on dead fish trapped against intake mesh. At night-cycle, when many lamps dim and the water becomes a layered gloom, they emerge in numbers, climbing the sides of service platforms and picking over sediment drifts of concrete dust, shell fragments, and iron grit.

Their world is vertical. A wall is a floor if claws can grip it. A ladder is a reef. A warning sign is shelter. They move through human instructions without reading them, over words like DANGER, FLOW CONTROL, ACCESS POINT, SUBMERGED CLEARANCE, making those words decorative.

There are predators that enter from outside.

Seals, or things close enough to seals that the distinction matters only to biologists, come through the intake locks when timing and hunger allow. They are temporary monarchs of the upper water: sleek, warm-blooded, bright-eyed, shockingly quick among the columns. They hunt beneath light panels and along the edges of open basins, scattering schools into silver explosions. Their calls travel strangely through the chambers, bouncing from concrete to steel to water to air pocket and back again until one animal sounds like many.

They do not live here always. They visit, learn routes, remember gate rhythms. Some become specialists, returning to the same warm outlet or fry-lit ceiling channel. They surface in maintenance caverns, breathing in air that smells of salt, oil, and ozone, then vanish before drones arrive. Workers, if any still walk the dry galleries, know them as unauthorized fauna. The sea knows them as weather with teeth.

There are also birds in the upper pockets where air persists.

Not many. Gulls sometimes enter through exterior openings and become confused by the false horizon of roof lights over water. Small dark birds nest on unreachable beams in condensation zones, feeding on insects hatched from algae mats and filtration ponds. Their droppings mark handrails and control housings. Their calls are thin compared to the pumps. They seem accidental, yet they too have found a way to use the place: a ceiling for a cliff, a catwalk for a ledge, a maintenance lamp for morning.

In the warmest industrial margins, stranger communities gather.

Around thermal vents, where the water is several degrees richer and minerals leach from old systems, life grows faster and less neatly. Mats of bacteria form orange and white veils. Tubeworms cluster on cracked housings. Fish hang in vertical columns, facing into the outflow, taking food as it comes. Shrimp pick at the edges of chemical crust. The water there smells different even underwater, sharp with metal and heat. These zones are small artificial estuaries, not made by rivers but by waste energy.

The animals treat them as oases. Predators treat them as ambush sites.

Everything has learned the alarms.

The red lamps send the smaller fish outward before the pump starts. The crabs withdraw their claws from exposed seams. Eels turn deeper into pipework. Barnacles close. Mussels tighten. Jellyfish do nothing and are torn or spared by chance. When the blue lamps begin and aeration columns wake, fry gather in the rising bubbles. When white strobes flash before pressure equalization, the more experienced fish vanish from the throat of the gate, while young ones remain too long and are flung into the next basin.

This is how natural selection speaks here: not through seasons, not through tides, but through signal color, vibration pattern, schedule drift, maintenance outage, emergency overflow.

The young of many species are born into a world where machinery is older than memory. A fish hatched beneath a ceiling strip does not consider the light artificial. A crab grown under hazard paint does not know rock. A mussel attached to an intake grille does not know a tidepool. The machine is not an intrusion to them. It is geology.

And the machine itself is inhabited by non-animal lives.

Drones crawl the rails and walls with a stubborn, insect patience. Scrapers move over intake screens, shaving away mussel spat and algae. Welding units flare blue-white in the depths, attracting fish that scatter at the heat. Inspection vehicles travel the bottom trenches with small yellow lamps, slow as grazing animals. Some are tethered. Some are autonomous. Some have been operating long enough that barnacles grow on their casings and shrimp shelter in their recessed joints.

They do not live in the biological sense, but the ecology includes them. Fish follow them for disturbed food. Crabs learn to avoid their tracks. Algae vanishes where they pass and returns after. A drone stalled under a platform becomes a reef within weeks, then a mound, then a territory. Its inactive limbs shelter eels. Its camera eyes cloud over with mineral film. Its purpose decays into habitat.

If humans remain, they are among the rarest creatures.

They live in the dry pockets, in sweating galleries and pressure rooms above the green water. They move behind glass, along catwalks, inside service capsules. To the animals they are irregular disturbances: bootfalls, lamp beams, dropped tools, silhouettes crossing the ceiling. Their voices arrive underwater as muffled impacts. Their maintenance schedules shape entire feeding cycles, yet their bodies are seldom seen.

They are not the masters the architecture remembers. They are caretakers, inspectors, trespassers in their own interior ocean. They smell of fabric, plastic, skin, disinfectant, old coffee. When they descend in suits, fish inspect them and flee; seals watch them with bold suspicion; crabs raise claws at their lights. The humans still have names for every sector, but the living things have renamed them by use.

There is no single great beast at the center.

The hook hanging beneath the crane does not bait a leviathan. The planetary basin does not hide one enormous eye. The size of the place suggests monsters, but what lives there is more distributed and more convincing: billions of small hungers, colonies, routes, reflexes, nests, shells, stains, alarms remembered in nerves. The sea’s creature is the system of creatures.

It lives as silver schools turning under roof-light.

It lives as mussels sealing a door that was meant to open.

It lives as algae making dawn out of inspection lamps.

It lives as eels in conduits, seals in locks, crabs on hazard stripes, shrimp in cable trays, jellyfish in the slow green hush.

It lives as microbial fur on concrete too new to be old and too wet to remain purely built.

And beneath all of them, around all of them, the machinery breathes. Pumps draw, gates shift, lights roll on, currents reverse. The inhabitants do not merely survive these movements; they read them, exploit them, breed inside them. The place has not become wild by escaping its design. It has become wild by accepting design as climate.

What lives there is a young, opportunistic, mechanical sea-life: not ancient, not lush, not innocent. It is sharp-edged and quick-learning. It has shells on steel, fins in service roads, roots in gasket seams, nests in condensation, hunger timed to warning strobes.

It is life making a coastline out of every wall.
