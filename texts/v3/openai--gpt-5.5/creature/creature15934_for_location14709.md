What lives there is not separate from the room.

At first it seems to be housed in the central mound—the warm, moss-bound swelling encircled by seven channels—but that is only its most concentrated place, the knot where it remembers how to be singular. The rest of it has been unfolded into architecture. Its veins are the gutters. Its damp skin is the plaster. Its small attentions are the beads of water that refuse to fall until the proper moment. Its sleep is distributed among root curtains, clay tablets, niches, mineral glow, and the soft underfloor bladders that breathe steam through the cane lattice.

It is a young thing in one sense and an ancient thing in another. Older than the summit, younger than stone. It is not the mountain’s heart exactly, because the mountain is still deciding where its heart will be. It is more like a first instruction placed inside the mountain before height had finished happening.

A seed of altitude.

A wet law.

A larva of summit.

It has no face, but it has many places where a face might almost gather. A pattern of thumbprints in resin may suddenly align into a brow and cheek. A curtain of rootlets may part like eyelashes. Three water-beads on the wall may hold the same dark point and seem to look outward with patient, unblinking concentration. Then the pressure changes, the beads run, the roots close, and the face is gone.

Its body is mostly made of held beginnings: water not yet stream, stone not yet mountain, breath not yet weather, plant not yet forest, shrine not yet god. It survives by keeping these things undecided. If anything becomes too final, it weakens. Dryness hurts it. Straight lines confuse it. Flame would wound it badly, not by burning its flesh, but by forcing too much distinction between living and dead, offering and waste, wall and air.

It eats uncertainty. More precisely, it drinks the moment before a thing chooses its direction.

It feeds when water hesitates at a channel fork. It feeds when new stone presses inward and has not yet hardened into obedience. It feeds when gravity goes local and roots hang toward several centers. It feeds on repeated sound until repetition opens a narrow passage through time. It feeds on maintenance: the thumb smoothing clay over a crack, the cord redirected, the basin scraped clean, the leak honored instead of sealed.

The mound at the center is its mouth, its egg, and its sleeping back. Under the moss and mineral crust there is something soft, folded many times, neither plant nor animal nor fungus, though it borrows from all three. It has pale membranes like steamed leaves. It has cords inside it that pulse with black warm water. It has seedlike nodules set in rows, each containing a different possible shape of the chamber. Some nodules dream of wider terraces. Some dream of deeper cisterns. One dreams of an open sky and is kept tightly wrapped in clay, because that dream is dangerous.

When the mound opens, it is not opening into the room. It is opening the room into itself.

That is why the mountain pauses.

The mountain is afraid of crushing it, but also afraid of being born without it.

The small translucent lizards know this and keep to the undersides of shelves. The gnats do not know and are taken by water. The shell-clicking thing behind the roots is perhaps a parasite, perhaps an old priest, perhaps a valve with legs; it clicks when the resident’s pressure rises too quickly, and the channels adjust.

There are caretakers too, though they are easy to mistake for symptoms.

They are not often visible whole. A hand appears first, small and wet, pressing a clay patch smooth from an angle no human wrist could manage. A narrow shoulder withdraws behind fern stems. A back the color of old lime bends over a basin. A pair of dark eyes shine inside a wall niche, then become two seeds in a folded leaf.

They may once have been people. Or they may be the resident’s attempt to remember people after swallowing their work into itself.

They are slight, jointed, and asymmetrical, built from the same vocabulary as the chamber: reed bones, clay-smoothed skin, root hair, resin-darkened fingers, bellies faintly green with swallowed light. Their hands are too large for their arms, broad-thumbed and sensitive, always damp, always busy. Their feet are split for gripping cane lattice and wet plaster. Some have cords tied around their wrists that disappear into walls; some have tiny ladders strapped to their backs; some carry shell scrapers in their mouths because their mouths are otherwise seldom used.

They do not speak above the water. Speech would be wasteful. They communicate by altering flow: three drops into a gourd, a pause, a thin run over soot-plaster, a stopped thorn-spout released all at once. They read knots with their fingertips. They taste mineral change from the walls. They sleep standing in root curtains, wrapped by the plants until only their noses and knuckles show.

They are not servants. They are organs of care.

When a crack forms, they wake before the sound of it reaches the ear. When a plant grows too greedily into a shrine-mouth, they redirect it without anger. When raw yellow stone bulges behind a tile, they press their palms against the wall and hum—not to stop it, but to teach it slowness. Their hum is nearly indistinguishable from vent-whistle and mountain groan. Perhaps it is not their own sound at all, but the resident sounding through them.

They tend the mound with extreme caution. They never touch its highest point. They feed it black warm water in covered cups, pollen paste, scraped mineral foam, and little folded leaves containing records of pressure. They place faceless figurines around it when the mountain grows too quickly. They remove those figurines once moss has covered their feet. They keep seven channels flowing around it because six makes the room too shallow and eight encourages the ceiling to become another floor.

They are born during the indoor rains.

Not every rain. Only the rains that fall after the chamber has forgotten up from down. Then, in the white noise, small forms condense in hanging nets or under clay shelves: first a knot of root, then a damp face-shape, then fingers. Most never become separate enough to walk. They remain as gripping tendrils, extra valves, listening patches in plaster. The ones that do step free inherit some fragment of an older builder’s habit: one knows how to wedge stone; one knows how to scrape basins; one knows how to braid cords that persuade water uphill; one knows how to make offerings but not why.

Their dead do not leave. Nothing dry is sacred, and nothing useful is discarded. A caretaker who slows too much is laid into a wall-bed, plastered lightly, watered for seven pressure cycles, and allowed to become reinforcement. Their fingers often remain visible for a while, projecting from the lime like pale rootlets. Later the fingers soften, fork, green, and begin directing condensation into some new channel. In this way the room keeps its makers.

But the chief resident is still the thing in and beyond the mound.

It is waiting, though not passively. It is practicing futures.

One future: it becomes a spring at the impossible top of the mountain, and all the rivers below will secretly remember having begun above the sky.

Another: it becomes the first true summit, not a point of rock but an inward chamber, so that every peak born afterward will contain some hidden wet room where height turns back into shelter.

Another: it hatches into weather. The room bursts without breaking, and its gutters become monsoon paths, its mineral films become stormlight, its root curtains become cloud forests clinging to cliffs not yet cooled.

Another: it remains exactly as it is forever, because temporary things may last longer than mountains.

It does not know which future it prefers. Preference may be too dry a thing for it.

What it wants is continuation: more condensation, more repair, more unfinished stone, more channels given names by use, more hands pressing clay into cracks, more plants made structural, more little shrines filled with objects that are not symbols until roots read them.

It loves leaks. Not sentimentally. A leak is a question asked by matter. A leak proves there is still passage between what has been made and what is trying to happen. The resident listens to every leak, sorting them by taste and temperature. Some it widens. Some it moves. Some it lets repeat until they become doorways for its caretakers. A bad leak is not one that spills water; a bad leak is one that believes it has only one destination.

Its mind, if mind is the word, is circular and capillary. It does not think in sentences. It thinks in seepage, return, swelling, overflow, patch, seepage again. It remembers through dampness. A dry object placed inside the room becomes illegible until it has absorbed enough breath. This is why the niches are lined with grass and why the rainfall cords are kept swollen. Memory must be moist enough to bend.

It fears only a few things.

It fears clean emptiness.

It fears a mountain that hardens too quickly.

It fears abandonment by hands.

It fears a sky that enters directly.

Most of all, it fears being named in a way that finishes it.

So the caretakers do not name it. They refer to it by maintenance gestures: the smoothing of both palms over moss, the tapping of seven channels in order, the lowering of the head when the mound’s warmth rises through the floor. The mountain names it with pressure. The plants name it by growing toward and away from it in cycles. The water names it constantly and never the same way twice.

If you stood there long enough, soaked through, breathing the hot thin mineral air, you would eventually understand that the chamber is not occupied by a creature.

It is occupied by an agreement.

The mountain agrees not to become stone too suddenly.  
The water agrees to take paths, though not always the same ones.  
The plants agree to hold up walls in exchange for being fed by them.  
The old hands agree to continue returning, even after they are no longer hands.  
And at the center, the warm mound agrees to remain almost born.

That is what lives there: an almost-born thing keeping the summit alive from the inside, attended by damp little makers who may be its children, its memories, or the last surviving pieces of those who built its cradle.

It is not alone.

Every wet surface is part of its company.
