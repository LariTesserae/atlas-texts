What lives there is not a single tribe or a single species, but a listening arrangement: plant, animal, stone, tool, and person all pressed into one dry, resonant society.

The people are few.

They are small and narrow-bodied, with the look of things that have learned not to waste surface. Their limbs are long, their joints prominent, their ribs visible not through hunger but design. Their skin is not soft skin as lowland people know it. It is matte and sealed, rubbed daily with violet oil and powdered salt until it becomes a flexible rind, gray-brown, ocher, or greenish-black depending on age and work. In the old ones it cracks in fine pale maps around the eyes and knuckles. They do not sweat. They shed dust.

Their mouths are usually closed.

Speech, here, is dangerous and expensive. The hollow hears too well. A shouted word can come back from the copper fans wearing the wrong intention. A careless laugh can wake the gas-vines. So the people speak mostly with teeth, tongue-clicks, finger taps, reed-whistles, and the small percussion of bone rings worn at wrist and throat. Their language is half gesture, half vibration. A sentence may be drummed against a sitting stone, scraped along a slate wall, clicked into a hollow seed pod, or breathed through a pierced horn-reed until it becomes a thin, questioning tone.

They have large ears, or seem to. Not pointed or animal-like, but widened by ornaments: shell hoops, mica plates, little carved resonators tied behind the lobes. Children wear no such things until they have learned which sounds are merely wind and which are warnings. The first lesson of childhood is not walking or naming. It is stillness. A child is placed on the swept ground beneath the seven trees and taught to feel the difference between root-pressure, insect-drum, distant stonefall, and the slow low note that means the oldest pods are filling.

Their eyes are dark, often filmed with a coppery shine from the dust. They blink sideways with the help of a translucent inner lid, a trait not entirely inherited and not entirely made. Some line their eyelids with black resin to keep the electric grit from burning them. Some wear masks of beaten plant-hide when the pollen storms rise: narrow masks with mouth-slits like gills and cheek chambers packed with comb-fiber. The masks make them look more insect than human, but without them they would spend half their lives coughing up yellow powder and glittering mica.

They do not drink.

They eat what the place permits: resin scraped from armored shrubs, mineral cakes made from powdered husk and alkali, roasted beetles, lizard flesh dried to the texture of bark, pale root-mat rodents split and cured in hot ash. They chew salt-bloom mixed with sour plant gum. They inhale certain vapors from cut reeds, not for intoxication, or not only for it, but because the body needs volatile warmth the way other bodies need water. Their cooking is careful and mostly flameless. Heat is taken from stones left in vents, from covered kilns, from black glass slabs that hold the sun long after dusk. Open flame is a tool of last resort, handled with the solemnity other peoples reserve for knives near the throat.

They are not many enough to fill the huts. Perhaps nine live there now. Perhaps thirteen. It is hard to count them because they move like parts of the terrain and because the old ones spend long periods underground in the warm fissures, listening to the knocks below. Outsiders might think some of them are dead when they are only resting in the dry way: wrapped in plant-hide, powdered over, heartbeat slowed, hands tucked beneath the ribs to conserve their inner heat.

Their children are rare and cherished uneasily.

A child may be born in the ordinary fashion, though even that is surrounded by rules. Birth is done during the low resonance of the trees, when all sharp noises are softened into order. The mother squats on a ring of warm stones. The others hum through closed mouths. The newborn does not cry if it is wise. If it cries, someone quickly places a hollow pod near its lips so the cry enters the pod and returns as a smaller sound.

But not all children come from bodies.

Sometimes, when the seven trees align and a pod cracks, what falls is not an animal, not a seed, not a tool, but a folded little person lacquered in resin, curled tight around a cord of dry green fiber. Such children are called returned ones. They are accepted, but watched. They often know songs no one taught them. They dream of paths beneath the basin floor. Their handprints, when pressed in ocher on the trunks, show too many lines.

No one asks whether the trees made them or remembered them.

The people live as caretakers, scavengers, interpreters, and tolerated parasites of the hollow. They prune the horn-reeds with obsidian blades, but only after tapping the stalks to ask which ones are hollow with pressure. They gather shed reed-shells for roofing. They collect bladder-vine skins after the birds have punctured them and stretch the membranes into bags, drums, and sleeping wraps. They sweep the ground beneath the seven trees not because the trees require cleanliness, but because anything left there will tremble outward anyway, and it is better to know what the trees have rejected.

Their huts are not homes in the comforting sense. They are wind-breaks, tool-stores, memory chambers. Inside are low platforms covered with woven scale-leaves, stacks of ceramic bowls, bundles of scraping tools, masks hung face-inward, and strings of labeled bones. The labels are not written words but knots, cuts, beads, and tiny resonant stones. Each object has a sound by which it is known. A dead grandmother’s awl, struck lightly, gives a dull three-part click. A forbidden seedpod answers with a whining tone and is kept in a clay jar under slate.

They own very little metal and fear it slightly. Copper hooks are used for work that cannot be trusted to bone. The bronze bell is not rung by hand because it does not belong to hands. It is the hollow’s mouth. When it sounds once, people look to the rim. When it sounds twice, they cover the amber curtains. When it trembles without striking, they go to the sitting stones and wait for the seven trees.

Their dead are not buried.

There is no moist earth to take them. There is no rot of the ordinary kind. The dead are dried in warm updrafts, rubbed with resin, and placed for a season among the ivory combs, where small lacquer-backed animals clean the last softness from them. Afterward the bones are sorted. Finger bones become chimes. Ribs become roof braces. Skulls, if the person was a listener, are hung near the old boundary posts with the mouth facing inward, so the hollow may continue to speak through them. Those who feared the trees in life are carried to the black stones with white rings and left there whole, wrapped and uncut. No one explains this to children until they have heard knocking from beneath the ground.

They are religious, though not in a way that separates worship from practical caution.

Their gods, if gods is the right word, are behaviors. Static that arrives before dusk. The bitter taste before a pollen column forms. The kneeling of the shovel-headed grazer. The bad echo under the copper fans. The seven trees’ resonance. A god is a pattern strong enough to survive generations. A prayer is an action performed correctly in the presence of danger.

They do not ask the seven trees for mercy. They ask them for sequence.

What comes first? Wind, spark, seed-fire, or podfall? Which fissure will breathe pepper-iron today? Which gas-vine is full enough to lift a sleeping bird but not full enough to burst? Which child’s voice has been stolen by the fans? Which black-ringed stone has begun to warm from within?

Their priests are called, by outsiders, the Tooth-Hearers, because they listen with their jaws pressed to stone. They file their teeth flat and inlay some with small bits of obsidian, not for beauty but for conduction. During the deep tones of the trees they sit with mouths slightly open, letting the pressure enter the skull. Afterward they draw patterns in pale powder: spirals, branching faults, hooked bird-tracks, rows of mouths. These drawings determine when to harvest, when to seal the huts, when to descend the root ladder, and when to leave a thing untouched.

The most feared among them are not warriors. There is little war in such a place; the hollow itself is enemy enough. The most feared are mimics.

A mimic is someone who has spent too long beneath the copper fans when the sky was red, or too long answering echoes alone. Their voices come back wrong. They begin to speak in the tones of absent people. A wife dead for thirty years may ask for resin from the mouth of a young man. A child may repeat the warning of a boundary post in the voice of a hooked bird. Mimics are not killed. They are given masks without mouth-slits and taught to drum instead.

Some leave, but not many.

Beyond the rim the world is too open and too quiet. Lowland air, when they encounter it, feels heavy and indecent. Moisture sickens them. Rain is not a blessing to their sealed skin; it is invasion, dissolution, a million cold mouths. A person of the hollow caught in rain will crouch and cover their face as if under a swarm of biting insects. They speak of rivers with the same wary respect others give to fire.

The animals live with the people, not tame, not wild in any simple way.

The glass-winged beetles are tolerated because they tune the reeds. Children learn to read their chew-holes as lowlanders read weather signs. If the beetles make round holes, the wind will be steady. If they make slits, pressure is building. If they abandon a reed entirely, that reed is cut and buried under salt before it can boom itself apart.

The pale rodents are thieves and omens. They nest in root mats and come out at night to lick tool handles for the salt left by hands. If one sleeps inside a hut, the inhabitants will not drive it away. It means the root mats below are shifting, and the hut may need to be moved before morning.

The feather-nostriled lizards are respected for their violence. Their skulls are worn by adolescents during initiation. A young person must sit at the edge of a salt channel with a dead moth tied to one wrist and wait for a lizard to strike. If they flinch too soon, they are still a child. If they do not flinch at all, they are foolish. The proper movement is between fear and timing.

The ragged birds are messengers, liars, and occasional food. They sleep inflated with vine-gas, drifting upside down like torn leaves with claws. When the people need to know the mood of the air above the rim, they watch how high the birds dare circle. A bird that leaves the basin and returns is never eaten. It has touched the outer silence and may carry news in its laugh.

The shovel-headed grazer is almost holy. There may be only one. There may have always been only one, wearing different hides across centuries. It breaks salt plates and opens paths for root-mats. Its velvet symbionts are harvested in tiny amounts with bone combs and used to seal wounds, patch masks, and coat newborns’ palms. When it kneels during the resonance, everyone kneels. Not because they worship it, exactly, but because the grazer has never been wrong.

And beneath all of them are the knockers.

No one sees these clearly. They live under stone, or inside root systems, or in the black-ringed stones, or in the old graves that may also be eggs. They answer certain screams. They tap back at mite-song. They shift powder in the central depression when the air is still. Sometimes they push shaped things upward: a hook, a tooth, a bead, a shard marked with a sign no living hand remembers. The people feed them dust, shed skin, and the powdered remains of broken tools. They do not feed them blood. Blood, when it exists, is too loud.

The true rulers of the hollow are the seven mineral trees.

They are alive, but not like plants alone. They remember pressure. They make tools. They make animals. They make instructions. They may have made the people, or remade them after ordinary people failed here. Their ceramic trunks hold handprints from generations whose bones are now wind chimes. Their pods are wombs, mouths, kilns, and sealed thoughts. Each tree has a temperament known by its tone.

One gives low warnings.

One produces mostly seeds.

One drops malformed tools that must be buried quickly.

One answers children.

One is silent except before deaths.

One attracts birds.

One, the most twisted, sometimes releases scraps of skin marked with symbols. These are taken to the sitting stones, weighted at the corners, and copied into powder before they curl and blacken. Some show maps of the basin as it is. Some show maps of the basin as it was. Some show arrangements of bodies and tools that become ceremonies. Some are never understood, but still obeyed.

So what lives there?

A dry people with careful hands.

A choir of plants that eat stone and weather.

Animals built from salt, resin, gas, dust, and appetite.

Old trees that think in resonance.

Buried things that knock from below.

Voices that outlast their speakers.

Nothing there lives alone. To live in the hollow is to be part of the argument: to click, scrape, bloom, molt, listen, split, harden, answer. The inhabitants are not softened by water or gentled by shade. They are varnished, tuned, brittle, alert. They survive by knowing when to be silent and when to join the noise.
