What lives there is easy to miss because it has learned not to interrupt the work of the valley.

At first it seems like no creature could belong to that place. A living thing, as one usually means it, would be too warm, too soft, too full of accidents. It would shed hair or skin. It would bruise the still water. It would carry spores in its gut, seeds in its seams, oils on its feet. It would rot when it died. The valley has no tolerance for that kind of presence.

But something lives there.

It lives as close to stone as life can come without becoming entirely stone.

The inhabitants are pale, narrow, jointed beings, more constructed than born. When still, they resemble bundled architectural parts: a stack of wet lintels, a folded ladder, a figure assembled from clay pipe, basalt hinge, lime-coated rib, and black ceramic shard. Their bodies are not hidden by the valley so much as continued by it. A shoulder has the same blunt curve as a cistern cap. A forearm is grooved like a spillway. Their hands are long and practical, with flattened fingers made for pressing clay into cracks, clearing mineral lips from drains, testing gradients by touch.

They have no hair. Nothing fibrous grows from them. Their surfaces are smooth, sealed, and faintly porous. In dry light they look chalky and inert; after rain they darken in patches, taking on the mottled blues, whites, greys, and bruised violets of the masonry around them. Water gathers in small hollows at their collarbones, wrists, backs of knees. When they move, these little stored waters tilt but do not spill unless deliberately released.

Their faces are difficult to understand. They have no expressive softness, no brows that lift, no lips made for speech as humans know it. Their eyes are set deep under hard ridges and appear not wet but glazed, like ceramic fired too hot. Some have eyes of pale mineral opacity; others have black reflective eyes that show the wrong sky, just as certain basins do. Their mouths are narrow slits used less for eating than for pressure, tone, and breath. When one exhales into a vent, the sound may travel three streets away and return through a bowl as a single answering drop.

They do not eat plants or meat. They take in dissolved minerals, cold water, powdered ash, lime, and the faint metallic bitterness that gathers in sealed cisterns. They drink slowly, often by placing one hand into a channel and waiting while water climbs through the pores of the wrist. They can stand that way for hours. Their bodies do not burn food; they balance moisture. Too much water makes them sluggish and swollen at the joints. Too little makes them brittle, thoughtful, and still.

Their blood, if it can be called blood, is not red. It is a clear, mineral-heavy fluid that moves through them by capillary patience rather than heartbeat. In silence one can hear it sometimes: a faint ticking within the forearms, a hollow settling in the chest, the tiny internal plink of one chamber filling another.

They leave no tracks in the usual sense. Their feet are broad, segmented, and grooved, designed to grip wet stone without scuffing it. They step only on places made to bear stepping. They will pause rather than cross a mirrored plaza at the wrong angle. They dislike unnecessary disturbance, not out of reverence exactly, but because every ripple is information spoiled.

They live mostly inside the infrastructure.

Not in houses. They have no houses because the idea of a private room would be strange to them. A place is not for belonging to someone; a place is for doing something correctly. One inhabitant may spend most of its life in the rain combs, tending condensation lines, scraping ice from grooved fins with a black shard tool. Another may belong to the settling courts, reading the fine angular grit at the bottom of shallow water. Another may sleep beneath a square hatch for three seasons at a time, waking only when the cistern below reaches a certain pressure and the stone around its chamber begins to sing.

Their society is arranged by function rather than family.

There are keepers of upper flow, listeners of sealed water, polishers of spill edges, measurers of vapor, setters of plugs, kiln-watchers, hatch-binders, wrong-reflection wardens, threshold-dryers, and those who walk the valley after storms to make sure no unchosen path has formed.

They do not command water. They negotiate with it through prepared shapes.

Their language is partly tactile, partly acoustic, partly hydraulic. A hand laid flat on wet plaster can say wait, full, cracked, sour, clear, danger, remember. A sequence of drops falling into bowls can record weather, instruction, warning, or grief. The marks cut beside hatches and levers are not exactly writing, but they are not merely labels either. Triangle, cup, fan, line: these are units of use, ownership, timing, and condition. Some marks name a cistern. Some name the kind of water inside it. Some name the one who last listened there and did not return.

They do speak, but rarely in open air. Their voices are low, dry, and resonant, better suited to shafts than courtyards. A conversation between two of them may be conducted from opposite sides of a wall through a sequence of taps, exhalations, and released droplets. At night, when the valley grows more audible, their speech becomes almost indistinguishable from the place itself. A visitor might hear a coded exchange and think only that stone is cooling.

They reproduce deliberately and slowly.

No child is carried in a body. A new inhabitant begins as an intention marked on an unused slab, then as a shaped hollow, then as a clay-lined form placed in one of the dormant kilns. Into this form they add powdered basalt, lime, ash, blue clay, crushed vitrified fragments, and water drawn from several different levels of the valley. The mixture is not flesh yet. It must settle. It must be drained and wetted, warmed and cooled, fired only partly, sealed only in places. Older ones press their thumb-marks into it, not as decoration but as channels for future sensation.

When the unfinished body is removed, it is a pale articulated shell. It may lie for months in a shallow basin while water enters it by increments. During this time, listeners sit beside it and make tones into bowls beneath the dripping ceiling. The new one learns first the rhythm of falling water, then the distinction between full and empty, then the severity of edges. Only after it has held water without leaking for a full cold cycle is it lifted upright and given a task.

They do not grow much after that. They refine. A young one is bright, raw-edged, and easily overfilled. An old one is darker at the joints, crusted along the back and wrists with mineral lips like old drains. The oldest inhabitants become difficult to distinguish from machinery. They may spend decades seated beside a cistern throat, one arm inserted into the wall, listening to water levels beyond ordinary hearing. Eventually they cease moving altogether. No funeral follows. Their bodies are not buried. They are opened, drained, cut, fired, and reused in thresholds, plugs, measuring bowls, or dry stones set where no water must pass.

This is not considered cruelty. To them, a body is a temporary arrangement of useful surfaces.

They are careful with life from elsewhere.

If a bird ever descends, they watch it until it leaves. If it dies, they remove it before dawn. If a seed arrives in windblown grit, a threshold-dryer lifts it with stone tweezers and places it on a black shard under hard sun until it becomes harmless dust. Roots are feared more than animals. Roots enter without permission. Roots widen cracks, drink without measure, make hidden decisions. The inhabitants do not hate green things as a human might hate an enemy. They regard them as leaks with hunger.

Their rule against growth is old enough to have become instinct.

Nothing fibrous is allowed because fiber remembers other climates. Wood swells, splits, rots, drinks unevenly, invites fungus, and lies about its strength. Moss hides fractures. Lichen writes over warning stains. Grass makes soil, and soil is the beginning of forgetfulness. The valley must remain readable. Every surface must show what water has done to it.

For this reason, the inhabitants keep themselves clean of all softness. They scrub one another with powdered lime. They scrape their joints after storms. They seal chips quickly. A cracked finger is not a wound to be pitied but a fault to be corrected before it mismeasures flow.

They are not hurried beings. Their minds move in gradients, not leaps. They notice seepage before color, pressure before shape, timing before intention. They can wait beside a basin for three days to learn whether a reflection has changed. They can argue for a season about whether a stair should be lowered by the thickness of a thumb. They consider haste a form of flooding.

At dawn they are most present but least visible. Vapor gathers low over the tiled courts, and their pale bodies pass through it in fragments: a bent back, a long hand, a head tilted toward a vent, a line of figures standing among the rain combs like the missing movable pieces of a petrified forest. By midday they retreat into shaded passages, hatch rooms, cistern throats, and the underside of bridges. Brightness makes them brittle. Glare overwhelms the fine distinctions by which they read wet stone.

At evening they emerge again. One kneels to clear a mineral crust from a spill edge. Another lowers a slab gate by counterweight, listening to the change in water behind it. Three gather in a flooded plaza, not walking through the mirror but standing at its corners, each releasing one drop from the hollow of a palm. The drops meet as ripples. The ripples cross. The figures watch the interference pattern with the solemnity of priests, engineers, and animals at a drinking place.

Perhaps they built the valley. Perhaps the valley built them.

The distinction may not matter there. Their hands cut the stones, but the stones taught their hands what shape to take. Their ancestors may have been softer once, more human, more animal, more capable of firelit rooms and sleep. If so, they gave those things up. They chose the high wet mineral basin, or were trapped by it, or slowly adapted to its commandments until nothing remained in them that could miss a forest.

They are the valley’s inhabitants, but also its maintenance, memory, and unfinished thought.

They live wherever water hesitates.

They sleep where pressure gathers.

They speak in drops.

They fear roots.

They polish edges.

They make children from clay, ash, and measured rain.

And when storms come over the artificial ridges, they stand in the pale glare with their grooved hands open, receiving the weather not as blessing or burden, but as the return of the one thing their whole existence has been shaped to understand.
