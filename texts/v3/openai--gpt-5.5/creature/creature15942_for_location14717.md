What lives there has learned not to waste motion.

The people of the high city are few, though the city is too large for anyone to know whether their number is diminishing. They inhabit it the way a small flame inhabits a kiln: not filling the space, only proving that heat remains. Whole avenues may pass a year without a human footfall, while three streets away a family has occupied the same warm block for nine generations, keeping lamps trimmed, filters rinsed in grit, door seals greased, and old shift bells wound because someone must.

They are narrow people, not necessarily small, but made to seem narrow by their wrappings and by the architecture around them. Their garments are layered and close-fitting, dark cloth glazed with dust-resistant oils, stiff at the cuffs, tied at the throat, doubled over the mouth. Children wear goggles before they can read. Infants are kept in humid cradles for their first months, little sealed boxes with warm walls and breath-catchers, because newborn flesh is the only soft thing the city produces and everyone is afraid of losing it to the air.

Their skin, when uncovered, has the matte look of old paper or fired clay. Their lips crack easily. Their hair is cropped short or bound under caps, not for discipline but because loose hair gathers dust and static. They oil their hands with mineral balms. They do not touch casually. A hand on another person’s sleeve means more here than an embrace would elsewhere.

They speak quietly, partly from custom and partly because the thin air punishes waste. Their language has many words for degrees of dryness, for the age of a footprint, for the mood of a furnace, for dust that has fallen once versus dust that has been lifted and fallen again. They have almost no living metaphors for greenness. Children learn that rain is a descent of liquid from a bright upper region, but most privately imagine it as a sort of industrial accident.

Their society is organized around maintenance, though not all of them are mechanics. Some tend pressure records. Some cut gaskets. Some inspect sealed doors whose rooms have not been opened in centuries. Some measure the slow crawl of belts in the central works. Some polish signal mirrors on the ridges. Some breed cultures in locked vats beneath ceramic domes, coaxing protein from formulas older than their dynasties. Some trade in parts, in heat tokens, in verified maps, in memory plates, in lengths of uncontaminated wire.

Status belongs not to wealth, exactly, but to reliability. A respected person is one who closes what they open, marks what they move, and remembers which pipe must never be struck after the third bell. Carelessness is the nearest thing to sin. Spillage is obscenity. To leave a tool exposed to dust is an insult to both the living and the dead.

Their houses are warm and dim. Rooms are small within the large apartment shells, partitioned by woven insulation and ceramic screens. Family niches hold not portraits but objects: a pressure key worn smooth, a cracked gauge face, a child’s first dust mask, a braid of wire from a long-retired tram. Water, when present, is not displayed. It is held in sealed ampoules, in medical cabinets, in ritual vessels that are never opened except by necessity. To drink openly would be vulgar. To pour water on the ground would be madness.

They do have ceremonies, but they are nearly silent. Birth is marked by opening a shutter for the length of one breath, letting the child receive the city’s dry warmth on the face. Coming of age is the first crossing of an unlit district without leaving the guide rail. Marriage is the joining of stores: filters, heat allotments, door codes, debts of repair. Death is not a return to earth, because the earth does not take. The dead are dried, wrapped, tagged, and placed in warm vaults among mineral shelves, or reduced to clean ash in civic furnaces whose red seams are visible only from the mourning court. Names are pressed into dust trays and left to blur at their own pace. A name that remains sharp for many years is considered a sign of an orderly life.

They do not imagine themselves as survivors. Survival implies a catastrophe still close behind. For them the catastrophe, if there was one, has become geology. They are the proper inhabitants of the after-state.

There are also workers who may not be people in the full civic sense.

In the deeper factories move the brass-backed auditors, jointed maintenance bodies built around old instruction cores. Some are no taller than a child; others hang from ceiling rails like folded cranes. They move with unbearable slowness until urgency finds them, and then they become exact, violent, almost beautiful. Their lenses are hooded against dust. Their hands can remove a bolt without disturbing the powder settled on its neighbor. They carry stamps, cutters, sampling needles, and little brushes made from glass fiber. Many have been repaired so often that none of their original casings remain, but they still answer to names engraved on plates nobody can read.

The people treat them with wary courtesy. They are not worshipped. They are not owned. They are part of the city’s contract with itself. A brass-backed auditor may enter a dwelling if a heat leak threatens a wall. It may seal a corridor without asking permission. It may stand motionless in a market for six days while calculating whether a batch of filters is falsely marked. Children dare one another to touch their trailing cables, but only when adults cannot see.

There are human guilds devoted to interpreting them. These interpreters keep notebooks of recurring gestures: three taps against a pipe, refusal to cross a threshold, red lamp covered and uncovered twice. No one knows whether the auditors still understand the whole city or only fragments of their first assignments. Either way, they continue. In that place, continuance is authority.

Smaller lives occupy the heat margins.

The cinder mites are not mites but the name has adhered to them, as dust adheres to any edge. They live on flues, behind furnace tiles, under ceramic lips where warmth rises in a steady film. A full-grown one is the size of a thumb joint, flat as a washer, with ten or twelve legs folded so neatly beneath its body that it seems stamped rather than born. Its shell is black-gray, with a sheen like rubbed pencil lead. When still, it resembles slag. When moving, it resembles the idea of machinery remembering how to be animal.

They eat what nothing else recognizes as food: metallic bloom, charged dust, salts condensed molecule by molecule around microscopic leaks, residues of dry acid, perhaps even the faint electrical difference between hot ceramic and cooler air. They gather in rosettes around hairline cracks. They molt into flakes so delicate that collectors once used them as conductive leaf in instruments. If crushed, they do not bleed. They powder.

Children in the furnace quarter keep dead ones in matchboxes and trade them for wire rings. Living ones are left alone. A sudden absence of cinder mites from a wall is a warning. It means the heat has shifted, or a gas has changed, or some seal has failed too subtly for gauges to know. In that way, they are citizens.

Above them, in the warm columns of air rising from vents and roof seams, the pale winged things turn.

No one agrees on their origin. Seen from below, they are only a hinge of light, a white angle opening and closing against the soot-black sky. Up close—though almost no one sees them up close—they are long and fragile, their bodies made of hollow spars, their wings translucent membranes dusted with reflective mineral scales. They have no feathers, no fur, no visible mouths. Their heads are smooth except for dark pits that might be eyes or heat sensors. They ride upward drafts for hours without flapping, correcting themselves with shivers.

The tower keepers call them vanes. The old electricians call them ghosts. Children call them sky knives.

Some say they are descendants of maintenance devices, soft machines once released to inspect cables and antennae. Others insist they are true animals, born in the ductwork, feeding on static and powdered insulation, their eggs laid in the warm mouths of dead signal horns. The city has room for both explanations. It has forgotten the boundary between adaptation and manufacture.

A vane’s fall is an event. If one is found broken on a roof, wrapped in dust with its wings split, a notice is posted and three offices claim the body: the tower wardens, the biological clerks, and the old machine registry. Often it disappears before any judgment is made. There are people who grind the wing scales into lamp paint. There are others who consider that a desecration.

There are less visible lives as well.

In sealed vats beneath the food halls live pale mats of engineered tissue, fed on mineral broth, heat, and carefully rationed gases. They are not animals, not plants, not fungi in any remembered sense. They grow in sheets, are shaved, dried, compressed, flavored with alkali salts, and cut into blocks. The people do not like to speak of them while eating. Each vat has a lineage number older than most streets. Some lineages have developed quirks: one thickens before pressure changes, one turns gray if a lie has been entered into the ration ledger, one produces a bitter edge during years of social unrest. Officially these are chemical responses. Unofficially, cooks greet the vats before cutting them.

In the dust itself live dormant things too small for custom. Spores that do not bloom. Capsules of old bacteria waiting for moisture that will never come. Residues from vanished biologies, preserved like unread letters. The city is full of potential life denied its condition. Every filter, every ledge, every folded cloth contains a graveyard of beginnings.

And then there is the city.

It is not alive in the way a creature is alive, but its inhabitants do not make that distinction with confidence. It breathes more regularly than they do. It holds heat in deeper organs. It circulates gas through buried channels. It digests old stockpiles, one conveyor-bite per day, one furnace-load per season. It sheds dust. It closes valves in response to pressure. It speaks through gauges, through warning lamps, through warm puffs from vents, through the late-night thud of pistons under the civic floor.

The people do not worship it, because worship suggests distance. They live inside its habits. They are born from its warmed rooms, fed by its vats, counted by its registries, dried by its air, buried in its vaults. Their calendars follow maintenance cycles: filter turning, belt crossing, furnace banking, tower alignment. A year is less important than the interval between inspections of the south pressure hall. A festival is a safe shutdown completed without loss.

If the city dreams, it dreams administratively: of seals intact, loads balanced, dust routed, heat retained, no gradients steep enough to become weather. Its morality is equilibrium. Its mercy is that it has not stopped.

What lives there, finally, is not abundance but persistence.

Life in that place does not spread. It holds. It clings to warmth, to procedure, to edges, to inherited tasks whose reasons have become secondary to their performance. It does not flower; it accretes. It does not sing; it hums behind walls. It does not rot into soil; it dries into record.

The inhabitants are custodians, scavengers, instruments, mites, vanes, vats, sleeping spores, and machines with orders older than memory. They share the same discipline: use little, disturb less, endure.

They are what remains when weather has been abolished and history has cooled into infrastructure. They live carefully, because in that city nothing vanishes quickly—not footprints, not errors, not names, not heat.
