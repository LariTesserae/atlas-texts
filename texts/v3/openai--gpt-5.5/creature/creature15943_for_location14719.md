What lives there has learned not to look alive.

The first inhabitants you notice are mistakes in texture: a seam that changes direction too deliberately, a scatter of pale chips arranged with too much spacing, a black patch on a vent that tightens when your shadow crosses it. The hollow continent is full of creatures that have made stillness into camouflage, metabolism, language, and prayer. They do not hide behind leaves or in water or under weather. They hide by becoming nearly indistinguishable from the unfinished mineral world that made them.

Most are small. Smallness is favored there, because small bodies lose less, require less, and can sleep between moments.

There are the ledge-beads, shell-backed and thumb-sized, clinging in gray rows beneath overhangs. From a distance they seem like mineral deposits, regular but not suspiciously so. Each has a flattened oval carapace, matte as ash, with a seam around its underside where its soft working parts are folded away. They feed on dust caught in the undersides of stone lips, on metallic salts that bloom too faintly for any eye to see, on warmth condensed into chemistry. When disturbed by breath or touch, they do not flee. Flight is too expensive. Instead, the whole colony loosens its grip one body at a time and reorders itself into another pattern, as if the stone has reconsidered its ornament.

Their young are not born helpless. They are deposited as hard grains in shallow scratches, each grain already sealed against dryness. A single season means nothing to them, since there are no seasons. A ledge-bead may remain a grain until heat, gas, and the chemical spoor of an adult align. Then it opens with a tiny dry click and begins its life already old.

Across the salt flats live the seam-grazers, long, narrow animals with backs like mineral faults. They lie under the crust with only their ridged spines exposed, and a traveler may step over one thinking it is merely a wrinkle in the floor. They have no eyes because there is nothing for eyes to do. Along their flanks are shallow pits that read warmth, pressure, vibration, and the flavor of stone. Their mouths are paired grinding plates, slow and tireless, made for shaving invisible life from the ground: bacterial films, mineral skins, colonies of powder that feed on vent seepage.

A seam-grazer moves with a patience that feels geological. Its body contracts in sections. Its plates rasp. The crust above it folds and settles. The sound it makes is like paper being creased in another room. Predators follow that sound, but only when hungry enough to spend the effort.

The glass moths live higher up, in cracks and mineral shelves where warm air gathers in thin layers. They are brittle, hollow, and almost beautiful by accident. Their wings are not membranes but transparent mineralized plates grown from their sides, sharp-edged and faintly prismatic. They cannot beat them. Instead they launch from ledges and fall with discipline, angling their bodies to cross warm updrafts and land far below or far across. In lamplight they appear once as a bright fracture and then become darkness again.

They feed by touching their narrow faces to glowing crusts, vents, and the bodies of sleeping animals. Some drink heat directly, or something close enough to heat that no surface word quite fits. Their bodies contain little fluid. If crushed, they break into curved shards and pale dust. There is no gore, only ruin.

There are also the heat-eaters, though that name makes them sound more singular than they are. Around vents they gather in heaps like black seeds, each body no larger than a fingernail, armored, faceless, and glossy. Alone, a heat-eater is almost nothing: a speck with legs, a mouth, and a dense appetite for warmth. Together they are a listening surface. A colony can turn toward a living body with the unity of an opened eye.

They do not swarm in the frantic way of surface insects. They flow reluctantly, as if poured from a vessel with a narrow mouth. They test the air, count pulses of heat, and decide whether a body is worth approaching. A sleeper too near a vent may wake to find one side of their blanket darkened by them, not bitten exactly, but being measured. They can strip heat from small animals until their inner gels stiffen. Larger animals tolerate them in moderation, the way surface beasts tolerate fleas, because removing them may cost more energy than surrendering a little warmth.

The larger creatures are seldom seen clearly because they have no reason to offer themselves as images.

There are slab-bellies, broad animals that drag their undersides along the floor, polishing grooves over centuries. Their backs grow mineral plates that match the ground they frequent: salt-white on flats, amber in resinous fields, black where volcanic glass rises. They may spend years moving from one vent cluster to another. Their mouths open downward. They do not chase; they arrive. Smaller creatures caught beneath them are ground, warmed, chemically read, and absorbed through slow plates of tissue. A slab-belly’s passage can redirect whole local ecologies, because after it moves through, the ground is polished, warmed by friction, and seeded with the powders from its skin.

There are listening spars: tall, jointed animals that stand among mineral blades with their limbs folded into angles indistinguishable from stone. They are predators, but not quick ones. Their bodies are thin and ribbed, built around tension. They sense the tremor of feet through the fins and plates around them. When something living enters the right arc, the spar unfolds with terrifying economy, one limb crossing a distance it has been preparing to cross for months. Then stillness returns. A listening spar may digest for longer than a human lifetime, its prey drying inside it, becoming powder, grease, and memory.

There are pale fissure-dwellers deeper down, flat as hands, soft-looking but armored with translucent scales. They live in cracks too narrow for most bodies and emerge only when a vent closes. They feed on the chemical distress of cooling stone. Their eggs are threaded into seams like beads on a cord. When a seam reopens and warms, the eggs do not hatch outward; the young dissolve part of the shell and enter the rock first, spending their earliest life as pressure between layers before acquiring a body.

Not all inhabitants are animals in any familiar sense.

Some are colonies of charged dust. Some are mats of granules that darken when fed by gases and pale when exhausted. Some are mineral-animal agreements: a crust that grows only on the back of a grazer, a shell that is partly secreted and partly accreted, a vent colony whose members are born from eggs but finish themselves by incorporating glass needles, salt nodules, and metallic powder. There are beings there that cannot move unless warmed by another creature’s passing. There are organisms that reproduce by leaving chemically prepared scratches in stone, waiting for seepage to write the next generation into them.

The hollow continent has no plants, but it has pastures. They are invisible to the eye: warm films, bitter blooms, metallic skins, static-fed granules. Grazers know them by taste and heat. Hunters know the grazers. Larger things know the hunters. The whole food web rests on vents, powders, seams, and the slow generosity of unfinished rock.

And then there are the people.

If they are people in the surface sense, they have been changed by the place, or else they were never entirely surface-born. They are few, and they do not gather in towns. A town would be too loud, too warm, too fragrant with breath and waste. It would announce itself to every patient appetite in the surrounding dark. Instead they live in pauses: under ledges, beside dependable vents, in niches rubbed smooth by generations of shoulders and hips.

They are narrow people, economical in gesture. Their skin is dry and often dusted pale, not by ornament alone but by necessity; mineral powders protect, conceal, signal, and seal small wounds. Their lips are usually covered with masks or wrappings of cured hide, shell-fiber, and ceramic filters. Their eyes, if they still rely on sight, are secondary organs. They listen with their feet, palms, teeth, and the bones of the face. They know the difference between a cooling tick and a hunting click. They can tell by the taste of air whether a vent is closing. They walk as if each step asks permission.

Water, to them, is not a substance of daily life. It is a dangerous myth, a solvent, a destroyer of records, perhaps a sacred poison. Their bodies conserve what little internal moisture they require with extreme severity. They eat gels from certain animals, powdered films, dried organs, eggs like fired beads, and mineral pastes processed through heat. They do not boil, wash, stew, soak, or ferment. Their cuisine is grinding, warming, sealing, curing. Their medicine is abrasion, powder, pressure, and cautery.

They build low because height is wasteful and attracts attention. Their shelters are made of flat mineral plates joined with dust-cement and animal resin. A dwelling is not meant to defy the continent; it is meant to resemble an acceptable part of it. Entrances face away from vents unless warmth is needed. Sleeping niches are just large enough for a curled body. Tool racks are placed where the wall’s natural seams already suggest them. Nothing dangles unless it is meant to be read.

Their tools are patient things: glass flakes resharpened until they are too small to hold, shell scrapers, ceramic knives fired in vent heat, bone ladders, sinew knots, dark hammered metal used sparingly for marks rather than wealth. They do not seem poor. Poverty implies a different world’s expectations. They own what can be carried, repaired, hidden, or left behind without grief.

Their markings are everywhere once you learn to see them. A polished handprint may mean safe warmth. Three dots and a line may mean do not sleep here. A row of shallow crescents may record the passage of slab-bellies over generations. Some carvings are maps, but not maps of distance. They map temperature, silence, animal habit, waking intervals, reliable crusts, dangerous membranes, vents that lie, basins that erase tracks. Their art is practical until it becomes devotional, and devotional until it becomes practical again.

They have a religion of restraint.

They do not worship the stone as dead matter. They know better than that. They treat stillness as an active condition, a force with moods. To alter something is to invite reply. A broken crust, a moved cairn, a warmed passage of black glass: all these are statements, and statements may be answered. Their rites are often acts of non-action performed with great care. Sitting on polished platforms among the hollow cones. Listening through masks. Holding a hand above a vent without touching it. Lying flat among mineral blades while others watch for what approaches.

The recurring figure carved on chamber walls—the person lying among listening animals—is probably not a victim. Or not only a victim. It may be a saint, a hunter, an ancestor, a warning, an initiation posture. To lie still enough that the animals accept you as terrain; to hear what comes near; to survive being considered by the dark—this may be one of their highest disciplines.

They know many silences and name them with precision.

The ordinary silence is habitable.

The feeding silence means make yourself smaller.

The vent silence means leave before the chemistry fails.

The old-structure silence means respect a fear whose cause may have died before your ancestors.

The total silence means do not interpret. Withdraw.

Those violet basins are central to their caution. No one lives beside them. Nothing nests there. The people mark them from a distance in the same repeated pattern: three upright stones, a gap, one stone laid flat. This is not decoration. It is grammar. It may mean absence. It may mean refusal. It may mean here the world does not keep what is done to it. In a continent where tracks can persist for generations, a place that takes no tracks is more frightening than a predator.

The people’s dead are not buried deeply. There is no soft earth to receive them, no dampness to unmake them. A body is dried, opened of what must be removed, packed with powders, and placed where it can become instructional. Some are set in niches facing vents. Some are disassembled into tools, tokens, masks, and markers. Some are laid beneath mineral plates to become part of a shelter’s memory. Grief there is not expressed by flowers or water or flame, but by maintenance: polishing a hand-worn place, renewing a cairn, replacing a broken shell token, leaving a tool weighted on a ledge for the dead to continue possessing.

Children, if they are born there, are born into quiet before language. They learn not to cry loudly. They learn heat before color, texture before distance, silence before song. Their toys are safe shards, hollow shells, carved vent maps. Their first lessons are probably negative ones: do not slap the wall; do not run on white crust; do not warm black glass with your back; do not step where the beads have rearranged; do not answer a sound only once.

Their songs, if they have songs, are almost certainly felt more than heard: tooth-click rhythms, palm-taps on ceramic, breath passed through shell masks, low vibrations pressed into stone. Music that does not travel far. Music that stays with the body that makes it.

They are not masters of the hollow continent. No one is. They survive by literacy. They read heat, dust, fracture, arrangement, hesitation. They annotate the world but do not presume to revise it.

There may also be older inhabitants, less easily separated from the place.

The hollow cones in the great chambers may be vents, husks, nests, chimneys, bodies, or all of these in sequence. The rings of tiny footprints around them suggest tending, feeding, worship, or predation. The platforms among them suggest that someone once sat there regularly, perhaps to listen to the cones breathe, perhaps to be listened to by them. If the cones are alive, their life is too slow to satisfy ordinary attention. They may bud once in a century. They may remember warmth by altering the flavor of their exhalations. They may lure colonies, people, and animals into patterns that resemble ritual because that is how they feed.

The continent itself also behaves too much like an organism to be dismissed as setting. Its vents are nostrils, but not metaphorically enough. Its black glass keeps the outlines of passing bodies. Its basins refuse history. Its dust drinks from skin. Its surfaces wait to be crossed before deciding whether to remain surfaces. Perhaps the creatures are not merely living in it. Perhaps they are local expressions of it: limbs, mites, thoughts, scars, immune responses.

The inhabitants of the hollow continent are therefore not one people or one fauna, but a whole civilization of restraint spread across animal, mineral, and almost-human forms.

They live by dryness.

They live by waiting.

They live by not spending motion unless motion will matter.

They live without bloom, without rain, without rot, without dawn.

They are shells that think slowly, powders that feed, hunters that sleep for decades, moths that fall instead of fly, people who build as though making footnotes to stone, and perhaps stone itself, not asleep, not awake, but considering articulation.

Nothing there hurries to prove it is alive.

That is how it survives.
