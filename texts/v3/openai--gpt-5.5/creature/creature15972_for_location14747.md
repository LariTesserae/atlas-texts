What lives there is not a cave animal, not exactly a plant, and not a people in the ordinary sense. The hollow’s inhabitants are boundary-creatures. They keep to edges. They cling, listen, drink, prune, and wait. Almost nothing truly lives in the center.

The first resident is the hollow itself: the green rind of it, the braided ceiling and breathing wall. It is not one plant, though it behaves at times like a single body. It is a congress of roots, vines, mosses, membranes, fungi, tubers, sap-veins, and opportunistic shoots, all bound together by pressure and accident. The rupture made a room, and the plants have accepted the shape of that room as an organ.

They do not simply grow into the available space. They study it.

Root-tips test the air and withdraw. Leaves flatten where light arrives. Hyphae cross fresh clay as if suturing flesh. Certain tendrils hang like whiskers, trembling at sound. Others thicken into load-bearing cords and hold back the collapsing earth. When the chamber exhales, it is the plant-wall doing it: millions of small wet mouths opening together, releasing breath, scent, and warmth.

The plant-mass has memory, but not memory as an animal keeps it. Its memory is in preferred routes, sealed cracks, repeated flowers, roots that avoid places where roots once vanished. A cut root will grow away from the hand that cut it if the hand returns with the same rhythm. A hummed note, repeated over days, may cause pale listening leaves to gather along one wall. The hollow remembers pressure. It remembers water. It remembers injury most clearly of all.

The water-cups are its eyes, or one kind of eye. Each curled leaf that holds a clear lens is sensitive to motion above it and inside it. The cups reflect more than light because the plant does not distinguish cleanly between sight, moisture, and time. It drinks possible versions of itself. It sees itself young, choked, empty, healed, broken. Perhaps that is how it knows not to fill the center.

The center is also alive, though not as flesh.

The empty space is the hollow’s second resident: a warm, transparent, unreliable thing, like a lung that has not decided whose body it belongs to. It eats falling seeds. It lengthens drops. It bends distances without moving. It is why the walls grow around rather than through. The plants treat it the way animals treat fire, cliff-edge, deep water, or a sleeping predator: not necessarily with fear, but with inherited attention.

When the boom comes, it is the center speaking in its own material. Not sound exactly, but volume changing its mind. The boom shakes the walls and revises the room. Leaves trade positions. Cracks close. Shadows are reassigned. Those who live here do not ask what caused it. They brace, flatten, go silent, and afterward begin counting the hollow again by touch.

The small animals are few because the place does not permit casual life.

The translucent millipede is one of the oldest ordinary residents. It moves along the wall in long circuits, never crossing open floor unless the floor has recently shifted and become wall for a while. Its body is nearly colorless except for the green glow of digested leaf-skin moving through it. It grazes on fungal lace, newborn moss, and the soft films that form over clay wounds. Its many feet read the chamber more faithfully than eyes could. It knows when a ledge has become farther away. It knows which roots are under tension. When the boom is near, it coils under a shelf of loam and becomes indistinguishable from a wet spiral of root.

The pale mites are less like animals than spilled punctuation. They appear around sap-wounds, hundreds at once, quick and soft and almost transparent. They drink, clean, vanish. They may hatch from the sap itself. They may live in the skin of the roots until injury calls them out. They do not tolerate attention. A watched gathering thins without dispersing, as if each mite has slipped behind the surface of the world.

The boneless frog-thing is more mysterious. It clings to the underside of leaf-cups with its whole belly. Its limbs are suggestions, its mouth barely a seam, its black eyes set too deep in a head that might still be forming. It drinks through its skin. It does not hunt. It waits under water it does not enter.

It may be an animal.

It may be a juvenile stage of the tool-users.

It may be a fruit practicing how to move.

The tool-users themselves are rare. The hollow could not support many, and the chamber’s uncertain distances would punish a crowd. Usually there are one or two. Sometimes none are visible for long periods, though the signs of their presence remain: a shaved root, a channelled drip, a cracked pod, a thorn-pin, a stone flake hidden in damp clay.

They are small, though not dwarfish; compact rather than stunted. Their bodies are made for compression, clinging, and hesitation. A grown one can crouch inside a fold of roots and become only another knotted shadow. Their bones are flexible, especially in the ribs and shoulders. The hands are the hardest part of them: long-fingered, careful, with dark crescent nails good for scraping moss, peeling stems, and holding stone flakes without cutting themselves. Their thumbs are strong. Their wrists can turn too far.

Their skin resembles wet bark over clay. In some light it is brown-green; in other light pale grey, almost fungal. It is thin along the throat, belly, and inner arms, where moisture can be taken in directly. Along the spine and scalp grow short rootlike filaments, not hair exactly, but sensitive strands that lift when the air pressure changes. These filaments collect droplets. They also taste the chamber.

Their eyes are black and small, with little visible white. They do not trust sight much. Sight lies here. They read with palms, soles, tongue, and breath. Before crossing even a handspan of open floor, they touch the air. They tap roots and listen through their knuckles. They smell which wall has recently been elsewhere. They know a shadow can be more dangerous than the object casting it.

They do not build because building is a misunderstanding in this place. They alter. They persuade. They borrow form for a while.

A folded leaf becomes a water channel until it roots into the wall. A cord twisted from fibers becomes a living tendril by morning. A thorn-pin swells, flowers, and drops its own small seeds. A bone splinter used as a lever is not discarded; it is left where roots will take it, mineral and memory together. Their craft is temporary not because they lack skill, but because permanence here would be rude, and probably impossible.

They keep a few true tools if they can: black stone flakes, polished tooth-edges, hard seed-shells split into scoops. These are hidden in places that are not too alive. Fresh clay pockets are favored. Sometimes a tool-user presses a flake into clay and covers it with a flat dead leaf, then marks the spot with a pattern of taps rather than a visible sign. Visible signs grow.

They eat carefully. Sap from permitted cuts. Soft fungal pads. The green-lit droppings of the millipede, when hunger is bad. Tender shoots that have already split themselves. Seed interiors opened by repeated striking. Occasionally the pale mites, swept up with a wet fingertip and licked away. They drink only from cups that show the ceiling plainly. Never from a pool that shows their own back. Never from a pool where the reflected chamber has no empty center.

Their language is mostly vibration.

They click stones softly against clay. They tap with nails. They hum against roots with lips closed, sending tone through bone and wall. Spoken breath is reserved for alarm, grief, or calling across a distance that may not really exist. A name is a rhythm given to a surface. A warning is a pattern that makes nearby tendrils tighten. A question is a pause placed where a sound should be.

They do not sing loudly unless they want the plants to answer.

A long low hum can make root-hairs extend. A sharp repeated tick can call sap toward a cut. A trembling whistle may cause alarm-blossoms to open, washing the chamber in pale color for a few seconds. Their oldest knowledge is not verbal law but practiced rhythm: which sounds invite growth, which sounds close membranes, which sounds should never be made during the wet interval.

Their children, if the frog-things are children, begin without bones.

A clutch is not laid in the open. It is tucked beneath a leaf-cup where condensation gathers but does not fall too hard. The young adhere to the underside and feed through their skins. For a long time they are little more than dark-eyed softness, absorbing mineral water, sap vapor, and whatever the leaf chooses to release. Hands form early. Teeth late. The first lesson is not walking but holding to the wall when the boom comes.

As they mature, they learn the chamber by being carried along its perimeter. An adult will place a child’s palm against a root that casts no shadow, then pull the hand away before the child reaches. It will show the child a safe pool, then an unsafe pool. It will cut a root and let the child watch how quickly mites arrive. It will tap a clay patch until rootlets begin to gather, teaching that the hollow listens to repetition.

They never teach the center by entering it. The center teaches itself.

An adult can cross the open middle if necessity demands, but it is done rarely and with dread. They move low, not stepping so much as placing themselves from one certainty to another. Sometimes the crossing takes three breaths. Sometimes it takes much longer, and the one crossing returns with droplets dried on one side of the body and fresh clay on the other. They do not speak of what happens there. They scrape their palms afterward and press them into moss until the moss closes over the prints.

Their dead do not leave the hollow as bodies.

A dying one usually retreats into a wall-niche, into a place where roots have already made a partial cradle. The others bring water in leaf-cups and press fungal felt around the limbs. The skin loosens. The back filaments lengthen. Fingers curl around roots and may remain visible for weeks, then months, then become difficult to distinguish from pale underground stems. A dead tool-user becomes mineral, memory, and listening surface. Sometimes a wall will grow a hand-shaped leaf after such a death.

The living visit those places and tap news into them.

Whether the dead hear is uncertain. The hollow does.

They do not worship the hollow, though an outsider might think so. Their relationship is more intimate and more practical than worship. The hollow feeds them, misleads them, shelters them, absorbs their tools, takes their dead, changes its exits, and occasionally shows them versions of themselves in water. They treat it as one treats a powerful relative whose moods are weather.

They are not masters of the place. They are not prisoners either.

They are tenants of a wound.

The thing they maintain, if they maintain anything, is the central emptiness. They trim growth that leans too far inward. They redirect drips away from certain floor patches. They harvest fast shoots before they can knot across the open air. Not because they understand the physics of the hollow, but because every generation has learned that when the center narrows, the booms become more violent, the light detaches more often, and exits forget to return.

So they keep the room breathing.

The plants allow this. More than allow: they seem to depend on these small corrections. The tool-users wound roots in ways that encourage branching. They carry spores on their skin. They crack seedpods that would otherwise rot unopened. They move fertile muck from one wall to another. They listen for dangerous tension in roots and cut before the ceiling tears. Their temporary craft is a kind of grooming.

In return, the hollow gives them warmth, concealment, water, and a world too unstable for most predators.

What lives there, then, is an arrangement:

A young plant-wound learning to be an organ.

A breathing wall with fungal nerves.

A central emptiness that behaves like an unborn animal.

Small translucent grazers and sap-drinkers.

Soft clinging almost-frogs.

And, at the edge of sight, careful-handed people who own nothing that will not sprout, rot, or be taken back.

They live by touch, rhythm, taboo, and revision. They do not carve their presence into the hollow. They leave shaved roots, pinned leaves, opened pods, and the occasional black stone hidden in clean clay. The place erases them gently and continuously, but not completely. It keeps the shape of their habits the way wet soil keeps the memory of a footstep after the foot has gone.
