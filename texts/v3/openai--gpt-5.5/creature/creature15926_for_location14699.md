What lives there is not easily separated from what grows there.

At first it seems the drowned highland has no true inhabitants, only vegetation, mineral breath, and the few pale animals that pass like afterthoughts through the green dimness. But after long enough, the place begins to disclose its residents. They are not hidden behind the weed or inside the ruins. They are the slight decisions in the weed. The repeated curve of a corridor. The way a grove opens when approached, then closes without haste behind the swimmer. The way certain stones, worn nearly featureless, are always facing the same direction.

They are a people only if one is generous with the word.

Their bodies begin as drifting transparencies: motes, skins, seedlike sacs no larger than a tear, carried in spore tides through amber water. Most become food for moss, vents, grazers, or time. A few lodge in cracks where warm mineral water breathes upward. There they unfold into soft white knots, half fungus, half animal, feeding on dissolved iron and the slow rot of drowned leaves. They grow roots before limbs. They learn pressure before sight.

In youth they are mobile.

These younger ones are the pale eel-shapes seen between terraces, the many-jointed ribbons vanishing into weed. Their bodies are long, almost flat, with translucent fins running the length of them like torn banners. Under the skin move threads of green, black, and milky white: algae, nerves, mineral veins. They have no eyes at first. Instead they read warmth, salt, pressure, memory. They know where a ravine will be before the swimmer sees it. They know which currents are old and which have only just been born.

When they do grow eyes, they grow too many, and then slowly lose them.

An adult may resemble a floating disc, a collapsed bell, a mantle of living membrane spread between antlered spars of calcified cartilage. Others stand rooted upright like tapering stones, their lower bodies fused into terraces, their upper bodies waving hundreds of sensing filaments in the water. Others become almost indistinguishable from bladder trees: brown-purple layered trunks, translucent sacs, hanging cords, a soft pulsing in the core where thought gathers and disperses.

Their faces are temporary.

If watched, a fold in the membrane may arrange itself into something like a brow, a mouth, a row of dim lantern organs. If ignored, it smooths away. They do not keep expressions when no one is there to receive them. Attention matters to them. Some of their fronds open only under a gaze; some of their thoughts require witness the way plants require light.

They move rarely, and never all at once. A rooted one may take three years to turn toward a warmer vent. A grove of them may migrate across a basin over centuries, leaving behind only low walls, sponge growth, and stones that look arranged by hand. The free-swimming adults are solemn, immense, and almost silent. They cross the drowned plateau like pieces of dusk, their many limbs trailing in the current, stirring spores that glow and then extinguish.

They do not hunt in any hurried sense. They absorb.

Small grazers cling to their undersides and clean them. Eels nest in their folds. Red filament grows across their backs and is allowed to remain until it has eaten enough mineral, then is peeled away and consumed. Dead leaves drift into their open membranes and are digested over months. Occasionally one will take an animal, but without violence: a closing of soft curtains, a dimming, a long exchange of salts. Predation here is not a chase. It is an invitation that becomes irreversible.

They are descended, perhaps, from the builders.

Or the builders were descended from them.

The ruins are not abandoned in the way surface ruins are abandoned. The fitted blocks, half-stairs, causeways, roofless chambers, and upright rings are parts of an old conversation between body and stone. The inhabitants still tend them, though not to preserve their original shapes. They dislike right angles. Not emotionally, not as an aesthetic objection, but as a kind of wound in the world’s pressure. So they soften them. They seed corners with pale crust. They coax walls to bow. They let roots enter mortar and teach it patience. Over ages, every deliberate line is persuaded toward curve.

Their architecture is therefore difficult to recognize. A chamber may also be a lung. A causeway may be a migration organ. A ring of stones may be a council still in session, though its members have taken on algae, calcite, and the posture of drowned elders. The white sponge tree in the roofless room is not decoration. It is an archive.

They remember by growing.

Memory in them is not stored in a mind alone but in rings of mineral, in scars of current, in root networks crossing buried faults. A single individual remembers only a little: the taste of a vent before it cooled, the shape of a shadow, the direction from which a vanished sun once pressed. A grove remembers more. A basin remembers entire climates. The plateau remembers almost everything, though not in order.

Their speech is slow and layered.

They speak with pressure pulses too low for most animals to hear, with clicking shells cultivated along their limbs, with bubbles released in measured strings from bladder sacs, with chemical stains that drift through water and mean different things depending on temperature. They also speak by rearranging growth. A corridor of vine opened through a kelp labyrinth may be a warning. A field of fronds all leaning toward a ravine may be a question. A silt storm released from a basin may be grief.

When they gather, they do not form crowds. They become weather.

A council may appear as a rotating spore tide around a warm spring, each participant rooted miles apart but connected by drifting cells and pressure. Decisions take seasons. Agreement looks like all the bladder trees in a district filling at once. Disagreement looks like leaves turning their pale undersides toward the dark. Mourning is a dimming of bioluminescent motes across an entire valley.

They have little need for tools now. Their tools became organs long ago.

A cutting edge became a rasping colony of red filament. A lamp became a lantern gland. A wall became a current-shaper. A boat became a life stage. A map became a seeded memory in the roots, instructing the young where to swim when distance lies.

They understand the unstable laws of the place better than anything else does, though even they do not master them. They know which ravines repeat and which repetitions are hungry. They know when a distant ridge is far in miles and when it is far in centuries. They anchor themselves near seams in reality and feed on the difference between possible waters. The fishlike shadows that flicker between green living plants and black fossil plants are not prey to them, exactly; they are unborn relatives, failed ancestors, alternative bodies. The inhabitants let them pass.

Some elders live partly in the present and partly elsewhere.

These are the ones mistaken for low hills, sponge-covered masonry, or long mats of velvet weed. Their bodies may extend through several versions of the same basin. Touching one can slow the hand because the elder is not entirely alive at human speed. Its outer tissues are in the current moment, soft and responsive. Its inner tissues are still considering an age before the plateau drowned. Its deepest roots have not yet accepted that mountains ended.

The oldest of them are almost flat.

They lie beneath the green pelt of the final plains, spread wide through silt and stone, no longer individuals in any simple sense. Their breathing causes vents to sigh. Their dreaming loosens gravity on the long cycles when stones lift a finger-width from their beds. When dusk passes through the whole high ocean and the plateau glows gray-green from beneath its skin, it is their nervous system briefly visible: fault, root, ruin, reef, body, memory, all lit from within.

The smaller animals live around them as thoughts live around a sleeping mind.

The translucent grazers are their cleaners, children, parasites, and messengers. The pale eels are scouts and adolescents, still restless enough to cross open water. The lantern-eyed swimmers in the far dimness are mature wanderers who have not yet chosen where to root. The many-limbed shadows may be elders lifting themselves from one basin to settle in another, a journey that can take a thousand years and disturb no more silt than a falling leaf.

They are gentle because haste has been removed from them.

Not kind, exactly. Kindness is too brief a virtue. They are accommodating in the way old stone is accommodating, in the way water accepts a body and then alters it. They make room. They overgrow. They digest. They remember. They turn violence into sediment when they can.

A traveler who enters their country may never be attacked. Instead, paths will become confusingly hospitable. Curtains of weed will open. Warm currents will carry the body away from sharp drops. Silt will settle just enough to reveal stairs that lead nowhere useful. A pale eel may follow for days, not watching with eyes but with its whole length. If the traveler is afraid, the fronds may fold inward, tasting the fear as a change in salt. If the traveler is calm, some of the attention-fed leaves may open, drinking the gaze.

Those who die there are not wasted.

The inhabitants do not make graves. They make continuations. Bone takes mineral. Skin feeds velvet weed. Breath becomes bubbles, bubbles amber, amber memory. A name, if spoken often enough in the water, may be caught by sponge and repeated years later as a faint chime from hollow stems.

What lives there, finally, is a drowned civilization that has stopped distinguishing between citizen, city, garden, ancestor, and weather.

It has no engines because currents are enough.

It has no lamps because darkness is food for some of its mosses and attention is light for some of its leaves.

It has no borders because distance cannot be trusted.

It has no monuments except the ruins it is patiently rounding back into life.

It is not dead. It is not fallen. It is not waiting to be restored.

It has accepted the ocean of rain as its body and the worn mountain range as its skeleton. It lives slowly, in membranes and terraces, in pale eels and bladder groves, in pressure songs, in fault-glow, in the soft erasure of every edge. It is the inhabitant and the habitation both: an ancient people become ecology, an ecology become memory, a memory still warm enough to grow.
