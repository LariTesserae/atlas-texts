What lives there is not one people, but a dry republic of appetites.

The basin is inhabited by creatures that have learned to treat water as a secret rather than a substance. Almost every body there is sealed, waxed, furred, scaled, folded, or armored. Nothing sweats. Nothing wastes. Even the voices of the place seem adapted to dryness: clicks, rasps, wingbeats, chirps, the brittle thunder of many small bodies moving over salt.

The true citizens are the insects.

They are the builders of the city, though none of them knows the whole shape of what they have made. Pale ants run the streets in organized streams, their bodies almost translucent, their heads amber, their jaws polished dark from generations of cutting root and husk. They do not wander. They follow grooves worn into the floor by ancestors, trails so old they have become shallow canals in salt and clay. Some carry seeds. Some carry larvae. Some carry the dead. Some carry nothing visible at all, only scent, instruction, urgency.

The termites are slower and more architectural. They live inside the towers and chimneys, in chambers of packed dust, chewed fiber, guano, and salt-cement. Their mounds breathe. Warmth rises through one shaft, cool air descends through another, and the colony lives inside the difference. They repair constantly: a crack sealed with pellets, a vent narrowed, a bridge thickened, a dead root plastered over until it becomes part of the structure. Their soldiers have blunt heads like plugs and stand in broken openings, sealing the colony with their bodies. Their workers are nearly white, soft-looking, tireless, and solemn in their endless mouth-by-mouth masonry.

There are beetles everywhere, each kind belonging to a different layer of the city. Broad black scavenger beetles work the plazas after the herds leave, rolling pellets, stripping carcasses, polishing bone. Smaller gray beetles live in the fungal gardens, tending mats of dry felt that grow on dung and root-pulp. Long-snouted beetles bore into the hanging root-curtains. Flat beetles hide beneath gypsum plates and emerge only when bat guano falls fresh from above. Some have fused wing-cases and cannot fly; others open mineral-colored shells and vanish into the dim air like chips of old stone becoming alive.

The bats rule the upper vaults.

They are not silent guardians or solitary things, but multitudes: dense, quarrelsome, warm-blooded weather. They hang in curtains from the highest cracks and folded limestone shelves, packed so tightly that their bodies make a second, living roof. There are several kinds. Small needle-faced bats hunt moths and midges in the root groves. Heavy-jawed fruit bats, pale-bellied and dust-colored, leave the basin through fissures at certain seasons and return carrying seeds in their bellies and fur. Long-eared bats descend to lick salt from the flats, their tongues flicking against mineral crust. Others never seem to leave, surviving on the insect abundance below, stitching the air with sound.

Their guano is one of the great currencies of the place. Where it falls heavily, whole neighborhoods grow around it. Fungi bloom in dry, leathery sheets. Beetles gather. Ants harvest. Pale shoots rise from old dung. The city eats what the bats drop, and the bats eat what the city breeds.

Below them live the browsers.

The cave hares are long-legged, narrow-bodied animals with immense ears and soft gray hides the color of dust. They are not blind, but their eyes are large, dark, and slow, made for twilight and movement rather than detail. They feed on root-falls, waxy rosettes, curled leaves, and the tender skins of subterranean shoots. They stand upright to nibble hanging fibers and sit in groups beneath the root-curtains, chewing with grave concentration. Their pellets form chains along the avenues, and entire insect guilds follow those chains as if reading script.

There are also small hoofed animals, no larger than goats but built lower and more compactly, with flexible lips and narrow faces. Their hooves are soft-edged, adapted to salt crust and clay rather than rock ledges. They gather in the old plazas to sleep, flank to flank, conserving warmth and moisture. Their coats are pale umber, their horns small or absent, their nostrils slitlike against dust. They move carefully, as though the whole world might crack if stepped on too hard. Where they rest, the floor becomes polished. Where they browse, gardens are pruned into strange, ancient shapes.

The predators are thin, patient, and mostly quiet.

Blind lizards hunt in the lower walls and among the roots. Their skins are matte white, faintly pink at the throat, with tiny recessed eyes or none at all. They taste the air with black tongues and move in stops and starts, mechanical and precise. A cricket may sing for an hour within a finger’s length of death before the lizard finally knows exactly where it is.

The foxlike mammals are sharper, quicker, and more opportunistic. They have oversized ears, narrow muzzles, long feet, and tails carried low to avoid brushing walls. Their fur is gray-tan, powdered constantly with dust. They sleep in abandoned termite pockets or under mineral shelves, curled so tightly they resemble discarded rags. At dusk-tide, when the colder light enters through the roof chimneys and the bats grow restless, they emerge to raid nests, seize beetles, dig out lizards, and steal the eggs of ground-roosting birds that sometimes blunder in from the surface. They are clever in the way hunger is clever, but not technological. They know routes, seasons, smells, sleeping places, and the moment when a colony’s attention is elsewhere.

There are snakes too, though they are seldom seen. They live in the temperature differences: under warm tower walls, inside old root masses, beneath clay shelves that hold the day’s faint heat. They are narrow, dust-pale, and slow until they are not. Some eat bats fallen from the roosts. Some specialize in hares. Their shed skins remain for years in the dry air, translucent ribbons caught on salt edges.

The smallest lives may matter most.

Crickets form the voice of the underplain. They live in cracks, wall pockets, abandoned tunnels, under dry leaves, inside old bones. Their singing is not background but architecture, a constant acoustic occupation of space. Some species sing only when the upper fissures admit light. Some answer the wingbeats of bats. Some fall silent when ants cross their stones. Together they make the cavern seem awake even when little is moving.

Moths cling to pillars in pale drifts, wings marked with patterns like seep stains, chalk scars, and mineral blooms. Their larvae eat lichens, root skins, fungal felt, dung paper, the edges of stored seeds. The adults visit the rare flowers of the root gardens: small, powdery blossoms almost scentless to anything without the right antennae. Pollination here is not lavish. It is a narrow agreement between famine-resistant plants and insects that can find sweetness where there seems to be none.

The plants are inhabitants too, though they live at a different speed.

Most are not rooted in the floor but suspended from above. Surface plants send roots down through cracks, and in the basin those roots become groves: pale cords, veils, ropes, curtains, swollen storage knots. Some are alive only at their cores, with dead outer fibers still hanging as shelter and food. Around them grow lichen mats, dry fungi, cushion plants, and gray rosettes with inward-curling leaves. Their surfaces are furred, waxed, or powdered with mineral dust. They open slowly after changes too slight for animals to notice: a cooler season, a breath of damp from above, a new fissure, a colony of bats shifting overhead.

The fungi are not lush mushrooms but felt, crust, thread, and hidden lace. They digest what cannot otherwise be used: dung, dead root, insect bodies, shed fur, the films left on stone by breath and touch. Some are cultivated by beetles and termites. Some colonize abandoned walls, turning architecture into food. Some wait dormant for years, then spread invisibly through a root mass after one rare pulse of moisture.

And beneath all of this are the crusts: lichens, bacteria, mineral skins, black-green growths in cracks of salt and limestone. They seem almost geological until disturbed. They bind dust. They trap traces of moisture. They begin the slow conversion of bare mineral into something that can be eaten, walked on, burrowed into, inherited.

No single creature owns the city.

The termites build its towers, but the bats fertilize its districts. The ants maintain its roads, but the hooved sleepers polish its plazas. Roots define its quarters, fungi feed its nurseries, beetles clean its dead, crickets fill its silence, lizards and fox-things shape its fear. Even the bones become part of the design. Even waste has address and purpose.

The inhabitants are not civilized in imitation of humans. They are civic by accumulation. Their city is made of repeated need: the need to breathe, hide, breed, conserve, migrate, warm, cool, feed, and return. A street is where bodies have agreed to pass. A wall is where mouths have placed earth. A garden is where dung, shadow, seed, and patience happened to meet. A tower is not planned, but remembered physically by the colony that repairs it.

They are creatures of restraint, not abundance. Their beauty is in thrift. Their intelligence is distributed across trail scent, nest heat, wing echo, root chemistry, old habit, and the shape of the stone itself.

What lives there is a whole underground economy of dryness: pale, crowded, careful, ancient, and continuously building itself from dust.
