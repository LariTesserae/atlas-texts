What lives there is not, first of all, animal.

The primary inhabitant is the plateau itself: a many-bodied plant organism, or a federation of organisms so completely intergrown that individuality has become a local habit rather than a rule. It lives as ground, bridge, reservoir, wall, column, cup, canal, hall. It is not a forest standing on land; it is land that has chosen the methods of a forest.

Its life is architectural but not mechanical. Each tower-trunk, each rib-path, each basin-rim and hanging root-curtain is an organ performing both structural and biological work. The pillars are lungs and buttresses. The pools are eyes, stomachs, memory-cups, weather-makers. The blade-fields are skin and weather-vane. The root-halls are conductive tissue, carrying pressure, sound, and water-information through the whole suspended marsh.

If it has a mind, that mind is not located anywhere obvious.

It is distributed through wet fiber, fungal mesh, mineral nodule, hydraulic pulse, and the slow exchange of pressure between roots. It notices by saturation. It remembers by shape. A new opening in a membrane, a sealed seam, a redirected spillway, a root grown around an absence—these are its thoughts made physical. It does not think in words or images. It thinks in maintained conditions.

Its greatest instinct is preservation.

Not preservation against death exactly, because death here is not excluded; it is hidden, digested, translated. Fallen matter is taken inward before it can become litter. Rot is not an event but a department. Decay occurs behind green walls, under mats, inside warm chambers where pale fungi and root-mouths divide old tissue into usable sweetness. Nothing is wasted enough to become evidence.

The plateau-organism is patient but not passive. It repairs before damage becomes visible. It grows supports before sagging becomes collapse. It lines channels before water learns to cut. It makes stone in cradles, slowly, as if rehearsing geology under supervision. It allows mineral matter to appear only where it can be held, rounded, softened, incorporated. Rock is being born here, but as a tame child of root and water.

The smaller creatures live inside this rule.

They are few because there is little need for haste, scavenging, competition, or flight. The place has no exposed abundance to swarm over. Food is offered in measured permissions: nectar beneath water-cup lips, films on submerged leaf-skins, soft mineral gels in shaded seams, dissolved sugars along root pores, tiny pale growths that bloom and vanish under membranes.

The insects are almost still.

They are small, milk-colored, and delicate, with bodies like beads of wet rice and antennae longer than their legs. They gather beneath the rims of cup-leaves and along the undersides of spillways, where they drink from sweating green tissue. Their wings, when present, are transparent and usually folded flat. They do not buzz. They make no clouds. Their movement is slight and exact: one foot placed, antennae tasting, abdomen lifted, then stillness again.

They seem less like pollinators than attendants.

Some clean the lips of water-vessels, preventing film from sealing the surface. Others chew minute openings in membranes where drainage is needed, always along existing veins, never through living strength. A few carry fungal dust in soft pockets along their sides. They do not cultivate in any intentional sense, but their habits complete circuits the plateau has grown around them. They are tolerated because they are useful; useful because they have never learned to be disruptive.

The amphibians are longer-lived and stranger.

They are thin, translucent-limbed things, neither frogs nor salamanders exactly, with narrow heads, wide quiet mouths, and eyes like dark seeds suspended in clear flesh. Their bones show faintly through them. Their internal organs are greenish from the algae that live in their tissues. In full light they almost disappear against wet leaves; in shade they look like drawings of animals made in water.

They move rarely, and when they do, the whole place seems to grant them passage.

They travel the rib-paths after long intervals of stillness, placing their soft feet on the damp raised growth as though reading it. Their bellies absorb mineral salts from the surface. Their skins drink constantly. They do not hunt with violence. They wait at the mouths of root-slits for small blind larvae to drift past in films of water, then open their mouths without lunging. Their feeding is an omission, a quiet correction in the flow.

They breed in basins that are not the silent basins.

Their eggs are laid in strings under the lips of warm pools, embedded among water-filaments. The young hatch nearly transparent and spend their earliest lives inside the plateau’s channels, where they drift through root-lined pipes like thoughts not yet attached to bodies. Many are absorbed by the system. This is not predation in the ordinary sense. It is more like selection by belonging. Those that return to the surface do so already marked by the chemistry of the place.

The broad-winged creatures overhead are not birds, though from below they may pass for birds because they own the sky.

They are slow gliders with wide membrane-wings stretched from wrist to flank, their bodies flattened and dark, their heads small beneath a forward crest. They rise from lower weather on warm columns of vapor and cross the plateau without landing often. Their shadows move over basins like drifting pieces of night. When they do descend, they cling upside down to high root-curtains and drink the water that sweats from pores in the bark.

They nest at the outer terraces, in the exposed lattices beneath the lifted marsh. There, hanging in the warm mist and root-shadow, they weave no nests of their own. They tuck themselves into chambers already grown by the plateau, dry enough for young but close enough to constant seepage. Their young are born pale and folded, with enormous closed wings and gripping feet. For a long time they listen before they fly. The organ-tones of hollow stems teach them distance. The steady fall of edge-water teaches them height.

There are also lives that are almost not creatures.

Fungal intelligences occupy the undersides and interiors. They are pale, felted, and extensive, living in the dark between root layers. They translate rot into structure. They lace the mats with fine threads and receive minerals from the forming stone. In some places they produce soft white nodules that harden slowly under the influence of dripping water. They are the plateau’s digesters, archivists, and masons.

They may be older than the green growth above them.

Their memory is chemical and stubborn. When an old channel is sealed, its outline remains in fungal pattern long after the surface has forgotten it. When a new basin begins to form, fungal mats gather beneath it first, thickening the future floor. They know weight before weight arrives. They know failure as a change in dampness. Their messages move slowly through acidity, sweetness, pressure, and warmth.

There are aquatic things too, but they are mostly soft and partial.

Ribbon-worms live in the tea-colored basins, almost the same color as steeped leaves. They coil among submerged ribs, scraping films from the plant-made surfaces. Little shelled grazers, no larger than seeds, move across carbonate skins and polish them. Blind pale eels, thin as roots, inhabit deeper channels beneath the mats. They are seldom seen except when bubbles rise and one pale body turns below the surface like a loose thread.

None of these animals dominate.

They do not build the place. They do not challenge it. They occupy permissions within it.

The true residents are the growth-forms that resemble civic structures: the court-trees, aqueduct-vines, basin-rims, membrane-walls, root-benches, hanging curtains, and walking leaves. Each is alive, but each is also a role. They are not separate species in any simple way. They may be castes of one great organism, or compatible organisms bound into a permanent society.

The aqueduct-vines are among the most active. They carry water at shoulder height through braided stems, tightening and loosening internal fibers to keep the flow steady. If a section grows heavy, support-roots descend from it and take hold in the mat below. If too much mineral collects, the vine sweats acids into the channel and clears itself. Its bead-curtains are not leaks. They are measurements, keeping humidity constant in the districts below.

The membrane-walls are more sensitive.

They are translucent sheets of living tissue, veined like leaves and thickened like skin. They divide warm from warmer, saturated from drowned, growing from sleeping. Their openings shift over long periods, not quickly enough to watch but clearly enough to alter routes. They taste the air passing through them. They admit mist, insects, spores, and sound according to tensions in their veins. To pass through one is to be briefly considered by a surface that has no eyes.

The great pillar-trees are the old inhabitants.

They stand alone because their crowns require exact distances from one another. Their trunks are not trunks but bundled histories: fused stems, old roots, vertical water-pipes, woody muscle, fungal seams, mineral splints. The curtains hanging from them are both roots and rainfall. They drink from the air as much as from below. Their slow pulses press water outward, upward, downward, through tissues too large to be called a body and too responsive to be called terrain.

At their bases, the moss-domes sleep.

They are low, rounded colonies of dense green felt, dry on top despite the wetness around them. Inside they are warm. Small insects overwinter there, if winter exists. Amphibians bury themselves at their edges during long still intervals. The domes release spores only under conditions so precise that generations may pass between visible emissions. When they do, the air above them fills with a pale breath that settles on water and vanishes.

The luminous fruits are another kind of inhabitant.

They hang in shaded halls like closed lamps, green-gold and faintly warm. They do not invite eating by sweetness alone. Their light draws the pale insects, which clean them, dust them, and sometimes die beneath them. When one fruit finally drops, it does not fall to the ground and rot in the open. A cup opens below it before it detaches. It is received, softened, and drawn inward. Its seeds are not scattered outward but routed through channels to places already prepared.

This is how reproduction happens here: not by chance dispersal, but by placement.

The plateau does not fling itself into the world. It extends by making a place ready, then occupying it. Along the edges, root-lattices test the air over the drop. Water bladders swell. Fungal felts thicken. Vine-cables reach across emptiness, anchor to other growth, then broaden into new mats. A future terrace begins as tension, then shade, then seepage, then surface. What looks like expansion is more like the careful addition of another room to a living house.

If there are people here, they are not separate from this.

They would not have houses, tools, fires, roads, or fields. They would have postures, routes, agreements with membranes, knowledge of drinkable rims, and bodies trained to move without demanding hardness from the ground. But there is no sign that such people exist now. The root-benches remain empty. The courts are unused. The halls carry no voices except water and hollow stems.

Perhaps the architecture is not waiting for occupants.

Perhaps it is the occupant.

The plateau’s civilization, if that word applies, never divided maker from dwelling, law from metabolism, memory from growth. Its public spaces are clearings of regulated humidity. Its monuments are basins that do not ripple. Its roads are ribs that distribute weight and water. Its archives are vein-patterns almost becoming script. Its industry is the slow manufacture of stone. Its music is the constant organ-tone of water moving through hollow stems.

What lives there, then, is a complete ecology with the temperament of a single being.

It is warm, wet, elevated, and slow beyond animal impatience. Around its edges glide winged drinkers. In its channels drift transparent young. Under its cups wait pale insects. In its underskin, fungi remember and transform. Across its surfaces move rare amphibians like small ceremonies. But all of them live inside the larger resident: the grown plateau, the marsh-city without builders, the body that has made ground out of itself and now maintains that ground against history.

It does not welcome or refuse.

It accommodates what fits.

It continues.
