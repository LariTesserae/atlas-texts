What lives there is not only a population, but a habit of endurance.

The people of the plateau are spare in their gestures and exact in their attention. They are not a hurried people. From childhood they learn that haste is a form of ignorance: stone is slick, pressure is invisible, a quiet pipe may be more dangerous than a roaring one, and water can come upward through a floor with no warning except a change in the smell of the air.

They walk with their weight slightly forward, knees loose, soles testing. Their boots are thick, ridged, often repaired with patches of rubber and wire. Most carry gloves. Many carry a small hooked tool used for lifting grates, testing drains, tapping walls, opening valves, or knocking mineral scale from a threshold. Even those who do no industrial work know how to read a gutter, how to listen through a railing, how to judge whether a sheen on stone is rainwater, oil, seepage, or something brought up from deeper caverns.

Their faces tend to be weathered early, not by desert dryness or sea wind, but by damp heat, mineral air, and the constant squint of people looking through pale glare and steam. Their clothes are layered strangely for a warm place: light inner garments, waterproof outer aprons, short capes, sealed cuffs, hoods with stiff brims, waxed cloth wraps for papers and tools. Bright colors are rare in ordinary clothing because everything fades, stains, or mildews, but signal colors matter greatly. A strip of blue cloth at a belt may mean potable-water duty. Amber badges mark pressure crews. Red sleeves belong to slope wardens. White lacquered helmets are worn by cistern inspectors and by certain priests, though the distinction is not always clear to outsiders.

They are excellent at touching things.

A hand laid on a wall is not casual. It is inquiry. A palm against a pipe can read temperature, flow, obstruction, strain. A foot on a stair can sense hollowness. A knuckle tapped against brick can find the wet behind it. They listen with their bones because the air is too full to be trusted. Many of them have some hearing damage by middle age, but this is not considered a tragedy in itself. True deafness, to them, would be losing the ability to feel vibration.

Their speech is shaped by the city. Outdoors they use abbreviated phrases, close to the ear, and a broad vocabulary of signs. There are work signs, market signs, family signs, district signs, rude signs, emergency signs, and old signs whose meanings survive only in jokes and festivals. Children learn to curse silently before they can write. Lovers develop private hand languages. Arguments in public can become almost theatrical: hands flashing, shoulders cutting, lamps flicked on and off, boots stamped into resonant metal plates set into street corners for emphasis.

Indoors, where walls are baffled and rooms are padded against the engines, they speak more softly than lowlanders expect. Their homes are not quiet, exactly, but tuned. Every household knows which rumble belongs to the nearest pump, which drone comes through the foundation at night, which faint ticking means the roof traps are filling. The rooms are arranged around dryness rather than light. Shelves stand high. Beds rest on stone feet. Cupboards have raised lips. Important objects are hung, sealed, or set in lacquered chests. The poorest families still own waterproof boxes; the richest own rooms that can be closed like compartments in a ship.

They are not romantics about water. They do not worship it in any simple way. They respect it, bargain with it, curse it, measure it, fear it selectively, and depend on it absolutely. Their old sayings are practical and severe:

A covered drain has a memory.  
The leak you hear is not the leak that kills you.  
Dry stone lies.  
Downhill is a neighbor.  
No wall belongs to one house.

Their ethics follow the slope. A person may be forgiven laziness, rudeness, debt, even drunkenness, but not neglect that endangers those below. To block a gutter with rubbish is a moral offense. To conceal seepage in a party wall is close to assault. To falsify pressure records is infamous. Families have lost names over such things.

Many of their trades are forms of custodianship. There are pump families, cistern families, valve families, quarry families, bridge families, condensation tenders, roof claim assessors, ravine mappers, wall readers, scale cutters, ladder inspectors, tram brakers, seepage clerks, flood archivists, and guilds of masons whose authority is older than the present government. A mason of high standing can stop a public works project by placing one chalk mark on a retaining wall. No one ignores it.

The engineers are not sleek modern figures set apart from labor. They are half scholar, half mechanic, half weather prophet, and the fractions do not add cleanly because their work does not divide cleanly. They keep notebooks in oilskin. They inherit rumors about old channels. They know equations and also which elderly woman in which terrace remembers the flood that came through a bakery floor forty years ago. The best of them are humble before the plateau. The worst become dangerous because they believe a new instrument has made humility obsolete.

Children grow up in courtyards of tanks rather than gardens. They play games of balance on painted dry-lines, race floating seedpods through gutters, dare one another to touch warm pipes, and learn district maps as vertical puzzles: above, below, under, through, beside, draining toward. Their toys often imitate the city—little wheels, locks, ramps, siphons, weighted gates. They build miniature aqueducts in runoff mud. They are scolded not for getting wet but for wasting clean water or stepping into unknown water.

Old people sit near thresholds and know everything. Their authority comes from having survived enough seasons to become unofficial instruments. They can identify pump cycles from the tremor in a cup. They know which public square will fill first in a pressure rise, which tram line sings before a cable fault, which household is hiding an illegal cistern, which shrine has shifted three finger-widths since last year. During return tides, when plazas darken from below and the stone begins to sweat, people watch the elders. If the elders remain seated, business continues. If they stand, shutters close.

There are religious people, though religion here is hard to separate from maintenance. Shrines stand beside gauges, culverts, old springs, sealed shafts, and places where disastrous water once emerged. Offerings are not flowers but useful things: lamp oil, chalk, copper wire, folded rubber, clean rags, new bolts, salt, wax, blue glass vials of potable water. Prayers ask not for miracles but for tolerances: let the wall hold; let the seal seat; let the rain divide evenly; let the night crew stay awake; let what is hidden announce itself before it breaks.

Their festivals are not lush. They do not have harvest abundance. They have releases, closures, recalibrations, commemorations of repairs completed before anyone now alive was born. The great festival silence is their most unsettling rite. When the engines are brought down one after another, the people gather as if for an eclipse. Some weep. Some laugh too loudly. Some cannot bear it and go indoors, where they play recorded pump-noise through old speakers. In the thinning quiet, they hear small things usually erased: a child coughing across a ravine, water ticking from moss, lizards skittering on warm brick, their own blood in their ears. The city seems, for a few hours, less alive and more exposed.

They are suspicious of untouched beauty. A dry meadow would make them uneasy. A silent house would seem abandoned or deceitful. A forest would feel like concealment. They admire visible function: a clean spillway, a well-laid stone course, a valve that turns under one hand after fifty years, a bridge that sheds water exactly where intended, a roof that gathers condensation without fouling it. Their ornaments imitate utility. Jewelry is often made of polished washers, old gauge needles, porcelain insulator fragments, mineral accretions cut and sealed like gems. Wealth shows itself in dryness, in quiet rooms, in private cistern rights, in the ability to keep paper unwarped.

They are hospitable, but their hospitality includes instruction. A guest is told where to step, what not to touch, which basin is for washing and which for emergency storage, which night sounds may be ignored and which require waking the house. Visitors who treat these warnings as quaint are not invited back. Visitors who listen carefully are respected, even if clumsy.

They have humor, dry as the upper terraces. They joke about leaks, inheritances, municipal codes, failed waterproofing, and the arrogance of people from lowlands who think water only travels downhill. They give nicknames to machines. They mock engineers, then obey them. They mock priests, then bring them bolts. They mock the city itself most tenderly, as one mocks a difficult elder whose moods feed the family.

Besides the human population, many smaller lives persist in the margins.

There are the pale lizards of the steam walls, almost translucent at the belly, quick as flicked water. They bask where pipes warm the stone and hunt the insects that gather near condensation lines. Children believe they can predict pressure rises because they vanish before certain vents open. Engineers deny this publicly and watch them privately.

There are thin-legged insects that skate on drainage films and oily basins, living on microbial slicks no one likes to name. Their bodies flash silver-green in reflected factory light. Some appear only after return tides, hatching from cracks that seemed sterile the day before.

There are cliff frogs in the ravines, few and secretive, their calls spaced far apart as if conserving sound. They cling to wet seams in the dark and lay eggs in pockets of mineral water where no fish can reach. Their presence is considered a sign that a ravine is not yet dead. Survey crews mark frog-water with small white symbols and reroute work when they can.

There are birds that use the city but do not trust it: high-winged thermal riders, stack-circlers, ravine swifts, occasional black scavengers that perch on obsolete pylons after storms. Their nests are rare within the industrial districts because the vibration unsettles eggs. When a pair successfully raises young on a bridge girder or crane arm, workers often protect the site with absurd seriousness.

There are mosses that are less like plants than weather made visible. They darken seams, soften old inscriptions, and reveal where hidden water moves. There are algae in the cistern lips, fungi in felted shutters, salt-loving films in abandoned settling ponds, and orange bacteria painting slow wounds around iron bolts. The city scrubs them away and depends on them as indicators. Growth is contamination, evidence, warning, calendar.

And then there are the machines.

No one says, officially, that the machines are alive. That would be childish or heretical or legally inconvenient. But the people speak of them as if they have tempers, appetites, sleep, grudges, and old injuries. A pump that has run for ninety years is not replaced lightly. A wheel that complains in a familiar rhythm is preferred to a silent new one. Engines are coaxed, cursed, rested, commemorated. Their maintenance logs read like medical histories and family chronicles: fever in the north bearing, coughing at half-load, improved after relining, refuses cold starts, sings under flood demand.

The oldest cisterns are treated with even greater unease. Some predate the factories, some predate the city’s present name, and some may have been caverns before they were architecture. Workers entering them do so in pairs, with lamps, chalk lines, and a kind of formal politeness. Voices behave strangely inside. Water answers too late. A dropped tool may sound as if it falls farther than the chamber permits. The cisterns do not move, but they receive, hold, and release the life of the plateau. In a place like this, that is close enough to breathing.

What lives there, finally, is a people who have accepted that their city is not built upon the land so much as in negotiation with it. They are not conquerors of the plateau. They are its current maintenance crew.

They inhabit a civic organism of stone, pipe, pressure, vapor, law, habit, and remembered flood. Their lives are measured not only in births and deaths, but in inspections passed, walls stabilized, pumps overhauled, drains cleared before rain, children taught where not to step, and old leaks found before they become disasters.

They are loud-world people with quiet interiors. Wet-stone people. Gauge readers. Threshold builders. Keepers of roofs and sumps. Citizens of downward obligation. They live among sparse plants, cautious animals, mineral blooms, old engines, and water that refuses to remain merely below.

And like the city, they continue: slowly, loudly, deliberately, with one hand on the wall.
