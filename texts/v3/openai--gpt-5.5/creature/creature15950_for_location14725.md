What lives there is life that has never learned to look upward for permission.

Most of it is pale, shelled, blind, or shining; most of it is half builder and half scavenger. The animals do not merely inhabit the basin. They finish it briefly, damage it, digest it, armor themselves with it, and leave it altered for the next bodies to use. There are few clean separations between creature and country. A shell is old roof-water. A tooth is hardened vent-salt. A nest is chewed clay. A wall is bone and lime and abandoned carapace. Even the smallest mite carries a little of the cavern’s unfinished geology on its back.

The first true inhabitants are the films.

They spread where warmth, mineral seepage, and animal residue meet: slick varnishes on stone, dull amber smears around pools, black-greenless stains that look like damp shadow. They are not moss, not algae, not anything that has ever wanted sunlight. They feed on heat, sulfur, iron, carbonate, rot, and the slow chemical generosity of new stone. Everything else begins by eating them or by eating what eats them. When the basin is quiet for a few hours, the films bloom invisibly. When the herds arrive, they are scraped down to shine.

The scrapers are everywhere: fist-sized, many-legged, armored in overlapping white and gray plates. Their mouths are paired chisels, always working. They rasp mineral skin from rock, polish old bones, clean the shells of larger animals, and sometimes strip the dead before the dead have fully stopped moving. They are the basin’s punctuation—clicking, pausing, lifting their feelers, then clicking on. When disturbed, they fold into hard knots and wait to be mistaken for stones. Many are carried accidentally in the mud packed around larger feet; in this way they colonize new cracks almost as soon as cracks appear.

The low herds come after them.

These are broad-backed shelled grazers with heads tucked under frontal shields and mouths like wet files. They move in bands along the edges of water, scraping luminous residue, bacterial crusts, salt skins, and the softened margins of calcite veils. Their shells are maps of where they have fed: orange where iron is plentiful, chalk-white near drip fields, black-glossed after crossing volcanic plates. Their young ride under the adults’ rims, clinging to the warm undersides until their first shells harden. A herd sounds like a hundred baskets of stone being dragged across a floor.

Predators follow the herds, but few hunt cleanly. This country favors ambush, patience, opportunism.

In the fog borders wait tall hinged things on stilt legs, their bodies held high above the heat-shimmer. They are jointed like insects and armored like crabs, with feathery rods combing the air ahead of them. They do not see their prey. They read pressure, salt, breath, vibration, the tremor of shell on crust. When they strike, they fold downward all at once, a collapsing scaffold of knives and hooked limbs. After feeding, they stand motionless for hours, vents steaming around their ankles, while parasites comb the joints they cannot reach.

There are also the steam bells: translucent floating animals that drift in warm vapor pockets and shallow hot pools. They look delicate until they close. A mite-cloud, a hatchling, a finger-long swimmer brushing the wrong membrane—then the bell snaps inward and drops, trapping its prey against stone or water. Larger ones hang in columns of rising heat like inverted cups. Their bodies pulse faintly with borrowed light from whatever luminous things they have eaten.

The pools have their own citizens.

Ribbon-creatures live there in water too shallow to hide them. They are long, pale, muscular strips, sometimes forked, sometimes braided in mating knots. They can cross wet stone by folding themselves end over end, but they prefer the narrow black pools and sudden runnels left by roof-rain. Some feed by sifting mineral-rich mud through fringed throats. Others enter eggs, carcasses, or soft-shelled young and eat from inside. When a flood comes, they travel everywhere. When the dry phase tightens the pools into brine, they coil under salt skins and wait.

Blind fish exist too, though “fish” is only a convenience. They have soft skulls, paddle fins, and mouths that open downward to vacuum larvae from the silt. Some carry lights in paired pouches beneath the jaw. Others are transparent enough that their organs show as faint violet knots. They spawn in channels that may not exist a week later, so their eggs are adhesive, heat-sensitive, and patient. A clutch can lie in dry clay through one whole phase of the basin and wake when warmth and moisture return.

Beneath the floor are the burrowers.

They are warm-bodied, blunt-headed, nearly hairless except for sensory whiskers around the mouth and along the flanks. They shove through soft sediment in family knots, churning clay, opening drainage, collapsing drainage, burying nests, unburying older bones. Their skin is waxy and often plated with mud that dries into temporary armor. They are prey for almost everything when young, but an adult burrower is difficult to kill: heavy, muscular, able to vanish downward with a wet thud, able to crush a stilt-beast’s leg in its jaws if cornered. Their tunnels make the basin breathable in places and treacherous in others. Whole shelters have sagged into their abandoned runs.

Above all of them live the roof colonies.

They are not plants, though from below they may seem like pale hanging orchards or soft mineral fungi. They are colonial animals, shell-bodied and luminous, attached in sheets to the high vault. Each individual is small, cupped, and rimmed with fine feeding hairs that comb condensation and airborne nutrients from the damp air. Together they glow cream-white, brightening when warm gusts rise, dimming before tremors, rippling in slow communication across the ceiling. They shed residue that feeds the grazers. They drop young like sparks. Their dead shells become part of the roof’s ribs and curtains. In a real sense, they are the cavern’s weather lamps.

Among these larger lives moves the small airborne multitude: winged mites, thread-hangers, droplet larvae, heat fleas, ash-colored gnats that cannot truly fly but leap and glide on convection. Many spend one life stage in water, another on shell, another in the warm vapor clinging to a vent. They are food, infection, pollination without flowers, rumor made flesh. When a large animal passes, clouds of them lift from its wake. When a carcass opens, they arrive as if the death had rung a bell.

And there are the hand-users.

They are people, or close enough that their hands make the question painful.

They are small-banded, cautious, and never numerous. The basin will not support arrogance. Their bodies have adapted not toward conquest but toward noticing. Their skin is pale or clay-darkened by constant mineral paste; their eyes are large but not dominant, useful in the diffuse glow but useless in fog. Their ears are keen. Their bare feet, when they go unwrapped, feel warmth gradients through crust and mud. Their noses and tongues read the air for metal, rot, steam, poison hatchings, fresh collapse. They walk with one hand free and one hand near the wall.

They do not build towns. They make pauses.

Their huts cling to pillars and ledges, placed where the floor has been trustworthy for several seasons of seep and heat. Walls are mud-block, shell, sinew, broken flowstone, patched endlessly because permanence is a surface superstition. Doorways are low, not for defense alone but to keep out drifting mite-clouds and sudden warm gusts. Shell strings hang in entrances to rattle when air pressure changes. Children learn those sounds early: rain-coming, vent-cough, herd-near, roof-shed, bad fog.

They keep tools as if tools are relatives. Quartz scrapers. Bone needles. Gut nets. Bowls fired beside vents and accepted as imperfect because the heat chooses its own work. Wood is nearly sacred: carried from above, inherited, guarded, repaired with shell splints until it is more repair than original branch. A wooden tripod may be a shrine, a memory, a measure of distance from the surface, or all three at once.

Their language, if heard, would likely be full of verbs for temporary states. Not “home,” but “the place holding.” Not “river,” but “water passing this way today.” Not “road,” but “safe until the next trembling.” They name phases more confidently than places: Wet-Hanging, Salt-Tight, Deep-Heat, After-Breaking. Their calendars are pecked into stone not to command the future, but to remember how often the country has refused prediction.

They hunt, but carefully. They trap blind fish and larvae in narrow channels. They harvest roof-fall after the poisonous softness has hardened. They crack abandoned shells for mineral and tool blank. They follow herds at a distance to collect what the herd exposes. They eat burrower when they can get it, steam-bell when they know it has emptied its sting, mite-cake when hunger lowers pride. They cultivate nothing green. If they cultivate anything, it is placement: encouraging films on chosen stones, stacking shells where scrapers will clean them, tending warm mud beds where edible larvae gather after rains.

They fear many things, but not darkness most of all. They fear silence, because silence means pressure building, gases pooling, predators listening, or colonies about to drop. They fear smooth floors that were rough yesterday. They fear beautiful new crust. They fear fog with no smell. They fear a pool that has become warm too quickly.

Their dead do not stay separate from the basin. Some are placed high in dry niches, wrapped in shell strings and mineral cloth, where the air slowly salts them. Some are given to warm pools with spoken apologies. Some bones become needles, rattles, map pegs, handles. This is not desecration there. It is continuity. In that country, refusing to be reused would be the stranger sacrilege.

No creature there lives alone as an individual for long. A body is habitat before and after it is a self. Shells host mites. Mites carry eggs. Eggs wake in mud opened by burrowers. Burrowers follow warmth made by vents. Vents feed films. Films feed herds. Herds break terraces. Broken terraces make pools. Pools breed ribbons. Ribbons feed bells. Bells fall and rot. Rot brightens the roof colonies.

What lives there, finally, is a whole unfinished ecology of thresholds: between animal and mineral, shelter and collapse, weather and architecture, birth and scavenging, person and prey.

Its inhabitants are not primitive. They are immediate. They belong to a world where everything is still becoming solid, and so they have become expert in incompletion. They do not wait for the earth to finish making a place before entering it. They arrive while the stone is warm, while the water is uncertain, while the light is still being secreted by shells overhead.

They live in the pause before permanence.

And when the pause ends, they move.
