What lives there is less a collection of species than a single, distributed condition of being.

The first inhabitants are the forests themselves.

They are not plants in the lesser sense of green things rooted somewhere and waiting for weather. They are the weather, the ground, the architecture, the engines, the law. Each colossal root-mass is both organism and province. Each trunk-column is less like a tree than like a slow organ of the continent: vascular, resonant, mineral-hungry, exact. Their leaves are not passive surfaces but working plates, angled to pressure and light by ratcheting joints of cartilage-hard cellulose. Their blossoms do not invite in the manner of flowers on land; they regulate, admit, sort, and seal. A bloom may be large enough for a whale to swim through, yet its opening is no wider than the slit required for one particular pollinator’s body.

They are ancient without seeming old. Nothing about them is withered. Their age has become precision.

Some of them have no single visible body. A meadow folded across three depths may be one individual, its root nerves braided through kilometers of buoyant fiber. A black lacework near the upper membrane may be another: thin, delicate, almost weightless, drinking light where water meets air. A trunk descending into green-black pressure may belong to both of them, or to neither, or to a third body whose true shape is only intelligible as sound.

They think, if they think, in maintenance.

Not in plans. Not in desires. Not in memories of before and after. Their awareness is the constant registration of pressure, salinity, sugar, mineral, cold, light, strain, vibration. A torn frond is answered by chemistry. A crowded colony is answered by bitterness. A shift in current is met by vanes turning one half-degree more open. Their intelligence is not a voice inside the world but the world’s endless correction of itself.

The great plants are not silent minds. They are thunderous ones.

Their thoughts are pumps. Their decisions are valve-sequences. Their attention passes as changes in resonance through rootwood and suspended fluid. A continent-root can feel a grazing shoal as a soft dimming across its underside. A trunk can know the weight of nesting animals by the alteration of its hum. A flower can recognize the approach of its pollinator long before touch, from the minute pattern of wing-fins beating in the water.

They do not hurry toward response. They are already responding.

Around and within them live the animals.

The most common are the ribbon grazers, pale and long as strips of moonlit cloth. They move in loose shoals, never dense enough to darken the water, their bodies flattened for the permanent currents. Each has a row of fine stabilizing fins along its sides, transparent except at the joints, where small blue bones show through like writing. Their mouths are soft, circular, black, and patient. They scrape mineral sugars from leaf-undersides and root-skins, leaving behind combed trails that the plants seem to expect, even require.

They do not school in fear. They align because the water aligns them. At certain depths their bodies form slow bands across the green light, each animal separated from the next by the exact spacing of current and finwash. When one is taken by a hunter, the band opens, closes, and continues.

Near the trunks live the fan-winged ones.

They are larger, heavy-browed swimmers with six articulated fins that unfold like wet machinery. Their heads are armored with ridges of living enamel and dark horn. Their throats glow faintly, not to lure, but to communicate with the plant columns whose hollows they shelter among. The glow pulses in time with the sap hammers. A blue pulse means feeding. A white pulse means mating. A long green dimness means pressure-change. But these are not messages in any dramatic sense. They are confirmations, like gauges agreeing.

They hang almost motionless beside the massive trunks, occasionally turning all six fins at once to correct their station. Moss grows along their backs in formal patterns. Small crustaceans clean the moss. Worms live in the moss. Threadlike algae grow through the worms. Nothing is merely itself.

There are blind plate-brows in the lower reaches, slow animals the color of old bone. Their eyes have sealed over into polished scars. Their faces are broad and downturned, made for browsing the heavy ribbed leaves that grow in the dark. Along their flanks sit white colonies of soft animals like folded hands. The plate-brows never scrape them away. In exchange, the colonies drink waste heat and bleed a mild antifreeze into the host’s skin.

They are solemn creatures, though solemnity may be only the shape of their slowness. They move through the deep roar as if through thought. Their calves are born already coated in pale symbionts, already smelling of the particular darkness they will inhabit.

There are predators, but none of them seem cruel.

The shaft-hunters are almost invisible until they open. They live in permanent columns of clearer water, bodies stretched upright like panes of glass. Their organs are arranged along their edges, leaving the center transparent. Particles pass through combs in their flesh. Light passes through them. Small fish pass too near, and then the hunter becomes a mouth.

It does not chase. It does not rage. It occupies the place where capture happens.

When closed, it resembles a flaw in the water. When open, it is a white geometry of ribs, membranes, and inward-pointing teeth. The event lasts less than a breath. Then the shaft clears again. The hunter resumes its upright waiting, digesting in faint cloudy pulses.

Other predators are more mobile but no more dramatic: crescent eels that patrol the underside of folded meadows; armored polyps that close over sleeping crustaceans; soft-bodied lanterns that imitate blossoms and consume the pollinators they attract. Even deception here has the calm of a completed equation. Every lure is known. Every appetite has its place.

The small lives are innumerable.

Beetle-crabs travel bark ridges in processions, each wearing a saddle of moss and shell-dust. Their legs are hooked for fibrous terrain, and their undersides are lined with fine brushing hairs that harvest bacteria from the plant skin. They polish the trunks by feeding. They carve no path, yet their repeated routes make satin-dark seams along the ridges.

Glass mites live inside bubbles that never rise. They cling to the inner surface of each permanent bead, grazing on films of trapped light. Their eggs are transparent. Their young hatch into the same bubble, inherit the same curved sky, and spend their entire lives inside a sphere no larger than a fruit.

Threadworms braid themselves through root hairs in living cables. Some are red with dissolved metals. Some are green from borrowed chlorophyll. Some are clear enough that only their motion reveals them, and even that motion is more like the tightening of a knot than travel. They help sort nutrients at scales too fine for the great plants’ coarse organs. They are the capillaries’ capillaries.

Anemone colonies grow from old scars in the plant flesh. Blue, white, violet, black: each color belongs to a different pressure band. They open when the sap pumps strike certain notes and close when grazers pass too heavily. Their tentacles taste sound. A deep vibration makes them bloom. A sharp one folds them into themselves like fists.

The pollinators are swimmers, not flyers, though their bodies remember wings.

They have long segmented abdomens and broad side-fins veined like petals. Their bellies carry waterproof pockets where golden dust collects. They enter flowers through valve-slits made exactly to admit them. Inside, the water is warmer by a degree. They turn slowly in chambers of pollen, nectar, and mineral vapor, then depart with their bodies shining.

Some species pollinate only one bloom, and the bloom may be larger than a city. Generations of them live and die traveling between folds of the same floral organ, believing, if belief exists for them, that the world consists of fragrant chambers separated by cold green corridors.

There are animals that are half plant by treaty.

The antlered drifters are among the most beautiful. They resemble narrow deer only in the branching crowns that rise from their skulls and shoulders, but those crowns are not bone. They are living green fans rooted into the bloodstream. The animals swim slowly through light shafts, turning their antlers to the brightness. Sugars pass from the plant tissue into the animal; warmth, nitrogen, and motion pass back. Their eyes are dark and widely spaced. Their mouths are small. They do not graze much. They bask underwater.

Their young are born without antlers. For a brief interval they are merely animals, quick and nervous, hiding in root shadows. Then spores settle into prepared grooves in their skulls. Green threads enter the blood. The young grow calmer. By maturity they have become wandering gardens, less anxious, more exact.

There are spine-lit swimmers whose dorsal ridges contain algae in glassy tubes. There are shell-backed crawlers with miniature fern forests growing from their armor. There are pale worms whose tails end in rootlets and whose heads feed like fish. There are transparent larvae that choose whether to become animal-heavy or plant-heavy depending on the sound of the place where they settle.

No creature there is entirely independent. Independence would be a kind of exile, and exile has no ecological room.

The closest thing to a people are the listeners.

They are not numerous. They live in the middle depths, where the roar is complicated but not crushing, among the broad intervals between trunk-columns. Their bodies are long, dark, and flexible, with four primary limbs and many fine sensory streamers trailing from the head, ribs, and spine. They have eyes, but their eyes are secondary: wide silver organs suited to gradients, not detail. Their true perception is through pressure and resonance. The world speaks through their bones.

They do not build.

They do not carve chambers, bind tools, cultivate gardens, or mark territory with objects. To make a structure would be redundant, perhaps even vulgar. Structure is already everywhere, alive and exact. Instead they inhabit resonant niches: hollows in root vaults, quiet lenses of water between competing hums, the lee of great leaves where vibrations arrive sorted into layers.

Their hands are delicate, webbed, and many-jointed. They use them mostly to touch living surfaces, to read the pulse of sap, to groom symbiotic growths from one another’s skin, to steady children in currents, to press their bodies against vibrating bark and listen.

They speak in harmonic posture.

A lifted streamer changes the tone passing through the skull. A curled limb interrupts a vibration and sends back a shaped echo. Their language is not projected into silence but braided into the existing roar. To an outsider it would be almost impossible to distinguish conversation from the background industry of life. A family gathered on a root ledge may look like a cluster of dark leaves trembling in current; in fact they may be exchanging lineage, warning, hunger, recognition, grief.

But even their grief is not quite like grief elsewhere.

They do not understand loss as subtraction from a forward-moving life. When one of them dies, the body is anchored gently in a nursery of root hairs. Moss saddles over the ribs. Blue anemones open along the spine. Beetle-crabs arrive. The listeners return often, not to mourn an absence, but to attend the body’s change of participation. They know the tone of a dead relative entering the larger hum. They can hear, for a while, the difference.

Their children are raised communally because parentage is diffuse. A child may be born from one body, fed by three, taught resonance by five, and chemically steadied by the root-vault where it sleeps. Infants cling to the underside of adults with ventral pads and drink a mineral-rich milk that tastes of cold iron. Before they can swim, they can listen. Before they can speak, they can distinguish trunk thunder from flower ticking, predator hush from rain-fall, safe shadow from hungry transparency.

They have no maps. They have chords.

Each region is known as a pattern of vibration: the low twin beat of adjacent sap cathedrals, the hiss-field of folded meadows, the clear bell tone of a pressure front, the granular fizz of bubble fog. To travel is to modulate from one chord to another. To be lost is not to lack direction but to hear no familiar interval in the roar.

They do not seek the upper air. The shining membrane above is sacred to some, irrelevant to others, but never imagined as escape. It is an organ of the world, not a door. A few listeners tend the lace roots there, hanging upside down beneath the skin of the sea, their backs nearly touching sky they cannot breathe. Their bodies are patterned with black and silver to match the rootwork. They feed on light-rich films and on tiny animals trapped against the surface tension. They are considered strange because they live so close to brightness that their speech becomes thin.

Others dwell low, where their bones thicken and their skin loses pigment. These deep listeners are broad-headed and slow, with sensory streamers shortened to heavy cords. Their language is bass. Those from higher provinces can feel it in the sternum but cannot reproduce it. The deep ones tend the blind plate-brows, clean parasites from their colonies, and sleep inside the shelter of leaves that are more like cold iron than vegetation.

If the listeners possess religion, it is the recognition that the world is already complete.

They do not pray for change. They do not tell stories of creation as a past event or ending as a future one. Their sacred forms are continuances: the pod that clicks, the rain that falls without depletion, the hunter that opens, the sap hammer that has never missed its interval. A broken rhythm is terror. A restored rhythm is joy.

Their art is temporary and leaves no artifact. They gather in resonant chambers and arrange their bodies so that the plant-thunder passes through them in altered harmonics. A dozen listeners, properly spaced, can make a root cavern sing with overtones otherwise unheard. The performance ends when they move apart. Nothing remains except the world, exactly as it was, now more fully known.

They do not count years. They count returns of bodily condition: the next hardening of skin, the next clearing of the throat-light, the next time a child can hear the low root beneath the higher one. But since nothing here advances toward a different state, even age is not a journey. An elder is not someone who has gone farther. An elder is someone through whom more of the same world can be heard.

There are diseases, though they too are balanced.

A silver fungus sometimes grows in the listeners’ sensory streamers, making them hear echoes that are not present. Such afflicted ones drift toward bright shafts and speak to predators as if to kin. They are not restrained unless they endanger children. Often the fungus eventually flowers from their skin in small white cups, and the mind clears. Sometimes it does not. Then the body joins the roots earlier than expected.

There are parasites that imitate symbionts. There are eggs laid under scales. There are blooms whose nectar induces stillness so complete that a feeding animal forgets to leave. But no plague spreads beyond its appointed range. No infestation becomes conquest. The system accepts harm as one of its regulating organs.

The lives there are therefore not peaceful in the sense of being gentle. They eat, pierce, scrape, invade, digest, compete, poison, mate, defend, and die. But violence never gathers into history. It does not accumulate into war, famine, collapse, or triumph. It is distributed into function. Every mouth is also a valve. Every wound is also a site of colonization. Every corpse is a scaffold.

The smallest larva and the largest forest-continent share the same fundamental gesture: remaining.

To live there is to be held in exact suspension.

A ribbon grazer remains by grazing.
A shaft-hunter remains by opening.
A flower remains by admitting one swimmer and excluding all others.
A listener remains by hearing the roar and placing its body correctly within it.
A continent-root remains by pumping cold green thunder through itself forever.

No inhabitant truly owns a place, because place is made of inhabitants. No creature stands apart from terrain. The ground may be digesting. The shelter may be breathing. The current may be partly muscular. The weather may be an organ’s labor. Even emptiness belongs to something, held open by pressure, sound, chemistry, and need.

What lives there is a civilization without construction, an industry without tools, a wilderness without disorder.

It is populated by beings whose bodies have accepted that motion need not become change. They swim, feed, bloom, pulse, listen, strike, open, close, hatch, and die inside a balance so vast it feels eternal. Their lives are not stories moving toward transformation. They are notes sustained inside the immense chord of the suspended sea.

And above them, below them, through them, the great vegetal engines continue to thunder.
