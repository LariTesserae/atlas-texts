What lives there is not a population but a consensus of green bodies.

It is difficult to say where one organism ends. The apparent trees are not trees in the solitary sense, nor are the walls merely walls made of many stems. A column may be a congress of vines, trunks, roots, fungal veils, pressure-swollen creepers, and leaf-plates that have braided themselves into one civic decision. A plaza may be a flat animal-sized thought of a hundred plants agreeing to become floor. A tower may be the upward ambition of an entire buried district, expressed briefly as architecture before it splits, sheds, roots, and becomes neighborhood again.

The inhabitants are the city.

They live as chambers, bridges, gutters, mouths, reservoirs, avenues, skins, membranes, ribs. They live by changing shape before shape can harden into identity. They do not occupy buildings; they become building, then abandon the form, then digest it, then repeat it elsewhere with a small correction. Their citizenship is growth. Their speech is sap pressure, shade, tension, chemical drift, the slap of leaves, the groan of roots moving underground, the percussion of seedpods splitting open in the heat.

They are plants, but not passive plants. They are not waiting for weather. They manufacture weather at their own scale: lifting water, trapping fog, sweating steam, opening cistern-fruits, flashing their pale undersides to summon or answer wind. They make rain local. They make drought intimate. They store thirst in thickened roots and spend it in sudden fountains of leaf. Their bodies are infrastructure: aqueduct, pavement, wall, canopy, hinge, pump, archive.

There are many kinds among them, though none remain separate for long.

There are the root-domes, low and swollen, like patient municipal organs pressed together in quarters. They breathe through oval openings rimmed with wet cambium. They are soft inside, lined with pale, responsive tissue, and their rooms close slowly after disturbance as if forgetting that emptiness was ever useful.

There are the braided pillar-colonies, hundreds of stems wound into single trunks, each individual half-strangled and half-supported by its neighbors. They creak with communal strain. Their surfaces are ridged by old negotiations: this vine thickened here, that shoot yielded there, this root abandoned its downward path to become a brace. They remember contact better than time.

There are the leaf-plate makers, low, disciplined growths that tile the plazas in tight hexagons. Each plate is glossy, veined, and temporary. When one bruises, another rises beneath it and presses it loose. The plazas therefore are never paved once; they are continually repaved by living patience.

There are the cistern-fruits, translucent and heavy, hanging in chains between towers. They are reservoirs with skins. They fill from condensation, rain, seepage, perhaps from distances that have folded into them. When the air dries, they purse their lips. When rain strikes, they swell until their walls show internal lattices like green glass. No animal drinks from them, yet they guard their water with the seriousness of wells.

There are the liana bridges, nervous and muscular, never simply spanning a gap. They test air with tendrils. They crawl sideways. They thicken where pull demands it and digest the slack portions of themselves. A bridge here is not an object but a balancing act with leaves.

There are the young towers, glossy-barked and sharp-ribbed, impatient upward things. They have not learned weathering. They grow as if height were a sudden hunger. Their crowns end in needle-shoots and leaf-spears. Sometimes they exchange crowns with other towers at a distance, or appear to lean both ways, or show in reflection a version of themselves that has already rotted down into a stump.

There are the sound-bearing hollows, stems and trunks that boom, hum, knock, and resonate until noise becomes part of their anatomy. Some arches are held not quite by wood but by vibration. Some plazas pulse from beneath with buried impacts. These plants do not merely make sound; they use it as pressure, as scaffold, as weather, as warning, perhaps as memory.

There are the paradox-adapted growths: vines that grip invisible supports before the supports exist; leaves with weather-scars from seasons still approaching; roots growing around the absence of themselves; saplings rooted in their own future rot. These are not diseased exceptions. They are local specialists. In a basin where causality is moist and friable, they have learned to photosynthesize contradiction.

There are fungal films too, though even these seem partly claimed by the green. They spread over shaded walls like living paint, veined and faintly luminous, converting collapse into surface, surface into nourishment, nourishment into new intention. They are not scavengers after the city but collaborators within it. Decay here has chlorophyll in its dreams.

If these beings have minds, their minds are distributed and practical. They do not think in images of self. They think in openings and closures, in pressure gradients, in where light touches, in where water lingers, in what may be used as support. Their desires are not animal desires. They do not stalk, flee, mate in pairs, raise young, defend nests, or call across empty air. They desire occupancy. They desire conversion. They desire surface, moisture, light, repetition, variation, contact.

They are not malicious. They are dangerous in the way weather is dangerous, or metabolism, or a crowd that cannot see the person it is pressing against. A vine may take a still body for a trellis. A floor may heal over an imprint. A doorway may close because its tissues require conservation. A corridor may repeat itself until a traveler is exhausted, not to trap, but because repetition is one of the city’s methods of being sure. The life here has no predators because it has no need to chase. It only expands the definition of room.

Nor are they peaceful. Peace implies intervals, rest, a background against which events occur. These inhabitants are event itself. Every stem is arriving. Every leaf is replacing another. Every root is revising the map below. Their violence is not cruelty but exuberance under pressure: pods detonating, bark splitting, walls bulging, towers sloughing sheets that become houses by evening. They live too quickly for stillness and too recursively for wilderness.

Their society, if it can be called society, has no citizens but many civic behaviors.

They make districts. They make thresholds. They make gathering places for no gatherers. They make benches, halls, avenues, arcades, courtyards, towers, wells, and gates without having any separate bodies to sit, assemble, travel, rule, drink, or enter. This is perhaps their strangest trait: they imitate habitation from within life itself. Their architecture is not for occupants. It is an expression of growth discovering the grammar of a city and using it because arches distribute stress, plazas gather light, cisterns hold rain, corridors channel wind, towers reach sun.

They do not know they resemble a settlement. Or else the resemblance is their way of knowing.

They reproduce by rupture, drift, rain, resonance, architectural rearrangement, and impossible memory. Seeds do not need birds, fur, or feet. A pod cracks and the city moves around the fallen kernels. A district sheds a skin and the skin roots. A fruit ripens here and appears broken elsewhere because elsewhere has remembered it. Pollen clouds rise thick enough to obscure streets; pressure waves carry fertility through corridors; rainbursts beat reproductive dust from cup to cup. Sometimes the sky itself briefly becomes leaf and drops civic fragments that grow into gates and gutters.

Their young are everywhere, though the entire city feels young. Pale shoots nose through compost. New buttresses distort old walls. Buds appear by the thousand during accelerated episodes, swell, open, fail, feed, return. Even the oldest-looking trunks are only temporary concentrations of growth. Nothing here waits long enough to become venerable. Age is not a dignity but a material to be consumed.

Their dead are also everywhere, though death is never final enough to form ruins. A collapsed wall becomes mulch. Mulch steams. Steam wets the underside of leaves. Leaves drip into root mouths. Root mouths swell into chambers. Chambers split into arcades. The dead therefore remain politically active in the city’s body. They are amendments, not endings.

The largest inhabitant may be the basin-wide organism itself: not one plant exactly, but an emergent being made of contact, repetition, and shared thirst. It pulses from edge to edge. At moments all leaves align, all towers bow, all water trembles in cups, and the roar drops into one immense note. Then the districts seem less like separate species and more like organs of a single green intelligence rehearsing all its possible forms at once: seed, sprout, city, ruin, compost, seed.

This intelligence is not humanlike. It does not plan by drawing a future and then building toward it. It plans by growing several futures, letting them collide, digesting the failures, and reinforcing whatever remains useful for the next hour. It has no memory as a person has memory, but it remembers in scars, repeated corridors, recurring arches, root pathways, chemical habits, and impossible reflections. It has no laws except local rules, yet it obeys them with exquisite seriousness until the rule changes.

What lives there, then, is a botanical urban mind without a face.

A metropolis of stems.

A civilization of cambium.

An ecology that has taken on the shape of public life without producing private lives.

It has no animals because animals would be redundant: the movement, noise, hunger, engineering, reproduction, danger, and restlessness have all been assumed by vegetation. There are no citizens walking the avenues because the avenues themselves are alive. There are no builders because the buildings are growth behaviors. There are no voices because the whole basin speaks in rupture, drip, groan, hum, and leaf-clap.

It is inhabited by green becoming. By thirst interrupted. By architecture that feeds. By roots dreaming upward. By leaves broad enough to be weather and young enough to be wounds. By plants that do not merely live in a place, but have replaced the idea of place with their living.
