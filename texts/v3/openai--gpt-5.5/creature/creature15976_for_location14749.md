What lives there is not a single people, nor even a set of creatures that can be cleanly separated from the place. The inhabitants are continuations of the wet ground: mouths given patience, roots given hunger, stones given skins, water given nerves.

The oldest presence is not old in years but in recurrence. It lives in the mud as warmth, fermentation, and appetite. Every fallen leaf, egg-case, shed scale, drowned wing, and softened bone enters it. The mud is crowded with minute lives that do not pause: bacteria, threads of fungus, translucent worms, blind larvae, burrowing crustaceans, root-hairs fine as breath. They turn death into heat and sweetness before memory can form around it. They are the basin’s stomach. Nothing lies unused. Nothing is allowed to remain merely dead.

Above them live the grazers of surfaces.

They are small, innumerable, and specialized almost to absurdity. Beetles with polished backs travel the undersides of floating leaves, scraping films of algae with combed jaws. Pale shrimp-like things sift the mineral seepage at the edges of warm pools. Blue-black worms braid through the upper mud, tasting iron, rot, and pollen. Long-snouted larvae drill into swollen stems and drink the diluted sugars inside. Tiny fish, nearly blind, browse on bacterial mats that glow faintly in the heated water. Their bodies are soft, silver-white, and almost unfinished-looking, as if they were made from the same vapor that rises around them.

There are frogs everywhere.

Not merely frogs, perhaps, but a whole nation of amphibious forms: stem-clingers, pool-singers, mud-burrowers, glass-skinned night feeders, heavy-throated boomers with eyes like wet seeds. Some are no larger than a thumbnail and live their entire lives in the cups of leaves, where rainwater, insect husks, and pollen collect. Some hang in clusters from reed stems, their bellies pulsing in sequence. Some are broad, patient, and mottled like stones, sitting half-submerged for days until a fly, smaller frog, or careless fish crosses the narrow kingdom of their reach.

Their eggs are strung under leaves in clear ropes, packed into foam on warm puddles, hidden inside hollow reeds, or carried in damp folds of skin. Tadpoles graze not only on algae and rot but on the backs of larger animals, cleaning them with small, nibbling mouths. The frogs are prey to almost everything and predators of almost everything smaller. Their voices make much of the basin’s audible fabric: clicks, purrs, wooden knocks, liquid bells, nasal drones, and deep pulses that seem to come from the mud itself.

The reptiles are fewer but more deliberate.

They bask where raw stone shoulders through moss, lying in the sweating heat with jeweled stillness. Some are small, bright, and fast, with throat-fans that flash blue, orange, or black when they signal across the low stone ridges. Others are long-bodied, eel-limbed things that slide between water and root-shadow, taking frogs from below. There are turtles or turtle-like creatures with soft, dark shells furred in algae, so still that insects nest on them and young plants root briefly in the muck on their backs. There are narrow-headed hunters that float like sticks in the pools, eyes and nostrils raised, their bodies invisible beneath brown water.

They do not rule the place. Nothing rules it. They simply occupy a patient layer of danger.

The air belongs to insects.

They hang in columns, veils, knots, and trembling rooms. Some never seem to travel more than a few body-lengths from the patch of air into which they hatched. Midges collect over warm pools in vertical swarms that hold their shape for days. Broad-winged flies drink from flowers that smell of milk and blood. Beetles crawl through moss towers and mineral fuzz. Winged termites or termite-like creatures build damp, pulpy galleries inside reed clusters and fallen stems, though nothing here stays dry enough for true permanence.

There are dragonfly forms with glassy wings and heavy jaws, each one the color of tarnished copper or green oil. Their larvae wait below, masked and jointed, clinging to submerged roots until something passes. There are wasps that lay eggs in caterpillars, in frogspawn, in the soft seams of fruiting fungi. There are moths whose wings resemble wet leaves so perfectly that frogs place their feet on them without striking.

The insects do not visit the basin. They are one of its weathers.

The birds, if birds is the right word, are mostly heard rather than seen.

They move in the broken green overhead: small, restless, reed-clawed things with long toes and quick throats. Some creep along the undersides of branches, taking beetles from moss. Some spear fish in shallow pools with needle bills. Some feed only on the hidden waxy flowers, their faces dusted yellow, red, or gray with pollen. Others are meat-eaters, silent until they drop through the leaves and rise with a frog or lizard struggling in their feet.

A few are large enough to disturb the canopy when they shift. Their calls are harsh, barking, or metallic. They nest in woven platforms of vine and reed, but these nests sag, rot, sprout, and are rebuilt constantly. Nothing they make becomes an artifact. Even their abandoned eggshells dissolve into the green digestion below.

There are mammals, or things approaching mammals, in the floating mats and root islands.

Small furred creatures with webbed paws move at dusk through the low vegetation, though dusk is only a thickening of the same dimness. Their fur is slick, close, and dark, shedding water badly but holding heat well. They feed on eggs, beetles, soft fruits, and the blind fish trapped in leaf-cups after rain. Some have long whiskers that tremble constantly in the saturated air. Some carry their young attached to the belly or tucked into damp reed nests raised just above water level. They seldom run; there is nowhere to run to. They climb, swim, press through, pause, listen, and vanish.

Larger furred things exist too, but mostly as sounds, displaced reeds, and wallows that never dry. They browse on succulent stems, chew mineral-rich mud, and sleep half-submerged in warm hollows. Their bodies are barrel-heavy, low to the ground, with wide feet that spread their weight over the treacherous surface. Moss and flies gather on them. Birds perch on them. Tadpoles feed along their flanks when they stand in pools. They are less like wanderers than moving islands of appetite.

In the deep interior live the great marsh beasts.

They are seldom seen because seeing is not the sense by which the basin knows them. They announce themselves by pressure: a tremor through water, a slow collapse of reeds, a line of bubbles rising from a channel that seemed empty, a low call that enters the ribs before the ears. They may be amphibian, reptile, mammal, or some unresolved compromise among them. Their skins are dark, wet, and plated in places with mineral scale. Their eyes sit high. Their mouths open sideways into pale interiors. They do not chase often. They wait in habitual basins worn not by erosion but by weight.

Around each one is a small traveling ecology. Leeches cling to them. Fish shelter beneath them. Flies drink at their eyes and wounds. Birds pick parasites from folds of skin. Plants seed in the mud they carry. When one dies, it does not become a skeleton for long. It becomes an event: a hill of feeding, blooming, hatching, dissolving life, gone before the place can remember its outline.

The plants are not passive residents.

They are among the most active lives there, though their movements are slow and hydraulic. Broad leaves tilt and shed condensation into the cups of their own roots. Vines braid the air, not climbing toward some high sun so much as thickening the dim world into chambers. Ferns unfurl with animal tenderness. Mosses climb raw stone and make it breathe green. Floating mats interlock and separate, opening black seams of water and closing them again.

Some plants pulse visibly in the warm pools, their translucent stems swelling and narrowing as fluids move through them. Faint chemical light gathers inside them after long heat. Their flowers hide under leaves, just above the waterline: thick, waxy, and secretive, smelling of rot, fruit, milk, blood, or fungal bread. They do not invite pollinators delicately. They overwhelm them. A single blossom may be crowded by flies, beetles, mites, and small nectar-feeding frogs until it seems less like a flower than an organ exposed.

There are carnivorous plants too, though they are not theatrical. Their traps are slick cups, closing pads, adhesive hairs, drowned chambers, fermented hollows. They take what the air gives them. Insects vanish into them by the thousands, and still the swarms do not diminish.

Fungi live everywhere that light weakens, which is almost everywhere.

They make pale antlers under leaves, black fans on dead stems, orange films over sweating stone, soft nets through mud, and hidden threads through living roots. Some fruiting bodies emerge only for a few hours, swelling like tongues and collapsing into slime before night. Others glow faintly in root-caves, not enough to illuminate, only enough to suggest depth. Their scents are sweet, meaty, mineral, intoxicating. Animals seek them, avoid them, nest in them, die in them.

They are the basin’s memory, but a memory that does not preserve shape. They remember chemically. They know what has fallen, what has opened, what has bled, what has sprouted. Then they erase the evidence.

There may be intelligence there, but not the kind that builds.

If any creature watches with something like thought, it has learned the uselessness of lasting marks. A clever wader may drop shells into a pool to lure fish. A reed-dwelling mammal may weave a cradle that survives one season. A canopy bird may remember the sequence of fruiting flowers across miles of identical green. A marsh beast may know wallows older than its own body, returning to them as if to inherited dreams.

But no mind there straightens the world. No one clears, stacks, burns, carves, or names in a way the basin will keep. Intelligence, if present, is humid, immediate, and bodily. It exists as timing, mimicry, patience, route-memory, breeding-call, ambush, and the exact pressure of a foot placed where mud will hold.

The true inhabitant is the chord itself.

Every animal is a note in it. Every throat, wing, bubble, stem, mandible, and seep contributes. No single creature owns the sound, and none can interrupt it. Birth enters it. Death enters it. Rain enters it. The boom of the great unseen beasts enters it and is absorbed. The basin does not fall silent to acknowledge anything.

What lives there are beings adapted not to change but to endless beginning.

They are soft where the world is soft, wet where it is wet, quick to hatch, quick to feed, quick to vanish. They have no need of long bones, deep burrows, dry nests, high vantage, or durable monuments. They live in layers rather than territories, in surfaces rather than paths, in cycles so immediate they resemble stillness.

They are newborn creatures in a place that never finishes being born.  
They are the fauna of first mud, held forever at the edge of becoming old.
