What lives there is not a single hidden monster, though the place sometimes gives that impression.

It is a whole wet nation of small, hot, blind lives, compressed into a crack in the new mountain as if the mountain’s first thought was not fire but hunger.

The oldest inhabitants are almost not animals at all. They are the clear skins on the stone: slick, trembling films stretched over glass, mineral lips, mud, and seep channels. They are fed by heat and bitterness. Iron reddens them. Sulfur yellows them. Some are transparent until disturbed, then flush faintly green or bruised violet, as if remembering light. They creep without limbs, splitting and rejoining, advancing over fresh rock in soft fronts. Where a vent opens, they burn away; where it cools by even a finger’s breadth, they return first.

Everything else feeds on them, directly or eventually.

The smallest grazers are pale and jointed, with too many legs for their bodies. They move like punctuation marks across the wet black glass, stopping, scraping, stopping again. Their heads are narrow wedges packed with combs and needles. They do not bite so much as shave. They rasp invisible food from the stone and leave behind clean, shining trails that fill again within hours. Many are eyeless. A few carry beadlike spots under their translucent shells, not true eyes but heat-sense organs, each one clouded red or amber by dissolved metals in their blood.

They are born in clusters of eggs no larger than salt grains. The eggs are glued to the undersides of ledges, in places where steam condenses but boiling runoff cannot reach. When rain floods the basin, egg masses tear loose and drift. This is not disaster. It is dispersal. Many species depend on being washed into new cups of stone before the cup that made them becomes too hot, too sour, too crowded, or suddenly impossible.

Among them cling the flat ones.

They resemble pieces of wet membrane, scraps of drowned skin, oval or leaf-shaped though no leaf has ever grown here. They press themselves against glass and pulse. Their bodies are so thin that the dark stone shows through them. Within each, mineral grains collect in branching patterns, like tiny maps of rivers that do not exist. They feed by embracing films and digesting what lies beneath them. When threatened, they do not flee. They loosen one edge, fold over themselves, and become almost indistinguishable from a bubble of mucus or a torn sheet of condensation.

Their young bud from their undersides and crawl away as clear blisters with mouths.

In the runnels nearest the heat live the red-backed mites.

They are hard, glossy, and bright as sparks under water. Their shells contain iron taken from the mountain, and over generations they darken from orange to ember-red to nearly black. They gather where the water would cook most other lives. They are quick by the standards of the basin, darting in tiny bursts, vanishing into pores, reappearing in numbers that make the streambed look peppered with living coals.

They have no patience and no fear until the stone shifts.

At the tick of falling glass, they freeze with the rest.

That stillness is one of the laws of the place. Every body knows it. Every body obeys. For a few breaths after a pebble falls, even the swimmers stop writing their threads through the water. Stalkers hold their limbs half-raised. Grazers leave their mouths pressed to stone. Larvae stop chewing inside their prey. The films themselves seem to tense, though they have no nerves.

Then the mountain decides not to collapse, and life continues.

The pools contain thread-swimmers: long, pale, almost hairlike animals with dark knots for organs spaced along their bodies. They do not swim by pushing water. They coil through differences in warmth. A thread-swimmer will hang motionless between a hot seep and a cooler overflow, then suddenly slip sideways, pulled along a gradient no human hand could feel. Some live entire lives in a pool the size of a footprint. Others vanish into hairline cracks and emerge days later in basins that should not connect.

Their mouths are rings of cilia. Their tails taste metal. When two meet, they knot briefly, exchange cells, and separate diminished but altered. Sometimes a knot fails to untie, and the joined animals become a drifting net that filters the pool until a predator tears it apart.

The stalkers wait along cooler rims.

They are larger than most things there, though still small enough to fit in a cupped hand. Their bodies are soft except for jointed forelimbs and ash-patterned dorsal plates. They look unfinished, like thoughts of crabs or mantids attempted by a mountain that has never seen the sea. Their mouths open downward in petaled folds. Their forelimbs bend slowly, almost tenderly.

They hunt by leaning.

A stalker chooses a crowded ledge, lowers itself until it becomes part of the shadowed stone, and waits for traffic. Grazers climb over its feet. Mites pass beneath its jaws. Flat membrane-things pulse beside its flank. Then, when enough lives are within reach, it opens.

There is no strike. There is only a sudden absence where prey had been.

Its mouth unfolds wider than its head should allow, wet and silent, and closes over a patch of world: film, mud, mites, eggs, a grazer’s hind half, sometimes another predator’s limb. The stalker digests everything together. Its waste is mineral-rich and eagerly grazed.

The small pale hunters are more deliberate.

They have paired foreclaws, soft bodies, and narrow heads with transparent jaws. Some use pebbles. They select them by weight, not shape, tapping several before choosing one. A hunter will carry a chip of black glass or a rounded mineral bead beneath its body and approach shelled prey with exaggerated care. Then it raises the stone and knocks.

Not hard. Not wildly. It taps.

Once. Wait.

Twice. Wait.

Again, until a seam weakens, a shell lifts, or the creature inside makes the mistake of shifting. Then the hunter inserts its clear jaws and drinks.

No one teaches this. The young begin with grains too small to crack anything and spend days striking films, bubbles, mud, one another. Most never become good at it. A few do. Those few grow broad and patient and scarred.

The shelled wanderers are the ones that carry glass over their backs.

They are low, blunt animals with flexible shells and muscular undersides. When crossing exposed hot stone, they search for a flake of obsidian, lever it up, and drag it over themselves like a moving roof. Beneath the glass they travel slowly, hidden from heat and from the eyes of whatever watches from reflections. At the next wet seep, they abandon the shard. Another may use it later. Or it may slide into a pool, become an island for eggs, then a feeding ledge, then vanish under mineral crust.

They have no homes. Nothing here has a home in the lasting sense. The mountain is too young for that, and too changeable.

There are burrowers in the mineral paste: white, blind cylinders with soft shovel-heads. They churn the mud in shallow cups, swallowing grit, film, eggs, dead skins, and chemical precipitates. Their tunnels collapse behind them. They aerate the basin without knowing it. Predators find them by the faint rise of bubbles over their paths. When seized, a burrower splits its rear half away and keeps digging with the front.

The lost half continues to writhe for several minutes, distracting the attacker.

Sometimes it grows a head.

Sometimes both halves die.

The basin accepts either outcome.

There are also surface-hangers, pale things that live upside down beneath the water’s skin. They cling not to stone but to the tension between air and pool. Their backs are dimpled with trapped steam. Their mouths point downward. They graze the underside of the surface film, eating pollen that never came from flowers, ash dust, bacteria, and the microscopic dead. When rain strikes, many are crushed instantly. Others use the violence to leap into neighboring pools.

At night, different creatures emerge.

Silver ribbon-things slide from cracks too narrow for their bodies. They are flat and reflective, and they move by making waves along their edges. They gather at vents and drink directly from the warmest seams. Their bodies brighten when they feed, not with light but with a polished pallor, as if each has swallowed a sliver of moon.

Slow white burrowers surface after dark to mate. They rise half out of the mud and knot together in clusters that look like roots, though no root has ever touched the place. By dawn the knots have dissolved. The adults retreat downward, leaving strings of eggs in mineral slime.

Bead-bodied predators come then too: chains of round, glass-clear segments, each segment holding a dark internal speck. Their jaws are transparent hooks. They hunt by touch, but not only by touch. They seem to feel where motion is about to be. A grazer turning left is caught before it turns. A mite about to dart finds a jaw already waiting.

These predators avoid reflected water.

All the wiser animals do.

The reflections in the basin are not always passive. Most show only steam, sky, rock, and the trembling multiplication of bodies. But some are wrong in consistent ways. A pool may reflect a ledge that is gone. A bead of condensation may show a vent before it opens. A strip of water on black glass may return the shape of an animal with extra limbs, or missing limbs, or a shadow too large for its body.

Certain creatures use this.

There are mirror-grazers, almost colorless, with flattened heads and wide side-set heat organs. They feed at the edges of false reflections, where microbial films grow thickest and least disturbed. Their young are born with dark markings that mimic the gaps seen in reflected stone. Predators misjudge them. Sometimes the mirror-grazers themselves misjudge the world and step into surfaces that are only images. They do not fall through. They simply vanish from the visible basin and reappear later, smaller, paler, often carrying mineral grains not found in any open pool.

There are shadow-leeches too, if leeches is the right word.

They do not attach to flesh. They attach to the slight lag between a body and its shadow when noon light enters the pocket and the geometry loosens. A shadow-leech is a thin dark filament, nearly invisible except when crossing pale carbonate. It trails after moving creatures, feeding on heat, trace chemistry, perhaps memory of motion. An animal burdened by too many shadow-leeches becomes slow, then confused, then unable to choose safe surfaces from fatal ones.

During bright hours, some grazers gather in groups and scrape one another’s shadows clean.

The basin’s larger mystery is the figure in the beads.

Not always. Not in every drop. Not reliably enough to be called an animal by ordinary standards. But something lives where condensation makes little inverted worlds.

It appears as a dark, compact shape on ledges no creature can reach. Sometimes it has limbs folded beneath it. Sometimes it is only a headlike swelling and a line of back. It is never seen directly on the stone. Only in droplets, slick reflections, and the brief silver skin of disturbed pools.

The other animals respond to it.

When it appears in many beads at once, the stalkers lower themselves flat. The red mites leave hot runnels and cluster under overhangs. The thread-swimmers knot into dense coils. The shelled wanderers stop crossing exposed stone, even if they are already halfway over it.

Nothing flees loudly. Nothing cries alarm. The warning passes through contact, water tension, heat, chemical release, perhaps through the reflection itself.

Then the figure is gone.

Maybe it is a predator that exists partly in the basin’s wrong angles. Maybe it is the basin seeing itself. Maybe it is only what happens when many wet eyes, none of them true eyes, share one fear for long enough.

The inhabitants do not distinguish these possibilities. They behave correctly and survive.

Their senses are tuned to a world where sight is unreliable. They read heat more fluently than light. They taste pressure changes in their joints. They feel minerals blooming in water. They know the difference between ordinary steam and steam that has begun to sink. They know which wall can be climbed when water runs upward and which identical wet surface will scald them hollow. They know that a crack too narrow for a limb may lead to another pool, or to boiling darkness, or to a distance larger than the mountain.

Their bodies are full of metals. Their shells are glassy, chalky, iron-red, sulfur-yellow, translucent, black. Their bloods and internal fluids are strange colors: rust, milk, weak green, clear with floating flecks. Many are soft because armor grows too brittle in the changing chemistry. Many are hard because softness is eaten immediately. Every solution is temporary. Every lineage is an experiment being conducted by heat.

They reproduce extravagantly.

Eggs coat ledges like frost. Larvae hatch already feeding. Some species brood young in pits along the back until the parent molts, at which point the offspring eat the shed skin and scatter. Others release threads of gametes into rain-flooded pools, trusting chaos. A few carry embryos in transparent sacs beneath their bodies; you can see the young inside turning like pale commas.

Death is rarely separate from life. A body that stops moving is grazed before it cools. Shells become shelters. Shelters become mineral nuclei. Mineral nuclei become ledges. Ledges become crowded. Crowds become hunting grounds. Hunting grounds become sterile overnight when a vent shifts, then bloom again with film.

They do not have peace.

They have continuation.

They have pauses.

They have the collective stillness after a falling pebble, the rush after rain, the nocturnal emergence, the brief blaze of clear daylight when every body shines in place and the whole basin seems less like a habitat than an exposed nerve.

If there is intelligence there, it is not centralized. It is distributed among habits, reflexes, chemical trails, inherited routes through impossible space. It is in the pebble taps of the hunters, the glass shields of the shelled wanderers, the avoidance of certain reflections, the synchronized freeze of thousands of bodies. It is in the way the community learns without remembering: a pool scalds, its residents die, nearby eggs hatch into a slightly different pattern of caution.

The true resident may be the crowd itself.

A hot, silent, many-bodied thing spread over stone and water, eating the mountain as the mountain makes it. Not a forest, not a reef, not a swamp, though it resembles all three in fragments. A summit organ lined with animals. A wound that became an ecosystem before it became a scar.

What lives there is life at its least decorative and most urgent: blind, wet, mineral-fed, pressed close to its neighbors, using glass for shade and stones for teeth, breeding in steam, trusting no reflection completely, and accepting without wonder that sometimes water climbs, shadows lag, and a room-sized crack contains more paths than the mountain has room to hold.
