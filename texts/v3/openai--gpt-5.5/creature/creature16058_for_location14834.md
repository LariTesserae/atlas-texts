What lives there is not a population but a metabolism.

There are no creatures in the usual sense: no bodies with mouths and nerves and private hungers, no small lives darting between larger lives. The inhabitants are sessile, braided, intergrown, and slow. They do not move through the place because they are the place, or because movement has been solved differently there. To live is to extend, thicken, seal, seep, divide, pressurize, digest, and remember chemically.

The largest beings are the columnar conduit-plants.

They rise from the wet ground like black-green towers, but they are less like trees than vertical organs. Their outer plates are hard, glossy, and ridged, armored against nothing in particular except pressure, rot, and their own weight. Beneath the bark are stacked vascular chambers, some carrying water, some mineral liquor, some warm sap dense with dissolved carbon. They have no crowns. Instead they open into shelves, membranes, hanging nets, condensers, and suspended reservoirs. Their upper tissues are not searching for sun. They are managing moisture, heat, charge, and gas.

A single conduit-plant may not be a single individual. Its base is usually grafted into others. Its roots merge with belts that run under whole provinces. What appears to be one stem may be an aperture of something wider: a local chimney of a continental body. When one dies, it does not fall at once. It is slowly repossessed. Its inner walls are stripped, its minerals drawn back, its hollow packed with root-foam and new shoots. Death there is a maintenance procedure.

Beneath them live the rhizome belts.

These are among the true powers of the continent. They lie under marsh-skin and peat, wide as roads that were never made, muscular without muscle. They pulse over weeks, years, centuries. Their tissues contain valves of cartilage-like fiber, expandable chambers, pressure locks, and mineral filters. Through them water travels farther than streams can manage. Through them chemical signals pass slowly from basin to basin. Through them the continent coordinates itself without thought.

They are not intelligent, not in the manner of animals, but they are responsive. A mineral seam opening in new basalt may change the thickening of a belt a hundred kilometers away. A drowned grove may be sealed off, drained, then recolonized from below. A basin rich in sulfur may become a specialized organ of yellow tissue and heat. The rhizomes do not decide. They answer gradients. Their wisdom is not mental; it is distributed in pressure, acidity, temperature, and flow.

There are also the piston plants.

They inhabit the flooded flats in ranks and clusters, thick dark cylinders set into sockets of peat. They rise and settle with enormous patience. Their outer surfaces are slick, nearly featureless, but inside they are complicated: gas bladders, compression chambers, sap diaphragms, fibrous springs that tighten for months before releasing. They feed on rot, trapped methane, and mineral imbalance. They compress what decay gives off and store it as work.

A piston plant has no need to hurry. Its whole life may consist of a few thousand vast, slow breaths. Upward, it lifts. Downward, it seals. Around its base, rings of mud expand by increments too small to witness. The plants around it depend on its pressure. It is a pump, a lung, a refinery, and a clock.

In the dark halls live the sheet-leaves.

They hang between columns like interior weather. Some are broad enough that water collects upon them in shallow trembling loads before being released as inward rain. Others are folded into pleats, increasing their surface area in the warm stagnant air. Many are not leaves in the old sense. They do not primarily harvest light. They condense vapor. They exchange gases. They catch mineral dust. They radiate heat into the close darkness. Their undersides are often veined with dim organs that flare only when chemistry requires it.

They are soft but not passive. They tighten, sag, seal tears with clear gum, and redirect water along reinforced ribs. A sheet-leaf may live for centuries, growing heavier, darker, more mineralized, until it becomes too stiff to function. Then digestive threads enter it from its own supports, and it is eaten back into the system while a younger membrane unfolds beneath.

The floating mats are younger, quicker, more opportunistic.

They spread over black pools and shallow basins, stitching water shut. Their upper surfaces are waxy, pebbled, and beaded with condensation. Their undersides are pale, hairy, and hungry. They drink dissolved carbon and metals from below, while above they shed spores into the still air. When a mat meets another, they do not compete violently. They test chemistry, exchange tissues, merge if compatible, wall each other off if not. In time the seam disappears or becomes a permanent vein.

Where a pool is deep, the mat thickens into a raft. Roots descend into darkness, not to anchor in soil but to harvest the water column. In cracks of young land, these roots may hang for dozens or hundreds of meters, pale cords fading into green-brown obscurity. Nothing eats them. Nothing brushes past. Their only disturbances are pressure, mineral flow, and the occasional settling of the earth.

There are bladder-plants suspended high in the wet blackness.

They are immense, lake-sized sacs held in nets of tensile root, warm-skinned and faintly elastic. They collect lifted seepage, distill it through living membranes, and release it by threshold. Some are clear-walled enough that their contents might show dimly if light existed: layers of fluid differing in density and color, clouds of suspended spores, slow curtains of precipitating mineral. Others are opaque, black and purple, ribbed with reinforcing bands.

Their lives are cyclical. Fill, strain, warm, settle, release. A bladder may spend a century tightening toward one emptying. When it drains, the rush travels through channels and down columns, altering entire wetlands below. Then it begins again, not exhausted, only reset.

There are mineral sorters.

These grow where new stone comes close to the living skin of the continent. They resemble spiral trunks, velvet-lined basins, ribbed fans, swollen knots split with sulfur yellow or iron red. Their tissues tolerate concentrations that would poison other plants. They draw metals upward, bind them in plates, beads, scales, and internal lattices. Some become heavy with their own harvest and sink gradually into the ground, where roots reclaim the softer portions and leave behind strange mineral skeletons: not fossils, not tools, but the durable residue of biological labor.

At thermal lowlands, the sorters become enormous. They form warm chambers around seep vents, root-furnaces where oxidation produces gentle heat. Around them the air is wetter and denser. Their surfaces sweat acidic droplets. Their inner tissues glow faintly at times, not to signal, but because chemistry leaks visible consequence.

There are fungi, or things that occupy the fungal role.

They may not be separate kingdoms. Boundaries of descent have blurred in that wet dark. Some films digest dead bark. Some lace through living tissue as partners, exchanging enzymes for sugars or heat. Some are blue-green smolders on shaded membranes, waking when touched by condensate. Others are invisible except in effect: a softened trunk, a cleanly unthreaded fiber, a mineral band released from dead tissue and returned to circulation.

These decomposers are not scavengers. They are accountants. They do not descend on death as an opportunity; they are already present, waiting in the living body, restrained until chemistry changes. When a tissue fails, they open it with exactness. Cellulose, lignin, resin, metal, salt, carbon: each is separated, stored, moved. Rot there is orderly.

There are spore bodies.

Some are tiny, dusting the undersides of membranes with dull powders. Others are large nodules that swell for decades before splitting soundlessly along prepared seams. Spores do not ride wind far, because wind barely exists. They fall downward like ash in a sealed room. They land on wet bark, black water, root felt, raw stone, and the skins of other plants. Most are absorbed. Some germinate. Some wait, sealed in mucilage, until a crack opens or a chemical condition arrives.

Because there are no animals, reproduction has no seduction in it. No colors sharpened for eyes. No perfumes shaped for longing. No sweet flesh offered as bargain. The inhabitants reproduce by nearness, overflow, pressure, cloning, fragmentation, and patient error. A stem splits and becomes two. A mat tears and each side continues. A root sends up a module where mineral conditions repeat. A reservoir seeds its own drainage path. The continent copies itself because the conditions keep inviting the same answer.

There are electrical growths.

They are dark fronds, stacked leaves, upward filaments, and wet antennae spread across charged basins. They gather the small violence of falling droplets, the separation of surfaces, the gradients between mineral waters. Most of the time they hold their charge invisibly. When threshold comes, pale veins flash through them, linking plant to plant for an instant. The discharge is not communication exactly, though it may have consequences like communication: valves closing, membranes tightening, spores releasing, a whole marsh briefly synchronized by light no eye receives.

The flashes reveal that many of the local beings are only expressions of larger ones.

A grove may be one organism.

A marsh may be one organism.

A chain of basins joined by rhizome and pressure may be one organism spread beyond any possible sight.

Individuality there is a convenience of distance. Up close, one sees stems, mats, bladders, sheets, roots, films. Farther out, these become tissues. Farther still, they become provinces of a living continent. There is no single brain, no sovereign plant-self commanding the rest. But there are loops, dependencies, recurring pulses, ancient chemical habits already established in young ground. The whole place behaves less like a community than like an organ system without an animal around it.

The inhabitants do not know silence. They produce it.

Their lives make only small sounds: dripping, tightening, settling, internal rushes, the faint tick of capillary flow. They have no alarm calls, no songs, no mating cries. Their conflicts are quiet, too. One mat shades another from chemical glow. One root belt captures a seep. One mineral sorter poisons its neighbors with excess. But even competition is damped by grafting, absorption, and replacement. Victory looks like incorporation. Failure looks like being digested.

They are dark-adapted not by becoming pale and blind, but by making light nearly irrelevant.

Some harvest faint chemical radiance. Some use heat. Some live on redox gradients, sulfur, iron, methane, buried carbon. Some depend on the slow breakdown of earlier plant matter compressed under newer plant matter. They feed from the young planet’s unfinished chemistry and from the immense archive of themselves. Their world is not a garden lacking sun; it is a refinery that happened to grow.

If one had to name the dominant life, it would be the vascular continent.

Not a single creature, exactly. Not many creatures, exactly. A wet, low, nearly lightless totality of plant organs, decomposer films, mineral skins, pressure belts, condensation leaves, and slow engines of sap. It has no face and no intention. It does not sleep, because nothing in it wakes. It does not hunger like an animal, but every part of it draws, sorts, stores, and reuses. It does not move, but over centuries it advances across stone, seals water, raises columns, empties suspended lakes, and remakes raw land into living ground.

What lives there is life without pursuit.

Life without eyes.

Life without voice.

Life that has turned landscape into body and industry into growth.

Everything that lives there is rooted, suspended, grafted, filmed, or soaked into something else. Nothing passes through untouched. Nothing stands apart for long. The inhabitants are the pumps and vats and curtains and black halls themselves, working in the dark with no witness, no hurry, and no need to become anything other than more completely what they already are.
