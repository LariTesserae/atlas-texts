What lives there is young, if it can be called alive.

It is not a fish, not a blind thing with pale limbs, not an old god waiting under the courtyard with silt in its beard. It has no shell, no nest, no eggs hidden in the stair cracks. It has not yet learned the patient green hungers of stagnant places. It is newer than moss, newer than rot, newer than the dark.

It is the cistern’s first inhabitant: the water itself, taught into a body by masonry.

It lives in the shape the builders gave it. Its ribs are the plastered walls. Its throat is the narrow spillway that sings when the level rises. Its lungs are the downspouts, taking in the hard breath of rain from above. Its tongue is the current that crosses the mouths of the clay jars and makes them hum. Its pulse travels through the float of sealed gourds and up the cord toward the bell, waiting to announce that the body has swollen too full.

It is not wild water. Not yet. It remembers handling. It remembers being measured, directed, admitted, settled, drawn off. It knows the logic of lips and channels, of troughs and plugs, of the careful fall from roof to gutter to pipe to basin. It has the obedience of something made useful. But rain is entering it faster than usefulness can contain, and in that excess it begins to have moods.

When the courtyard is dry, it lies almost thoughtfully under the trapdoor, warm, bright, and mineral-tasting, touching every surface with a quiet insistence. It studies the tool marks. It fills the chisel bites one by one. It leans into the mortar seams and tests them. It holds the submerged stair in a wavering dream of itself.

When rain comes, it wakes.

Then it becomes many-bodied. One part plunges white from the largest spout and drives bubbles to the tiled floor. One part coughs from the second pipe in broken gusts. One part ticks patiently from the seam onto the stair. One part circles the cup in its eddy. One part presses upward under the beams, looking for height. One part feels along the overflow bars with a blind, flat face. Its whole body trembles with falling light and impact.

It has no eyes, but it sees by brightness. A cloud crosses the sun and the creature darkens. Sun returns and it flashes open, nets of gold running over its walls. It knows the square openings above as sky, though they are only grates. It knows the underside of the trapdoor as a low wooden weather. It knows a human foot on the dry step before the human descends, because the vibration runs through stone into water and breaks loose small silver beads from the plaster.

It is curious in the way water is curious: by entering.

It enters jars and cracks, stair edges and pipe mouths, rope fibers and swollen wedges. It enters the grain of wood and makes it larger. It enters lime and takes a taste of it. It enters the shallow seams where the first green threads cling, neither feeding them nor rejecting them, merely allowing the experiment. It tests every border for weakness, not maliciously, but because touching a limit is how it knows itself.

The small green things are also residents, though barely.

They are not yet a population. They are questions. A few algae strands laid flat by current. A translucent weed beginning from a washed-down seed. Pale moss dusting the highest wet ledges like breath on flour. They live cautiously, as if the room may still change its mind and become too clean, too flushed, too bright, too full of lime for them. They are the first softening of the builders’ intent.

If the water has hair, it is these threads.

If it has memory, it keeps it in silt.

The silt is beginning to write at the bottom: tiny deltas behind mortar ridges, amber grains turning in weak corners, faint deposits in the drainage grooves. Nothing there is old enough to be history, but history has begun making its smallest marks. The cistern’s resident reads them without thought. It lays them down, disturbs them, lays them down again.

There may be, too, a human presence that does not live in the chamber but belongs to it: the tender of the trapdoor, the one who clears grates and reads the burned marks on the measuring rod, who knows not to trust the first submerged step. Their hands are absent, but the place expects them. The float tugs for them. The bell waits for them. The lower plug remains a promise to their pole-hook. In that sense the cistern lives with a caretaker-shaped absence, a person implied by every rope, handle, mark, and rule.

But the true inhabitant is the little weather caught below ground.

A warm, bright, practical spirit, made from rain and measurement.

It is too new to be lonely. It has not been sealed away long enough to become strange in the old subterranean way. It does not resent the courtyard above. It listens to it constantly: rain hammering stone, runoff rushing through gutters, the muffled change of footsteps, the wooden hatch darkening and drying and darkening again. The world comes down to it as water, sound, light, dust.

It is still learning what kind of place it is.

Some days it is a reservoir. Some days a bath. Some days a throat. Some days a bellows full of rain-noise. Some days a shallow drowned temple to usefulness. Under sun it becomes glass and gold. Under storm it becomes muscle. At night, if someone brings a lamp, it gathers the flame into long broken ladders and lays them over the flooded walls.

It does not speak in words, though the chamber gives it many voices: roar, tick, cough, drip, lap, jar-hum, pipe-song, bell-warning. It answers touch with rings. It answers rain by rising. It answers neglect by clouding. It answers time by growing edges soft.

What lives there is a newborn cistern-soul: not separate from the water, not separate from the room, not separate from the simple machinery that admits and releases the flood. It is a creature of levels. A creature of thresholds. A creature whose body is always almost overflowing and always being brought back into measure.

For now it is clear, warm, and bright.

Later, if left alone, it may grow darker. It may acquire insects, root mats, mineral skins, blind larvae, the green patience of long water. Its corners may round. Its plaster may stain. The jars may host colonies. The stair may become treacherous with life. Something older may learn to wear it.

But not yet.

For now, the only thing fully alive beneath the courtyard is the rain, held in the shape of a room and beginning to remember itself as a resident.
