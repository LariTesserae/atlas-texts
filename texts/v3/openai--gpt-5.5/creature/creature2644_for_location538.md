What lives there has learned not to announce itself.

Most of it is not animal in any familiar sense, not at first. It is film, rind, breath without lungs. It lives as a dark slick on stone, as pale fur around mineral seepage, as a greasy gray bloom in the upper finger-width of mud. It eats what falls: powdered shell, dead plankton sifted down from a sunlit world it will never know, torn weed, the dissolved bodies of smaller lives, the faint chemical offerings of stone and brine. It does not reach upward. It spreads sideways. It stains the basin rather than inhabiting it, making every surface slightly less mineral, slightly more alive.

In the soft bottom are the buried ones.

They are worms mostly, though “worm” is too simple a word for them. Some are no thicker than threads and live by combing the water with hair-fine appendages. Some build chimneys from grains of silt glued with their own bodies’ secretions, little brittle reeds clustered in fields where the current brings food. Some never show themselves at all. They lie folded in the mud, pale and red and blue-black, tasting darkness through the sediment, drawing water through themselves, withdrawing from every tremor. When a stone knocks, when a fish passes, when a bank slumps far away and the pressure shivers through the bottom, whole forests of them vanish at once into their tubes. The ground seems suddenly deader. Then, slowly, the bristles return.

There are shell-creatures too, but they are not bright, not clean. They wear the colors of the basin: ash, clay, old bone, green-black tarnish. They live half-buried with only a seam exposed, siphons tasting the water for edible dust. Their shells are thick, blunted by abrasion, often scarred by chemical pitting or crusted over by bacterial mats until they look like stones that have chosen to breathe. Some spend decades moving no farther than the length of a hand. Some die standing upright in the mud and remain there until a current uncovers them, a little graveyard of open mouths.

Small crustaceans live under the stones.

They are jointed, translucent, quick when they must be, but usually still. Their bodies have little spare material in them. They are all legs, plates, feelers, stomach. Many have lost color. Some have lost eyes, or keep only dark pinpricks that register no image, only difference. They wait in cracks and beneath shell bands, claws folded close, antennae extended into the flow. When food passes, they seize it. When something larger moves near, they flatten themselves into the gravel and become part of the bottom’s texture.

They are scavengers, most of them. They do not have the luxury of disgust. A dead fish, a drowned jelly, a shredded ribbon of kelp, a burst worm-tube—anything soft becomes a summons. The first to arrive are the small pale runners, darting over the mud on legs like eyelashes. Then come the heavier ones, blunt-clawed and armored, pushing into the body, worrying it apart. Around them the bacteria thicken. The mud itself seems to lean toward the feast. In time nothing remains but a loosened stain, a little concentration of shell and bone, a place slightly richer than before.

The fish are few and subdued.

They are not sleek hunters made for open water. They are bottom-things, slow, narrow-headed, soft-finned, with skin the color of diluted milk or wet clay. Some have eyes filmed over; some have eyes too large and useless-looking, as if inherited from ancestors who believed in light. They move by sensing pressure, electricity, scent, the faint impact of life against water. Their mouths are often downturned, made to root through silt. Their whiskers drag along the bottom, reading ridges, burrows, bodies, edible decay.

A fish crossing the basin does not cross it like a traveler. It follows invisible sentences written in current and chemical trace. It pauses over a depression where something died three days ago. It noses into sediment and raises a small cloud. It hovers beside a worm field until the bristles withdraw, then snaps at the slowest. Its heart beats in the cold with an economy almost geological. It may live a long time by doing very little.

Some fish are flatter, made for concealment. They lie on the bottom with one side turned to the dark water, their bodies dusted by settling clay until they resemble a patch of floor. Only the gill’s faint opening gives them away. When prey comes close, they rise in a sudden ugly flutter, all mouth and silt, then settle again as if nothing happened. Their violence is brief because violence is costly.

In the water above drift the nearly bodiless.

Jellies pass through like thoughts the basin is having in its sleep. They are transparent, bluish, sometimes edged with faint milk-white organs. They pulse softly and are carried more than they swim. In the mineral fog they are nearly indistinguishable from torn membranes of current, until their bells contract and they turn, deliberate for a moment, then surrender again. Small things strike their tendrils and are held. Larger things tear them open without noticing. Their bodies collapse into food almost as easily as they live.

There are comb-creatures too, ribbed with lines that would flash if there were light enough. Here they glimmer only as pressure-shapes, soft ovals moving through particulate dark. They eat larvae, eggs, microscopic swimmers, whatever the cold water offers. Their beauty is wasted, or perhaps preserved, because nothing down there requires witness.

Where stone is exposed, there are fixed animals pretending to be geology.

Sponges grow low and thick in crevices, gray as drowned felt. They drink the sea through thousands of pores, sieving it grain by grain. Some are hard with mineral spicules, unpleasant to bite. Others are soft and tear easily when a current drags debris over them. They heal slowly. They grow slowly. A sponge no bigger than a hand may have outlived many fish.

Anemones cling where the flow is steady. They do not bloom brightly; they open like pale, muscular mouths fringed with fingers. In the dark their tentacles are less flower than instrument. They hold themselves wide to the passing water, gathering what blunders into them. When silt storms come, they close. When the mud settles over them too deeply, some die sealed shut, becoming knobs under the new skin of the bottom.

There are starved stars and brittle stars, long-armed and patient, drawing themselves over gravel with a motion so slow it seems imposed by the landscape rather than chosen. They find what has fallen. They enter shell beds. They pry at weak hinges. They leave trails quickly blurred by sediment.

And there are larger things, though not many, and not often seen entire.

One may be a broad, low-bodied scavenger that keeps to the colder channels, its skin pebbled and dark, its mouth set underneath like a wound. It senses carcasses from far off and arrives without haste, preceded by a pressure that sends the small crustaceans deeper under stone. It moves over the floor with a heavy undulation, lifting silt in sheets. Worm fields vanish before it. It does not chase much. It waits for the basin to provide.

Another may be long and almost eel-like, but heavier, with a head blunt as eroded rock and sensory pits along the jaw. It lives in the grooves of drowned riverbeds, where darker sediment gathers. It emerges when currents shift and the water brings the smell of opened mud. Its body is pale on the underside, slate along the back, scarred by old parasites and abrasive stone. It can hang motionless in water dense with silt, mouth slightly open, feeling the world enter it.

There may be cephalopods in the deeper hollows: not many, not restless, not jewel-colored. Their skin changes only among the basin’s limited palette—mud, shale, bruise, bone. They flatten against shelves, arms tucked close, eyes wide and dark but adapted to almost nothing. They hunt by touch. They learn the positions of stones, the regular collapses of banks, the places where shrimp gather after a turbidity flow. Intelligence down there does not become invention. It becomes timing. It becomes memory of currents. It becomes knowing when to remain indistinguishable from rock.

The young of many creatures live here briefly without belonging to it. Larvae drift down from upper waters, delicate and doomed, transparent bodies spinning in currents too cold or too dense for them. Some settle and change. Many are eaten. Many simply fail, becoming part of the suspended food that keeps the true residents alive. The basin accepts immigrants mostly as nutrient.

Nothing here sings. Nothing displays except by accident. Courtship, when it happens, is chemical, tactile, a meeting in darkness arranged by dissolved signals and seasonal pressures from a faraway surface. Eggs are hidden in mud, attached beneath stones, brooded under shells, or released into water that will scatter them without care. Many are buried. Some wait in cold for a long time before becoming anything.

The inhabitants are shaped by interruption. They must survive burial, abrasion, hunger, and the sudden arrival of too much world at once: a storm of silt, a slump of clay, a cold brine flood dense enough to smother a hollow. They seal themselves, retract, burrow, cling, or abandon their bodies to the current. Those that cannot endure being covered do not last. Those that cannot feed on the nearly dead do not last. Those that require certainty do not last.

Their senses are not arranged around sight. They know pressure as touch from a distance. They know vibration as warning. They know chemicals as history: who passed, who died, what stone has opened, what mud has been disturbed. A blind fish reads a slope failure hours after it happens by the taste of fresh clay. A crab knows a carcass by a thread of dissolved fat in moving water. A worm feels a shadow it cannot see and withdraws before the body that cast it arrives.

They are not lonely, because loneliness belongs to creatures expecting company of a certain kind. But they are sparse. Between one animal and another there may be long reaches of mud occupied only by bacteria and buried tubes. The basin is crowded with matter, but life appears in knots, stains, pulses. A stone may hold more inhabitants on its underside than an acre of open silt. A seep may become a dim city of films, worms, crusts, shrimp, grazers, and the predators that memorize them.

If there is a ruler there, it is not one of the animals.

It is hunger, perhaps. Or cold. Or the slow rain from above. Everything waits for descent: food descending, silt descending, brine descending, dead things descending, even the memory of continents descending into mud. The creatures live with their mouths, pores, feelers, and open tubes turned toward whatever the water brings.

They do not conquer the basin. They do not make a home in the way land creatures do, by clearing, building, defending a bright center against the outside. Their home is the outside. Their shelters are temporary agreements with stone and sediment. Their bodies are made to be overlooked, pressed close, folded away. To live there is to accept being half-buried already.

And yet they are not merely remnants. They are exact. Every pale worm, every gray sponge, every blind fish moving through mineral fog is a precise answer to the basin’s question. How little warmth can a life spend? How little light can it need? How slowly can it grow and still be ready when food arrives? How completely can it disappear and still remain alive?

What lives there is life reduced of ornament but not of purpose.

It clings. It filters. It waits. It tastes the dark.

It survives by becoming almost as patient as the drowned land itself.
