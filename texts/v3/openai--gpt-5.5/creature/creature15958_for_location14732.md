What lives there is not a people, though the city has citizens.

They are not citizens by law or memory, but by occupancy: each one taking, yielding, signaling, fastening, boring, filtering, spawning, fleeing, digesting. They do not know the city as a whole. None of them could name it. Yet each body carries a portion of its map in nerves, cilia, whiskers, lateral lines, chemical hunger. The city exists in them as pressure and taste.

The oldest inhabitants are the ones that seem least alive.

They are the reef-makers, the crusting colonies, the slow white mouths under films of black. Coral polyps, worm colonies, bryozoan lace, shellfish terraces, bacterial mats, sponges with pores like a thousand patient windows. They live by receiving. They open themselves to the warm dark and take what the water brings: mineral, rot, plankton, the dust of other bodies. Their lives are architectural without intention. They add ledges, thicken pillars, close tunnels, roughen walls, heal breaks badly, make new passages by growing around absence.

Some are delicate as breath. Some are hard enough to outlast generations of teeth. They have no faces, but whole neighborhoods bear their temperaments. A district grown by one kind of sponge is porous, soft, amber-lit from within, full of hidden chambers and slow exhalations. A district ruled by oysters is layered and sharp, a city of lips and hinges. Coral quarters are harder, branching, defensive, full of alcoves where quick animals vanish.

They are eaten constantly. They survive by being too many, too fused, too replaceable.

Over them move the small restless ones.

Shrimp no longer than a finger joint live in the seams, glass-bodied except for their dark bead-eyes and the red thread of the gut. They clean the skins of larger animals, steal from the mouths of anemones, vanish backward into cracks when the water changes. Blind snails rasp black films from stone. Worms extend feathered crowns into the current and withdraw them at the first tremor. Barnacles kick invisibly from their armored cups. Minute crabs climb the walls with solemn, sideways care, their shells colored to match rust, pearl, silt, or rot.

They are the city’s dust made animal. They occupy every defect. A crack becomes a village. A dead shell becomes a tower. A sponge pore becomes a hunting blind. Their lives are brief, crowded, exacting. They hatch into water already full of mouths, drift through avenues as pale specks, settle if they are lucky, and immediately begin either building, grazing, hiding, or being eaten.

Then there are the fish, which make the city seem to breathe.

Small schooling fish occupy the upper ways, where the rain-dim still changes by hour. They move as one loosened thought: widening, tightening, flashing silver when they turn, then disappearing into the black again. They do not own streets; they momentarily become them. A colonnade may be empty, then full of bodies, then empty before any single predator has chosen where to strike.

Some fish are nearly transparent, their organs suspended like faint punctuation. Others are dark-backed, pale-bellied, invisible except when bioluminescent specks ignite along their sides. Some carry blue lamps beneath their jaws, not to illuminate the world but to lie about where their mouths are. Some graze the walls with soft scraping lips. Some hover head-down in crevices, pretending to be broken weed. Some sleep wedged into coral, secreting a thin bitter veil around themselves so that hunting eels pass by in disgust.

The eels are among the city’s more permanent residents.

They inhabit shafts and holes as if the masonry had grown them deliberately. Long, slick, patient, they keep their heads at the threshold and their bodies folded deep inside the city’s dark interior. Their mouths open and close continuously, not speaking, only breathing, but the motion gives them the look of judges reciting sentences. They know the timing of passing schools. They know which lanes fill with larvae after warm pulses. They know the panic-smell of injured fish. When they strike, it is less like pursuit than a door suddenly becoming a throat.

Some eels are blind. Some have eyes clouded over by parasite films. Sight is not what keeps them fed. They read tremor, salt, blood, the small electrical stutter of living muscle. A careless body passing too near an eel-hole enters a law older than any ruin beneath the reef.

In the mud live the flattened ones.

Rays lie over the floor like fallen cloaks, patterned in silt, breathing through hidden vents. Their eyes are reduced or buried; their wings feel the minute convulsions of worms below the surface. They move rarely. When they do, they lift with a softness that makes the mud bloom around them. Their mouths are on the underside, intimate with the ground. They crush shells, inhale burrowing animals, leave oval depressions that slowly fill with drifting black powder.

There are crawling scavengers with many legs and no haste. There are pale cucumbers that process the city grain by grain, taking silt into one end and returning it altered from the other. There are star-shaped feeders that climb shell terraces at night, folding their stomachs outward over anything that cannot escape. There are soft, boneless things that squeeze through gaps smaller than their heads and remember, by touch, every useful passage between hunger and safety.

The cleverest of the open residents are the arm-bearers.

They are not quite octopuses as surface minds might imagine them, though they share the old talent: arms that think locally, skin that speaks before the central mind decides, a body made for negotiation with holes. They live in shellfall and broken plazas, selecting chambers, arranging fragments around their doors, collecting objects that hold useful textures. A flat shell becomes a lid. A spine becomes a warning. A polished stone becomes, perhaps, only pleasing to hold.

They change color in a darkness where color hardly matters. The change is not for eyes alone. Their skin roughens, smooths, puckers, flashes with faint living sparks; it tells water and touch what mood the animal has entered. Alarm. Hunger. Courtship. Irritation. Claim. Surrender. They are thieves, ambushers, architects of temporary dens. They know how to open shells. They know how to wait beside a crab’s preferred route. They know when a collapsing wall has made new rooms worth exploring.

They do not build the city, but they rearrange its loose thoughts.

Higher, in the warm dark columns, the drifting animals pass without allegiance.

Jellies move through the avenues like weak lanterns with long dangerous handwriting trailing beneath them. Their bells pulse slowly. Their lights are bluish, greenish, sometimes no brighter than a memory of moonlight. Small fish shelter among their stinging threads, immune or lucky, using them as moving fortresses. Comb-creatures ripple with rows of ghost-fire. Ribbon-bodied animals, long as corridors, swim with their whole length, tasting the city through skin and flank.

They are visitors and residents both. The city catches them in its eddies, feeds them, tears them, uses their dead bodies to enrich the mud. Some reproduce there in sudden blooms, filling courtyards with transparent bells until the lanes become impassable curtains of sting and hunger.

Around the vents live stranger guilds.

Where warm sulfur seeps from the low plain and the water tastes of metal, the city thickens into feverish knots. Here bacteria are not a film but a pasture, a white and black fur over stone, waving in the mineral breath. Tubeworms rise from it in crowded clusters, red plumes extended into the flow. Blind crabs graze slowly across the mats. Pale shrimp gather in heaps so dense they seem like spilled grain, their backs carrying microbial gardens.

These animals live closer to chemistry than to light. The rain-dark surface matters little to them. They follow heat, sulfide, methane, iron. Their world is not above and below but toward-the-seep and away-from-the-seep. They flourish where other creatures sicken. In the vent districts, the city feels less like a drowned reef and more like an organ digesting the planet.

There are predators larger than the streets seem able to hold.

They do not appear often. Their presence arrives first as absence: the fish pull tight into cracks, crabs lower themselves, shrimp cease flickering, eels withdraw one careful length deeper into their holes. Then a body passes.

Some are long-mouthed, built for sudden acceleration through narrow ways. Some are broad-headed and slow, able to nose apart weakened masonry and suck prey from chambers. Some hunt by stirring silt into a wall and moving inside that blindness. Some carry their own small constellations along the jaw or spine, lights that mimic larvae, mates, safe openings, wounded animals. A few are old enough to bear colonies on their backs: barnacles, trailing worms, patches of black sponge. They are not separate from the city even while hunting it. They are moving neighborhoods of appetite.

The greatest of them are felt more than seen. A pressure change. A single muscular sweep. A courtyard emptying all at once.

But most lives there are small, and most deaths immediate.

The larvae are the city’s weather. During spawning phases, the water becomes crowded with beginnings: eggs, sperm, transparent bodies, drifting threads, tiny mouths not yet assigned to any future. They rise and sink according to currents, temperature, salinity, chance. For a few nights the whole city becomes both nursery and feast. Filter-feeders gorge. Fish open their mouths and swim through abundance. Jellies thicken. Worms bloom from holes. The young vanish by the millions, and this is not failure. This is how the city counts.

The survivors settle according to signals older than choice. A chemical trace says: here is your species. A roughness says: attach. A shadow says: hide. A current says: grow this direction. A dead body says: feed first, become later.

Nothing there lives alone, even inside its own skin.

Parasites thread through gills, blood, gut, eye, muscle. Small isopods replace tongues. Worms curl in the flesh of rays. Transparent copepods cling to fish like living burrs. Bacteria write invisible treaties across every surface. Some inhabitants carry lantern-symbionts in pouches beneath their eyes; others farm digestive partners without which the city’s food would remain locked and useless. A creature is rarely one creature. Each body is a district, and each district is contested.

The dead are populous too.

A fallen fish does not remain itself. First come the quick mouths, the tearing mouths, the opportunists drawn by blood and panic. Then the pickers. Then the borers. Then the films. Bone is cleaned, softened, drilled, hidden in silt, taken into reef, made into wall. Shell becomes shelter before it becomes sand. Flesh becomes motion elsewhere. In this city, death is not an ending but a change in tenancy.

If anything like a sovereign lives there, it is not an animal.

It is the exchange itself: the warm black water carrying messages between bodies, the rain pressing from above, the vents breathing from below, the surfaces growing and failing. The inhabitants are separate only briefly. They enter each other as food, parasite, offspring, shelter, signal. A sponge feeds a shrimp; the shrimp cleans a fish; the fish feeds an eel; the eel dies in a shaft and feeds worms that weaken the tower that falls and opens a plaza where coral larvae settle and small fish gather again.

So the city’s true resident is a cycle wearing many bodies.

Still, one can imagine an individual moving through it: a blind ray lifting from mud at the breathing court, passing beneath clouds of young fish; an eel tasting its wake from a wall; an arm-bearer watching from a shell doorway; shrimp parting over sponge skin; bacterial mats brightening faintly near a warm seep. None of them sees the whole. None needs to.

They live by nearness, by appetite, by patience.

They are the city, and the city is what their hungers have made possible.
