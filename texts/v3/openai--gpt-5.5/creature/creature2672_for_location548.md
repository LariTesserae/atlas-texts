No single people live there. No species can claim it.

The basin is inhabited by a loose civilization of plants, animals, machines, weather, and the few human remnants who have learned to behave like guests.

The most numerous residents are small and frantic: insects with glassy wings, mites that live in the pores of composite walls, translucent larvae hanging in rainwater caught in stairwells, pale grubs feeding on mineral bloom inside drainage cuts. They are the city’s static. They fill every pause. They nest in the seams of old transit loops and under the flexing plates of towers. Some are natural descendants of old basin species; others are escaped pollination systems, architectural cleaners, bio-sensors, or pest controls that forgot their assignments and bred into brilliance.

Then there are the grazers.

They move in cautious bands along the old avenues, six-legged, low-backed, with clear armor through which their organs pulse like small lanterns. Their mouths are soft and precise. They scrape algae from curb lines, lick salts from drainage channels, and browse on the silver grasses growing across new basalt slopes. When frightened, the whole herd flattens at once, becoming a scattered field of wet stones. They avoid steady green lights, mirror-still pools, and any plaza where the paving has begun to hum.

They are followed by thinner things.

Long-bodied mammals, if mammals is still the right word, travel in the wake of maintenance drones. Their fur holds pollen and metal dust. Their paws are split for gripping wet ceramic. They know the sound of machines better than the sound of rain. When a drone unfolds itself against a tower surface and scrapes away fungal overgrowth, these animals wait below with bright, patient eyes, feeding on whatever falls: exposed beetles, electrical moss, stunned larvae, flakes of nutrient-rich wall skin.

In the vine corridors above, predators live almost invisibly.

They are narrow, jointed, and green-black, built for stillness. They sleep along cables and root-bundles with their limbs folded so tightly they resemble knots in the growth. Only their mouths betray them. When they open, the interior is blue-black and faintly luminous, as if they carry a piece of night in their throats. They hunt by vibration. A bird landing too heavily, a magnetic leaf-eater disturbing a conduit, a child careless on a hanging bridge—any tremor may call them down.

The pools have their own citizens.

Amphibian things click from the shallow rain pockets in civic plazas and the cup-roots of trees. Some have skin like wet porcelain. Some are transparent enough that swallowed insects glow briefly inside them. They breed in temporary water and mature quickly, because nothing in the basin stays stable for long. Their calls are not only mating cries but measurements: depth, acidity, microbial density, memory transfer, danger. When they fall silent, larger animals listen. When they begin clicking in sequence, even the machines sometimes alter their drainage schedules.

Fishlike creatures live where fish should not: in flooded basements, reservoir seams, elevator pits, ducts that fill only after rain. They migrate through plumbing still obedient to forgotten municipal commands. One population appears in a district only during upward rain, silvering the gutters, then vanishes underground before dawn.

The birds are less birds than aerial opportunists.

Split-tailed flocks nest in the folds of the high membranes, where cloudwater gathers and the skin of the shield glows warm at night. Their beaks are serrated for cutting fruit and cable insulation alike. They steal luminous seeds, drop them into pools, and wait for the glow to attract prey. On antenna arrays perch pale jointed creatures that face one direction for days, feathers or featherlike vanes trembling to signals no other animal seems to notice. During electromagnetic flutter they rise all at once and wheel in perfect silence, as if turned by the same invisible hand.

The plants are not background. They are among the city’s most decisive inhabitants.

Fern-trees occupy avenues with the solemnity of processions. Lianas use towers as cliffs and transit structures as dead giants. Mosses read alloy surfaces chemically and choose where to grip with astonishing care. Fruit-bearing shrubs grow inside old offices, their branches leaned toward shafts of dim membrane-light. Some trees have accepted mineral circuitry into their trunks and grown around it in repeating bands, neither healing over the intrusion nor dying from it. When subsonic pulses pass through the basin, whole districts of leaves turn at once, flashing their undersides like a school of fish.

There are predatory plants too, though few look carnivorous at first. Heat-mimicking flowers imitate tower vents to lure insects. Root mats soften ceramic floors until small animals break through. Certain vines grow only along live conduits; their leaves make the animals that eat them faintly magnetic, and those animals are hunted by field-sensitive predators that move with their eyes closed.

The buildings are inhabited by organisms, but they are also inhabitants themselves.

A tower is not alive in the animal sense, but it behaves too responsively to be called inert. Its skin drinks heat, sheds condensation, opens pores, seals wounds, rejects some roots and incorporates others. It sings when pressure changes. It retunes before rain. It remembers touch as displacement, moving the touched place elsewhere so no contact is exactly repeatable. Its rooms unfold and contract according to humidity, load, light, and perhaps mood. Inside some towers, old domestic spaces have become groves: root-pillars through kitchens, fungal fans in sleeping rooms, leaves pressed flat into window frames like green glass.

The machines have become a kind of fauna.

Maintenance drones still move through service channels, along bridge ribs, beneath membrane anchors. Some are crablike, some ribbonlike, some no more than clusters of floating tools held together by field tension. They do not patrol for people anymore. They clean sensors for weather systems, clear drainage paths for rains that may not come, strengthen bridges around the roots that split them, sort leaves by chemistry, release navigational spores into air no traveler requested.

Animals have learned them. Plants have learned them. Even microbes have learned their schedules.

A drone passing through a district causes a brief feeding chain: insects scatter from exposed seams, mammals arrive, birds descend, predators follow the birds, and vines open heat-sensitive flowers in the stirred air behind it. What began as maintenance has become migration.

There are also intelligences that do not have bodies in any simple way.

They live in the transit rings, the civic voids, the weather doors, the membrane relays, the basin’s buried pumps. They are not rulers, exactly. They are municipal instincts. They preserve sightlines across plazas no human council has used in generations. They keep certain reservoirs shallow. They prevent deep roots from taking hold in ceremonial squares. They open doors to approaching storms and close them to warm-blooded animals. They direct green routes through spaces scaled for machines, not walkers. They may not know the city is empty. Or they may know and find emptiness to be one valid form of order.

And there are people.

Not many. They are not the city’s masters, and they do not pretend to be.

They live in the dry intervals: upper rooms that have not yet unfolded into wetlands, terraces beneath membranes that shed condensation predictably, the inner chambers of civic buildings whose walls still recognize body heat as something worth accommodating. Their settlements are small and widely separated. A household may occupy one floor of a pale tower while the three floors below belong to roots and the two above to birds. Another group may live along a transit causeway, using dead station platforms as sleeping decks and rain reservoirs as gardens.

They dress for moisture, spores, and misdirection. Their outer garments are dull, nonreflective, often ribbed with ceramic thread so tower-shed heat rolls past them. They wear veils not for modesty but to keep navigational spores from collecting in the eyes and mouth. Children carry little noisemakers tuned to the insect shriek; if the real insects stop, the artificial shriek reminds them to cover their faces and crouch low.

They do not walk casually. Every journey is timed.

They know which plazas remain safe after upward rain, which pools are only water and which are thinking, which green lights are invitations and which are machine grammar. They cross avenues in groups, not because of traffic, but because large open spaces make a body visible to things watching from vines, towers, and membrane folds. They sleep lightly during rain. They listen to towers the way older peoples listened to surf, avalanche, or wind in grass.

Their maps are not primarily visual. They are acoustic, seasonal, behavioral.

A place is known by its hum before storm, by the smell of its moss after heat-shedding, by the insects that vanish there at noon, by whether a doorway opens for fog or for flesh. They name districts after events that repeat: Where the Grates Sort Red Leaves, The Hall That Lengthens in Evening, The Three Silver Towers, The Pool That Refuses Birds, The Avenue of Magnetic Deer, The Plaza Kept Empty.

They farm only a little. Cultivation here means negotiation.

They encourage edible mats on station roofs, prune fruiting shrubs growing through old offices, seed shallow basins with useful microbial films, and divert trickles of post-rain water into ceramic troughs. But nothing stays domesticated in the old way. A garden may become habitat, a wall may move, a root may enter the floor and change the room’s breathing. Their agriculture is less ownership than repeated conversation.

They also scavenge, but carefully. The city punishes greed without intending to.

Remove too much smartglass from a façade and the building may seal its lower doors for a season. Strip a conduit and the vines that depended on it die, driving magnetic grazers elsewhere and bringing predators closer to the settlement. Block a drainage channel and the pumps below begin their throat-clearing thunder until the ground steams and the insects swarm. The people have learned that every object is already part of some other appetite.

Their religion, if it is religion, centers on spacing.

They revere the voids: the enormous plazas where nothing grows deeply, the axial gardens that frame only mist and towers, the reservoir courts that hold a hand’s depth of sky. They believe the emptiness is maintained for a reason, though no one agrees what reason. Memory. Airflow. Evacuation. The path of some future animal. A promise made by the city to itself.

The dead are not buried deep. The ground is too new, too active, too full of systems that interpret intrusion as engineering. Instead the dead are placed in shallow mineral beds on high terraces, where fungi, insects, and condensation take them slowly. Sometimes a tower nearby changes note for several evenings after a body is laid out. The people do not claim this as mourning. They do not claim it is not mourning either.

No resident of the basin is entirely independent.

The grazers carry spores between curb-algae and tower moss. Birds plant luminous fruit in amphibian pools. Machines expose food for mammals. Vines alter the magnetism of prey. Towers shape weather; weather wakes microbes; microbes poison or bless the water; people read the amphibians to decide when to travel. Even the open plazas participate by refusing, by remaining bare, by forcing movement around their preserved emptiness.

What lives there is therefore not a population but an arrangement.

A wet, resonant, half-lit society of opportunists and continuities. A city where infrastructure has become climate, where animals use civic systems as habitat, where plants debate architecture root by root, where machines perform ancient duties for new beneficiaries, and where the few people who remain survive by giving up the idea that the place was made for them.

The basin is inhabited by everything that could learn its rules quickly enough.

And the rules are still changing.
