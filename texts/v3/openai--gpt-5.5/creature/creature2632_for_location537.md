What lives there is not one kind of creature, but a whole society of dampness, pressure, and delayed motion.

The first inhabitants are the mats.

They are not passive ground-cover. They are the oldest, widest bodies on the plateau: mosses, lichens, root-felts, cold vines, bladdered leaves, fungal cables, mineral-filming algae, all braided into continuous living skin. In places they are only a hand deep; in others they are layered through centuries, thick enough to swallow a leg or roof over a pool without revealing the water beneath. They feed on mist, stone, dissolved metal, animal waste, frost, stray light. They remember pressure. A hoofstep, a falling seed, a wing landing, a body dying — all of it travels through them as a tremor and is kept for a while.

These mats are not intelligent in any human way, but they are responsive beyond mere growth. They open valves. They close wounds. They alter drainage. They warm certain hollows and chill others. They lure insects with steam, grazers with salt, fungi with darkness. They form temporary arrangements that resemble machinery because machinery is what happens when purpose passes through matter repeatedly. Under them, water is not simply water; it is labor.

The plants are the plateau’s industry.

There are reedbeds with blue-black plated stems that ring in the wind. Their joints are grown around deposits of nickel, iron, and stranger trace metals drawn from the old stone. When rain strikes them, they clatter like hanging tools. Some turn their seed-heads toward approaching pressure changes before the sky has changed at all. Their seeds are shot not by explosion but by calculation: water weighting one chamber, wind spinning another, a final tilt, a rattle, a spray of black dust.

The pod-plants are taller and more solitary. They stand far apart as though listening to one another across miles. Their skins are translucent, veined, and faintly warm. Inside them, gases collect, separate, and recombine. Some contain cloudy weather in miniature: pale flashes, green vortices, drifting crystals of frost that melt before touching the inner wall. Animals avoid breaking them unless starving. A ruptured pod can fill a hollow with narcotic vapor, sour rain, or a swarm of spores that germinate briefly in fur and then die as silver threads.

Near the warm seep-holes live the gelatinous mounds. They look like slumped heaps of green glass or melted fat, but they are among the most active things on the plateau. They crouch over geothermal leaks and feed on minerals brought up in the water. Their surfaces shiver with cilia. Their undersides are full of red mouths too small to see unless the mound lifts itself, which it does only during ground-storms or when something dead lies near enough to be worth flowing toward. They digest slowly, patiently, with chemical refinement. Bones left inside them come out months later plated in a dull metallic bloom.

Among all this move the small animals, which are everywhere.

Pebblebacks are the commonest grazers: segmented, low, wet-shining things no longer than a hand. Their backs are armored in overlapping plates that mimic stones, moss-lumps, old seed capsules, whatever surface they hatch upon. They move in crowds, scraping films from stems and rocks. When alarmed they lock their legs and become part of the ground. Flocks of birds may land among them and fail to find a single body until the first pebbleback panics; then the whole patch ripples away like gravel remembering it is alive.

In the root channels live pale amphibious creatures with soft heads and long, searching mouths. They are blind except for dim pressure-sense along their flanks. They move through the water beneath the moss and surface through pores to breathe. Their calls are felt more than heard: small knocks in the saturated ground. They feed on larvae, root tips, dissolved rot, and the eggs of vent insects. When the cold deepens, they gather in knots around warm seams and wrap themselves in mucus, becoming nodules that can be mistaken for mineral deposits.

The vent insects are glass-shelled, quick, and impossible-looking. Their bodies are almost transparent except for dark organs pulsing inside like ink drops. They live by extremes: scalding steam, then frost; heat burst, then sudden cold. They crowd the mouths of vents seconds before eruption, feeding on condensed minerals and heat-grown films. When the steam stops, they vanish down cracks so quickly that it seems the ground has inhaled them. Their larvae grow in hot water chambers under the mats, where they spin threads of mineral silk that help line the plateau’s pressure-throats.

There are silver larvae in the drowned tunnels, not young of any obvious adult form. They may be a life stage shared by several species, or something older that borrows the bodies of others later. They flicker in schools beneath the water-skins, turning as one when a shadow passes overhead. During ground-storms, they sometimes rise into the air inside bubbles of vapor and drift for several minutes, suspended in mist, before raining back into pools.

The flying creatures are mostly dark, narrow, and silent.

They have long wings built for crosswinds and sudden plunges. From below they look like strips torn out of the dim sky. They do not sing. They communicate by wing-clicks, throat pulses, and synchronized turns. A flock may circle for hours above a reedbed, waiting for some sound not yet made. Then all at once they drop into the stems, and the whole reedbed convulses with clatter as they seize insects, seeds, and small hidden bodies. When they rise again, they carry the metallic smell of the plants with them.

Some birds — if they are birds — have mirrored eyes that show weather from elsewhere. Predators dislike looking at them. So do the larger grazers. They appear before storms, perch on pod-plants without breaking them, and leave no droppings behind, only small pellets of condensed frost.

The large herbivores come out of the mist as if assembled by it.

There are soft-crowned beasts, high-shouldered and slow, with slick fur lying in dark ridges along their bodies. Their antlers are not bone but living fungal coral: pale, branching, damp, constantly growing and shedding spores. These antlers change with the season, the animal’s diet, and perhaps its memories. Old individuals carry elaborate crowns full of small chambers where insects overwinter and lichens bloom. The beasts graze on bladder-plants and mineral moss, moving carefully, each step placed as though the ground has to be asked permission.

They travel alone or in pairs. Their eyes are filmed against wind and condensation. When they breathe, vapor streams not only from their nostrils but from vents along the neck, where symbiotic fungi regulate heat. Calves are born nearly white and almost translucent. For their first weeks they are hidden in moss hollows, warmed by fermenting plant matter and guarded not only by their mothers but by the fungal antlers shed nearby, which continue releasing bitter defensive spores after separation from the body.

Then there are the linked herds.

At a distance they seem like one low, many-legged organism dragging itself across the plain. Closer, they become separate animals: squat, broad-footed grazers with flexible spines, blunt heads, and wide mouths designed for cropping mats without tearing them loose. But each is connected to others by trailing vines rooted in their hides. These vines are not parasites exactly. They carry nutrients, warning chemicals, warmth, even simple impulses from one animal to the next. A frightened member can make the whole herd turn before the danger is visible. A sick one is fed by the rest until it recovers or is quietly overgrown.

The herd’s young are born unconnected. For a brief season they run freely, bright-eyed and foolish, slipping over wet stone on too-large translucent feet. Then the herd-vines find them. The first attachment is gentle, almost accidental: a tendril around the ankle, a rootlet in the shoulder fur. After that they become part of the larger body. Adults separated from the vine-network grow agitated, then sluggish, then dangerously bold. Predators prefer such strays, though eating one can infect the predator with exploratory tendrils that root in the mouth and lungs if not torn out quickly.

The predators are fewer and harder to see.

They follow the old waterlines, even when the waterline has moved or has not existed for years. They are long, low animals with narrow heads and wet, dark hides that reflect almost nothing. Their feet spread wide and silent. Along their jaws and forelimbs run rows of pressure-sensitive pits, able to read tremors through moss and shallow water. They hunt by listening to the ground’s future: the shift before the hoof, the intake before the cry, the tightening of roots around a fleeing body.

Their voices are high and metallic. They do not often use them. When they do, the sound makes reedbeds ring in sympathy and sends pebblebacks locking into stone-shapes for hours. A hunting pack may move without apparent coordination, separated by mist and distance, yet converge at the exact instant a herd reaches uncertain ground. They eat quickly. They cache remains in cold seep-holes, where acidic water preserves meat and stains the bones blue.

Older predators develop lichen along the spine and shoulders. This growth breaks up their outline and may also feed them in lean seasons, drawing minerals from rain and passing faint sugars into the blood. Very old ones become almost indistinguishable from wet stones until they stand.

There are also things that do not fit cleanly into plant or animal.

The echo-mollusks live in shallow pools and inside pockets of water under the moss. They have soft coiled bodies and shells patterned with miniature landscapes. They feed on vibration. A footstep, a cry, a thunder pulse from underground — they absorb it, digest part of it, and release the rest later. This is one reason echoes return wrongly on the plateau. A shout may come back changed because something ate it first. During mating season, the pools murmur with old sounds: wingbeats, animal calls, stonefalls, storms from years before.

The lapse-worms are seen mostly as tracks. They move through soft mats, leaving narrow raised seams that sometimes appear before the worm arrives. Their bodies are ribbonlike, translucent, filled with dark particles that drift against the direction of motion. They eat decayed time as much as decayed matter: places where events have doubled, reflections that do not match, afterimages of herds, temporary ridges left behind by storms. Where lapse-worms gather, the land becomes more reliable for a while. Their castings are prized by plants. Moss grows thick and orderly on them, though the order never lasts.

Certain lichens may be the closest things the plateau has to scholars.

They grow in recursive patches on stones, bones, antlers, and old exposed root. Their forms repeat the shape of the land in smaller and smaller versions. They map drainage, warmth, mineral veins, animal paths, storm scars, and possibly events that have not yet happened. Grazers avoid eating them. Predators step around them. After ground-storms, these lichens often expand rapidly, recording the temporary lakes and mist-ridges before they vanish. Later, when the pattern fades, some trace remains in the stone beneath, as if knowledge has become stain.

No one builds here because building is not a useful thought.

The inhabitants make shelters only by becoming part of the processes already moving. A burrow is a borrowed root-channel. A nest is a fold in moss. A nursery is a warm seep, a carcass, a pod shadow, a collapsed pressure chamber. Even the most complex lives do not assemble homes; they negotiate with temporary shape. They use what the plateau permits, and they leave before permission is withdrawn.

If there is a ruling creature, it is not the largest predator or the oldest moss.

It is the pulse shared among them.

The plateau lives by pressure: water pressing through stone, roots pressing through cracks, weather pressing downward, ancient rock pressing upward without rising, animals pressing tracks into mats that heal and remember. Every inhabitant has adapted to that pulse. They hear with their feet. They breathe through wet membranes. They trust neither distance nor reflection. They know that a pool may be shallow today, bottomless for ten minutes tomorrow, and a patch of humming moss by dusk. They move as if the world is not a surface but a conversation.

The old beasts pause before stepping because the ground might be listening.

The reedbeds lean because sound may arrive before cause.

The predators wait beside waterlines that are no longer there because hunger remembers older maps.

The moss shivers for miles because one deep motion beneath it belongs to all of them.

What lives on the plateau is an ecosystem that has learned not to distinguish sharply between body, weather, machine, and dream. It is made of cold flesh and mineral sap, fungal antlers and glass insects, valve-leaves and silver larvae, soft-footed herds and metal-voiced hunters. It has no city, no road, no master plan. But everywhere there are workings. Everywhere there are signals. Everywhere there is the sense of life continuing after geology has finished speaking.
