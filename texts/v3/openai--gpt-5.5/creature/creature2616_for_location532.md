What lives there is not one population, but a compact treaty.

The visible inhabitants are people, though they have become difficult to separate from the hollow that shelters them. They are small or made small by habit: shoulders folded, knees bent, heads inclined as if listening for permission from the roof. Even when they stand in an open chamber, they do not straighten fully. Their bodies have learned the city’s height. They move with the careful economy of animals that cannot afford a splash, a broken stem, a wasted ember.

Their skin tends toward the colors of the place—peat-brown, mushroom-pale, grey with cold, olive under lamplight. Many have hands darkened by tannin and clay up to the wrists. Their nails are kept short and sometimes sealed with resin. Their hair, if they wear it long, is braided flat against the skull or wrapped in waxed thread so it will not trail into water, flame, or larval beds. The very old often smell faintly of smoke, moss, fish oil, and medicinal bark; the children smell more sharply of mud and crushed cress.

They dress in layers because warmth is wealth. Inner wrappings of soft woven fiber. Outer mantles of felted moss, stitched hide, waxed leaves, and reed-cloth. Hoods hang low. Mouth-coverings are common, not only against cold, but against moths, spores, and the small night things that investigate breath. Their clothing is patched endlessly, each patch recording a season: pale winter reed, dark spring barkcloth, summer insect-silk, autumn fur.

Their eyes are not blind, but sight is only one sense among many, and not the most trusted. They read the world by touch. A fingertip on a wall knows whether the root behind it is swelling, drying, sickening, listening. A bare foot knows which mats are safe, which are nurseries, which are water wearing a skin. They hear through their palms, through bowls placed against channels, through teeth lightly set against tool handles. They can tell rain above from thaw below by the way a bridge tightens.

Their speech is soft and close. Much of it is not speech at all: taps on reed, knuckles against clay, breath through teeth, a hand laid on another person’s wrist. Children are corrected less often for disobedience than for loudness. A shouted child is taken aside and taught where sound goes when it leaves the mouth. They learn early that a voice can wake wings, loosen stone, frighten fish from a trap, call attention from beneath the central pool.

They do not consider themselves owners of the city. Ownership is too crude a word for a place that grows back, shifts, drinks, and remembers. They keep rights of use, duties of repair, permissions inherited from grandparents, apologies owed to roots. A sleeping alcove may belong to a family for three generations, then be surrendered when the wall buds. A bench may be public until fruiting season. A bridge may be everyone’s path except during larval migration, when it belongs to the pale insects.

Their leaders are not kings. Authority gathers around those who can interpret danger before it becomes visible.

There are root-keepers, who know which living beams may be trimmed, which must be fed, which are bearing too much weight, and which have begun to dislike being touched. They carry wooden wedges, soft mallets, packets of mineral paste, and knives used only in extremity.

There are drip-counters, patient people who sit in cold niches and listen to water. They know when a blocked seep will flood a nursery, when a new channel is forming behind a wall, when the great roof has drunk too much rain and will sweat for three days.

There are coal-minders, whose work is treated almost like priesthood. They bank the hearths, coax heat without flame, read smoke behavior, and decide when a room may cook, when it must only warm, and when all fire must be smothered before the roof takes offense.

There are eel-tenders, beetle-breeders, fungus-setters, stone-listeners, moth-watchers, children who are small enough to crawl into drainways, and old women and men who do little but remember which doors appeared after which rains.

Their food is pale, cold, and carefully earned. They eat peat tubers, cress, fungus caps, blind fish, eel when permitted, beetle oil, larvae roasted on hot stones, algae cakes, sour preserved roots, and thin broth enriched with marrow if hunting above has been good. Sweetness is rare and seasonal: fruit from a bench-vine, sap thickened in a warm cup, the honeyed bodies of certain grubs. Salt is treasure. Smoke is flavor but also cost.

They do farm, but their farming resembles persuasion more than command. They tuck spores into prepared wounds in deadwood. They comb algae from basins and leave enough to green again. They rotate rot heaps by warmth, smell, and insect sound. They move pollinating insects in reed cages from flower chamber to flower chamber, whispering as if the insects might be offended by rough handling. A good farmer there is not the one who makes the most grow, but the one who leaves the fewest angry consequences.

They share the city with animals in numbers too great for comfort and too necessary for hatred.

The white frogs are protected. No one kills them. They sit like wet stones in cups and gutter mouths, their throats moving silently. People say they carry messages through water tremor. Whether this is true or only useful superstition hardly matters; when the frogs abandon a channel, everyone notices.

The eels are fed and feared. A cistern with a healthy eel is a clean cistern. A cistern without one may breed biting threads, fever gnats, or worse soft-bodied things. Eel-tenders know individuals by scars, color, temper, and the way they take offerings from a stick.

The root-mice are tolerated but cursed. They steal insulation, chew sleeping mats, carry seeds into impossible places, and sometimes save lives by fleeing before a wall slump. Children trap them with great seriousness and then argue over whether a particular mouse is food, nuisance, omen, or neighbor.

The moths are treated like weather. In their season, mouths are covered, lamps are hooded, and no one shakes the upper vines. Their wings can fill a chamber with choking dust. Their larvae, however, produce a fine silk used for wound-binding and infant cloth.

There are spiders whose webs mark safe crossings, beetles whose oil burns cleanly, snails that must be scraped from signposts, limpets in cisterns, mites in every handle, worms in the floors, and blind fish that belong partly to whoever catches them and partly to the water that raised them.

And beneath this crowded smallness lives the older resident: the plant-mass itself.

It is not worshiped exactly. Worship would imply distance. It is lived with, negotiated with, feared as one fears a sleeping parent, a landlord, a weather system, a body one has been born inside. The people call it by many practical names depending on which part they mean: roof, mother-root, greenstone, the drinking wall, the old braid, the cold lung. No one name contains it.

It makes the city possible. It also slowly tries to close every room.

Its roots thicken around doorways. Its tendrils test unused tools. Its leaves redirect condensation. It opens hollows during long rain and seals them during dry cold. It can accept a clay wall into itself over years until no one can tell whether that wall was built or grown. Its pulse under the central knot is used for counting, storytelling, and certain oaths. Children fall asleep to it. Builders wait for it before setting supports. The dying sometimes ask to be carried near it, where the pressure rhythm can be felt through the back.

The people do not agree on whether it thinks.

Some say no: it is only growth, thirst, pressure, memory without mind.

Some say yes, but so slowly that a human lifetime is only a touch to it.

Some refuse the question. If a thing can shelter you, feed you, drown you, warm you through rot, close a passage behind your enemy, and open a door where there was none yesterday, then whether it “thinks” is less important than whether you have behaved properly toward it.

There is also something in the deepest pool.

The people do not describe it to strangers. Children are told only the rules: do not lean over the black middle, do not trail fingers, do not throw bright things, do not mock offerings, do not count the ripples after something surfaces. Adults speak of it indirectly. The Below-Keeper. The Old Mouth. The Listener Under Root. Sometimes only “there.”

It may be an eel grown impossibly large. It may be a remnant from before the basin lifted. It may be the animal part of the plant’s dream. It may be several things taking turns with one shape. Whatever it is, the city has made room for it in its laws. Spoiled tuber, fish gut, crushed beetles, and the first blind fish of certain thaws go into the pool. In return, or by coincidence carefully maintained, the lower waters remain livable.

The dead are not wasted. Nothing there can afford to be only grief.

Bodies may be wrapped in sedge and placed in warm rot chambers until bone and nutrient can be separated. Some bones become tools, beads, hinges, needles, children’s counting pieces. Some are set into walls near family alcoves, where condensation runs over them and moss eventually softens their edges. Ash is rare because flame is rare. Burial in earth is impossible; the earth is already occupied. To be given to root, water, beetle, and fungus is not shame. It is citizenship continuing in other departments.

Their celebrations are quiet but intense. A first ember kept alive through winter. The safe reopening of a flooded lane. The hatching of pollinator insects. The harvest of a fruiting bench. A child’s first successful crossing of the reed causeway without waking the frogs. The return of a root-keeper from an unexplained chamber after long rain. They mark these with warmed oil, painted bark, low songs hummed into bowls so the sound trembles in the hands more than in the air.

They are not a sad people, though the place would make outsiders think so. They laugh softly. They decorate what can be decorated: tool handles etched with touch-marks, clay cups impressed with fern ribs, children’s hoods sewn with beetle shells, bridge knots tied in family patterns. They tell jokes about rot, eels, bad mushrooms, and the arrogance of drylanders who trust floors. They flirt by sharing warmth, by offering dry gloves, by repairing a leaking roof-screen without being asked.

Their virtues are patience, thrift, attentiveness, and restraint. Their vices follow from the same roots: secrecy, suspicion, hoarding, refusal to abandon a failing chamber until the last possible moment. They do not welcome strangers easily. A stranger is heat loss, noise, unknown illness, broken rules, a foot through a nursery mat. But neither are they cruel without reason. Anyone who enters cold, soaked, and frightened may be wrapped, warmed, examined for leeches, and told the rules before being asked their name.

They measure wealth in strange ways: dry tinder, clean cord, uncracked clay, permission to cut a root, a breeding pair of oil beetles, a map of winter passages, a grandmother who remembers the last great flood, a child quiet enough to hear stone shift.

What lives there, finally, is a society that has accepted being outnumbered.

Outnumbered by roots. Outnumbered by insects. Outnumbered by drops of water. Outnumbered by spores, larvae, fungi, fish, unseen wings, and the slow intentions of the green roof. Its people survive not by clearing space once and calling it theirs, but by asking for space every day in a hundred small acts.

They are the keepers of openings.

They keep doorways from sealing, drains from choking, coals from dying, pools from fouling, paths from forgetting feet. They live inside a breathing wall of the world, and they have made a civilization out of not waking it too suddenly.
