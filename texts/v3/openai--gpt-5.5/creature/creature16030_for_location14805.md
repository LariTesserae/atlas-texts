What lives there is not a civilization, though from a distance it can seem organized.

The inhabitants are the pale inheritors of an architecture they did not make and cannot understand as architecture. They know it only as contour, shelter, pressure, mineral taste, current-shadow, breeding hollow, hunting slot. To them a doorway is a throat of faster water. A stair is a ribbed chute where food catches. A ceremonial basin is a reliable gyre. A tower is a vertical migration route, a cold chimney, a place where larvae rise and predators wait.

They are creatures of aftermath, but not mournful ones. They have no memory of before.

Most of them are small.

Under nearly every ledge, in the lee of every mineral fin, colonies of white sieve-crustaceans cling in dense overlapping sheets. From afar they look like rime or salt accretion; close up they are restless, each body no larger than a finger joint, folded into translucent armor, with many delicate fans unfolding and folding in the current. They do not travel much once grown. They choose a ceiling, a crack, the underside of an old beam, and become a trembling weather instrument. When the water thickens with food, the whole colony opens at once, thousands of pale combs catching invisible matter. When pressure shifts, they close so quickly the ledge seems to blink.

Their young are not fixed. They are glassy specks with black pin-eyes and too many legs, swept through halls and shafts for weeks or months before settling. Many are eaten. Some find the right roughness, the right chemistry, the right slow back-current behind a broken cornice. There they attach, harden, whiten, and join the living frost.

Among them move thread-eels, thin as cords, almost entirely transparent. They inhabit the cracks between fitted plates and the old seams of mortar where no larger thing can follow. Their organs hang inside them like a string of dark beads: heart, gut, reproductive sacs, pressure bladders, all visible through the skin when they pass through clear water. They are scavengers, thieves, egg-eaters, sometimes cleaners. They slip their narrow heads into the cups of sessile animals and steal trapped particles. They strip the soft undersides of molting crustaceans. They taste the water constantly with whiskering filaments around their mouths.

They know the ruin as a lacework of passages, not as open space. A collapsed wall may be a catastrophe to a large swimmer, but to them it is a new province.

Across the wider floors creep the flat scavengers: low, oval animals with flexible armored backs and fringed rims that lift and settle like skirts. They are the color of old porcelain stained with blue. Their many feet are hidden beneath them, moving in soft coordinated waves. They browse the sediment spirals in plazas, feeding on dead larvae, mineral films, fallen tissue, bacterial skins, powdered bone-stone, anything softened by time. When a pressure wave passes, they press themselves flat and seal their rims to the floor. A storm can roll loose slabs across a court and leave these creatures unharmed beneath a skin of living cartilage.

Their mouths are underneath. They leave clean pale arcs behind them where they have rasped.

Some carry small ecosystems on their backs: hitchhiking crusts, egg strings, blind worms, tiny cup-animals that open only when the scavenger moves into better current. Older individuals become walking islands. Their shells are etched with paths made by smaller mouths. Their edges are chipped. Some have outlived entire chambers: hatched in a hall that still had a roof, grown in its collapse, matured in the basin it became.

In the deep bowls gather the bell-colonies.

They are not quite jellyfish and not quite coral. Soft-bodied, translucent, joined by long veined cables, they hang in slow curtains from broken arches or pulse in clusters above silted amphitheaters. Each bell produces a cold blue light, faint and delayed, as if remembering lightning seen through ice. They do not illuminate so much as disclose: the edge of a column, the belly of a passing fish, the fact of depth beneath them.

Their light is communication, lure, warning, waste heat, and accident. Sometimes a whole colony answers a pressure tremor, and a submerged hall blooms blue for several seconds before going dark again. Animals use these pulses. Predators time their strikes by them. Larvae drift toward them. Scavengers gather below them, waiting for what the bells fail to digest.

The bells feed on what the currents bring: microscopic flesh, spores of bacteria, powdered organic remnants, the dead young of larger animals. Their tentacles are too fine to see unless silt catches on them. A creature swimming through an invisible curtain may emerge slowed, numbed, lightly stitched with venom.

There are fish too, if fish is a sufficient word.

The black angular schools are all edge and reflex. Their bodies are flat-sided, metallic, and nearly without visible eyes. They move as one shattered thought, turning in perfect unison through colonnades and over roofless courts. They feed in bursts, cutting through swarms of smaller animals with narrow shearing jaws. One moment a basin is empty; the next it is filled with black facets; the next they are gone, leaving only disturbed silt and a few spinning fragments of translucent bodies.

They navigate by pressure changes along their flanks. A wall speaks to them before they touch it. A predator’s opening mouth is a shape in the water. A collapsing slab is a sudden sentence shouted through their bones.

The pale many-limbed swimmers are slower and stranger.

They resemble drifting hands, or torn parasols, or old ceremonial garments given animal patience. Their central bodies are soft and bulbous, protected by partial internal plates. From them trail numerous limbs, some used for swimming, some for tasting, some for holding, some for delicate abrasion. They move along walls and inscriptions with a careful, almost scholarly attention, placing their mouths against worn markings, calcified seams, mineral films. They are not reading. They are grazing, sampling, remembering routes chemically. But their behavior gives the impression of reverence.

They visit the same surfaces generation after generation. Along certain walls, their feeding has polished bands at a consistent height, as though the dead builders made friezes for them. Their eggs are laid in recesses where currents neither stagnate nor scour too hard: old alcoves, socket-holes, the mouths of conduits, the hollows behind broken reliefs. The eggs are clear capsules fastened in strings, each with a curled silver embryo inside, each embryo turning slowly in response to pressure.

The largest inhabitants are seldom seen fully.

There are mantled beasts with hides like stone soaked in milk. They travel the lower terraces and the bases of towers, grazing on thick bacterial and mineral films. Their bodies are broad, heavy, and soft beneath a crust of embedded grit. Over decades, they accumulate fragments of the place: ceramic chips, shell cups, glass flakes, carbonate knobs. An old one can be mistaken for fallen masonry until it lifts one edge and exhales a cloud of pale sediment.

They have no eyes worth naming. Their heads are blunt, downward-facing, equipped with muscular lips and rasping plates. They move by slow contractions, gripping and releasing the floor. Their passage polishes ridges. Their feeding widens cracks. They are agents of erosion as much as animals, patient mouths through which the old construction passes grain by grain.

Smaller creatures follow them in trains. Worms feed in their wake. Crustaceans pick at parasites beneath their mantles. Black fish dart around their stirred sediment. Sometimes torn-cloth predators settle on their backs and wait for attendants to come close.

Those predators are among the few things that make the place feel hostile.

They live in vertical shafts, under broken spans, and inside the mouths of drains wide enough to hold darkness. At rest they are almost two-dimensional, spread against stone or hanging in folds from a ceiling, their bodies ragged and matte, colored like shadowed slate. Their edges are lined with sensory hairs and retractable hooks. They wait where currents compress passing life into predictable lanes.

When they strike, they do not chase. They unfold.

A sheet of body becomes a net, a cloak, a closing wound in the water. Small fish vanish inside them. Thread-eels are pinned against walls. Even the pale many-limbed swimmers avoid certain shafts where the pressure tastes of old venom. After feeding, the predators contract into dense knots and remain motionless for days, digesting, looking again like torn banners left by a people who never return.

Lower still, in the cold courts where light is only rumor, live the heavy shelled things.

They may be the oldest animals there.

Their shells are domed, ridged, and asymmetrical, grown around the shape of whatever shelter they first chose. Some are fused to old steps. Some occupy the corners of basins. Some drag themselves, very slowly, on thick jointed limbs, carrying encrusted towers of shell, mineral, and inherited debris. Their heads emerge rarely: pale, blunt, beaked, whiskered with sensory threads. They feed on carrion, on bacterial mats, on the soft bodies of sessile colonies when storms tear them loose.

Their lives are measured in collapses. A young one may settle when a wall is whole and reach maturity after the wall has become gravel. Their shells contain layers of chemistry recording centuries of water: cold years, silt years, years of mass spawning, years when some distant structure failed and filled the currents with powdered ash-glass. If anything in that drowned highland remembers, it is not by thought but by accretion.

There are also lives too small to see except by their effects.

Bacterial veils thicken in sheltered hollows, dark and smoky, feeding on minerals, dissolved organics, old chemical gradients left in the made materials. They are the closest thing the place has to a pasture. Blind grazers rasp them. Bell-colonies trap them. Sieve-crustaceans strain their fragments from the water. When disturbed, the veils lift in black-brown sheets and vanish like breath.

Inside the ancient mortar are boring worms with mineral teeth. In glassy seams are colonies of nearly invisible cells that make the surface slick and dangerous. In the hollows of metal ribs, if any true metal remains, are red-black crusts that feed on oxidation and release faint warmth, attracting larvae in winter-cold upwellings. The whole ruin is being digested at many scales.

Nothing there farms, but many things cultivate by accident.

A cup-animal’s shell changes the current enough for silt to settle. Silt lets bacterial films thicken. Films draw grazers. Grazers attract predators. Predator scraps feed scavengers. Scavengers clean a surface where new larvae attach. Over time a bare wall becomes a feeding station, then a colony, then a brittle reef of animals fixed to an object made by vanished intention.

The inhabitants do not divide the place into ruin and nature. That distinction is absent from their bodies.

Their senses are fitted to a world without air, horizon, or green.

They feel distance as pressure. They taste direction. They know the difference between water that has passed through a narrow ceramic channel and water that has rolled over an open plaza. They can detect fresh collapse by the mineral sharpness of newly exposed material. They know old stable surfaces by their muted chemistry, their worn bacterial signatures, their familiar eddies. Some follow magnetic traces trapped in the construction. Some return to breeding sites by currents so specific that if a wall falls, a migration line vanishes.

Light matters, but not as it would to surface creatures. The dim pewter glow from above is a seasonal rumor, a vertical suggestion. Many animals rise when it strengthens, not toward air but toward suspended food stirred by upper turbulence. Others hide then, because silhouettes become possible. Bioluminescence is more intimate: a pulse to startle, a lure in a shaft, a mating shimmer beneath an overhang, a brief blue confession of scale.

Their colors are mostly absences: white, clear, gray, black, faint blue, bruise-violet in the organs, dull silver in schooling bodies. Brightness would be wasteful here. Pigment has withdrawn into function.

Their breeding follows storms.

When pressure fronts move through the drowned plateau and the water fills with mineral snow, many species release eggs, milt, larvae, spores, fragments. The turbulence that threatens adults distributes the young. A quiet basin may become cloudy with life for an hour: transparent embryos, coiled thread-eels, glittering fish fry, bell-polyps, drifting packets of bacterial gel. Then the currents divide them. Some are crushed into filters. Some are carried into dead chambers and starve. Some pass through old gates, over terraces, into cracks that will be their whole world.

Collapse is also a season.

A failing bridge, a wall giving way, a floor opening into blackness—these events send pressure through the region and draw life from every side. Scavengers arrive first to feed on stunned animals and exposed films. Sieve colonies benefit from the burst of suspended matter. Predators take positions along the new current. Larvae settle on fresh fracture surfaces. What was disaster becomes habitat before the silt has cleared.

The most successful creatures are not the strongest but the ones that accept impermanence as geography.

There are clingers for the stable seams.

There are drifters for the uncertain corridors.

There are burrowers for the softening mortar.

There are grazers for the dark films.

There are hunters for the shafts.

There are immense slow mouths for the lower courts, converting architecture into sediment.

And everywhere there are opportunists: tiny, jointed, translucent things that bloom in abundance whenever water brings food and vanish when it does not.

If there is a dominant life there, it is not a single animal. It is the current-shaped community itself: the frost colonies under ledges, the blind eels in seams, the bell-light in basins, the flat scavengers, the black schools, the mantled grazers, the torn-cloth ambushers, the ancient shelled crawlers, the bacterial darkening of sheltered walls. Together they form a living occupation of a place built for other purposes.

They do not restore it. They do not ruin it. They translate it.

A plaza becomes a feeding gyre.

A shrine becomes a nursery.

A drain becomes a hunting throat.

A tower becomes a chimney.

A wall becomes pasture.

A collapse becomes birth-ground.

They are quiet because the water is quiet, pale because the light is poor, patient because change is slow until it is sudden. They live without speech, without fire, without leaves, without any upward dream of air. Their world is pressure and mineral, hunger and drift, shelter and abrasion.

They are what remains possible after purpose dissolves: not ghosts of the builders, but descendants of water, cold, chemistry, and time.
