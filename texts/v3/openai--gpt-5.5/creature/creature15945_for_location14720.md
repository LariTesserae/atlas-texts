What lives there is not a people, not in the way a road implies feet or a doorway implies hands.

It is a city whose citizens are functions.

The oldest inhabitants are the roots: pale, brown, black, amber, hair-fine, wrist-thick, blind and exact. They live underneath everything and remember what the upper growth cannot. They know the stone by taste. They know where the mountain sweats iron, where the seep-water carries lime, where buried leaves have gone sour, where warmth collects after a thaw. They are the city’s memory and its masonry. They grip, braid, descend, split, and rejoin. Some have become streets by refusing to grow upward. Some have become walls by growing too closely together. Some have become the ribs of chambers, the lips of wells, the hidden pipes through which cold water is persuaded to move.

They do not think quickly. Their intelligence is pressure, hunger, avoidance, return. But they have made decisions for so long that the decisions have become architecture.

Above them live the mosses, innumerable and soft, the city’s skin. They are not one kind but many: black-green mats that drink from the air, silver cushions that catch frost, red-brown velvet in the dimmest cracks, tiny starry colonies along the rims of pools. They fill every pause in the larger growth. They soften corners, seal wounds, hide seams, keep warmth from escaping the composting cores. If the roots are memory, the mosses are attention. They notice every change first: a new draft, a drip diverted, the salt of some animal’s passing, a hand-sized patch of light where a leaf has torn away. Slowly, grain by grain, they respond.

The walls are alive with shrubs that have forgotten the open world. They do not spread loosely, as shrubs do on hillsides. Here they grow pressed shoulder to shoulder, forced upright by neighbors, their trunks flattened on one side, their branches woven into latticework. They have become civic plants: hedge-walls, arch-plants, stair-plants, rail-plants, threshold-plants. Their bark is dark and wet, split by seams where separate stems are learning to fuse. In some places two or three species have grown through one another so thoroughly that no single plant owns the structure. A branch of one becomes the brace of another; a root of another feeds a flowering vine that shades them both.

There are vines too, though they are cautious in the cold. They are the city’s reach. They test openings, hang down into lanes, bridge gaps, climb toward vapor, descend toward warmth. Some are thin as thread and tremble with droplets. Some are woody cables, slow and stubborn, tightening year by year around living pillars. Some bear small pale flowers that open only in the hour when the cloud thins and the whole basin brightens from black-green to green-black. Their blossoms do not advertise themselves with color. Color would be wasteful here. Instead they smell faintly of thawed bark, mineral water, and bruised mushroom, calling to the few insects that can endure the place.

The fungi are perhaps closest to being the city’s mind.

They live below the streets, inside rotten chambers, under root bridges, in the seams between old leaves. Most are unseen except when they fruit: a shelf of white ears on a trunk, a cluster of gray cups holding meltwater, a line of beadlike caps along a wall where warmth leaks through. But beneath the visible city they run everywhere in threads finer than hair. They enter roots. They lace the compost beds. They open dead wood and return it as heat. They carry nourishment from a drowned quarter to a flowering crown, from a fallen branch to a new buttress, from an overfed cistern edge to a starving wall in shadow.

They are brokers, undertakers, messengers. They do not command the plant-city, but without them nothing would know enough of anything else. The roots taste locally. The leaves turn locally. The mosses remember the surface. The fungi connect these small knowings into something broader: not a thought, exactly, but a weather of intention.

In the warm seams live the small animals.

They are not citizens in the same way. They are tenants, tolerated because they fit into what is already happening.

The pale insects move as if time is thick. Some are wingless and translucent, with bodies like splinters of wax. Some cling beneath leaves and drink the thin sugars gathered there. Some burrow through moss, cleaning rot from places too small for water to scour. Their eggs overwinter in capsules no larger than grit, glued beneath bark lips where frost cannot quite reach. When they hatch, they do not swarm. Nothing here has the luxury of swarming. They appear singly, slowly, like thoughts that almost do not form.

The amphibious things under the rootwalks are older in manner, if not in age. They have narrow heads, black bead eyes, and skin so clear that the pulse within them can sometimes be seen when they press against a membrane of root or ice. They live in the passing warmth of decay. Their toes are delicate, almost rootlike themselves. They hunt the pale insects, sip films of algae from stones, vanish into slits where water sounds louder than it should. In deep freezes they become nearly still, folded into mud pockets beside the compost cores. When thaw returns, they loosen and resume their soft, secret traffic.

The dark birds are visitors from the outer weather. They are sharp, soot-colored, spare. They come to the upper crowns when the mist lifts, not because the city welcomes them, but because the crowns collect what birds need: seeds hardened by cold, insects numbed on bark, droplets caught in cupped leaves. Their feet prick the living towers. Their beaks tap frost from buds. They click to one another in low, dry notes, never wasting song on a place that swallows sound. Sometimes one dies in the canopy. The city receives it without ceremony. Feathers mat into moss. Bones settle into root-felt. By spring, a vine flowers more strongly there.

But the true resident, the one that gives the place its occupied feeling, is larger than any species and smaller than a mind.

It is the collective life of the city itself.

It lives in the repeated agreements between forms: leaf to water, root to stone, fungus to rot, moss to frost, vine to height, hollow to air. It has no face, but it has centers of awareness. The black pool in the sunken plaza is one. The composting towers are others. The cistern blooms, the fungal beds, the root-knotted crossings where many lanes meet — these are organs, not monuments.

The central basin is its deep mouth and its listening ear. All water speaks there eventually. Drip-water, fog-water, meltwater, seep-water stained by bark, mineral water risen from the young stone: each arrives with information. The pool darkens with it. Beneath the surface, root fibers trail like nerves in a cold broth. They taste acidity, thaw, ash, pollen, the faint animal salts washed from a bird’s foot, the crushed bitterness of a damaged leaf upstream. Nothing is announced. There are no signals that flash, no orders that travel. Yet after the pool receives a season’s worth of such messages, the city alters. A channel narrows. A wall thickens. A canopy opens by a hand’s breadth. A root abandons one seam and seeks another.

Its senses are not human senses arranged differently. They are other senses entirely.

It feels pressure as weather. It feels light as food and risk. It feels open air as both wound and possibility. It feels warmth as a debt that must be stored quickly. It feels stone not as obstacle but as slow animal bone, something to clasp and enter and split. It feels time in wetness, in rot-depth, in the number of freezes a branch has survived.

Its children are not born. They accumulate.

A loose mat of moss becomes a threshold. A threshold becomes a lane because dripping keeps it open. A lane becomes important because creatures pass through it and their faint warmth delays frost. Nearby roots thicken to hold the passage. Leaves avoid the draft. A chamber appears. Then another. The city does not plan this, but it repeats what works until intention is indistinguishable from habit.

If it has desires, they are simple and immense.

It wants to hold.

It wants to drink without drowning, freeze without dying, climb without tearing itself from the basin. It wants every bare stone covered, every leak understood, every pocket of rot turned to heat. It wants height for cloud-water and depth for mineral water. It wants shade where growth would otherwise run wild; it wants openings where air must move. It wants the central pool unfrozen. It wants no emptiness that does not serve a purpose.

It is young, and that youth shows in its impatience. It overgrows its own lanes. It sends sharp shoots into passages and then kills them back when traffic of water and cold proves them foolish. It builds too densely around cisterns and must split itself open after rain. It fuses trunks before their inner bark has settled, leaving swollen scars and wet seams. It is still learning the mountain. It has not yet acquired the grace of an ancient forest or the stability of an old bog. It is experimental, crowded, almost feverish despite the cold.

Still, it has already learned defense.

Not defense by thorns alone, though there are thorns in the narrowest places, pale and hooked under leaf-shadow. Its real defenses are tightness, wetness, cold, and digestion. A large animal entering the outer lanes would find no room to turn. Its heat would wake moss-curtains and soften closed membranes; its weight would sink into root-mat; its scent would travel downward into fungal beds. Broken branches would callus. Crushed moss would regrow. Droppings, hair, blood, anything left behind would be quietly taken apart. The city does not attack. It incorporates.

It has no need to hurry. Almost nothing here does.

Even its motion is mostly invisible: a leaf pivoting during the length of an afternoon, a root thickening through a winter, a doorway narrowing over three days of frost, a bridge repairing a crack by laying down pale new wood along the wound. Yet if one could leave and return after a month, the place would be different. A lane would have shifted. A balcony would have sagged and become a roof. A cistern flower would have opened, filled, collapsed, and become a nursery for moss. The central pool would show a new ring of roots just below the surface, like fingers reaching inward.

So what lives there is a society without speech, a body without a single skin, a builder without tools.

It is roots acting as memory, moss acting as skin, fungi acting as exchange, water acting as blood, leaves acting as hunger, frost acting as law. The insects, birds, and transparent amphibians live in its margins and folds, but the city itself is the main inhabitant.

It lives there by becoming there.

And each year, just below the unmelted snow, in the notch of young stone, it becomes more difficult to say whether the plants occupy the city or the city is the plant.
