What lives there is not a population so much as an arrangement of appetites that has learned manners.

The human inhabitants are easiest to mistake for caretakers, as if the town were a greenhouse, a factory, or an animal kept barely tame. They are not caretakers in that simple sense. They are part of the maintenance. They move through the cavern with the particular competence of creatures who know that a wall may be drowsy, a railing may be listening, and a quiet alley may only be quiet because something has just fed.

They are mostly small-framed, though not frail. Generations under low ceilings have taught them to carry themselves with a forward tilt, shoulders narrow, heads slightly bowed, as if always passing under branches. Their hands are strong and scarred in fine pale lines from thorn, wire, shell, and tool-slip. Many are missing the tips of fingers. Many have calluses along the backs of their knuckles from bracing themselves on damp bark and iron. Their eyes are large in the dimness, not unnaturally so, but watchful, reflective, often seeming green or amber because the town’s light has nowhere else to go.

Their skin has the colorlessness of things grown without sun, but it is never clean in the way surface people mean clean. It is stained by tannin, pollen, machine grease, rust, fungal dye, sap burns, copper dust. Children have moss-dark knees and orange spores in their hair. Workers come home striped by the day’s labor: black where they have leaned against belts, white where latex has dried on their sleeves, blue where bacterial pans have splashed them. Even the elders, who sit more than they climb, have green under their nails.

They dress for dampness, biting things, and sudden work. Aprons of treated fungal leather. Sleeves tied close at the wrist. Veils that can be dropped over the face when a hatch begins or a bloom releases irritant dust. Copper ear-caps not only to soften the machinery’s thunder, but to hear through it: some are shaped to catch changes in pump rhythm, some to amplify the tick of beetles in the walls. Boots are wide and soft-soled for moss streets, with little hooked toes for root bridges. Infants are carried high against the chest in waterproof slings, their ears stuffed with lamb’s-moss during factory acceleration.

They do not hurry unless the whole town is hurrying. Their caution is not fearfulness. It is courtesy extended to everything that might injure them.

A passerby touches two fingers to a doorframe before entering, feeling whether sap is rising behind the wall. A machinist pauses before stepping over a black hose, because sometimes hoses are snakes and sometimes snakes are hoses performing a municipal duty. Children are taught to look upward before looking both ways. Lovers part under lantern fruit by exchanging small objects that will rot if neglected: a graft, a warm egg, a cutting from a private vine. No one gives dead flowers.

Their speech is soft, economical, and full of borrowed sounds. Words click, knock, whistle, and hum, adapted to pass through factory noise or carry along pipes. They can hold conversations at three distances at once: fingers for the person beside them, spoken phrases for the room, coded taps for someone two streets away. In the market, bargaining is done with voice and scent both. A seller may dust a crate with yellow spores meaning fresh, blue meaning bitter, gray meaning do not feed to pregnant animals. Children learn colors as instructions before they learn them as beauty.

They are not numerous. The cavern could hold more of them, perhaps, but the town itself would not permit it. There is only so much breathing room, so much safe food, so much tolerance in the rootworks. Families are wide rather than large: parents, aunts, unrelated apprentices, old machine-uncles, widowed fungus-brewers, children fostered after a house-flowering or a culvert accident. Kinship follows obligation more than blood. Whoever teaches you which valves cough before bursting is family. Whoever knows the tone of your door when it is sick is family. Whoever will come cut you loose when a curtain vine mistakes you for trellis is family.

Their occupations are half trades, half negotiations.

There are rootwrights, who train the load-bearing growths. They are patient people with quiet voices and brutal knives. They bind saplings to frames, notch roots to bend them, whisper or sing to white feeder strands before cutting, and spend years convincing a wall to thicken where a family will need another room. They know the temperaments of species the way surface builders know brick and beam. Some roots sulk if cut in heat. Some must be bruised before they will branch. Some grow best near argument and are therefore planted outside council chambers.

There are pump-tenders, deaf in one ear more often than not, who can diagnose a failing pressure line by placing their teeth against a pipe. They carry wrenches, voltage rods, and little packets of ground shell to sweeten acidic leaks. Their arms are latticed with burn scars from steam and caustic sap. They speak of machines as if they are livestock: stubborn, hungry, lame, breeding trouble in the dark.

There are spore-readers who monitor bloom, blight, and contagion. They wear layered veils and keep beetles in lacquer boxes, because some beetles change color in bad air before any gauge responds. They are treated with wary respect. They can order a street closed, a house scraped, a marriage postponed, a corpse burned instead of composted. Nobody likes them until they are needed, and then everyone obeys them.

There are bell-children, usually between seven and twelve, quick enough to run the root bridges and light enough not to wake certain mats. They carry warning bells of different pitches. One bell means waste doors. One means warm-drip. One means dogs loose. One means mimic-call: ignore voices from drains. Their work is dangerous in the way all childhood there is dangerous, but it gives them a map of the town no adult quite retains.

There are moth-sweepers, salamander-guides, fungal bakers, salt-keepers, wire-grafters, nursery guards, compost judges, lantern-fruit pruners, bird-listeners, and those who do nothing but keep the old signs visible enough that no one forgets where the buried shutoff valves are.

Their food is grown, trapped, cultured, skimmed, and bargained from the living machinery around them. They eat pale tubers with peppery skins, steamed fern hearts, fungal loaves with dark chewy crusts, broth thickened with root starch, eggs stolen back from half-domesticated birds, blind fish from sump cages, sweet latex curds, sour preserves sealed in insect wax. Meat is common but seldom celebrated; too many things are meat. A feast is not a roasted animal but a clean crop of lantern fruit, a vat of safe ferment, a spice mold blooming on schedule.

Their homes are intimate and damp. Beds hang from walls rather than rest on floors, because floors belong at least partly to insects, condensation, and the small nightly traffic of useful scavengers. Cupboards are sealed with waxed leaf. Lamps are grown into alcoves. Family records are not kept on paper, which mildews, but on strips of hammered copper etched with names, graft lines, debts, hatch years, pump failures, marriages, quarantines. These strips hang in doorways and chime faintly when someone enters, so the dead are always being disturbed and always answering.

They have old people, but very few idle ones. Elders become listeners. They sit in plazas, pump galleries, nurseries, and courts, attending to rhythms others are too busy to notice. An elder may hear that the blind birds have shifted roosts and know a ceiling pocket is swelling with gas. Another may notice that frogs have gone silent in the west drains and send for hunters before centipedes boil up through the grates. Their authority comes not from memory alone, but from pattern-recognition sharpened over decades of not dying.

They have children, and the children are half-wild in the way of all children raised among nonhuman rules. They play at being vines, machines, predators, inspectors. They dare each other to touch sleeping pods, to stand beneath the upside-down tree until three drops strike the same shoulder, to call into forbidden culverts and run before anything answers. They keep beetles on strings, train small frogs to leap through finger hoops, and build miniature towns out of discarded seed husks, complete with tiny laws and punishments. They are taught early how to be still. Not obedient—still. There is a difference. Obedience is for adults and ordinances. Stillness is for survival.

Their dead are difficult. Burial is almost impossible; the town eats what is given to it. Cremation wastes fuel and releases things into the air. Composting is honorable but not simple, because not every body is safe to return, and not every family can bear watching a loved one become municipal fertility. So there are rituals of division. Hair to the bird nests. Teeth to the pump foundations. Bones ground for nursery walls if the spore-readers approve. Names etched in copper. A small portion of ash or mineral sealed into the roots of a household threshold, so the dead continue to be stepped over, touched, asked permission.

Their religion, if it is religion, has very few gods and many obligations. They do not worship the town exactly. Worship implies distance. They bargain with it, thank it, resent it, feed it, prune it, cut it back, apologize to it, and occasionally poison parts of it for the common good. They believe in balance because imbalance is audible. They believe in debt because every surface bears the evidence of use. They believe the surface exists, but with the vagueness of myth or disease. Some imagine it as a place of fatal brightness, where water falls all at once and stone lies naked. Others think of it as a childhood story meant to frighten people away from service tunnels.

But the humans are only one kind of citizen.

The plants live there with a slow, possessive intelligence—not a mind like a human mind, but a distributed insistence. They remember pressure, light, injury, tone, mineral taste. A doorway grows wider for a family that passes gently and narrows against one that hacks at it. Witness-vines in the court-garden tremble at certain voices because generations of dispute have trained them to respond to pitch, heat, breath, perhaps guilt, perhaps only moisture. Some houses are docile. Some are ambitious. Some must be pruned hard each year or they will seal their own occupants inside in a green act of mistaken protection.

The engine-pods are among the strangest residents. They hang in their frames like enormous sleeping hearts or skinned fruit, ribbed and muscular, fed by pipes and roots together. No one agrees whether they are plant, animal, or invention. They contract when warmed, when electrically stroked, when sung to in the low work chants of the press crews. Each has a name, though the names are not spoken in public. A good pod may serve for sixty years. A bad one convulses, snaps chains, bruises workers, or refuses to pull during condensation peak. Retired pods are lowered into compost pits with ceremony and great caution; even dying, they may tighten once more.

The blind birds are indispensable and hated. They hang from roof-vines in quarrelsome clusters, all beaks, claws, and membranous ears. Their feathers are sparse and powdery. Their eyes are sealed under skin or reduced to wet pearls. They shit on tools, steal insulation fibers, scream at nothing, and bite sleeping children’s toes if allowed near nurseries. Yet they hear pressure changes, gas shifts, distant wall-cracks, the minute prelude to pump failure. When the birds fall silent, every human stops. When they burst upward all at once, people reach for masks, hooks, children, shutoff cords.

The pale dogs are older than anyone’s records. No one knows whether they came down with the first settlers or crawled in later from some other lightless place. They have long bodies, too many visible joints, soft cartilage ears, and skin that shows the blue branching of veins beneath short white fur. They hunt frogs, rats, mimics when young, and anything wounded enough to smell simple. They also guard doorways, warm children, warn of centipedes, and lead drunk workers home from the pressing house. They are not owned. A household may be chosen by one, which is considered good fortune and an extra mouth in equal measure.

The root-pigs are municipal servants of the lowest and most necessary kind. Broad-snouted, low-eyed, bristled with fungal growth, they turn refuse heaps with patient violence. They can digest peelings, rotten leather, small bones, beetle shell, and some grades of insulation. Their tusks grow in flattened shovels. They are affectionate only when fevered, dangerous only when interrupted, and sacred only to compost judges, who know the town would choke in a month without them.

The antlered salamanders belong to the seep-stream and the hours when it appears. They are slow, solemn, and slick, with pale branching antlers that are not bone but sensory coral, feathered to read current, temperature, mineral change. Gardens grow on their backs: algae, fernlets, tiny red mites, sometimes lost jewelry caught in the mat. Children are forbidden to ride them, which means children ride them whenever possible. The salamanders tolerate this until they do not.

The arboreal thieves in the rafters are almost people when glimpsed badly. Dark hands. Quick faces. Ringed tails. Eyes with opinions. They travel in family gangs and have a passion for copper, eggs, bright buttons, and the little keys used on pressure cages. They can open simple latches, imitate coughs, and remember insults. Some workers feed them intentionally to keep them away from wiring. Others set spring traps. Both methods fail about equally. When one dies, its relatives gather in the vines above the body and make a sound like soft machinery grieving.

The insects are beyond counting and therefore beyond law. Fist-sized beetles under leaves. Glass-winged gnats in bacterial light. Moths in breathing curtains. White termites in warm beams. Singing crickets in pipes. Needleflies around exposed skin. Lantern grubs in the market rafters. Soft-bodied things that live only inside flowers bred for filtration. Armored things that wedge themselves into valves and cause civic emergencies. Some are pests, some are tools, some are omens, and many change category depending on season.

Then there are the mimics.

People do not describe them fully, partly because seeing one clearly usually means someone has made an error. They nest beyond the west culvert where old service tunnels sag into root and warm mud. They have learned voices from drains, lullabies from nursery walls, bargaining calls from markets, the little pain-sounds people make when they think they are alone. They call in the voices of lost children, dead spouses, supervisors, dogs, bells. They are not evil. That is said often, perhaps too often. They are hungry, clever, and acoustically gifted. Red fungus marks appear near their breeding places, and no one follows a familiar voice in the wrong direction unless grief has made them foolish.

The machines live there too, not metaphorically enough to dismiss. They have habits, dependencies, moods. Their metal bones are old, but their sinews are replaced constantly: vine belts, leaf-fiber packing, root gaskets, fungal grease. A flywheel that has turned for a century has a civic presence. A boiler that has killed three tenders is treated with the tense respect given to a violent elder. Gauges are polished like icons. Broken pistons are not discarded at once; they are leaned in courtyards, grown over, consulted for spare memory. Some machines start only after being fed chopped leaves and bone meal because roots have invaded the intake and learned to expect tribute. Some hum differently when storms pass far above, though no one in the cavern has seen lightning.

And through all of them—human, animal, plant, engine—move smaller lives of exchange.

Spores riding breath. Bacteria turning waste into glow. Fungi threading through walls, food, leather, lungs, law. Algae greening the backs of salamanders and the inner lips of water bowls. Parasites that know the calendar better than clerks. Seeds that germinate in boot seams. Mites that clean the ears of blind birds. Moss that records footsteps as temporary bruises. Every resident carries other residents. No body is singular.

What lives there, finally, is a community that has stopped believing in separation.

The humans do not stand apart from nature, nor are they swallowed by it. They live in an old compromise, revised daily with knives, songs, valves, bells, and the careful placement of salt. They are citizens of a town where citizenship is extended unequally but unmistakably to roots, dogs, pumps, birds, pods, fungi, and the dangerous dark beyond the culverts. To live there is to be useful, edible, watchful, and watched. To survive is not to conquer the cavern, but to participate in its ongoing argument.

They are damp, deft, superstitious in practical ways, unsentimental about death, tender toward tools, suspicious of silence, and loyal to rhythms older than memory. They know which flowers glow before a pressure drop. They know which alleys belong to mantises after heat-vent. They know how to sleep through engines and wake at the wrong kind of drip.

They have forgotten stone because stone no longer answers them.

Everything else does.
