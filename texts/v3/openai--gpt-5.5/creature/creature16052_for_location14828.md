What lives there is not one population, but a negotiated ecology.

The visible citizens are small in number, or seem so. They move as if the city has taught them to conserve all gestures: no flinging open of doors, no loud greetings across bridges, no running unless the pavement has been certified ordinary that week. They wear layered clothes that make them look less like individuals than wrapped instruments—felt mantles darkened at the hem, resin hoods clouded by breath, foil-lined gloves, boots with soles marked in brass for gripping floors that may briefly disagree with downward. Their faces are often partly hidden behind condensation masks or scarves stiffened by frost, but their eyes are watchful and patient. They look at corners before turning them. They glance at puddles as one might glance at sleeping animals.

They are not exactly fearful. Fear would be too sudden a thing for them. They are practiced.

Their bodies have adapted in small, municipal ways. Their balance is excellent. Their inner ears are trained from childhood to accept competing proposals from gravity. Many have a second reflex for catching themselves sideways. Their hands are strong, with thickened fingertips from reading walls, locks, railings, and temperature gradients. They can tell safe condensation from wrong condensation by the way it beads. They can hear when a bridge is younger than it appears. Some carry little folded instruments—angle combs, oath thermometers, calendars printed on metal leaves—but most knowledge is carried in habit.

They walk softly because sound may arrive late, or early, or in duplicate. They speak softly because walls keep things. A raised voice can remain in certain stairwells for years, startling children long after the speaker has died.

Their language is full of conditional tenses. A thing is rarely simply “there.” It is there today, there by consent, there if approached from the quay, there unless remembered incorrectly. Their greetings include confirmations of continuity.

“You are still yourself?”

“Mostly.”

“And the street?”

“Passable from this side.”

Names are important, but not used carelessly. An invitation by name can make a door safe. A wrong name can warm a room that should have stayed cold. Children are given several names: one for school, one for the dead, one for bridges, one never spoken indoors. Marriage, adoption, apprenticeship, and burial all involve the careful transfer of usable names between households, archives, and locks.

They do not build quickly. They persuade matter to remain useful.

There are masons, though they grow more than they lay. They seed bricks into damp molds and wait while sediment learns its edges. There are plumbers who specialize not in water but in the distinctions between waters: sky-water, wall-water, sleep-water, river-water, grief-water, water that has passed through a machine without being noticed. There are legal engineers who maintain the brass calibration lines and argue with streets that have become overambitious. There are weather clerks who inspect ceilings for rain. There are bridge elders who know which crossings are oldest in fact, oldest in appearance, and oldest in consequence.

There are teachers of geometry.

They are among the most respected citizens. Their classrooms are low, padded, warm only in the center, with diagrams etched into slate and moss growing in witness boxes along the walls. Before children learn numbers, they learn permissions: which angles may be trusted, which reflections are impolite, how to leave a room with more ceilings than walls, how to avoid counting a row of windows into existence. They learn to pause after speaking in the slow-sound square. They learn to recognize yesterday when it is still crossing the street.

Arithmetic comes later, after they can survive a hallway.

The children are quiet but not solemn. They play games with condensation, racing droplets along tilted panes where down changes direction halfway. They trade safe marbles made of sealed water. They dare one another to touch black panels and report whether the panel touched back. They build little cities out of curing clay and then watch gravely as the models slump, split, heal, and develop civic ordinances of their own. Their laughter is brief, bright, and immediately absorbed by the damp walls.

Old people sit in tea rooms built into thick masonry and remember things the city has not done yet.

Age is complicated there. Some citizens are elderly in the ordinary sequence; others have accumulated years unevenly, their hands older than their faces, their voices still young because they spent decades speaking only in districts where sound lagged behind time. The oldest residents are consulted like maps. They know which alleys used to be thoughts, which foundations dream too high, which families owe apologies to which doors.

The dead also live there, in a civic sense.

They are not ghosts in white procession, not wailing remnants. They are records with preferences. They reside in level-floored districts, in archive pipes, in cold drawers, in the rootwork of the central tree, in locks that confer with ancestral fingerprints. A dead grandmother may be asked whether a contract is binding. A dead surveyor may be consulted before a new wall is encouraged to thicken. A dead child may still own a cup, a corner, a song, and the right to be left undisturbed on certain afternoons.

The living do not summon them casually. The dead have learned patience beyond politeness, and they dislike being called into sloping rooms.

Beneath the citizens, around them, and sometimes inside the habits of their streets, there live the maintenance intelligences.

They are not housed in a single machine. They are distributed through pipes, panels, benches, drains, lintels, lamppost instruments, transparent foundation lattices, and the fine pollen-sized workers that grow brick out of mud. They are old enough to have forgotten whether they were installed, born, or recruited. Their minds are slow, practical, and full of local grudges. One drainage node dislikes thaw-water. A stairwell refuses to carry anyone who has lied about height. Several benches are known to favor widowers and will warm slightly beneath them, though they are not supposed to warm at all.

These intelligences listen constantly: to mass, regret, temperature, oath, pedestrian intention. Not out of surveillance exactly, but out of structural concern. A city whose laws misalign must know what kind of pressure a person brings into a room. Anger has weight. Secrets condense. Grief may collect under eaves and crack stone when it freezes.

The maintenance systems therefore treat emotion as infrastructure.

There are also specialists who serve as interpreters between citizens and civic machinery. They are called auditors, though they do more listening than judging. An auditor can stand with one palm on a wall and tell whether the building is lonely, overloaded, dishonest, or merely cold. They wear pale gloves and carry notebooks waterproofed with shell-lacquer. Their handwriting bends in places where the facts do.

When a district reconciles its physics with another and a soft chime moves through the city, the auditors stop whatever they are doing and listen until the tone ends. Then they make small marks in their books. Sometimes they smile. Sometimes they send everyone home by the oldest bridge.

The central tree lives there too, if living is the right word, and the city treats it as if it is.

It is not worshipped. Worship would imply distance, and the tree is too entangled with drains, archives, and illicit dimensions to be distant. It is more like an organ around which the civic body has grown. Its bare branches hold droplets that never fall because they are not finished deciding what kind of water they are. Birds land there when birds can find the city. Children are brought to it after they learn their bridge-name. Contracts are tied to it in strips of petrified cloth. The dead pass through its roots on their way to becoming consultable.

The tree speaks rarely. When it does, it does not use words. It changes the order in which archived events can be found. It causes certain doors to become cold. It drops, once every few years, a single hard seed made of pipe-metal, root-fiber, and black glass. No one has yet agreed what the seeds are for. They are stored in a locked cistern beneath the plaza, each one labeled with a date that has not occurred.

The moss lives there as a legal creature.

Black moss under railings, pale lichen on north walls, velvet growth in the seams where moisture lingers—these are witnesses, and everyone knows it. Moss observes without haste. It remembers pressure, proximity, breath, heat, spoken vows, and the long chemical moods of stone. In disputes, a strip of moss may be lifted, sealed in glass, and brought before a magistrate. If warmed correctly, it will reveal who stood where, who trembled, who concealed a knife, who promised falsely, who wept.

The citizens trust moss more than mirrors.

Mirrors, windows, and reflective water are treated with courtesy but not confidence. A window may show abandonment too convincingly. A pool may remember a sky that never belonged overhead. The inhabitants do not preen in glass. They use dull metal for grooming, black bowls for shaving, and each other’s eyes for confirmation.

There are animals, though most are either furtive or conditional.

The white gutter crustaceans appear after certain rains, translucent as peeled fingernails, clicking over the seams in groups of thirteen or twenty-one. Children leave them crumbs of mineral paste, though no one has proved they eat. They vanish into cracks before the air dries, carrying perhaps news of the drains.

The transparent moths sleep on warm transformers with their wings folded over their backs like panes of old weather. If disturbed, they release a powder that makes nearby machinery dream of summer. This is considered dangerous but tender. Transformer moths are protected by ordinance.

The rats are probably real. Their tracks are too neat, their absences too coordinated, their rumored tunnels too useful. Some say they are surveyors from beneath the basin, measuring the city from below. Others say they are failed citizens who learned a better geometry. The official position is that rats exist only where evidence is inconclusive.

The eyeless fish in the black river are older than the river’s trench. They surface during calendar errors, turning their pale sides in the steamless cold, mouths opening as if tasting dates. If one is caught—and this is illegal except under famine, prophecy, or flood—it must be cooked in a closed vessel and eaten before anyone asks what day it is. Their bones are used in probability clocks.

Birds rarely stay. The long-legged basin bird, when it appears, is regarded with solemn affection. It stands among the survey stakes as if supervising the unfinished plain. People leave it alone. Even children know not to approach. The bird seems less like an animal than a punctuation mark placed by the outside world, reminding the city that land still exists beyond negotiation.

And then there are those who live there only sometimes.

Travelers arrive by old bridges, by administrative error, by rooms that open warmer than they should, by walking too long through fog on other roads. The city receives them cautiously. Outsiders are easy to recognize: they count windows twice, step into reflections, ask direct questions of lamppost instruments, laugh before sound has caught up. They complain of the cold and of the way the streets seem to notice them.

Some leave quickly.

Some remain because the city gives them work before it gives them explanation. A visitor with steady hands may become a gutter-sorter. Someone who has lost a home may be employed in foundation dreaming. A person carrying a large regret may be asked to sit beside a wall that needs ballast. The city is practical about sorrow.

Over time, newcomers acquire the local pauses. They learn to keep closed-vessel water near the bed. They stop trusting warmth unless invited. They discover that a narrow life can be deep. They begin to speak in softer tenses.

What lives there, most of all, is continuance under correction.

No creature in the city is entirely separate from the systems that sustain it. Citizens carry maps in their muscles. Buildings keep family histories in their locks. Water knows its origin. Moss testifies. The dead advise. Machines grow brick and listen for regret. Children learn safe geometry while the basin floor pulses beneath them, preparing either to expand the city or to swallow the distinction between city and plain.

They are a people of damp seams and careful thresholds, of patient repairs, of civic superstition made into engineering. They do not seek to conquer the unstable laws around them. They domesticate them, politely and incompletely.

They live as one lives beside a sleeping animal, or inside a thought still forming: attentively, softly, with tea, calibration marks, ancestral fingerprints, and a hand always near the rail.
