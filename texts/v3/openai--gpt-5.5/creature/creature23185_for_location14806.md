What lives there is not a people, not exactly, and not a single dominant beast. It is a citizenship of builders.

The valley belongs to creatures that do not distinguish between body, dwelling, road, midden, nursery, and food. They live by making and by entering what others have made. They inherit architecture the way forest animals elsewhere inherit trees. Here a nest is not only a nest; it is future soil, future wall, future fungus bed, future frog bowl, future burrow roof. The inhabitants are all tenants of one another.

The oldest residents are probably not the largest, but the smallest: termites, ants, fungi, mites, beetles, larvae, bacteria, root hairs, molds. They are the deep population, the unseen majority. They live inside the valley’s thickness, in the packed black-brown strata underfoot, in the walls, in dead trunks, in roof matter, in dung and feathers and fruit pulp. They eat the place and remake it grain by grain.

The termites are masons. Their colonies inhabit the drier ribs of the saddle, where warm air can be caught and breathed through clay. They build upward and outward, sealing cracks after rain, opening vents in the heat, raising red-brown and gray towers whose surfaces are never still if watched closely enough. Their soldiers wait in blind galleries. Their workers carry chewed fiber, fungal paste, and softened mineral dust. They do not know the mountain, only the temperature of passages, the pressure of neighbors, the taste of breach and repair.

The ants are road-keepers and raiders. Some maintain shining black highways along roots and trunks, moving in disciplined streams from fruit fall to carcass to fungus garden. Others are thin-legged hunters that pour suddenly into rookeries, taking eggs, mites, sleeping chicks, anything soft enough to cut. Leaf-cutters climb into the canopy and return with green fragments held like little sails, making the forest appear to disassemble itself. Army ants move like weather. When they pass, frogs leap upward, beetles flee, roaches abandon crevices, and birds follow the swarm to harvest the fleeing.

The fungi are the valley’s hidden memory. They rise when the wet season swells the walls, pale shelves from old nests, orange cups from dung seams, black threads through packed leaf-fiber. Some are fed deliberately by insects. Some bloom from forgotten chambers where bodies have accumulated. Some live quietly in the timber until a tree weakens, then take it down by inches. They are the soft white engines beneath the architecture, loosening every certainty, preparing every wall to become floor.

Above this deep citizenry live the visible builders.

Birds are everywhere. They are the valley’s voice, its weather, its falling matter. The upper canopy holds the great fruit-eaters: hornbills, parrots, pigeons, toucans or their mountain equivalents, heavy-beaked and loud, with lacquer-dark eyes and feet made for gripping wet branches. They break fruit open carelessly. They waste abundance. Half-eaten figs, split drupes, seeds, skins, and pulp rain constantly downward, feeding mammals, insects, fungus, and root. They are gardeners without intention. Trees grow where they perch.

The weavers live in colonies so dense that a single tree may seem to have grown a second crown of hanging chambers. Their nests are grass globes, fiber socks, bottle-shaped pendants, stitched leaves, suspended knots. They squabble continually, repairing, stealing strands, courting, driving rivals from entrances. Their young make the whole roof tremble with hunger. When wind reaches the valley, all their nests sway together like fruit that can cry.

There are swifts and swallows in the clay seams, narrow-winged and tireless, sewing the dim air with speed. They nest in cracks of termite towers, in old burrow mouths, under the lips of hardened mud shelves. Their young cling in darkness, open-mouthed, while the adults carve insects out of the gloom.

There are carrion birds too, black-shouldered and patient. They stand hunched on the high platforms with the gravity of judges, waiting for a chick to fall, a monkey to misjudge a leap, a civet to leave something half-opened. They do not need to hunt often. In a place this crowded, death is a regular fruit.

The rookeries are cities suspended within the city. Great black birds nest there in masses, their platforms layered year after year until branches disappear inside woven weight. The birds are glossy, hoarse, red-eyed or pale-eyed, with strong bills and dirty feet. They leave the valley at morning in torn banners and return at dusk with crops full of stolen life. Their guano whitens leaves, burns seedlings, feeds mosses, feeds flies, feeds beetles, feeds the underfloor. Their chicks grow in ammonia heat, under a ceiling of screaming adults and shifting wings.

Bats claim the undersides.

They hang in curtains from platforms, hollow trunks, root chambers, and old termite alcoves. By day they are leather fruit, breathing softly, wrapped in themselves, their bodies pressed together for warmth and safety. If disturbed, they ripple before they fly, a single nervous shudder moving through hundreds. Their smell is sharp and mammalian, sour with urine and milk. At dusk they loosen from the roof and drop into the air, first one, then a handful, then a black spill. Some hunt moths and beetles. Some drink nectar from night flowers growing out of old nests. Some take fruit and carry seeds into places no bird visits. Their young cling blindly to mothers, tiny hooked things listening to the thunder of the colony.

The amphibians are another nation entirely.

They live wherever water can be briefly convinced to remain water. Tree frogs adhere to waxy leaves, bark, mud walls, nest rims, and the backs of broad sleeping creatures. Some are green as new moss, some brown as wet bark, some glassy-bellied and nearly invisible until they move. They call from bromeliad cups, root holes, rain-filled footprints, hollow fruit husks, abandoned bird bowls. Their eggs hang in foam masses over pools or lie like strings of pearls in dark water. Tadpoles grow in cups no larger than a fist, feeding on algae, mosquito larvae, fallen pollen, sometimes one another.

Larger frogs own the pools in the lower hollows. They sit half-submerged, throats pulsing, eyes gold or black above the green surface. They sound at night like struck rubber, like wooden drums, like wet thumbs drawn over skins. During the rains their voices overlap until the water itself seems to be calling.

Where frogs live, hunters gather.

Snakes move through the valley with a patience that seems almost plantlike. There are vine-thin snakes in the canopy, green or brown, balancing along woven bridges and root cables, taking eggs and nestlings. There are blunt-headed water snakes in the pools, sliding between frog calls. There are thicker constrictors that use the warm built floor as cover, appearing only as a slow muscular pressure under leaves before they rise around a roosting mammal or unwary bird. Some are venomous and bright in warning; others are dull, mud-colored, impossible to see until they become a line of motion.

Lizards live in the sunshafts. They are quick, nervous, jeweled things, emerging when the canopy opens briefly and warmth collects on bark. Geckos own the inner chambers and hunt by touch in darkness, their pale bellies flashing on mud ceilings. Larger monitors or monitor-like climbers patrol the edges of pools, raiding eggs, digging into rotting nest piles, facing down birds with the cold entitlement of reptiles.

The mammals are quieter than the birds but no less present.

Small rodents live in the walls: soft-furred, bright-eyed, whiskered, nesting in old cavities and feeding on seeds, insects, fruit pulp, and stored grains stolen from birds. They know passages too narrow for snakes and use them until snakes learn them too. At night they move over the springy floor in fits and starts, stopping to listen with their whole bodies.

Tree shrews, civets, and possum-like climbers haunt the middle levels. They are long-bodied and clever-footed, with ringed tails or dark masks, moving along root bridges and hardened clay paths. They eat fruit, eggs, beetles, frogs, sleeping chicks, nectar, anything sweet or slow. Their droppings are full of seeds, and seedlings rise from their latrines. They are thieves, pollinators, dispersers, and occasional prey.

There are monkeys, or something much like them, in the upper structure: dark-limbed, gripping, argumentative animals with pale faces glimpsed between leaves. They do not come down often. They travel by routes older than any individual—branch to root to platform to vine—shouting across gaps, breaking fruit, testing nests for eggs, grooming ticks from one another in crowded forks. Their young play on the suspended bridges and sometimes fall. The whole valley hears it when one does.

There may be small cats in the dark corridors: clouded, spotted, low-shouldered predators with wide paws for wet wood and organic ground. They live by patience. They lie on platforms above bird traffic, creep along root buttresses, and freeze when parrots alarm. They smell of musk and damp fur. Their eyes catch the little light there is. They take sleeping birds, rodents, frogs, young monkeys, civets if they can. They leave almost nothing except a drag mark, a few feathers, and silence in one place for a short while.

In the underfloor, in the wet seams beneath the packed organic crust, live pale crabs, worms, blind beetles, centipedes, burrowing frogs, and soft-bodied things that rarely see open air. They follow moisture. They live in the hidden circulation of the valley, in seepage and darkness. During heavy rains they surface into pools and channels, then vanish again as the water sinks. Their world is not canopy or sky but pressure, rot, clay, root, and the taste of dissolved life.

The insects are beyond counting.

Mosquitoes rise from cups and pools in whining veils. Dragonflies patrol temporary openings like bright needles. Beetles bore into wood, roll dung, chew fungus, clean carcasses, carry mites like passengers. Wasps rasp fiber into paper and hang their gray combs under leaves and ledges. Bees move in golden lanes between flowers that grow from old nests, their hives humming in hollow trunks. Cockroaches, crickets, katydids, and mantids occupy every fold. Moths sleep in drifts and rise like ash when touched. Spiders stretch nets between roots, across paths, over pool edges, inside chambers where the air is thick with gnats. Some spiders are tiny dew-beaded mechanics; others are hand-sized ambushers waiting in burrow mouths.

No animal here is clean for long. Feathers are damp. Fur carries spores. Scales collect clay. Feet are blackened by resin, guano, pollen, and rot. Even the eggs are quickly claimed by film, dust, and the attention of parasites. The residents are infested, and the infestations are infested. Lice live on birds; mites live on lice; fungi grow where bodies press too long against walls. This is not sickness exactly. It is intimacy.

The plants are residents too, but they behave like slow animals of structure.

Epiphytes live without ever touching original earth. Orchids, ferns, mosses, liverworts, bromeliads, and small figs root in guano-rich crotches, abandoned nests, and softened clay. Bromeliads hold little reservoirs where frogs breed and mosquito larvae write dark commas. Ferns turn old wasp paper into green shelves. Moss takes every damp surface and gives it hair. Vines climb everything, then become routes for animals and scaffolds for other vines. Strangler figs begin as seeds dropped by birds into high forks, then send roots downward through the air, enclosing older trees, old nests, old cavities, old histories, until they become columns with hollow memories inside.

The trees themselves are not solitary trunks but vertical neighborhoods. Their bark is pierced, wrapped, inhabited, polished by claws, bridged by vines, and patched with termite clay. Their crowns are roofs, feeding grounds, roosts, battlefields, nurseries. Their deaths are not endings. A dead tree becomes a bat hall, a beetle mine, a fungal tower, a seedbed, a corridor, a slow meal large enough to feed generations.

If there is a chief inhabitant, it is the colony as a principle.

The creatures here live in multiples. Flocks, swarms, rookeries, hives, colonies, choruses, troops, mats, clutches, broods. Solitary animals exist, but they move through the pressure of mass life. Even predators are shaped by abundance; they do not rule from empty territory but slip through congestion, taking what the crowd fails to protect.

Most residents are opportunists. They do not need open ground, clear water, or clean air. They need edges: the rim of a pool, the seam of a wall, the underside of a platform, the hollow behind a root, the fresh wound in a tree, the new fall of fruit, the unguarded egg, the recently dead thing. The valley is almost entirely edge.

They are adapted to dimness. Many have large eyes, reflective eyes, pale bodies, touch-sensitive whiskers, antennae, long toes, adhesive pads. Many call because sight fails. Many smell strongly because scent lingers in warm, wet air. Many build because the mountain gives them no bare place to be.

Their lives are vertical. Birth may happen in a hanging nest, feeding in the canopy, hiding in a mud seam, mating over a pool, dying on the springing floor, and becoming part of a termite wall. A seed dropped from a hornbill’s beak may sprout from a civet latrine, be wrapped by moss, pierced by ants, watered by condensation, and eventually hold a frog’s eggs in one of its leaves. Nothing travels in a straight line, not even decay.

They are loud because they must be. Sound is territory, mating, warning, location, threat, hunger. A chick screams so its parent can find it among ten thousand mouths. A frog calls because the pool is dark and crowded. A monkey barks because a cat has become visible for half a second. Termites are nearly silent, but the birds built above them are not; bats whisper and squeal; insects saw the darkness into pieces. The valley’s inhabitants make noise the way a body makes heat.

And beneath all of them, through all of them, runs the same exchange:

Eat. Build. Foul. Repair. Breed. Abandon. Enter. Hollow. Root. Collapse. Rise.

No creature here lives outside the others. The hornbill feeds the tree. The tree holds the weaver. The weaver drops the chick. The civet eats the chick. The civet’s dung feeds the fig. The fig root splits the termite wall. The termites seal around the root. The frog lays eggs in the water caught by the fig’s leaves. The snake eats the frog. The ants clean the snake’s shed skin. Fungi enter what remains. Moss covers the seam.

What lives there is a mountain that has been taken into mouths and given back as habitat.

It is a city of nonhuman urgencies, crowded past neatness, ancient without being still. Its inhabitants are not separate from their dwellings. They are the builders, the walls, the weather inside the walls, and the hunger moving through them.
