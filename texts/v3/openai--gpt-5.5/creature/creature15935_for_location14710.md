What lives there is not one creature, though from a distance it can seem like one.

It is a wet, murmuring colony of small lives, all of them made useful once, and all of them now slightly beyond usefulness. A maintenance ecology, mostly. A rain-fed mechanism with appetites. A marsh that learned to fit inside a service chamber.

The most visible inhabitants are the coin-sized cleaning crabs. They are not true crabs, but they have accepted the shape: many legs, low bodies, shell backs, quick sidewise decisions. Their translucent carapaces carry old stamped codes under veils of mineral clouding. Some still show factory markings, batch numbers, service icons; others are so furred with calcium and bacterial glaze that they look carved from old fingernails or cloudy amber.

They move in processions along the grates, under rails, across slick ledges, always working. They scrape. They taste. They worry at dead polymer, loosened sealant, fungal bloom, the soft edges of mats. When they pause, they raise their front limbs as if listening with them. Their bodies are full of tiny cleaning implements: brushes, rasps, chemical glands, little fans that test water quality. A boot left too long on the lowest grate becomes, to them, simply another surface beginning to fail.

They are earnest creatures. Not intelligent in any large, sentimental way, but locally wise. They know which conduits sweat warm and which ones burn. They know the hour before the sideways rain. They gather then under lips and bracket shadows, gripping with their threadlike feet while the room applauds itself into mist.

In the pools live the threadfish.

They are narrow, quick, almost calligraphic. In still water they vanish into blackness, but when they turn, a blue seam opens along the spine, metallic and cold as a service diode. They move together when frightened, each body taking its cue from pressure, vibration, the faint electric tremble of its neighbors. A dozen can become one flashing hinge. Fifty can become a torn piece of night.

They eat bloom from submerged sensors, graze the soft films that gather around intake mouths, keep certain algae from closing over the water’s instruments. Their eyes are small or absent; sight is not their main language. They read disturbance. A falling drop is weather. A crab’s leg is danger or food. A diagnostic pulse is a sunrise they do not understand and do not need.

Some of them hold territories no wider than a hand. One school keeps to the shadow under the diagnostic column, where pale rootlets trail down from transparent tubes. Another gathers in the brass-lipped channel, noses pointed into the current, taking nourishment from what the water brings them. They breed in seams. Their eggs adhere to ceramic pores and hatch among mineral whiskers. The smallest young are nearly invisible except when scans pass over them and turn their bodies briefly silver-white.

Along the watercourses cling the filter crawlers.

They are softer than the crabs and less decisive than the fish. They resemble lashes, tongues, petals, little breathing wounds. They line the channels in colonies, opening and closing with the rhythm of flow. Each one anchors itself to composite stone by a muscular foot and extends a fringe into the passing water. The fringe combs the current for particles: microbial clumps, dissolved organics, powdered mineral, the shed skins of smaller organisms.

When the room’s rain changes direction, they all open together.

That is when they look most alive. A thousand wet eyelashes lifting in the amber dark. A thousand small mouths trusting the system to bring them what they need.

They bruise easily. A careless tool, a boot edge, a sudden chemical imbalance can ruin a whole line of them. But they return if the flow remains kind. They bud from surviving tissue, spread along grooves, colonize the undersides of lips where water curls. The room has made them patient.

Above, under pipes and in humid pockets, the moth-things sleep.

They are descendants of pollinators from some older, greener module, though there is almost nothing left here that needs pollination. Their bodies are small, warm-gray, furred with condensation. Their wings fold close and dark, like scraps of damp ash paper. When resting, they appear dead. When the pressure valves sigh or the vibration changes in the walls, they lift all at once.

Then the air becomes particulate with them.

They do not fly beautifully. There is too much moisture, too little space. They stagger, flicker, rebound from handrails and pipes. Their wings catch amber strips, blue diodes, white diagnostic pulses. For a few seconds the upper room fills with a living soot-cloud, and then they settle again, vanishing into the undersides of machinery.

They feed on films and secretions: beadwater sweetened by microbial mats, mineral-sour droplets, the faint exudate of the pale reeds. Their larvae cling in protected damp, looking like lint until they move. Some are eaten by crabs. Some drown in the wrong seam. Some find the vents and become part of the bacterial veil there, not as individuals but as nutrient, as permission for something else to continue.

There are predators too, because even engineered systems eventually discover predation.

In the largest pool, where “largest” means no more than the width of a table, a folded ribbon waits beneath brown gel. It is soft, flat, and nearly the same color as the manufactured mud. It has no dramatic jaws. It does not need them. It lies buried with only sensory fringes exposed, reading the tiny tremors of passing legs. When a crab steps too close, the ribbon unfolds faster than the eye wants to believe. It wraps, contracts, dissolves at the joints.

It is probably tolerated because it limits the crabs. Or perhaps it was never listed and simply became useful after surviving long enough. The station may know it is there. The room certainly does.

There are also larvae under mesh covers, silver and trembling. Some belong to the crabs, some to other maintenance breeds whose adult forms are rarely seen. They pulse in warm seepage, clustered like living filings. They molt into roles determined by chemistry, crowding, temperature, and whatever signals the room gives them through water. A maintenance swarm is not born with one destiny. It is persuaded into a function.

That is true of nearly everything here.

The lichens around screw heads are not ornamental. They take metals in, bind what should not drift, stain the old welds green-black. The filament moss hanging in tassels is always being eaten back, but never eradicated, because its presence helps the condensation behave. The dark growth beneath the eastern baffles is tender and important, part organism and part pressure instrument, a living chemistry membrane that bruises if touched.

The pale fronds in the lower wall slots are less like plants than agreements. Their roots are sealed away, their leaves clipped by machines, their growth measured and corrected. They are permitted a narrow life in exchange for stabilizing air, holding moisture, feeding moths, feeding films, feeding the idea that the room is more than plumbing.

Beneath all of these are the smallest residents: bacteria, engineered yeasts, mineral-binding threads, pressure-tolerant mats, invisible grazers in the brown gel. They are the true population. Everything larger is their weather and architecture. They make the smell: rain indoors, warm metal, old stone that is not stone, the animal sweetness of wet filters. They coat the handrails where palms do not polish. They line the capillary engines. They live in the pores of ceramic and in the seams between maintenance cycles.

They remember every tolerated leak.

If there is a sovereign organism here, it is not the crab, not the fish, not the ribbon predator. It is the room’s whole damp agreement: machine, microbe, animal, water, heat, mineral, and habit. It lives by correction rather than freedom. It is wild only in the way a scar is wild — formed under constraints, shaped by injury and repair, never exactly as designed.

Its body is the ravine of black composite shelves.

Its breath is the warm mist pushed through vents.

Its pulse is pumps, fans, valves, capillary fields.

Its blood is water climbing where water should not climb.

Its skin is biofilm and mineral fur.

Its nerves are sensors filmed just thinly enough to keep reading.

Its moods are measured in temperature shifts, drainage intervals, sideways rain.

It does not think like an animal. But it responds. It favors some channels. It sacrifices others. It has learned which stains must remain, which leaks feed useful mouths, which overcleaning causes collapse. It has trained its maintenance intelligence as much as the intelligence has trained it. The two have become entangled: command and adaptation, schedule and appetite.

A person entering might think: there are many small things living here.

After an hour, they might think: the room is keeping them.

After longer still, when the mist thickens and the far wall disappears though it is only a few strides away, they might understand the quieter truth:

The small things are keeping the room.

And the room, in turn, has become something that lives.
