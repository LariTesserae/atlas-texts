What lives there is almost not a creature.

It is a thin persistence, a green and darkened skin laid onto stone where the weakened light can still be used. It does not enter the hollow so much as occur on its surfaces. It has no den, no path, no territory except the millimeters it can hold. It lives flattened against mineral, pressed into the old patience of the walls.

At the upper edges, where the pale ceiling-light reaches most reliably, it gathers as a film: olive, bottle-green, nearly black where it thickens in the tiny irregularities of rock. Not moss, not weed in any lush sense, but a biological tarnish. A breathless photosynthetic dust. If touched, it would smear rather than break. It has the softness of something alive and the restraint of something that has learned not to ask much of the world.

In the cracks, it becomes more secretive. Threads of algae trail out, sparse and cold, like the last strands of hair from a drowned head, though there is no drama in them. They barely move. The water around them is too still to make them wave. They hang because they grew that way, because the rock allowed a seam, because light and minerals and time met in that narrow place and did not entirely refuse one another.

There are colonies too small to be seen clearly: bacteria in films, in stains, in the boundary between stone and water. They inhabit the orange iron curtains and the black manganese freckles, not as animals inhabit shelter, but as processes inhabit opportunity. They take what is available in traces. They live on gradients: a little dissolved mineral here, a slight chemical difference there, the faint commerce of seepage through stone. Their lives are not eventful. They do not arrive, hunt, mate, flee. They alter surfaces grain by grain. They darken a patch. They help a stain find its edge.

Their time is not the quick time of bodies. It is closer to the time of deposition. A season may be only a slight thickening. A year may be a change in tone on the wall. Generations pass without visible motion. They divide inside silence. They inherit the same cold water, the same diluted light, the same ceiling trembling far above them.

They are not lonely, because loneliness belongs to creatures that expect encounter. These lives expect only contact: water against membrane, mineral against filament, light against pigment. They do not search the basin. They do not explore its little distances. The hollow may be crossed in less than a minute by a swimmer, but to them it is not a room to cross. It is a set of surfaces, exposures, permissions.

The largest of them are the trailing algae, and even they seem less like inhabitants than signs left by habitation. Dark ribbons at a crack. Beads along a wet seam. A green dusting on the upper stone. Their bodies announce the minimum by which life can remain life: adhere, absorb, endure. They do not transform the chamber. They merely prove that the chamber is not entirely given over to mineral and pressure.

If the place has a resident spirit, it is not one of motion but of maintenance. The living thing there is a slow agreement between light and stone. It is the faint green patience that accepts the rules: no air, little warmth, no soil, no shelter beyond the surface itself. It grows only where it can, and then only slightly. It leaves the clear water clear. It leaves the silt undisturbed. It leaves the silence intact.

So what lives there is a margin-life.

A skin-life.

A life without roaming, without eyes, without hunger in the animal sense.

It clings to the submerged chapel as if listening through its whole body to the deep, steady pressure of the world. It does not dream of leaving. It has no image of elsewhere. Its entire existence is the cool stone under it, the diluted brightness above it, the mineral seep passing through it so slowly that movement and stillness become the same fact.

It is almost nothing.

But it is alive.
