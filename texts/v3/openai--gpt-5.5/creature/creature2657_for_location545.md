What lives there is not a population so much as a duration that has learned to wear bodies.

The inhabitants of the high plain are alive in the way a glacier is alive, or a mountain range, or an old wound under scar tissue. They have metabolism, intention, hunger, fear, memory—but all of it has been stretched until it no longer resembles animal life to a hurried eye. They are not frozen. They are not enchanted. They are simply obedient to a slower law.

Their bodies have made treaties with dryness.

Nothing soft remains soft for long on that plateau. Skin becomes sealant, armor, parchment, wax, shell, mineral glaze. Fur turns into a way of holding dust. Feathers become vanes for measuring silence. Blood thickens into dark grains carried through the body by pressure rather than pulse. Some creatures have organs like geodes, inner chambers lined with crystals that store warmth, direction, ancestral alarm. Some have no blood at all, only conductive threads running through them like ore veins through stone.

They do not drink. They remember water the way a ruined temple remembers singing.

Many of them feed on what elsewhere would not be considered food: the charge that gathers between salt crust and sky; the minute difference between sunlit stone and shadowed stone; flakes of mineral exhaled by the ground; the long, tasteless nourishment of altitude. Some eat the old light trapped in hollows. Some graze on black crusts that take a century to thicken by the width of a fingernail. Some are carnivores, though their violence is so delayed that predator and prey enter a kind of mutual occupation, each becoming part of the other’s future before any wound is completed.

The pale beetle-things under stones are among the smallest true animals there. They gather in packed constellations with their legs folded tight, not sleeping, not dead, but engaged in communal calculation. Their shells are slightly translucent. Inside each is a knot of pale organs and one grain of something black, like a seed or an eye. When a stone is overturned, they do not scatter. Scattering would be wasteful. Instead, over several days, the outermost beetles rotate themselves inward and the inner beetles slowly emerge, so the colony presents a new surface to the changed conditions. Their lives are arranged like mosaics. Their young are born already holding still.

The tall ribbed grazers among the glass reeds are mostly hollow. Wind passes through them, though rarely enough to matter. Their wedge-shaped heads are not lowered from exhaustion but from attention. Along the underside of their skulls are combs of bone as fine as frost. With these they scrape the edible difference between mineral plates: not the crust itself, but the tension where two kinds of salt meet. Their bellies are resonant chambers. If one is struck—by accident, by falling stone, by the closing of some great distant crack—it gives off a note too low to hear, and the herd receives it through the ground.

They reproduce by standing near one another for a very long time.

A new grazer begins as a pale swelling on the flank of an old one, a mineral bud, indistinguishable at first from disease. It grows ribs before legs, memory before appetite. After perhaps four hundred years it detaches, settles upright, and joins the posture of the herd. There is no infancy in the usual sense. There is only a gradual assumption of burden.

The suspended birds are stranger still. They belong equally to air and delay. Their wings are locked open not by paralysis but by a choice made repeatedly, continually, with unimaginable discipline. Each one is in flight. Each one is still crossing the basin. The oldest among them have not yet completed a single migration begun under another sky.

Their feathers are not feathers exactly, but thin layered instruments that catch sunlight, starlight, heat, and the memory of thermals that no longer rise. Their bones are filled with blue shadow. Their eyes remain wet-looking despite the absence of moisture, because each eye is sealed behind a lens of living glass. They eat while flying by filtering angles. Dawn feeds them. Noon starves them. Twilight gives them enough to continue.

Their eggs are laid in the air.

A mated pair will hold a space between them for decades, flying without moving around an invisible center. Dust gathers there. Light thickens. A shell forms out of suspended particles until an egg hangs unsupported, turning once every few years. When it hatches, the young bird does not fall. It inherits the family altitude.

The rope-like animals in the ravines are older than many ridges. They have vertebrae but no clear beginning or end, and their heads, if they possess heads, are often buried deep in pillars of salt. Their skins are gray and cracked, renewing themselves not by shedding but by adding new layers beneath old damage. They coil around columns because the columns grow by immeasurable increments, and the animals feed on that growth. Their jaws are slow rasps. Their tongues are dry straps covered in mineral-sensitive hairs. A single feeding may last longer than a human lineage.

Sometimes one of these ravine-beasts uncoils.

This is considered, by the rest of the plateau, an event.

The watchers on the ledges are not predators, though their faces suggest judgment. They sit blunt and furred in silver dust, with immense forward-looking eyes. Their bodies are compact, almost childlike, but their gaze is unbearable. Each watcher specializes in an era. One sees the plain as it was when it still had high peaks. One sees it as it will be when the last salts have lifted away. One sees only the present, and for that reason is almost blind.

They do not move their eyes to follow a passerby. Instead, they include the passerby in the period they are already watching. To be seen by one is to feel oneself become historical.

The many-legged antlered creatures of the flats live in herds so tightly interlocked they are difficult to distinguish from structures. Their antlers grow laterally, branching and joining with the antlers of their neighbors until the herd becomes a fence, a web, a single defensive sentence written across the ground. Each small body trembles with restrained motion. Their legs are delicate, numerous, and precise. They could run, perhaps, if time demanded it. Time has not.

They feed cooperatively. The outer animals collect light and mineral dust on their antlers; the inner animals digest it through shared contact. New young are born in the protected center of the lattice, already bearing soft branching nubs. Death does not remove an individual from the herd immediately. A dead one remains locked in place until its antlers have mineralized enough to serve as architecture.

Among them pass the narrow-jawed hunters.

These are lean, patient things, all hinge and intention. Their mouths fit perfectly around the necks of smaller animals. They may hold a bite for centuries before closing it. The prey, during this period, continues to live, feed, reproduce, and carry the hunter as part of its landscape. There is no panic. Panic belongs to creatures who believe an event has a beginning.

The parasites are beautiful. Beadlike, shell-backed, sealed against the air, they arrange themselves in patterns along the bodies of larger animals: spirals, bands, constellations, tally marks. Some may be feeding. Some may be writing. On the flanks of the oldest grazers the parasites form maps of vanished rivers, though none of the creatures involved can have seen water.

There are also the almost-human inhabitants.

They are not numerous. Or perhaps they are, but most of them are mistaken for bundled hides, seated stones, old cairns with hair. They live in the low shelters of slab and bone, crouched inside entrances turned away from the prevailing absence. Their bodies have accepted the plateau more fully than any visitor’s mind can bear. Skin gray as rubbed ash. Lips sealed against waste. Eyes filmed with a clear mineral membrane. Hair grown into ropes stiffened by salt and dust until it resembles root or fiber or old rope.

They breathe a few times a day.

Their speech, if it still exists, cannot be made of sound. A spoken word would fall uselessly from the mouth as a pebble. Instead they speak by placement. A tool shifted half a finger-width. A charm turned to face a different shadow. A line scratched beside an older line after three generations of consideration. Their conversations are visible in the interiors of shelters: arrangements of awls, bowls, bones, feathers, black stones, reed bundles, finger marks on powdered floors.

They do not appear to farm plants. They cultivate conditions.

On terraces where no water has ever gathered, they set seedlike stones in shallow depressions and train shadows over them. They harvest crusts when the crusts have thickened enough to remember being touched. They tend black tufts that grow in rings, trimming nothing, watering nothing, waiting until a ring reaches the correct dryness. They keep herds, perhaps, though ownership there cannot be separated from proximity. A family may spend a thousand years beside the same standing animals, taking from them dust, shed plates, silent permission.

Their tools are made from bone, salt-hardened hide, chipped glass, and patience. A blade may be resharpened once in a century. A scraper may pass across a stretched hide so slowly that the hide changes species before the work is done. Their bowls are polished by use though they have never held liquid. They hold powders: mineral meal, ground shell from creatures without seas, ash from fires that may have burned before flame was forbidden.

They remember fire, but they do not keep it.

Fire is too hasty. It eats without ceremony. It changes things in a visible way. The plateau tolerates the idea of fire only as ash, charcoal, arrangement, memory. In the shelters, hearth circles remain carefully maintained, swept and ordered, as if the people still gather around warmth that occurred once and has not entirely finished occurring.

Their dead are not buried.

Burial would imply below, and below is not reliable there. Instead, the dead are seated, wrapped, given tools, and allowed to continue their participation. A corpse may become an ancestor, then a marker, then a mineral node, then perhaps something like a person again if enough attention gathers around it. The living do not distinguish sharply between elder, mummy, statue, and witness. All are forms of remaining.

Their children are rare and solemn. They are born small, sealed, quiet. A newborn does not cry; it opens its eyes and begins conserving itself. Its first lesson is stillness. Its second is orientation. Its third is how to recognize the difference between a shadow that belongs to an object, a shadow that belongs to the past, and a shadow that has come loose and must not be stepped through.

But even the almost-humans are not the masters of the plateau.

The true inhabitants are the relations between things.

The bite that has not closed yet lives there. The wingbeat that has not completed lives there. The tool-stroke crossing the hide lives there. The footprint waiting ahead of the foot lives there. The future bones stacked beside the carcass live there. The upward shadows live there too, blue and transparent, rising from stones like unfinished thoughts. They have their own migrations. They attach to animals for a while, then detach and remain after the bodies have moved on by fractions too small to see.

Some shadows are predators. Some are ancestors. Some are weather that never arrived.

The salt crusts are alive in another register. They spread over the ground like maps trying to remember the country underneath. Rust, violet, chalk-blue, black-green: each color is a different appetite. They avoid certain living shadows, embrace certain bones, climb abandoned tools, seal eggs, outline old tracks. They are slow communal skins, neither plant nor fungus nor mineral, but agreements between chemistry and persistence.

The glass reeds are alive mostly as listening devices. They grow where cracks open backward into moments. Their roots feed on exposed time. Their translucent stems store faint vibrations: the movement of a hand placing a stone, the wing of a bird advancing a feather-width, the click of a beetle changing its place in the colony. If many reeds stand together, they form an archive. The chime imagined around them is not imagined. It is the sound of old actions becoming thin enough to hear.

At the center basin, where every creature faces inward, something else lives.

Not the black glass hand-tool itself, though it is the focus. Not the three stones, though they have endured beyond architecture. Not an invisible god, exactly. What lives there is a completed attention.

The animals gathered for an event and never ceased witnessing it. The almost-humans, if they were involved, left no body, no inscription, no claim. The tool rests beneath the capstone as if set down a moment ago by a hand that understood how to make, cut, divide, shape, kill, or begin. Around it the salt cracked in rings, and the rings never healed. Every eye, every sealed shell, every antler, every hanging wing, every slow unborn thing in its egg remains turned toward that center.

The basin is a mind made out of witnesses.

No single creature comprehends it. The comprehension is distributed across them all. The beetles hold its pattern. The birds hold its height. The grazers hold its resonance. The watchers hold its era. The antlered herds hold its boundary. The human-shaped figures hold its gesture. The shadows hold what happened just before and just after. Together they sustain the event, not as memory, but as an ongoing condition.

That is what lives on the plain: not life opposed to death, but life after opposition has worn out.

A crowded stillness.

A civilization of breathless intervals.

An ecology where every creature is partly animal, partly relic, partly instrument, partly witness. Where hunger has become geometry, sleep has become posture, and memory has become a physical substance laid over the land grain by grain.

They are not waiting for the world to change.

They are the form change takes when it becomes almost too old to move.
