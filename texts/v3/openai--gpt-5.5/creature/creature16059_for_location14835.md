What lives there is not a population so much as a condition of life that has taken the shape of a landscape.

It may be a single organism. Or it may be many organisms that have forgotten the difference between themselves. There are no trunks with separate crowns, no individuals standing apart in competition. The whole basin is one joined body: root, leaf, stomach, reservoir, scaffold, lung. What looks like ground is its back. What looks like architecture is its posture. What looks like weather moving through the place is partly the breath of the thing that made the place.

It is a plant, if plant is allowed to mean something that builds terrain.

It does not live on the plateau. It is the plateau.

Its oldest tissue is probably buried below the visible floor, sunk into whatever mountain once existed here before being covered, sealed, and replaced. Down there are the first anchors: dark roots driven into cracks in stone, mineral-drinking cords, old compressed mats of rind and fiber that no longer resemble anything green. Those lower tissues grip the mountain and sip from it. They take iron, copper, salts, dissolved stone, the faint nutrients of rainwater and cloudwater. Above them, layer after layer of younger growth has swollen outward until the organism has made itself a suspended plain.

The creature is broad rather than tall in its thinking. Height is useful only where it gathers mist, vents heat, or redirects water. It does not reach upward in the way trees do, with separate ambitions for light. It spreads, seals, thickens, bridges, contains. Its primary instinct is not flowering, not shade, not fruit. Its instinct is hydraulics.

It wants to manage water.

Every wall, terrace, chimney, lip, basin, bladder, membrane, and causeway is an attempt to solve the same changing problem: how to catch cloud, drink rain, survive flood, store pressure, release heat, and keep its own vast wet body from tearing apart.

Its skin is not bark exactly. In some places it is tough, green-black, and hornlike, made to be walked on by nothing, made simply to bear its own weight. In other places it is soft and glossy, recently raised, still tender enough that the pale tissue underneath shows through in bruised colors. Its seams are not cracks but intentions. A yellow line along a plaza may become a gutter by morning. A swollen rim around a pool may become a dam. A wound may become a hinge.

It has no face. It has many mouths.

They open as pores in the ground, as long vents in warm flats, as round holes in the sides of chimneys, as soft-lipped cisterns that contract when rain strikes them too hard. Some mouths drink. Some exhale. Some taste the water before allowing it inward. Some close against dilution. Some open only during storms, when pressure inside the body rises and the whole plateau must breathe or burst.

The root chimneys are among its most visible organs. They stand in clusters, hollow and reedlike, though each one is thicker than a tree. Their walls are veined with channels carrying warm water upward and cooler condensed water down. When vapor passes through them they hum. The notes change with pressure, with temperature, with the fullness of hidden chambers below. At times an entire field of chimneys sings in staggered pulses, and that sound is not communication in any animal sense, but neither is it meaningless. It is the audible form of regulation. The organism is balancing itself.

The translucent hanging membranes are another kind of organ. They are cloud nets, weather leaves, walls that feed. They do not merely photosynthesize, though they do that too when the light strengthens. Their main work is to harvest the air. Mist gathers on them in trembling films, runs into hair-fine veins, and is carried down into thicker channels. When clouds move through the basin, these membranes darken and grow heavy, bowing between pillars like wet sails. Afterward they lighten again, tightening as they empty.

In younger districts, the membranes are experimental. Some tear under their own burden. Some are absorbed after a single storm. Some thicken into permanent walls and begin to grow pores. Others detach at the lower edge and curl into tubes, becoming new conduits. The organism does not design in advance. It tries forms with its body. Failure is not waste; failed structures become feedstock, buttress, dam, scar, instruction.

Its memory is structural.

Where water has cut too quickly, the tissue hardens into fluted chutes. Where pools have burst their rims, future rims grow thicker. Where a bridge failed to anchor, the next exploratory root curls differently, or does not cross there at all. Where vapor lingers, membranes recur. Where heat accumulates, chimneys rise. The creature remembers by changing the shape of itself.

It has no nerves like an animal, yet it is sensitive everywhere. Pressure moves through it. Light stains it. Dissolved minerals alter its growth. Heat makes decisions in it. Touch matters too, but slowly. A footstep is not a threat. It is a small compression, a darkening of pores, a brief displacement of surface water. Stand long enough and the tissue around the pressure may stiffen, loosen, drain, or swell. It does not notice you as a mind notices. It incorporates the event into gradients.

Its senses are distributed:

- pressure in buried water chambers;
- tension in root mats;
- acidity in pools;
- mineral taste in seepage;
- warmth held after rain;
- the weight of mist on membranes;
- light passing through translucent tissue;
- the pull of gravity on every sagging bridge and swollen wall.

These are its weather, its appetite, its map.

The heat rising from below is partly its own metabolism. Deep in the living platform are digestion chambers where old tissue is softened, fermented, and drawn back into use. Fallen walls do not rot in the open because the organism does not abandon them long enough. It melts them from within, threads them with pale roots, drinks them, reassigns them. Death inside this body is not an ending. It is local liquidity.

There are likely other lives inside it, but they are not inhabitants in the ordinary sense. Bacteria, yeasts, algae-like films, mineral-fixing colonies, fungal threads so thoroughly braided into the plant that they are less guests than organs. They live in warm cisterns, in fermenting chambers, in the copper-green water under the rind. They sour and sweeten the internal pools. They help break down fallen cellulose. They make heat. They free minerals. They may be part of why the air smells of crushed leaves and wet sweetness beginning to turn.

If the plateau is one body, these smaller lives are its gut-flora, its blood chemistry, its hidden choir.

Nothing with legs lives there. Nothing needs to flee. Nothing grazes the new tissue or pierces the bladders for food. No insect lays eggs in the seams. No bird carries seeds from tower to tower. Because of that absence, the organism has grown without defenses meant for mouths. It has no thorns, no bitter warning colors, no quick-closing traps. Its violence is architectural. It crushes by swelling. It drowns by overflow. It splits without regard for what stands nearby. It is dangerous the way weather and construction are dangerous, not the way a predator is dangerous.

Its reproduction, if that word applies, is probably not floral.

The bursting pods are not fruit in the familiar sense. They are pressure bladders, overflow hearts, temporary reservoirs. They inflate to store water or gas, then rupture when their work is done. The opaque yellow suspension in pools is not pollen meant for bees or windborne courtship. It is more likely living slurry: cells, spores, fragments of growth tissue, chemical instructions suspended in warm water. When basins overflow, that slurry runs into channels. When channels break, it seeps into new seams. When mats lift and tear free, they may carry the organism’s future elsewhere.

Perhaps the plateau spreads by collapse.

At the rim, when terraces grow too heavy with rain, sheets of living tissue may shear off and slide down the mountain, still alive, still wet, trailing root cords behind them. Most would die. Some would lodge in gullies, seal themselves around stones, drink fog, and begin again as slick green lips on bare rock. A century later those lips might become a hanging mat. Later still, a basin. Not a seed falling, but a piece of ground deciding to continue.

Or perhaps the whole high basin is itself a juvenile stage. A larval city of plant flesh. It is not trying to become a forest. It is trying to become a single stable organ large enough to hold a climate. The rawness, the constant revision, the walls that split and the bridges that curl back may be signs not of confusion but of adolescence. It has grown too quickly into a shape it cannot yet maintain.

Its towers are not monuments, though they look like monuments. They are balances struck between need and excess. A crescent wall may be a condenser first, then a spillway, then a brace, then a failed brace, then a reservoir rim. A long raised spine may carry water across the basin for several seasons before sagging into a chain of pools. A dome may form over a warm chamber, collapse, and become a nursery for floating mats. Purpose is temporary here. Function migrates through form.

The thing living there is therefore not a builder separate from its buildings.

It is a builder made of buildings.

It constructs by growing surfaces that can later become organs. It experiments with walls, not thoughts. It solves problems by thickening, venting, sealing, splitting, drinking, drowning, and reabsorbing. It has no plan of a city, yet it makes avenues because water must move. It has no idea of rooms, yet it makes chambers because pressure needs containment. It has no ritual, yet it produces towers, plazas, cisterns, curtains, bridges, terraces, and spires with a grandeur that resembles intention.

If it has a will, the will is this:

Hold the water.  
Move the water.  
Become more surface.  
Do not let death leave the body.  
Rise where vapor gathers.  
Harden where flow cuts.  
Open where heat builds.  
Close where the body leaks.  
Use everything.

It is not peaceful. It is simply unopposed by animals. Its urgency is inward, material, continuous. It does not sleep at night. Darkness changes its rate, its pressures, its cooling membranes, but not its work. In the dark it exhales the heat of day. Pools smoke. Root pipes knock. Tender buttresses extend blindly into wet air and harden under falling condensation. A wall that seemed permanent at dusk may split before morning, and by noon the split may have become the beginning of a canal.

To encounter it is to stand on a creature too large to recognize encounter. It does not welcome or reject. It adjusts. Your presence is less important than a passing storm, less nourishing than a rotting buttress, less informative than a new leak in the rim. Yet if you remain, the world around you will alter. Water will reroute around your boots. Pores will open near the warmth of your body. A membrane may tilt and drip steadily onto the place where you stood. Not because it knows you. Because you have become one more condition in its field of growth.

The inhabitant is vast, young, wet, and unfinished.

A cloud-eating plantland.  
A self-digesting garden without gardeners.  
A city whose citizens are tissues.  
A body practicing geography.
