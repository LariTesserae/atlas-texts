What lives there is not a fauna but a standing chemistry.

The inhabitants of the plateau are rooted, mineral, patient organisms—plantlike only because they rise from the ground and hold themselves toward the sun. They are not soft. They do not contain greenness as a wet world would understand it. Their bodies are pipes, vanes, ribs, condensers, heat sinks, batteries, archives. They live by arranging matter.

Their roots go down not in search of water, but of difference: warmer stone against cooler stone, charged ore against neutral dust, sulfurous breath against alkali crust, faint gas against thinner air. A root here is not a thirsty thread. It is a ceramic probe, a braided filament of carbon-dark fiber and mineral resin, widening into seams, splitting into conductive hairs, plating itself with metals as it grows. Some roots enter cracks no wider than a fingernail and spend decades following them downward. Others spread just beneath the surface in wide rigid mats, reading the plateau through pressure and heat.

Above ground, the organisms declare what they have found below.

Where the fissures breathe regularly, there are reed-forests: tall hollow columns in rows and clumps, ribbed and fluted, their surfaces waxy with ash-white deposits and blue-green corrosion. Warm gases pass through them. Inside, their walls are furred with crystalline organs, each layer tuned to a different element. They take in vapor, strip it, fix it, alter it, and release what they cannot use in pale sighs at their crowns. They are alive as a lung is alive, but they do not inhale from need. They wait for the earth to exhale.

Where the ground carries charge, black-leaved structures grow in racks and fans. Their leaves are flat, matte, and severe, as if cut from cooled slag. They turn by fractions through the day, not to follow light exactly, but to balance heat, electrical tension, and the slow migration of minerals through their stems. At noon they are almost still. Near sunset, when the rocks begin giving back the day’s stored warmth, thin lines of blue and violet travel through their veins. This is not display. Nothing watches it. It is circulation.

The glass grasses are the smallest of the obvious inhabitants, though some fields of them cover miles. Each strand is a transparent tube, hard and dry, with a mineral fan at its tip. They grow so densely that from afar they resemble fur on the land, but close up they are more like an array of instruments. Their tips open at sunrise to measure light, static, and airborne salts. They do not sway unless struck. They do not whisper except when crystals form along their edges and fracture in the heat. A field of them may spend a century advancing three meters toward a richer seam.

There are solitary beings too.

On the salt flats stand the tall black stalks, twenty meters high and barely thicker than a mast. They look impossible because nothing visible supports them. Their roots are deep needles descending through laminated crust into buried fractures. Their bodies are dark to gather heat, glossy in some bands, pitted in others, with small closed nodes arranged in vertical spirals. Once every few years, when ground charge and temperature align, a node opens and drops a single bead. The bead falls straight down, sinks a little into the salt, and waits. It may wait longer than a civilization would last elsewhere. It is not dead while it waits. It is an unopened instruction.

In the high nickel basins live the extravagant ones: amber-edged fans rising in dense ranks, interlocking until the groves become dry cathedrals. They are the closest thing the plateau has to abundance. Their blades are translucent at the margins and dark at the center, layered with metallic veins. They catch sunlight, not as leaves do, but as panels do—absorbing, storing, distributing. Under them lies a floor of shed scales, brittle and charged, each scale carrying a faint chemical memory of the blade that released it. The grove grows upward by lamination and downward by inheritance. It remembers through deposits.

None of these beings hurry.

Their movements are calibrations: a plate tilting one degree over a week, a valve tightening through an afternoon, a fan opening by the width of a hair as the air thins. They have no muscles. They have no nerves in the animal sense. Their awareness, if it can be called awareness, is a body-wide sensitivity to gradients. Heat is information. Pressure is information. Charge is information. The faint presence of a gas is information. A crack opening in stone is an event as loud to them as thunder would be to an animal.

They do not compete by chasing, poisoning, flowering, or devouring. They compete by placement and endurance. One grove may shade a seam of conductive ore for two hundred years, denying it to another. A field of glass grass may thicken its root mat until gas from below is diverted sideways, feeding a different colony at the edge. A condenser-shrub may alter the local chemistry so thoroughly that only its own fallen beads can germinate there. But none of this looks like struggle. It looks like standing.

Their reproduction is heavy and exact.

No wing carries their future. No insect visits them. No fruit sweetens. Seed bodies form as mineral beads, dense as shot, sometimes metallic, sometimes ceramic, sometimes glass-clear with a dark nucleus suspended inside. When mature, they drop. If the ground beneath them is wrong, they remain unchanged. If a crack opens, or a vent warms, or a charge passes through the basin after many years, they wake in place. Germination is not sprouting but anchoring: a bead splits, extends a probe downward, and begins to build a body around the conditions it has found.

Some release dust instead of beads, but even that dust does not drift far. It lifts only when heated sharply or startled by static discharge. The particles rise in brief veils and settle nearby, adhering to stone, stem, or salt. Most never become anything. Waste is accepted because motion is too expensive to avoid it. The plateau does not spend life on distance.

Their deaths are equally dry.

They do not rot. No mouths come to open them. No larvae enter. No fungus blooms in the softened interior. A dead reed becomes brittle from within, then splits along its ribs. A fan delaminates into scales. A black leaf curls only slightly before shattering into matte flakes. Their remains fall and become strata: fiber, resin, oxide, salt, carbon, trace metal. The living roots beneath them read these layers and reclaim them slowly. Death here is not decay but filing.

If there is a community, it is not social. It is infrastructural.

Each organism alters the ground for those near it. Reed-groves filter gases before they reach the air. Fan-forests trap heat and hold charge after sunset. Glass grasses bind dust and prevent salts from shifting. Tubular shrubs deposit minerals in ridges around their roots, building low terraces over generations. The landscape is being written by their metabolism, but so slowly that it still appears unused. They are not covering the plateau. They are teaching it how to have surfaces.

They have no fear, because nothing hunts them.

They have no calls, because nothing needs summoning.

They have no blossoms, because no one is invited.

They have no eyes, but they know the sun by heat and angle. They have no ears, but they know the earth by vibration. They have no tongues, but they taste through every buried filament. They have no memory as an animal has memory, yet their bodies preserve every season they have endured: rings of deposition, shifted mineral ratios, scars from static burns, sealed fractures from old expansion, bands of unusual sulfur from a venting year centuries past.

They are the plateau’s first citizens, though they possess no citizenship. They are its instruments, though no one made them. They are its industry, though no labor occurs. They live in the long tense of stone: not inert, not asleep, not waiting for animals to arrive and make the place complete.

They are complete already.

At night they stand under the hard stars with warmth stored inside them. Charge moves faintly through their trunks. Valves close. Inner walls precipitate new layers. Roots lengthen into microscopic cracks. Beads lie in salt, unbroken. Fields hold their geometry. The whole plateau, seemingly silent, is occupied by lives too slow to startle and too dry to wither—lives that have made patience into anatomy.
