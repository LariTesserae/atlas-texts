What lives there is less a population than a compression.

No creature in the hollow has much room to become large, extravagant, or careless. Everything has been made narrow by the place: narrow wings, narrow hooves, narrow nests, narrow lives fitted into cracks and ledges and damp seams. The inhabitants are not soft-valley animals that arrived by mistake. They are creatures of interruption and edge, born to live where the mountain is still unfinished.

The birds are the most visible, though even they vanish when still.

They are small cliff-birds, soot-dark on the back and throat, paler underneath where the cold light catches them. Their feathers lie close to the body, oiled by mist, and their wings are long enough for sudden gliding but not for generous flight. They move like sparks between ledges: one instant part of the rock, the next a black flick thrown across the hollow. Their feet are oversized for their bodies, with pale gripping toes and curved nails that fit into fractures too small to see.

Their eyes give them their unsettling look. Each bird has the ordinary two, glossy and side-set, but around them are pale speckles and dark feather-spots that make a clustered mask, so that a crowded ledge seems to be staring with hundreds more eyes than there are heads. When they turn toward a sound, they do so together. The motion passes over the colony like wind over water.

They nest wherever the mountain permits it. A hollow under a shard of stone. A pocket of dust behind a ridge of shale. A ledge scarcely wider than a palm. They make no beautiful nests, because beauty would blow away. Instead they press their bodies into grit and old down until the place remembers their shape. Eggs are laid in twos, seldom more, stone-gray and pointed at one end so they roll in circles rather than off the ledge. Chicks hatch dark-skinned, blind, and furious, with translucent throats that show the passage of food.

They eat midges, beetles, larvae, each other’s dead, and anything small enough to be swallowed before it freezes or escapes. They are not cruel, but nothing here can afford sentiment. A fallen chick feeds a dozen mouths by evening. A broken egg becomes mineral and protein for the ledge below. Their calls are sharp, metallic ticks and struck-stone cries, but beneath those are quieter notes: throat-clicks, beak-taps, wing-shivers against the rock. Much of their speech is felt through feet.

They know the cleft.

Before rain comes, the birds nearest the split grow silent first. Then the silence climbs the walls. Heads tilt. Wings tighten. The whole rookery listens to the low vibration inside the mountain, and when the tone changes they shift eggs inward, crowd chicks under bodies, and abandon exposed perches before the first sideways needles of rain enter the hollow.

Among the birds move the horned little mammals.

They are not goats, though a lowlander would call them goats at first and then hesitate. They are about the size of lean dogs, compact and deep-chested, with long blunt faces and dark nostrils always wet from stone-breath. Their coats are silver-gray, not from age but from hollow light and condensation: each hair pale at the tip, charcoal beneath. When they stand motionless against the walls they seem like damp projections of the rock itself.

Their horns are short, backward-curving, polished at the tips from scraping under overhangs. Their hooves are split and soft-edged, able to spread on slanting stone. They do not leap recklessly. They place themselves. A forehoof tests. Weight lowers. The rear follows. Their whole intelligence is in balance, patience, and refusal to hurry.

They feed on what the hollow gives grudgingly: moss pads, pale lichen crust, mineral salts, green film from wet stone, the occasional fallen bird or egg when hunger becomes too clean and sharp to ignore. Their tongues are rough, dark, and strong. The sound of them licking the walls is one of the hollow’s constant labors: rasp, pause, rasp, breath.

The tool-use among them is almost accidental, but repeated enough to be tradition. A mammal finds a shard of shale narrow enough to hold between lip and tooth. It worries the edge beneath a lichen crust, pries, eats, drops the stone. Later another finds the same kind of shard and does the same. No one keeps tools. Nothing is owned. But certain ledges glitter with flakes used again and again, as if the mountain has grown its own rough utensils.

They come to the central cleft singly.

Never in herds. Never hurried. One will pick its way down from a high shelf and stand before the split with its muzzle almost touching the black line. Its eyes half close. Its ears loosen. Frost dampens its whiskers. It may remain there for an hour, breathing the cold air that issues from below. Sometimes it hums back, so low the sound is more in the chest than the ear. Then it leaves, and another comes later.

Whether they worship, listen, smell minerals, or take comfort from the constant underground breath is impossible to say. But the cleft is important to them in a way food is not. Food they compete over. The cleft they approach with a kind of order.

The insects are the true majority.

The hollow belongs to them in all the places larger life cannot enter. Beneath feathers. Under stones. Inside dung. In the wet black line between two slabs. In the moss so dense it seems like a single old animal sleeping.

There are ash-colored crawlers with many joints and no wings, soft as damp paper until touched, when they fold into hard angular knots like chips of stone. They live under the bird ledges and eat feather-fall, mites, droppings, eggshell, and the bodies of their own dead. They are the cleaners of the rookery, and without them the ledges would sour.

There are glass midges whose bodies are almost transparent except for a dark green thread running through them. They hatch from the persistent pools whenever the water warms by even a fraction. They rise in vertical columns, hovering without purpose until a bird cuts through them and leaves the swarm briefly shaped like the passage of its wings. Their larvae hang beneath the water surface like commas, filtering the faint living sheen that grows there.

There are beetles with shells so pale and thin that their organs show through as smoky blots. They graze the green films at pool edges, moving slowly, always in groups, leaving tiny cleared trails behind them. When threatened, they release bubbles from beneath their wing-cases and sink under the water skin, clinging to submerged stone until danger passes.

There are pale worms that braid together in wet cracks. They are nearly blind, almost rootlike, and when the cleft’s vibration deepens they extend themselves farther into the open, tasting the air with their whole bodies. The birds eat them when they can. The worms retreat when light touches them. At night they write and erase silver lines across the stone.

The smallest builders are the pool insects.

Their larvae arrange mineral grains along damp edges, making repeated crescent shapes no larger than a fingernail. These crescents are not nests exactly and not dams exactly. They catch condensation, slow the loss of water, and direct thin films toward the larvae’s resting places. A single crescent is nothing. Hundreds alter the margin of a pool. In this way, without planning, the insects make weather in miniature.

The plants, if they can be called plants without insult to their severity, are ancient-minded despite the young mountain.

The moss is dark, tight, and inward-growing. It does not spread like carpet. It thickens like memory. Each pad is a reservoir, a nursery, a trap for dust, a field for insects, a meal for the horned mammals, and a small argument against bare stone. Tear one open and the inside is warmer than the air, smelling faintly of iron, feathers, and rain.

The lichens are paler, almost lunar. They crust the undersides of boulders and the protected backs of slabs where light arrives diluted and water lingers. Some are powdery and flake at a touch. Some are leathery and cling so tightly they must be rasped away with tooth or shale. They are slow enough to seem unconscious, but the whole hollow leans on them. They begin the work of making the mountain edible.

The tiny alpine flowers are brief visitors from another logic. For a few days they appear in cracks: translucent petals, white-violet or greenish, trembling in wind too weak to move anything else. The birds sometimes pluck them. The insects enter them. Then they vanish, and the crack seems never to have opened.

But the deepest resident is not any one animal.

It is the life around the cleft: the green-black film, the cold breath, the trembling water, the organisms that answer vibration with light.

Down inside the split, beyond where bodies can enter, there is likely a hidden wetness: meltwater traveling through inner stone, mineral-rich, lightless, cold but not dead. Something grows there on iron, on dissolved minerals, on the slow chemistry of new rock. It comes upward as sheen, as smell, as faint color in the pools. The surface creatures feed on it without understanding. Beetles graze it. Larvae filter it. Moss drinks from it. Mammals lick it. Birds eat those that ate it. The whole crowded chamber may be built on that dark green breath from below.

At night, when the pools glow after disturbance, it is this hidden life briefly showing its alphabet. Not bright enough to illuminate the walls. Not warm enough to comfort. Just cold points and lines where something has been touched and has answered.

So what lives there is a vertical city with no builders.

A rookery, a mineral pasture, a nursery of insects, a garden of cracks, a throat of the mountain lined with listening bodies. The creatures do not own the hollow. They are fitted into it the way water fits into fractures. They have made no monuments because the place permits none. Their marks are droppings, polish, shell fragments, grazed films, impact scars from dropped stones, crescents of grit around pools, and ledges worn warm by generations of feet.

They are all young in evolutionary courage and old in behavior. They know how to wait. They know how to spend nothing unnecessarily. They know that warmth is communal, water is temporary, and open space is another name for falling.

Individually, they are birds, mammals, insects, mosses, films, worms.

Together, they are the hollow’s answer to being broken open.
