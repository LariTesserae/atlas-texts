What lives there is not one creature but a civilization of smallness.

The plateau belongs, first, to the patient-bodied things: animals that have accepted stone as climate, shelter, calendar, and god. Most of them are built low. They flatten themselves against the world. They keep their bellies near stored heat and their backs near danger. They do not leap if they can creep, do not call if they can signal by scent, do not expose a whole body when an eye, a feeler, or a tongue will do.

There are beetles everywhere.

Not merely many beetles, but kinds of beetles so various they seem to have divided the plateau among themselves by texture. Some are black and polished like drops of tar, moving with a solemn, jointed certainty over the slabs. Some are pale and dust-furred, appearing already half-eroded. Some have wing cases ridged like dry seedpods; others are smooth, round, and sealed, able to pull in their legs and become pebbles when touched. Their undersides are crowded with hooks and brushes for holding to stone during wind. Many feed on lichens, scraping color from rock with minute mouthparts. Others eat seeds. Others eat the eaters of seeds. Some live almost entirely inside old termite galleries, blind and amber, attended by mites like moving pollen.

A few of the largest beetles emerge only after rain. They come from beneath slabs while the pools are still filling, their bodies so dark and wet they seem made from the first night after the storm. They fly heavily between basins, blundering but exact, landing in water with a slap. Their larvae are aquatic knives, transparent except for the black thread of the gut, feeding with urgency because the pool is already dying.

The termites are the plateau’s masons.

Their towers rise from the flatter places where dust has accumulated: fluted pale chimneys, warm to the touch, veined within by passages narrower than a fingernail. The towers look ruined because they are always being repaired. Rain softens their walls; wind abrades them; birds pick them open; lizards wait at the wounds. The termites answer without drama. They build at night, carrying chewed mineral, dry grass, fungal paste, and their own bodies’ moisture. Their queens are hidden deep under stone, swollen and pale, tended in dark chambers that smell of clay, rot, and breath.

Around the towers live whole dependent nations. Silverfish-like scavengers slide through the outer cracks. Tiny roaches graze on fungal bloom. Predatory ants raid the galleries after storms, when softened walls can be opened. Certain lizards sleep in abandoned flues. Certain plants root preferentially beside the bases, feeding on the slow upward transport of minerals and dead insects. The towers are not landmarks to the plateau’s life; they are organs.

The ants are less architectural but more political.

They cross the ground in disciplined rivers, copper, black, rust-red, sometimes two colors braided together where rival columns meet and negotiate by violence. Their trails are older than individual colonies. They follow mineral seams, lichen margins, the shaded sides of stones. In the morning they gather dew from plant hairs and carry it in their bodies underground. By noon many vanish, leaving only a few sentries at cracks. At dusk they return, harvesting seeds, dead flies, caterpillars, termite eggs, fragments of bone, each particle becoming part of an underground arithmetic.

Some species are harvesters, keeping granaries in cool cavities beneath the rock. Some are raiders, almost nomadic, appearing in dense living spills that make beetles freeze and mammals climb. Some are minute and yellow, living inside the thorny shrubs, farming scale insects beneath bark so thin it seems unable to contain a world. There are ants that tend the eggs of other insects, ants that live by stealing larvae from larger ants, ants whose queens found colonies in the chambers of dead queens. The plateau is crossed with their decisions.

Above them, in the wiry shrubs, live the dry little birds.

They are not colorful at first glance. Their feathers are stone-gray, ocher, lichen-black, the dun of dust and old grass. But when they move, hidden markings appear: a rust flash beneath the wing, a pale throat, blue skin around the eye, a tail edged white like a chip of quartz. They run more often than they fly. Their feet are long-toed and sure on slabs. Their nests are set low, in thorn knots or under rock lips, lined with hair, seed floss, insect silk, and the brittle skeletons of grass.

They do not sing much because song is expensive and carries too far. Instead they communicate in fragments: a tick, a double-click, a low descending whistle, a warning note that is almost indistinguishable from a stone cracking in heat. They hunt constantly. One pries larvae from termite walls. One turns over flakes of stone with its beak. One hovers briefly above damp basins to take rising midges. Hook-beaked little hunters sit on the low rims and watch for lizards, nestlings, beetles, anything that makes the mistake of becoming separate from the ground.

There are also birds that arrive only when water comes.

They appear with the rain as if condensed from it: long-legged, narrow-billed, pale-bellied, restless. They walk the pool margins, stabbing at tadpoles and red crustaceans. They know basins separated by distances that seem too great for such brief water. Perhaps they remember gleams. Perhaps they smell mud waking. Perhaps their ancestors learned the map of the plateau when the basins were deeper and have never forgotten. They feed, call softly, mate if the season permits, and leave before the mud seals.

The lizards are the plateau’s visible thoughts.

At midday they seem to be the only vertebrates brave enough for the light. They lie on stone in perfect extension, rib cages pressed flat, toes spread, throats pulsing. Their skins mimic weathered surfaces: black stippling on honey rock, pale bands on dark slabs, greenish crust patterns where lichens grow thick. Some are thin, whiplike, and fast; others are broad-headed ambush hunters that wait beside insect trails with the patience of carved things.

They drink from dew by licking plant stems and from rain by lowering their heads to stone channels. Some store fat in their tails against long dry intervals. Some shed skins in tight rolls, which beetles later carry away in flakes. Their eggs are laid deep in dust-filled fissures, where temperature, not parental care, determines the future. Hatchlings emerge already knowing the laws: flatten, pause, run, vanish.

There are snakes, but they are seldom seen whole.

A watcher might notice only the brief rearrangement of shadow between stones, or a line passing through grass without moving the stems much. They are basalt-dark after rain, pale-banded in dust, narrow-headed, heat-wise. Some wait at pool edges for frogs and tadpoles grown reckless in their hurry. Some hunt lizards in cracks. Some enter burrows at night and come out heavier. Their scales hold the faint sheen of wet rock even in dryness. They do not waste motion. When they strike, the act is so fast it seems less like violence than correction.

The mammals are mostly nocturnal and careful.

They have large ears, fine whiskers, narrow muzzles, soft padded feet. By day they are folded into crevices, communal dens, or burrows beneath stone lips where the temperature changes slowly. At dusk they appear as tremors at the edge of sight. Some are seed-gatherers, carrying cheekfuls of hard little grains to underground caches. Some eat beetles, cracking shells with delicate, rapid bites. Some climb the shrubs to strip fruit no larger than beads. Their eyes catch starlight and disappear.

A few are pouch-bearing or pouch-like in habit, young clinging to the belly while the mother moves through rock lanes. Others make grass nests deep in old termite hollows. They breed when rains have produced enough seed and insect bloom; in lean years they simply do not. Their lives are measured by restraint. They may know every shaded crack within a hundred body-lengths, every basin that holds water an extra day, every owl perch, every snake-smelling passage.

There are predators above them, but even predators here seem lean and formal.

Small owls nest in cliff-splits and tower hollows. They are silent except for soft, hollow notes after moonrise. Their faces are pale disks, their bodies mottled with rock-shadow. They take moths, rodents, lizards, sometimes other birds. By morning their pellets lie beneath perches, tight gray bundles of fur, wing cases, bones already being opened by beetles.

There are day hunters too: narrow-winged raptors that use the plateau’s broad warm air, kestrel-like birds that hang over basins, hooked-beaked runners that hunt on foot. They do not rule by abundance. They rule by attention. The small animals know the shape of their shadows.

And then there are the pool-born.

They are not always present, yet the plateau is arranged around their return. Their lives are sealed in dust: eggs like grains, cysts like invisible seeds, embryos waiting in mud older than the shrubs around them. When rain fills a basin, the dead floor becomes a broth of beginning. Within hours the water trembles with transparent bodies.

Tiny crustaceans unfold red, branching limbs and swim upside down through greenish light. Tadpoles hatch from jelly strings hidden under crusted silt. Worms uncoil. Ciliated specks graze on bacterial clouds. Mosquito-like larvae hang from the surface skin. Predatory nymphs stalk among them with extendable jaws. Everything eats. Everything grows. There is no childhood, only acceleration. Some creatures hatch already carrying the next generation’s eggs. Some mature, mate, and die before the pool has lost half its depth.

Their urgency feeds the plateau. Birds arrive. Beetles arrive. Snakes arrive. The mammals come at night to drink and harvest the stranded. When the water shrinks, the mud thickens with bodies, eggs, shed skins, droppings, spores. The basin closes again, but not into emptiness. It closes like a fist around futures.

The plants, though not animals, are among the plateau’s oldest inhabitants in spirit.

They are not forest plants diminished by hardship; they are stone plants, built for delay. Cushion mats lie close enough to the rock to be mistaken for discoloration. Their leaves are felted, gray, and intricate, each hair catching vapor from night air. The shrubs grow in knotted geometries, all thorn and small leathery leaf, hosting nests and scale insects and spiders in their cramped interiors. Succulents occupy cracks with red stems and swollen joints, scarred from browsing, sun, and old drought. Lichens write the longest biographies, spreading by a width of dust each year, digesting stone with chemical patience. Mosses keep their greenness folded away until moisture unlocks them.

The spiders are everywhere once one learns how to see silk.

Some stretch almost invisible nets between grass stems, catching gnats rising from damp hollows. Some live in silk-lined doors set into banks, the doors coated with dust so perfectly that only the hinge betrays them. Some hunt without webs, flat and mottled, moving sideways under ledges. Their egg sacs are tucked into lichened cracks, guarded until heat or hunger ends the vigil. There are scorpions too, translucent amber or dark as burned wood, hiding beneath slabs by day, walking slowly at night with claws open and tails held like questions.

Many creatures here are less species than intervals.

They exist as sleep, then swarm, then sleep again. Eggs under salt crust. Seeds in ocher dust. Spores in lichen pores. Larvae in dry mud. Mites in bird nests waiting for warmth. Termite queens sealed below towers through seasons of failure. The plateau’s inhabitants have learned that absence is a valid phase of life. To not be visible is not to be gone.

The largest animal may be rare enough to seem legendary: a lean, long-legged grazer or browser that crosses the upland by ancient lanes, traveling between depressions after rain. It would be narrow-bodied, high-shouldered, with rubbery feet suited to stone and a muzzle made for thorn tips, cushion plants, salt lick, and damp mineral crust. It would move in small groups at dawn, ears turning constantly, dropping beadlike pellets that vanish under beetles by noon. Its paths would be among those polished grooves worn into saddles and rims. It would not stay anywhere long. Large life cannot linger here without becoming a feast.

What truly lives there, then, is an ecology of waiting sharpened into motion.

Everything has two selves: the hidden self and the sudden self. The dry self and the rain self. The stone-colored self and the glistening, breeding, feeding self. The plateau’s inhabitants are conservative with water, extravagant with eggs, quiet with sound, lavish with generations. They have made a crowded world without making much height, a city without walls except termite towers and rock cracks, a culture without intention except repeated thirst.

They are the children of brief water on ancient stone: beetle, ant, lizard, bird, root, spore, cyst, snake, mouse, moth, moss. They live as if the whole plateau were a sleeping animal whose skin they occupy, and every rain were not weather but permission.
