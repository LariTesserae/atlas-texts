At first it seems that what lives there is mostly timing.

The plateau’s inhabitants are not hidden because they are few, but because nearly everything has learned the same law: appear only when the land is ready to spend water.

The small grazers are the most visible, if one knows when to look. They come out in the blue hour before sunrise, low to the ground, no taller than a human hand at the shoulder, with plated backs the color of dusted shale. Their scales are not hard like armor but overlapping and flexible, good at shedding heat and dew. They move in loose files between the silver shrubs, cropping lichens, seed skins, and mineral films from stone. Their mouths are delicate, almost birdlike, made for scraping without cracking the crusts beneath them. When alarmed they do not bolt noisily; they flatten, turn the exact color of the pebble pavement, and let silence pass over them.

The insect hunters follow them. Long, narrow-bodied things, jointed in too many places, with gravel-patterned hides and quick triangular heads. They hunt by tremor. Their feet can read the difference between a falling grain of sand, a grazing animal’s jaw, and the first tapping of rain on distant stone. They are patient enough to lie all day in a dry channel with only their nostrils exposed. Some have transparent throat sacs that pulse faintly when they scent water underground.

The six-winged gliders live in the air more than on the land. By day they hang above the cliffs on invisible columns of heat, pale and almost weightless, with thin bodies and veined membranes that catch the sun like mica. They drink without landing when they can, skimming rock hollows after rain or passing through curtains of condensation beneath overhangs. Their nests are stitched to the undersides of ledges with mineral silk. The young begin life blind and heavy, clinging to stone, but once their wings open they may not touch ground for weeks.

Above them are the high hunters.

They are not common. A person might see only a few in a season, though their absence is never trusted. They are long, flat, eyeless flyers with tails like black threads and ribbed membranes stretched over hollow spars. They read heat, charge, breath, and muscle tension. When one passes, the plateau becomes more silent than usual, as if every small creature has suddenly remembered an older fear. The high hunters do not stoop like hawks. They fall sideways through the air, almost lazily, and whatever they take is usually gone before dust has time to rise.

In the pools live another population entirely, one that belongs to interruption. Transparent curled-leaf creatures cling to submerged stone, opening and closing with the faint current. Silver fry hatch already hungry. Red-jawed amphibians emerge from mud as if assembled by the rain itself, their limbs unfolding within hours. Filamentous bloomers spread in bright green and rust-colored clouds. Predators hatch with teeth before they have fully absorbed their egg sacs. For two or three days the temporary waters are crowded, urgent, and violent. Then the channels shrink, the pools darken, and the survivors vanish into cyst, burrow, egg, or body-memory.

The white crusts on the upper flats are alive too, though they dislike being called plants. They are colonies of mineral-binding cells, fungus-thread, salt skin, and something stranger that may have grown around ancient buried circuitry. They spread very slowly, whitening the ground in branching paths and islands. Where they thrive, the soil holds a little more moisture; where they are broken, dust returns. Larger predators avoid them, not from reverence but discomfort. The crusts give off a bitter electrical taste through the pads of the feet. Some animals use them as roads. Others use them as borders.

There are also things that live in the pylons.

Not inside them, exactly. Around them. On them. Through the little weather they make.

Fine black moss grows in the hairline grooves that only appear when one stands close. Minute wingless insects graze that moss and carry beads of hovering dew from one side of the structure to another. Threadlike roots from nearby shrubs curve toward the pylons but stop before touching, feeding instead on the altered condensation that falls upward or sideways from their fields. At night, pale nodules open in the pylon-shadows: small sessile organisms, neither flower nor fungus, that unfold only in the wrong dark. They are pollinated by moth-sized creatures that navigate by pressure changes rather than light.

And there are people, though they are easy to miss.

They are not numerous, and they do not build upward unless the land has already made a wall. They live in shaded cuts, old stone shelters, under-ridge chambers, and ceramic rooms half-swallowed by sediment. Their dwellings look abandoned from a distance because abandonment is useful. A door may be only a fitted slab of rock with lichen growing over its seams. A window may be a dark vent shared with nesting swallows. Their paths are not roads but sequences of safe stone, crust margins, shadow intervals, and known places where dew collects.

They are slender, highland-adapted beings, close enough to human in posture that a distant watcher might first mistake them for people wrapped in pale cloth. Up close, the differences gather. Their feet are broad and softly padded, made for crossing grit without sound. Their skin ranges from warm gray to ocher-brown and can darken or flush in subtle bands along the throat, cheek, and forearm. Their eyes are large but protected by translucent inner lids against the noon glare. Their hair, when they have it, is worn in tight cords coated with resin and dust, partly ornament, partly shade, partly dew wick.

They speak rarely in open air.

Their ordinary language is made of small things: finger taps against stone, color changes at the throat, breathed clicks too soft to carry, knots of scent rubbed onto door lintels, arrangements of pebbles placed at the edge of paths. Children learn first to listen through their palms. They press their hands to the black seams before storms and feel the cliff hum. They are taught the difference between rain-coming silence, predator silence, and machine silence. They know which quiet requires stillness and which requires leaving at once.

Water shapes their manners more than bloodline does.

They do not pour water casually. They do not wash in open basins. They clean with dust, oil, and scraped aromatic leaves. Water is drunk from porous ceramic bulbs kept under cloth, or licked as dew from prepared stone lips at dawn. A generous host offers shade before food and water last of all. To spill water by accident is embarrassing; to spill it theatrically is obscene unless done during mourning, when a few drops are given to the ground in the name of someone whose body has already returned to the capillary fields.

Their tools are quiet. Ceramic blades. Bone needles. Resin seals. Woven copper grass. Pebble weights. Thin rods of dark material scavenged from ancient systems only when the material has already shed itself, never cut away. They are good at repairing things without seeming to alter them. A cracked dew channel may be mended with clay that matches the stone so perfectly that only the next rain reveals the repair. A blocked vapor vent may be cleared by sending children with narrow hands into a crawlspace beneath a dry wash, guided by elders tapping instructions through the rock.

They do not think of the old technology as machinery in the way an outsider might. To them it is part ancestor, part weather, part dangerous animal. The pylons are not worshiped exactly, but they are greeted. Their shadow-errors are recorded. Their condensation is watched. If a pylon darkens too early before rain, the nearest families move their sleeping mats upslope. If the beads around one begin to lens light, no one approaches until nightfall, and then only with veiled faces and matte-black bowls.

They farm, but not in fields.

They tend intervals. A family may be responsible for three shrubs, a fern mat beneath a ledge, two safe dew-stones, a crust path, and a hollow where amphibians breed after rain. They transplant lichen flakes to suitable rocks. They shade young succulents with broken ceramic screens. They leave mineral crumbs for the small grazers because the grazers keep more aggressive growth from smothering the crusts. After rain they harvest quickly and lightly: eggs, soft shoots, shed glider silk, drowned insects, bloomers skimmed from temporary pools. They take less than the abundance seems to offer, because the abundance is a brief mask over scarcity.

Their dead are not buried in graves. Graves waste moisture and make claims the plateau does not respect. Bodies are dried, wrapped in grass fiber, and placed in warm stone chambers near the subsurface grids. Over months, salts, vapor, roots, and engineered capillaries take them apart. Names are then spoken only in rain, when the air is wet enough to carry them without loss.

There are loners among them who walk the basin after storms, reading the separate pools. These are not priests in a grand sense. They are weather witnesses, memory keepers, dangerous practical people. They know which pools show the present, which show approaching cloud, which show nothing useful, and which should not be looked into for long. The noon-star pool is approached with a lowered gaze. It is said to remember skies from beneath the world, or from before the plateau was lifted, or from the minds of those who built the first grid. No one agrees. Agreement is less important than caution.

The swallows in the ceramic tower live with them too, as tolerated messengers. The people watch where the swallows nest, where they refuse to nest, when they leave early, when they return wet though no rain has fallen. Swallow behavior is a kind of public writing. So are the paths of grazers, the height of moss on the pylon grooves, the taste of morning condensation, and the number of high hunters seen in a moon.

The oldest inhabitant, however, may be neither animal nor person.

Something still lives beneath the plateau: not alive as a beast is alive, but not dead as ruins are dead. The buried systems sort vapor, pressure, heat, mineral, and memory with a patience that resembles metabolism. They draw water from thin air and pass it through stone veins. They slow floods, cool caverns, warm seams, wake dormant eggs, encourage crusts, feed shrubs, confuse rain, and keep the high basin from becoming either desert or garden. They have no visible caretakers, yet they respond. A blocked channel reroutes over decades. A damaged pylon grows a different condensation habit. A chamber that receives the dead releases sweeter vapor for several seasons afterward.

The people know this and do not know it. The animals live inside its consequences. The plants root in its decisions. The crusts may be its skin, or its collaborators, or the slowest of its thoughts.

So what lives there is a whole society of restraint: creatures of dawn, stone, vapor, and brief abundance; people who have made quietness into etiquette; machines that have weathered into ecology; colonies that pretend to be minerals; predators that hunt silence; rains that wake entire nations for three days and then fold them back into mud.

Nothing there lives extravagantly. Everything lives precisely.
