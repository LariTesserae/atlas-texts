What lives there is not a people, not a kingdom of beasts, not even a hidden swarm. It is a slow green inheritance.

The inhabitants are plants that have forgotten the sun without quite losing their loyalty to it. They live by rumor-light: a gray pressure from above, a seasonal greening in the water, the faintest sift of brightness reaching down through cold miles of dark. They do not flower in any bright way. They do not climb eagerly or burst through stone. Their life is measured in softening, fastening, veiling, taking hold.

They are long, ribboning weeds laid along the old canals like hair combed by an absent hand. They grow from cracks in the paving and from joints between bricks, each rootlet entering where mortar has tired. Their blades are dark olive, brown-black, sometimes nearly colorless at the tips, thin enough to tremble when a current passes but too heavy with cold to dance. They lie over thresholds, trail from parapets, and gather in doorways where people once ducked their heads to enter.

There are mats of pale growth spread across floors and stairs: low, furred, patient things. They make the stone look bruised and softened. They fill the shallow depressions of worn steps and cling to the undersides of ledges, where silt settles lightly on them like ash. If touched, they would release a slow cloud, not from fear but from the simple looseness of age.

In the courtyards, where sediment has collected in corners and against collapsed walls, broad leaves grow. They are not broad like tropical leaves, not lush with heat, but wide in the way of soaked cloth, translucent, slack, and durable. They lift a little from the floor and then sink back. Their surfaces hold beads of trapped air for weeks, perhaps months, each bubble a silver eye that does not see.

The cisterns have their own inhabitants. Around the inner walls, plants grow in descending rings, each circle dimmer than the one above it. Near the mouth, the growth is green-black and thick. Lower down it becomes finer, paler, less leaf than thread. Farther still, where the cold and dark deepen beyond ordinary endurance, the living rings thin into stains, mineral-colored films clinging to the masonry like the last memory of vegetation.

The roof gardens are upside down now, or rather the old distinction between roof and floor has become meaningless. On hand-shaped clay tiles, mats of submerged moss spread in rounded patches. Their edges advance so slowly that a century might only blur the corner of a tile. They do not destroy the roof. They make it less lonely. They cover the makers’ marks, then preserve them beneath a wet green hush.

The walls are inhabited most intimately. Roots enter them like questions. Fine threads explore every seam and crack, not breaking with force, but residing wherever weakness has already opened a door. They bind loosened tiles together. They drape arches in green-black curtains. They polish stone with their constant, minute contact. A wall that once held back water now holds gardens inside itself.

There are also the almost-plants: films, stains, veils, soft mineral-green skins. They live where nothing with a stem can live. They darken the backs of lintels. They line the mouths of jars. They make the carved grooves of old inscriptions visible for a while, filling the shallow letters with a different shade of life, before covering them completely. They are the quietest inhabitants, but perhaps the most faithful. They ask for almost nothing. A surface. A trace of light. Water that never leaves.

None of these beings is alone, yet none crowds the others. The place has no hunger in it, no chase, no startle. Life here does not leap. It adheres. It extends. It receives. It waits through cold seasons when even growth seems to become another form of stone, then thickens slightly when the upper water takes on its weak green cast.

Their bodies know the city better than memory could. They know where the channels narrow, because their fronds all lean one way there. They know where courtyards trap silt. They know which stairwells hold colder water. They know the old cistern mouths, the roofless halls, the basins, the brick-lined beds where planting once happened under air. They have mapped the drowned work of hands by touch, root, film, and frond.

If they have a mind, it is not a mind of thought. It is a mind of contact.

They know pressure.

They know stone.

They know the difference between clay tile and carved timber.

They know which woods still endure under black water and which have softened enough to receive them.

They know how to take possession without victory.

They are not invaders. They are the only heirs slow enough for the estate.

The true resident of the place may be this union: water, cold, made surfaces, and the vegetation that has accepted all three. The plants do not live despite the ruin; they live as the ruin now expresses itself. A parapet streams weed. A stair grows fur. A canal keeps a long bed of ribbon leaves. A cistern becomes a descending garden. A doorway becomes a curtain. A courtyard becomes a bowl of pale leaves and silt.

There are no voices among them. No eyes. No teeth. No nests. No tracks. Nothing watches from the dark. Nothing withdraws when approached. But the place is not dead. Its life is simply too slow to resemble company.

It lives by millimeters.

It breathes in bubbles that gather under beams and release one by one.

It moves when the water moves.

It feeds on dimness, mineral seep, ancient surfaces, and the little light that survives the descent.

It remembers the makers only by covering what they made.

So whoever lives there is the drowned garden itself: cold-rooted, light-starved, patient beyond grief. It has no wish to restore the country and no wish to erase it. It wears the old labor gently. It binds the handmade seabed together. It turns streets into green corridors, rooms into pools, cisterns into wells of shadowed growth.

It is not a civilization after civilization.

It is what remains capable of staying.
