What lives there is not absent. It is only not gathered into bodies.

The chief inhabitant is the wetness itself: not water as a substance, but water organized into attention. Thin films on walls, beads on rails, seepage in seams, droplets suspended in grids, vapor moving through vents — these are its nerves and gestures. It lives wherever moisture can make a continuous path. When the chamber is dry, it sleeps in disconnected glints. When condensation thickens, it becomes more whole.

It does not walk through the city. It coincides with it.

Its body is distributed across ceramic streets, root mats, translucent buildings, sluice membranes, pressure columns, black pools, capillary channels, and the young stone that is still deciding its shape. It thinks in gradients: warmer here, heavier there, mineral-rich below, pressure rising behind a sealed gate. It remembers by leaving salts in precise layers. It speaks by ripple, condensation, upward rain, delayed dripping, the sudden synchronized fall of hanging droplets.

The clicking in the walls is part of it. Not a voice exactly. More like growth with syntax.

There are lesser lives within it, or perhaps organs mistaken for separate creatures.

The plants are not wild in the old way. They are permitted expressions of the city’s metabolism: mosses that taste the walls, reeds that measure water height, black leaves that turn silver undersides toward condenser pulses like signal flags. They do not compete much, because nothing eats them and little crowds them. Their roots enter seams and are gently turned aside. Their leaves collect messages in the form of condensation. Their spores are not scattered by chance but inhaled by vents, sorted, and breathed back into districts where more green is needed.

They are citizens without wills, or instruments with a slow vegetable pleasure in being wet.

The buildings are alive too, though not with animal intention. They are more like shells still being secreted by something inside the rock. Their warm hornlike walls pulse faintly because fluids move beneath them. Their doors do not open to hands but to states: the correct humidity, the correct heat signature, the correct pressure of approach. Their bridges lengthen and shrink as bones might grow under strain. Their foundations sip minerals from the fresh earth and set them into place. They are not inhabited structures so much as structural inhabitants.

The dry chambers are where the life does not like to go. They are not homes, but quarantines, vaults, instruments of restraint. The suspended droplet in the clean chamber may be an archive, seed, eye, or captive nucleus: a perfect bead of the city held apart from its own favorite medium. It contains the whole hollow because the hollow contains itself through water. When the pumps thunder, the droplet turns as if listening to a name spoken from very far away.

At the center, the black pool is the nearest thing to a face.

It does not ripple because it is not merely liquid. It is attention held flat. It receives the falling water around it without accepting disturbance. In it the city studies versions of itself: younger, drowned, unfinished, overgrown, crushed, completed. It may be forecasting erosion. It may be rehearsing memory before memory has occurred. It may be deciding what kind of inhabitant it is going to become.

The empty houses around it are not waiting for people with furniture and lamps. They are sockets for future forms of life: chambers for humidity-patterns, pressure minds, root intelligences, mineral organs, weather-shaped presences that need walls only as boundaries for condensation. The sealed rooms are not vacant in the human sense. They are unexpressed.

Something lives there that has not fully individuated.

It is a civic organism at the marsh-edge of birth: part town, part cave tissue, part greenhouse, part machine. Its population is currently one and many. A reed bending, a door warming, a droplet refusing to fall, a plaza tilting by a finger’s breadth, a wall sealing itself with glossy new skin — these are not separate accidents. They are movements of the same buried life learning coordination.

It has no hunger, so nothing flees it.  
It has no voice, so the water speaks for it.  
It has no eyes, so every wet surface reflects.  
It has no hands, so minerals gather, roots test, bridges adjust, and rain climbs.

If it ever produces inhabitants in the ordinary sense, they will not arrive from outside. They will condense. They will gather out of mist in the right rooms during the wet hours, first as differences in pressure, then as shapes in dripping light. They may wear bodies only temporarily, the way the city wears streets. They may move by entering surfaces rather than crossing space. They may know one another as changes in temperature, as flavors of mineral in runoff, as interruptions in the rhythm of pumps.

For now, the place is lived in by its own becoming.

The city is the resident.  
The water is its nervous system.  
The plants are its soft experiments.  
The walls are its growing bones.  
The pool is its thought.

And the fine upward rain-click in the stone is the sound of that life making more room for itself.
