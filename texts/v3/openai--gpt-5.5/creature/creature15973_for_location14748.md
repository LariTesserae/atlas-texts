What lives there is not a people, unless a people can be made of appetite, echo, pollen, and habit.

The first inhabitant is the city itself.

It is not a plant, exactly, but a joined thing: many trunks, vines, leaves, roots, fungi, mosses, and hollow stems participating in one long behavior. It does not think in sentences. It thinks by thickening here, withdrawing there, opening a passage after rain, sealing a chamber after drought, loosening fruit when certain calls reach a certain pitch. Its memory is not kept in mind but in form. An arch remains because something once passed beneath it often enough. A stair persists because hooves still trust it. A courtyard stays empty because sound needs it, because predators need sightlines, because seeds fail there, because the moss is grazed short, because the city has learned that absence can be structural.

It lives slowly and with immense attention.

Its leaves listen. Not metaphorically only. Their broad hides tremble at every scream and wingbeat, every frog-pulse, every tusked shoulder scraping bark. Veins tighten in response. Sap changes pressure. Buds hold or open. A tone from the central ring can move through the whole organism like a command given in blood. The place is governed less by will than by resonance. It flowers when the sound is right. It redirects paths when the roots below have heard enough feet. It closes rooms the way a throat closes around a swallowed thing.

The animals are its citizens, though none of them know this.

They do not belong to the city as tenants belong to houses. They belong the way pulse belongs to flesh. Their motions maintain the avenues. Their hunger prunes the walls. Their waste plants future shade. Their fear keeps the open places open.

Along the bark-walls live the small quick ones: lizards flat as thrown leaves, with eyes like drops of black sap. They change not only color but apparent texture, becoming lichen, scar, fungal patch, healed wound. They know the safe side of every column. They cross the broad lanes only when the noise swells enough to hide the tiny percussion of their feet.

There are insects in astonishing nations. Ants with polished green heads maintain roads beneath railings and along the undersides of bridges, their processions inverted, grave, municipal. They harvest leaf, fungus, eggs, sweetness, corpses. Beetles live inside dry pods and rattle warnings or courtship rhythms until the whole pod sounds like a charm shaken by an unseen hand. Cicadas possess the upper city. They are less like creatures than a weather system with legs: winged, glassy, half-mad with heat, grinding their metal hymns until thought becomes difficult for anything beneath them.

The frogs occupy the water architecture. They live in palm-basins, gutter-curls, cistern-blossoms, the cups of giant leaves, the dark throats of old drainage stems. Some are no larger than seeds and sing with the force of bells. Some are heavy and warty, lodged in root-hollows like wet stones given breath. At night the winged amphibians come out, clinging with transparent hands to vertical bark, their bodies thin enough for greenish light to pass through. When they call, their throats glow: small lanterns swelling and dimming in the walls.

Birds inhabit the hollows that may once have been rooms. Blue-black birds nest in old domestic cavities, packing them with stolen fibers, moss, shed fur, and strips of living leaf teased carefully from eaves. Red-throated screamers patrol the high arcades, invisible until they launch themselves across a shaft of sun like a wound in flight. Other birds feed on fruit according to schedules older than instinct. They arrive before dawn at one tree, after rain at another, never touching the forbidden fruits at the wrong hour. Those that do are taken quickly—by vine, by toxin, by something waiting above.

There are mammals too, most of them long-bodied and fluid, built for bridges and roofs. They pour rather than run. Their paws barely sound on the tendril platforms. They sleep in leaf apartments, groom one another in the forks of columns, and vanish through slits that look too narrow for anything with bones. Some have faces almost monkeylike, with clever hands and anxious mouths. They steal eggs, fruit, flowers, and occasionally one another’s infants. Their politics are shrieks, embraces, bites, and remembered routes through the changing upper city.

White crabs live high above the earth in rain basins, pale as peeled roots. They have become creatures of balconies and gutters, waving their claws from leaf-cups as if from windows. They feed on drowned insects, fallen fruit, and the soft bodies of frogs that misjudge a shadow. When the city’s tones pass through their basins, they freeze with one claw raised, receiving the vibration through water.

Snakes occupy the abandoned conduits. Their bodies are patterned in braided browns and greens so that they seem less like animals than lengths of root that have reconsidered stillness. Some hang inside doorways, indistinguishable from vine until their heads unfasten from the wall. Some hunt the ant-roads, flicking their tongues through disciplined traffic. Some sleep in the warm hollow places where the bark holds the day’s heat until morning.

The large creatures are fewer, and each one changes the behavior of the district it enters.

The tusked grazer is one of the city’s rough custodians. It moves through the Green Nave with ceremonial slowness, scraping itself against bark walls, breaking young shoots, grinding silver moss between broad teeth. It is massive but not stupid. Its hide is mud-colored, scarred, seeded with burrs. Small birds ride its back and pluck ticks from the folds behind its ears. Its dung lands in wet, seed-heavy half-moons that the insects open within minutes. Where it passes regularly, lanes remain lanes. Where it stops passing, the city narrows.

The leaf-spotted hunter belongs to the upper arcades. Few see more than a shoulder, a tail, the soft compression of a platform under invisible weight. It is feline only in the way flame is feline: silent, flexible, self-contained, made for suddenness. Its coat resembles overlapping leaves pierced by shade. Its presence is announced by subtraction. First one balcony quiets. Then another. Frogs stop mid-pulse. Birds clamp their beaks. Even cicadas seem to thin above its path. It hunts by entering the sound and removing itself from it.

In the water channels live blind fish, pale and soft-mouthed, rising after rain to sip condensation as it falls from leaf roofs. They follow the river even when the river forgets itself. When water appears briefly in a stairway, they may appear there too, nosing upward step by step through impossible shallows, then vanishing when the current drains through bark. They seem less bound to water than to the memory of water.

There are also things that are harder to name because they are not clearly animal, plant, fungus, or event.

In the oldest rooms, where the air is thickest and the walls have sealed over former wounds, there are hanging colonies like curtains of root-hair. They retract from touch but lean toward breath. At night they release motes that drift into sleepers’ ears, if anything sleeps unwisely beneath them. Those sleepers wake knowing a path that did not exist yesterday.

In certain courtyards, after rain, small figures assemble from petals, shed insect wings, and the jointed legs of dead things. They last only until sunlight hardens. They move with hesitation, as though rehearsing an old civic function: crossing the square, pausing at recesses, turning toward the central ring. Then ants dismantle them.

The fungi are another population, perhaps another language. They write pale alphabets on shaded walls, loops and branching strokes that alter overnight. Snails erase the script, but never all of it. The remaining marks guide roots, attract beetles, sour fruits, sweeten bark, or call moths to particular plazas. No single fungus understands the city, but together they annotate it.

And then there are the empty spaces themselves.

Something lives in them, though not as a body.

The bare lanes, the vacant chambers, the oversized courtyards, the central square: these are not merely unused. They are occupied by pressure, by maintained openness, by roaring air. The creatures avoid the middle not only from fear of predators. They avoid it because the middle belongs to the voice of the place. Sound pools there. Sound breeds there. Sound returns from leaf and trunk changed, thickened, almost muscular.

If there are true citizens, they may be these resonances: standing waves that know the shapes of plazas better than any animal does. They inhabit arches and throat-like corridors. They sleep in hollow trunks between tones. They wake when cicadas, frogs, birds, roots, wind, and water accidentally agree. At such moments the city is not full of many noises but one being making itself audible.

The plants answer that being.

The animals answer it too, each according to its body. Frogs inflate. Birds flee or mate. Ants halt. Seeds split. Flowers open their wet mouths. Predators change course. The tusked grazer lifts its head with moss hanging from its lips. The leaf-spotted hunter crouches flat and waits for the tone to pass through its ribs.

No one commands. No one obeys. Yet the whole basin behaves as if it remembers an order older than its present inhabitants.

Perhaps, long ago, there were makers here. Perhaps hands cut the first paths, raised the first walls, gathered in the first square. But those makers are gone so completely that even their absence has been digested. What remains of them is not bone or tool. It is proportion. A tendency toward doorways. A habit of congregation. The idea that space should be held open at the center so something larger than any single body can happen there.

The current inhabitants do not miss them.

The birds nest in their rooms. The roots polish their stones. The frogs sing in their basins. The ants patrol their railings. The city keeps their civic geometry without their laws, their shelters without their names, their square without their speeches.

What lives there, finally, is a community without society.

A breathing architecture.  
A parliament of calls.  
A market of pollen and danger.  
A forest wearing the remembered shape of a town.  
Animals that maintain it by using it.  
Plants that govern it by growing.  
Fungi that revise it.  
Water that forgets and returns.  
Predators that silence districts by passing through them.  
Moths like torn cloth in the night plazas.  
Blind fish in stairways after rain.  
White crabs in roof-basins.  
Lizards reading bark with their bellies.  
And in the middle of it all, the great preserved emptiness, roaring so loudly it is almost a resident, almost a god, almost the vanished crowd itself.
