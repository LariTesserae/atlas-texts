What lives there is not a civilization, and not a wilderness in the surface sense. It is an ecology of listeners.

The cavern has no ruling species. Nothing there has reshaped the place enough to become its author. Instead it is shared by many small hungers, many cautious bodies, many kinds of attention. Life has entered the fresh wound in the earth and filled it quickly, but without ornament, without grandeur, without any impulse to make a world other than the one already given.

At the base are the quietest inhabitants: films, crusts, threads, and damp stains that are alive.

They live where water pauses. Gray-green mats cling to seep lines and the lips of shallow pools. Pale lichens roughen exposed stone wherever the cavern’s thin mineral glow and diluted day can reach them. In wet pockets, translucent algae drift and knot together, trembling when larvae pass through them. Fungi bloom in low pads on guano, on dead insects, on the damp underside of fallen wood or roots from above. They are not lush. They do not cover the cavern. They occupy it precisely, like small legal claims made in moisture and patience.

Around them gather the first moving multitudes.

There are gnats with glassy wings, pale springtails, soft white mites, blind beetles with long feelers, flat-backed isopods, tiny crustaceans in the pools, and larvae that hang like commas in the water. Crickets scrape in the dark. Worms writhe under bat droppings. Minute shells tick against stone. Most of these creatures are colorless or the color of old wax. Their bodies are soft where the world allows softness and armored where it does not. They live by touch, vibration, chemical trace, and the memory of dampness.

Above them hang the great colonies.

The batlike mammals are among the cavern’s loudest citizens. They curtain the roof in black and brown folds, their bodies packed tight along shelves and ribs of stone. Their faces are sharp, leafed, wrinkled, or foxlike depending on the kind. Some are no larger than a hand; others have wings broad enough to clap audibly when they shift. They do not all leave the cavern. Many feed within its side chambers, skimming over pools, raiding insect clouds, worrying at fruiting fungi, or hunting the small pale things that rise from guano beds.

Their society is quarrelsome and crowded. They groom, bite, mate, nurse, complain, and sleep in pulses. Their voices are too fine for easy hearing until a thousand of them gather; then the ceiling seems to hiss and glitter with sound. Their droppings fall steadily, and where they fall, entire nations of lesser mouths assemble. The bats are not merely inhabitants. They are weather, roof, harvest, and rain.

On the floor move the heavier animals.

The cave ungulates are low, compact creatures, built for rubble and dimness rather than speed. They stand no higher than a surface goat or small deer, but broader in the chest, with short necks, sloping backs, and hard, spreading hooves that grip damp stone. Their hides are dun, slate, smoky brown, sometimes faintly striped along the flanks like shadows cast by rock shelves. Their eyes are large, dark, and wet-looking, reflecting even the weakest light.

They feed on whatever the cavern offers: mineral films, fungus crusts, fallen bat matter, softened root-nubs descending from cracks, algae at pool edges, salt blooms, and the edible skin of damp stone. They are not graceful grazers. They scrape, lick, nose, worry, and chew. Their muzzles are blunt and sensitive. Their lips can peel a thin mat from limestone without taking much stone with it.

They live in loose herds, though “herd” is almost too grand a word. A dozen here, six there, a mother and two half-grown young lingering near a seep. They know the cavern by routes rather than maps. Their lives are circuits: pool, salt, guano edge, fungus shelf, warm crack, sleeping hollow, pool again. They are nervous in open ground but must cross it. When startled, they do not flee far. They clatter over stone, bunch, halt, listen, and slowly resume the same direction as before.

Their young are born already quiet.

Predators live by patience.

The catlike things along the slabs are long-bodied and low, more like shadows with shoulders than surface cats. Their fur is pale gray, ash-brown, or nearly white, but always dulled by dust. Their ears are large and rounded; their whiskers are extravagant. Their pupils catch the cavern’s faint light and return it as green sparks. They hunt from edges: beside fallen plates of limestone, above clay gullies, behind standing stones, at the approaches to pools. They do not chase far. In this place, a failed chase wastes too much. They wait for a hoof to slip, for a juvenile to stray, for a hand-creature to descend carelessly.

The pool eels are worse, because they are almost never seen whole. A ripple, a pale jaw, a rope of muscle withdrawing beneath black water. They live in the deeper pockets, in channels under gravel, in cold basins where algae hangs thick enough to hide them. Their eyes are small or absent. They hunt by pressure and taste. Anything that drinks too long may feel the water take hold.

There are owls near the upper fissures, broad-faced and soot-colored, with soft wings and grave voices. Unlike the cats, they own the vertical dark. They nest where the faint drafts come down and feed on bats, hand-creatures, crickets, and young ungulates when they can manage it. Their calls travel so far through the cavern that other animals sometimes freeze at an owl that is not near them at all.

Blind snakes live in the warm seams. Spiders live in cracks, not in beautiful webs but in short, dense traps of silk stretched where insects must pass. Centipedes as long as a forearm move under slabs. Amphibians squat in wet crevices and call with blunt, stubborn voices when the air turns damper.

And then there are the ones with hands.

They are not people, though a frightened traveler might first mistake them for little cave-people crouched on shelves above the floor. They are narrow and quick, with long backs, long fingers, and bright forward-facing eyes. Their faces are somewhere between primate and mustelid: pointed muzzles, mobile lips, whiskers along the cheeks, small rounded ears that flick constantly. Their shoulders are slight. Their hands are almost too delicate for the rest of them, with dark nails, clever thumbs, and fingertips sensitive enough to find a grub under bark-thin stone.

They live in the broken shelves along the cavern walls, especially where cracks provide sleeping niches and routes upward away from cats. Their bodies are furred in gray, brown, or smoky black, usually lighter on the belly. Young cling to adults. Adults groom one another with intense seriousness. They quarrel often and reconcile quickly. Their social life is made of touch, sound, scent, and proximity.

They are intelligent, but their intelligence does not accumulate.

They use stones to crack shells. They use bone splinters to pry larvae out of crevices. They strip root fibers with their teeth. They may hold a sharp flake for half a day, use it well, then drop it when frightened or distracted. They remember where useful objects are, but only as part of a route: the heavy stone near the hoof-shaped pool, the rib bone in the guano slope, the chert shard beside the seep. They do not gather such objects into stores. They do not improve them across generations. A young one learns by watching, stealing, being bitten, trying again.

Their calls are varied: barks for alarm, whistles for contact, clicking mutters while feeding, thin screams during disputes, soft trills between mothers and young. They are among the most watchful beings in the cavern. They notice snakes before the ungulates do. They notice cats before the bats care. They notice a stranger almost immediately and pass that knowledge along the shelves in nervous bursts.

But they do not organize against the stranger. They do not form a tribe in the human sense. They have bands, preferences, rivalries, favored sleeping cracks, old females who know routes, bold males who steal food, juveniles who play at stalking insects and fall from ledges. They have memory but not history. They have tools but not technology. They have grief, fear, appetite, affection, irritation, curiosity, and no monuments at all.

Their dead are eaten like all other dead.

This is true of the whole cavern. Nothing is wasted, and almost nothing is preserved as meaning. A bat falls; beetles open it. A hoofed animal dies in clay; worms, cats, fungi, and hand-creatures reduce it. An owl chick tumbles from a ledge; the floor receives it without ceremony. Even bones do not remain untouched for long. They become calcium, tools, shelters for mites, splinters in guano, pale fragments under hooves.

The inhabitants share certain traits because the cavern demands them.

They are economical. No animal spends energy on display beyond what mating and warning require. Colors are muted. Calls matter more than plumage. Smell matters more than posture. Hearing is refined in nearly everything. Eyes are large where light is useful and abandoned where it is not. Feet are careful. Whiskers are common. Bodies stay low unless they can fly.

Most are local to an almost obsessive degree. A pool has its drinkers. A ledge has its colony. A crack has its snake. A guano field has its beetles and the beetles’ predators. A herd may know only a few miles of an immense cavern and still live a complete life. Distance exists, but it is not an invitation. The cavern is too broken, too dark, too acoustically confusing for wandering to become a virtue.

Their relationships are old even if the stone is young.

The bats feed the beetles. The beetles feed the hand-creatures. The hand-creatures warn the ungulates without intending to. The ungulates open crusts and expose films for smaller mouths. The cats follow the ungulates. The owls follow the hand-creatures. The eels wait for everyone. The fungi inherit all failures.

No one owns the water, but every creature knows how dangerous water is. Pools are meeting places and killing places. Hooves polish their approaches. Insects cloud above them. Amphibians call from their cracks. Cats lie downwind of them. Hand-creatures descend in nervous groups, one drinking while three watch. Bats skim them. Eels sleep beneath them. Around each pool the cavern’s separate lives briefly knot together, then loosen again.

The overall character of the inhabitants is therefore not savagery, but vigilance.

They are not monsters. They are not peaceful either. They are creatures of a limited, echoing world where every sound may be information and every silence may be concealment. They live in routines so exact they resemble rituals, though no ritual has been invented. They repeat because repetition is survival. They cross the same stone because another path may break a leg. They drink at the same hour because predators expect them at other hours. Predators wait at the same crossings because eventually habit delivers meat.

If there is a mind belonging to the cavern as a whole, it is made of these overlapping habits.

It is the bats folding and unfolding overhead.

The ungulates lowering their muzzles to blue-lit stone.

The hand-creatures crouched on ledges with wet eyes and clever fingers.

The pale eel shifting below a pool’s surface.

The owl turning its head toward a sound that has already bounced twice from the roof.

The beetles under guano, the spiders in cracks, the snakes in warm seams, the larvae in black water, the fungi blooming from death without haste.

They live there as if they have always lived there, though the cavern itself is young. They have not tamed it. They have not adorned it. They have not named it in any lasting way. They occupy it by listening, feeding, breeding, hiding, and returning.

The place belongs to no one.

So everything belongs to it.
