What lives there is not one kind of life, but a climate that has learned to take bodies.

The first inhabitants are the films: skins of green, black, red, and opal slicking every surface. They live where nothing seems to offer purchase—on wet basalt, on the ceramic sides of dead pylons, on the underside of bridges, across the floating mineral crusts that flex and heal after being broken. Some are moss, some are bacterial mats, some are machine-fed lichens that have forgotten the difference between sunlight and current. In the morning they darken and drink. By noon they shine. At night they pulse faintly, not with thought exactly, but with stored heat and stolen chemistry.

They are the plateau’s true carpet. Larger things move over them with care.

The plants grow low and deliberate, as if the altitude taught them humility and the heat taught them appetite. The sedges are waxy, jointed, and tough, their stems filled with warm bitter sap. When wind passes through them they click together in long dry conversations, though everything about them is wet. Red thread-moss makes nets over the shallow pools, strong enough to hold insects, weak enough to collapse under a hoof. Blue-green cushion plants swell around vents and exhale a medicinal smell when bruised.

The carnivorous cups are everywhere in the marsh shelves. They are bright as painted lacquer—yellow, crimson, violet-black—and always full, not with rainwater alone but with a syrupy fluid that reflects the sky too clearly. Insects come to them for salt. Frogs use their rims as calling stages. Small mammals lick the droplets from their lips and sometimes lose a paw to the slow closing of the cup. The plants do not snap. They persuade.

The cloud trees are the plateau’s tallest patient creatures. They are not grand trees, not forest giants, but squat, wind-flattened things with dark slick trunks and exposed roots braided like sleeping serpents. Their leaves are thick on top and translucent at the margins, veined with gold fluid that circulates visibly during the hottest hours. They flower into vapor. When clouds descend, their crowns open thousands of small pale mouths and comb water out of the air.

Their roots do more than hold soil. They hold memory of terrain. They grow into old foundation rings and cracked conduits, into mineral seams and ceramic housings, and they learn the shape of the buried systems by touch. In some groves the roots hum softly with current. In others they have grown around weak gravity zones and trained whole slopes to remain in place.

The flowers are more animal than the trees. Some listen. Some cup sound the way other flowers cup rain. They turn toward waterfalls, toward frog choruses, toward the bass breathing of vents. Their petals are concave, waxed, and ridged with fine hairs that tremble at particular frequencies. When the right call passes over them, they open. When the wrong one does, they close and wait.

Their pollinators are heavy-bodied and damp.

There are beetles like polished coins, with wing cases of copper, emerald, and gunmetal blue. They crawl rather than fly in the thin air, ferrying pollen packed in the furrows of their legs. There are moths with glass wings and thick furred bodies, night-gliders that launch from warm stones and ride invisible lifts above the pools. There are little black flies that breed in mineral foam and emerge with silver dust on their feet. There are long-legged water crickets that walk the surfaces of boiling springs by keeping to cooler seams.

The frogs are among the most obvious citizens of the basin. They stand high on their legs and walk rather than hop, toes spread wide on the mud. Their skins are dark, slick, and jewel-marked, not for display alone but to regulate heat and hold moisture in specific bands. Their throats glow when they call: green, amber, milky blue. At dusk the marshes are full of these lights, pulsing from under ferns and between roots like lanterns carried by invisible hands.

Some frogs live in ordinary pools. Some live in pools that are no longer entirely in the present. Their tadpoles hatch during rains that have not yet fallen, or mature in water that remembers a colder century. These frogs have strange timing. They call before storms appear. They bury themselves before a machine wakes. They have no understanding of prediction; their bodies simply learned the plateau’s irregular grammar.

In the warm rivulets are transparent eels, thin as ribbons and almost boneless to the eye. Their organs hang inside them like threads of smoke. They move between pools by night, following heat gradients and mineral taste. Some carry tiny luminous parasites along their spines. Some are blind. Some are not blind enough; they stare upward from clear water with gold pinprick eyes, waiting for shadows to cross.

The grazers are compact, dark, and glossy, built low to the ground against wind and slope. They have no wool, only oiled hides that shed water constantly. Their heads are blunt, their lips mobile, their nostrils sealed by fine muscular flaps when mist thickens too much. They crop sedge, lick mineral crust, and stand in clusters with their flanks touching, making small islands of animal warmth in the marsh.

Their young are born with long legs and soft translucent hooves that harden after the first day. They learn paths by scent and pressure, not sight. They know which hummocks are real, which pools are hidden under fern, which lines of stones must not be crossed. When the air bends wrong, the herd stops. When a waterfall begins falling sideways, they wait until it remembers itself.

They are not timid animals. They fear only the sky shadows.

The sky shadows belong to gliders: vast, thin-winged creatures that cannot truly fly from the basin floor but can fall beautifully from the cliffs. Their bodies are narrow and dark, their wings ribbed with cartilage and stretched membrane, their tails long enough to steer through columns of heat. They perch on mineral-streaked ridges with wings spread to dry, looking like torn pieces of night pinned to stone.

They hunt by patience and geometry. They watch the herds move through fog. They wait for thermals rising from steam flats. Then they let go. Their descent is nearly silent until the last moment, when their wings flex and crack like wet cloth. They take the weak, the young, the unwary animal that steps too far from the cluster. After feeding, they climb the air again, circling in slow spirals over waterfalls that fall upward just enough to help them rise.

In the fern hollows live heavier things.

One is broad-footed and plated, with armor not like a turtle’s shell but like overlapping river stones grown into its hide. It has a long flexible mouth for scraping mineral lichens from cliffs. Its forelimbs are powerful and slightly splayed; it can lean against wet rock and browse vertical faces where lesser animals would slide away. When it moves, its plates creak. When it breathes, steam leaks from the vents behind its jaw.

It has no hurry in it. Moss grows on its back. Beetles live under the edges of its armor. Listening flowers sometimes open when it groans, and certain vines use its slow passage to spread their seeds. At dusk these animals descend to the bright pools and drink for a long time, their reflections interrupted by bubbles rising from below.

Another large creature is softer and stranger: a marsh-bodied browser with a narrow head, pale eyes, and a mane of sensory tendrils along its neck. It moves through reed beds on six careful legs. Its skin can darken or lighten with humidity, and during heavy vapor it nearly disappears. It feeds on the translucent leaves of steam vines, selecting only those that have absorbed enough mineral heat. Its calls are deep chest-notes that travel through mud as much as air. You feel them in your ankles before you hear them.

These large animals are overdesigned in the way old successful things are overdesigned. They carry solutions to problems that no longer happen every season but once happened often enough to matter: heat-spines, sealing nostrils, mineral teeth, secondary lids, pressure bladders, magnetic bones, larvae that sleep for years in warm mud until a particular machine-tone wakes them.

There are birds, though many of them are only partly committed to the air. Marsh birds stride between pools on hinged legs, slow and solemn, their backs dusted with pollen. Their bills are broad and flattened for skimming slicks of larvae from warm water. Some have hollow crests that resonate with the vents; they stand near fumaroles and answer them note for note. Others nest in the quiet zones where thunder cannot enter, weaving reeds through pockets of altered pressure so their eggs hang slightly lighter than they should.

The small climbing mammals are clever without being civilized. They have damp dark fur, long fingers, and tails used for balance among roots and cables alike. They raid carnivorous cups without being caught. They pry mineral scabs from old panels to lick the salts beneath. They sleep in tree hollows warmed by buried conduits. Their family groups maintain routes through the inconsistent distances, and elders teach the young which shortcut is safe, which returns you hungry to the same place, which makes milk sour in the belly.

They are among the few animals that use the old structures deliberately. They crack seed pods on ceramic pylons because the vibration stuns the grubs inside. They cache fruit in doorless domes where rot proceeds slowly. They have learned that the upward rain around the humming tower washes parasites from their fur, and they queue there after heavy fog, chittering and shoving like children at a bath.

If there are people, they are easy to miss at first.

They do not build upward. They do not cut clearings. They do not make roads across marshland that is already full of older, stranger paths. They live in the dry interiors of things that were not made as houses: under pressure ribs, inside foundation rings roofed with woven root, in the lee of ceramic towers whose warmth never fails. Their dwellings look like mounds, reed blinds, banks of moss, arrangements of stones that might be arrays and might be prayer.

They are small, broad-handed, dark-haired or hairless depending on lineage, with skin that beads water constantly and eyes adapted to glare, fog, and machine-light. They move slowly in the heat, not from weakness but from etiquette. Suddenness belongs to predators, vents, and failing devices. A person who hurries on the plateau is either very young or already in danger.

They wear lacquered plant fiber, beetle-shell ornaments, thin mineral plates, and woven mats that shed condensation. Metal is rare and treated less as a tool than as a temperamental substance. Ceramic is trusted more. Bone is trusted most. Their knives are made from the teeth of cliff-browsers and their bowls from seed pods hardened in vent steam. They keep no large fires; the land is warm enough, and smoke confuses the flowers.

They do not think of the old machines as ruins. Ruins are things that have stopped belonging to the world. These have not stopped. The people call them weather-bones, under-roots, sleepers, ribs, wells, wrong-stones, depending on what each does and how safely it does it. They know which towers hum before rain, which bridges should be crossed singing, which pools heal fever and which preserve a wound exactly as it is. They do not repair the machines in the ancient sense. They tend them the way one tends an old animal: scraping away choking growth, feeding certain vents with mineral paste, opening channels before pressure builds, leaving offerings of conductive lichen where cracked panels can grow their silver scabs.

Their priests, if that is the word, are not commanders of spirits but listeners. They sit with their palms on wet stone and count intervals between bubbles. They taste condensation. They learn the difference between thunder, avalanche, machine-shift, and the footfall of a plated browser two terraces away. Children are taught early that every sound has a lower sound beneath it, and that survival depends on hearing both.

They keep calendars, but not by stars alone. Stars are too cold and clean for the work of days. Their calendars are made of frog calls, cloud descents, flower openings, molt seasons, pylon hums, the angle of certain waterfalls, the years when seeds drift uphill, the nights when old rain comes back into the pools. Time here is not a line but a wet braid. They measure it by returns.

They herd almost nothing. They garden by encouragement. They plant sedge in places where mud needs holding. They carry beetles between isolated flower fields. They guide vines toward warm stone and cut them back from devices that would sicken if smothered. They break mineral crusts in patterns to let new pools breathe. Their agriculture is less possession than negotiation.

They eat carefully: steamed tubers from root mats, sour translucent leaves, frog eggs from permitted pools, insects roasted on hot stone, eel flesh cured in mineral vapor, fruit that ripens only inside fog. They avoid the lacquer cups except for ritual poisons and medicines. They drink from pools after watching the bubbles. If the rhythm is wrong, they wait.

Their dead are not buried deep. Deep belongs to machines, springs, and old pressures. The dead are placed in root cradles among cloud trees, wrapped in sedge fiber and mineral clay. Over time the trees take them. A family grove is therefore also an archive, and elders can name which branch grew from which grandmother, which root thickened after a child, which flowers opened the year a stranger married in.

But even these people are not masters of the basin. Nothing is. The plateau does not seem to allow mastery. It allows participation.

The true dominant life is relationship: frog to flower, machine to cloud, root to stone, grazer to moss, beetle to pollen, child to tower-hum, predator to thermal, rain to upward rain. Every creature lives by reading gradients—heat, pressure, moisture, sound, mineral taste, gravity’s small local moods. Intelligence here is not proven by tools alone. It is proven by routing correctly through a world that is always slightly more than terrain.

What lives there has become humid, patient, and exact.

It has slick skin or waxed leaves. It has broad feet, sealed nostrils, listening petals, mineral teeth. It glides instead of flies. It waits instead of runs. It drinks from stone. It treats old machinery as weather and weather as kin. It accepts that water may fall, rise, remember, or arrive from a season not yet reached.

The basin’s inhabitants are not relics of a lost age, nor simple wildness thriving among debris. They are the descendants of continuance. They live inside the third thing the plateau became: not nature, not technology, but a breathing country where every organism is partly climate, every machine is partly habitat, and every pool holds a little light from below.
