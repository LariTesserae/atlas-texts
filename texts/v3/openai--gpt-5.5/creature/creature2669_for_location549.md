What lives there does not announce itself as life.

At first it seems the basin is inhabited only by its own materials: salt, chalk, ash, heat, stone. The living things are so spare and withheld that they appear almost as decisions the ground made long ago and has never revised. They are not colonists. They are not growth in the ordinary sense. They are continuations of the crust by darker, more stubborn means.

The low black tufts are the commonest residents. They grow where a crack gives them permission. Each is a small, severe knot of blades, folded inward, waxed against loss, sharp-edged but not thorned. They do not lean toward light, because light is not generous here. They do not spread into mats or meadows. A tuft may occupy the same handspan for longer than an animal’s lineage would remember, adding one blade, losing one blade, tightening its roots deeper into the mineral seam.

Their roots are finer than hair near the surface, then become hard and dark below, like old thread soaked in resin. They do not search widely. They hold. They wait for traces too small to be called moisture: a change in salt, a night-breath in the stone, the faintest condensation inside a crack that never reaches the air. They live on accidents the basin barely admits to having.

The gray-green cord-plants near the rim are older-looking, though perhaps not older. They resemble bundles tied and retied until the idea of a stem has been forgotten. Their ribs are tough, dusty, and faintly elastic. If cut, they would not bleed sap so much as darken at the wound. They keep most of themselves below the crust, packed into swollen, fibrous bodies that hold not water exactly, but a memory of solvency: salts arranged so carefully that life can continue with almost nothing moving.

They do not bloom in any season one could wait for. Flowering, for them, is not display but risk. Once in many years, perhaps after a warmth held unusually long in the stone, one of them may loosen a seam along its side and expose a row of black seeds no larger than grit. There is no fragrance, no color, no invitation. The seeds fall close. Most do not wake. A few wedge into cracks and become indistinguishable from dust until time chooses otherwise.

There are also things still smaller, if they can be called inhabitants: dark films in sheltered hollows, mineral skins on the undersides of stones, colonies so slow they seem more like stains than organisms. They live where darkness and salt make a kind of fragile covenant. They do not soften the place. They do not green it. They take nothing visibly and give nothing visibly back. Yet they are alive in the old, minimal way: arranging, resisting, enduring.

Nothing here moves from place to place by desire.

The basin’s inhabitants have no appetite that can be seen. They do not pursue. They do not consume one another in any dramatic way. Their competition is conducted over decades, by millimeters of root and shade, by which surface hardens first, by which body can endure the longest interval between gifts. Death, when it comes, is almost indistinguishable from posture. A black tuft may stand dead for years before the blades loosen. A cord-plant may hollow from within while retaining its shape, becoming a husk that the dust respectfully fills.

If there is a dominant creature here, it is not a creature but a manner of being.

The basin is inhabited by patience so complete it has become physical. Every living thing has accepted the law of smallness. Nothing here believes in abundance. Nothing wastes itself on softness, speed, color, or sound. The plants are like vows taken by matter: to remain, to require little, to change without disturbing the form of the place.

They are not miserable. Misery would require comparison, and these things seem to have none. They belong to the warm stone and the black-violet dark as completely as coral belongs to reef or moss to rain. Their restraint is not suffering but design. They have learned the exact size of survival here, and they do not exceed it.

At the pale oval in the center, almost nothing lives. Even the hardiest roots stop short. The smooth crust there is too pure, too sealed, too final. Around it, the tufts and cord-plants form a loose, unintentional ring, not worshipping, not avoiding with fear, simply acknowledging a fact. The center belongs to the basin alone.

So the residents are few: black blades folded against loss, rope-bodied plants storing almost nothing, hidden films darkening stone by imperceptible degrees, seeds asleep so deeply they seem mineral. They have no voices. They leave no tracks. They do not make the basin feel occupied.

They make it feel kept.
