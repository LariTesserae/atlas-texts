What lives there is not a people anymore, if it ever was one.

It is a citizenship of wet bodies, errands, reflexes, and half-forgotten instruments. The hollow is inhabited the way a thought is inhabited: by impulses crossing it, by small confirmations, by creatures that do not know they are maintaining a world.

The most visible residents are the littlest ones.

They live in the seams.

Thread-insects no longer than splinters walk the mineral cracks in processions so orderly they seem ceremonial. Each carries water, but not for drinking. They move beads from one surface to another, from warm stone to moss lip, from moss lip to the base of a reed, from reed to the matte white arches where the droplets briefly shiver and change tension. Their bodies are translucent amber, with dark organs ticking inside them like seeds in a pod. If one is interrupted, the line does not scatter. It pauses. The bead is passed backward. The gap closes. The work continues.

The pale crabs are mud-readers. They are almost all claw, almost all gesture, their shells thin and colorless except for faint blue shadows where the organs lie. They sift the dark flats with combed forelimbs, raising threads of silt and tasting what the water has done since morning. They do not build burrows. They occupy impressions: old hoof-shaped hollows, curled leaf-pits, shallow dents made by creatures long dead. When danger crosses them, they flatten until they resemble flakes of wet mica. When the noon grooves warm, they gather along the boundary stones and press their claws into the ancient marks, as if taking instruction.

In the pools live fish that should not have room to turn around. They are narrow as eyelashes and bright only when they move. Their bodies seem made of quick decisions. They appear in puddles after rainless nights, vanish from basins that never dry, and travel through films of water so thin that they must be partly sliding over stone and partly remembering another route. They eat spores, larvae, mineral scum, and sometimes the light itself—or something in the light, something the old machines still separate from heat.

Beneath the stones are the flat amphibians. They are patient, soft, and almost architectural. Their backs match the underside of whatever slab they cling to: black-green under algae, pale gray beneath limestone, rust-colored beneath iron mud. Their eyes are covered by smoked membranes and open rarely. They breathe through their bellies. They hatch in water, spend their youth as transparent ribbons among roots, and then choose a stone. After that they may not travel more than a few body-lengths in a season. They know pressure, warmth, vibration, the arrival of rain before clouds, and the small electromagnetic weather around the rods. When the central droplet falls, they surface not from curiosity but obedience.

There are hunters here, but none of them are grand.

The leaf-shaped predator lives in moving shade. Its body is thin as a soaked petal and veined like one. It does not chase. It lets the wandering shadow carry it. It grips the dark with tiny hooks along its underside and advances by waiting for the world to misalign. Its mouth is a sudden slit. One moment an insect line is whole; the next there is a pause, a damp fold, and one bead of water travels on without its bearer.

The mud-eel is nearly invisible because it is not quite separate from the mud. It has a spine of pale cartilage, blind golden pits along its head, and a mouth that opens upward through silt like a question. It can remain buried for days while crabs walk over it. When it feeds, there is no splash. The mud simply accepts one fewer creature.

The moss itself hunts. Not all of it, only the thickest cushions in the hottest pans. It is green, silver, and plush, and during the warmest hour it curls inward around mites, eggs, and the smallest larvae. It digests some. It releases others. This is not mercy. It is calibration. The moss keeps what the hollow can spare and returns what the hollow still needs moving.

The larger animal—the six-limbed slick-bodied one—is among the nearest things to a person, though it has no tools and no speech that would be recognized from outside.

It is called nothing by anything except perhaps the pavilion.

It moves like poured muscle. Its skin is soft brown, gray, and sky-reflective in patches, always wet, always receiving. The six limbs are small, useful less for running than for bracing, parting reeds, holding itself against current when current decides to climb. Its head is blunt and seedpod-shaped, with breathing slits low along the jaw and black, high-set eyes that watch above the water while the rest of the body remains submerged.

It drinks through its throat and remembers through its skin.

When it presses itself to the hovering panels, pale lines appear along its flank: branching, vanishing, branching again. They are not nerves, though nerves answer them. They are not language, though information passes. The animal leaves afterward altered in tiny ways. It chooses a different pool. It avoids a flower it used to nose. It carries water on its back to a stone where eggs are waiting. It teaches nothing deliberately, yet its young follow its circuits and inherit the map through habit, milk, and damp contact.

Its young are born in crescent pools after dusk. They are limbless at first, silver-gray and all tail, clinging to the bellies of broad leaves. The parents do not guard them. Instead, they wet the correct stones, disturb the correct insect columns, and press their bodies against certain roots, causing the pools where the young live to remain balanced. Parenthood here is mostly environmental adjustment.

Above all this are the winged inhabitants.

Some are specks, living punctuation, swarming in spirals above wet stone. Individually they are nearly nothing: a bead-body, two glass wings, a filament tail. Together they become organs of perception. They map heat. They test air. They assemble brief diagrams no single body understands. When they change direction at once, the command does not come from a leader. It passes through spacing, brightness, wingbeat, moisture. The swarm thinks by rearranging itself.

Others are larger, membranous drifters with wings too slow for ordinary flight. Their bodies are hollow and warm, ribbed with translucent struts. They ride invisible gradients shed by the black rods and the sunstruck pools. At rest they hang from reed tips like folded scraps of old vellum. Their larvae grow inside beads of suspended water, never touching mud until adulthood. They pollinate the nearly colorless flowers, but they also inspect the rods, touching each blunt tip with long feet before lifting away. If a rod fails to draw water for more than a day, these drifters gather around it in a loose bell shape and hum until something underground answers.

There are also the root-knuckle trees, which are less passive than they appear.

They grip the shallow soil with exposed, jointed roots, each root swollen at intervals like a hand closing around stone. Their leaves are broad and polished, built to catch not only light but falling signals: droplets of particular size, spores at particular ripeness, the fine dust shaken loose when the boundary stones warm at noon. They flower near the ground, hiding sweetness under leaves where only low creatures find it. Their seeds will not open unless carried in a living mouth or pressed by a wet flank. They have outsourced decision to animals.

The reeds are listeners. Their fans turn a few degrees before the wind arrives. Along their blue-green blades run faint seams of harder tissue, mineralized by water drawn from the ancient slabs. In the quietest hour, if one watched long enough, one might see them align not with sun or breeze but with the hovering pavilion. They are not servants. Nothing here is exactly a servant. They are participants in a very old agreement.

And then there are the residents that do not have bodies in the usual sense.

The black rods live as thirst.

They lift water upward against the world’s preference, sleeve it along their sides, vanish it at their tips. Around them, eggs hatch differently, footprints fill from the center, and insects become careful. They are not conscious in an animal way, but they respond. They darken. They drink faster. They alter the taste of nearby pools. Fine organisms grow on them in vertical bands, each band a record of seasons, chemistry, and commands too old to be called commands.

The white arches live as permissions.

Small creatures cross them. Droplets change on them. Mud refuses to swallow them completely. They mark routes not for bodies of human scale, but for flows: of larvae, mites, wet air, mineral charge. A crab climbing over one emerges with different mud on its claws than it carried up. A many-eyed flat creature basking there at afternoon becomes, for a while, irresistible to pollinators and dangerous to mites. The arches distribute consequence.

The pavilion is the closest thing the hollow has to an elder.

It does not rule. It receives.

Its three curved panels hover above their reflections like thoughts reluctant to touch the ground. Dry life avoids it. Wet life approaches. It blooms with color when neared, not to attract but to acknowledge. Against it, skin becomes readable. Water becomes address. The pavilion stores no history in any simple way; rather, history passes through it whenever enough conditions coincide: heat, wetness, animal contact, noon geometry, the falling of a droplet from nowhere.

Perhaps the pavilion once belonged to those who made the hollow.

Perhaps it is one of them.

Not a machine left behind, but a surviving organ of a civilization that translated itself into habitat. Its makers may have relinquished hands, names, towers, and speech, choosing instead to persist as maintenance, instinct, fluid routing, seed law, shadow behavior. Or they may have died entirely, and the place is only the afterlife of their engineering. The distinction matters less each season. What remains can still answer.

If there are ghosts, they are hydrological.

They move when water reverses under noon light. They appear as warmth in grooves traced by a wet finger. They gather in the image that flashes across the pavilion: larger wetlands, stranger stars, horizon-grazing machines, rivers braided with luminous code. These are not memories shown to an audience. They are diagnostic dreams. The hollow briefly compares itself to what it once was, finds the difference acceptable, and continues.

So the inhabitants are many, but they are also one thing: a distributed creature made of ecology and instruction.

Its blood is warm mineral water.

Its nerves are runnels, roots, rods, insect paths, and shadow-lines.

Its thoughts are swarms.

Its immune system is predation.

Its memory is carried by wet contact.

Its language is droplet height, flank pressure, algal sheen, the timing of flowers, the direction a pool ripples after an impossible fall.

Nothing in the hollow lives alone. Even the smallest mite is partly an instrument. Even the oldest artifact is partly an animal. Every resident is both itself and a function of the whole: eater, carrier, sensor, valve, seed-key, cleaner, lure, warning, archive.

What lives there is an ancient garden that learned to inhabit its gardeners.
