What lives there is not a single animal so much as a crowded arrangement of dependencies, a vertical village of bodies fitted to stone.

The upper inhabitants are the birds.

They own the ledges by right of number and altitude. They are small to medium cliff birds, dark-backed and narrow-winged, built for sudden flight through broken air. Their throats are pale, so when they turn in the dimness the shelves seem to flicker with bits of cloud. They nest so close together that individual bodies vanish into a restless black-brown seam along the rock. Their feet are hard and gripping. Their wings are long enough for the open heights outside, but inside the cleft they fold themselves into cramped, exact motions: hop, sidle, flare, settle, strike.

They are never fully silent. Even sleeping, they shuffle and mutter. Their young rasp from shadowed nests. Their quarrels are sharp, brief, and constant. They bring the outside world inward—seeds stuck to mud, insects caught over the valleys, scraps of grass, feathers, bits of shell, dust from other cliffs. They also feed the throat with waste. Their droppings fall in pale streams and crusts, turning bare stone into a harsh kind of fertility. Much of the lower life is, in one way or another, eating the birds.

Below them live the cleaners and harvesters.

Beetles work the guano banks with a patient metallic seriousness. Some are glossy black, some rust-colored, some dusted gray-white until they look like chips of mineral. Their legs are strong, their bodies compact, their antennae always reading the air. Pale larvae fatten beneath crusts where the warmth holds. Flies gather in knots and lift all at once when disturbed, then resettle a handspan away. Moths cling to stone like flakes of soot or old skin. Crickets with absurdly long legs move in jerks through the crevices, making dry threaded sounds that seem to come from the rock itself.

The spiders are everywhere once the eye learns how to see them. They do not make grand webs. They fasten small traps between ribs of stone, beneath shelves, across the mouths of holes, in corners where falling dust and insect traffic meet. Some are pale and soft-bodied from generations in the dimness. Others are dark, flat, and quick, vanishing sideways into cracks. They live on hesitation: the pause of a fly, the misstep of a cricket, the moth startled from its wall.

Centipedes hunt where the stone sweats. They are the color of old blood or damp root, jointed and flexible, with antennae that taste the dark ahead of them. They move with unnerving purpose, not hurried but certain. Smaller creatures make way for them. Even the frogs, when they feel that many-legged touch in the grit, draw themselves tight and still.

The frogs belong to the water pockets.

They are small, blunt, and mottled, patterned like iron stains, lichen shadows, and wet grit. When dry weather comes they disappear so completely that the basins seem empty except for black water and a few drowned wings. Then rain arrives, and the frogs return as if condensed out of the walls. Their voices are not loud in themselves, but the cleft multiplies them until the floor seems to pulse. They sit half-submerged in basins no larger than a bowl, throats working, eyes bright and beadlike above the surface.

Their skin holds the place’s colors: red-brown, charcoal, gray, tarnished green. Their bellies are pale. Their toes are delicate and surprisingly strong, able to cling to damp stone faces where water slides in threads. They lay eggs in temporary pools, gambling each generation against evaporation. Many fail. Enough do not.

Under stones and in wet seams live the crabs, or things near enough to crabs that a traveler would use the word before doubting it.

They are high-mountain scavengers, flattened and strong-clawed, with shells the color of soaked basalt. They wedge themselves into crevices during the day, only their legs or one black eye showing. At night they emerge cautiously, tapping the stone ahead of them. They eat what the cleft gives: dead insects, fallen nestlings, eggshell, frog spawn, scraps of bird, softened moss, each other when necessary. Their claws are not large, but they are precise. They pick life apart grain by grain.

They seem improbable at that height, but the cleft has made a bargain for them: warmth from below, water from mist, food from above. They are creatures of that bargain. Outside, on the exposed mountain, they would be absurd. Inside, they are inevitable.

The lizards hold the warm stone.

They are flat-bodied, quick-eyed, and patient. Their scales match the mineral bands so well that they appear only when they move. In the morning, when the first heat enters the rock, they spread themselves along shelves and slabs, drinking warmth through their bellies. Their mouths open slightly in the damp heat. Their eyes do not soften. They wait for flies, beetles, moths, small crickets, careless froglets.

They know the exact borders between safety and exposure. A lizard may sit motionless for an hour, then become a streak and vanish into a slit too narrow for a finger. Their shed skins catch in rough places and hang there like translucent scraps of old weather.

The snakes come silently.

They are not many, but they are remembered by everything small. Their bodies are lean from climbing, patterned in broken bands that resemble shadow and mineral streak. They move through the cleft’s vertical maze with a slow liquid intelligence, crossing ledges, entering holes, following the scent of frog, mouse, egg, and warmth. After rain they visit the pools. During nesting season they rise higher.

A snake in that place is less an intruder than a question asked repeatedly: what is unwatched, what is reachable, what has grown careless? It leaves behind little evidence except a skin turned inside out among stones, or a sudden absence in a frog basin, or birds screaming at a ledge where nothing can be seen.

The small mammals live mostly in rumor.

Mountain mice, shrews, and narrow-faced insect-eaters use the lower shelves and dry pockets. They are quick, dusk-colored, and nervous, with whiskers constantly touching the air. They feed on larvae, beetles, seeds fallen from nests, and soft-bodied things found under crusts. They line hidden hollows with feathers stolen from the floor. Their paths are tiny polished runnels through dust and guano, invisible until a body runs them.

They are among the few warm-blooded creatures of the lower cleft, and because of that they are valuable. Owls know this.

The owls are the cleft’s quiet authorities.

They do not crowd the place like the smaller birds. One or two may occupy a high recess, broad-headed and mottled, nearly indistinguishable from stone until their eyes open. They hunt at the hour when the throat’s sounds change register: birds settling, insects rising, frogs beginning, mice taking risks. Their wings are soft enough that the cleft does not announce them. A mouse hears nothing. A frog may only feel a pressure in the air.

The owls cough pellets onto shelves below their roosts. In those pellets are bones, fur, beetle shells, lizard jaws: compressed records of the lower world. Beetles later open them. Moisture softens them. The cleft takes them back.

There are also the almost-inhabitants: mites in feather beds, worms in guano paste, blind springtails in wet powder, bacteria making invisible feasts of droppings and mineral seep, lichen eating light where light is scarcely available, moss gripping water one cell at a time. They are not background. They are the hidden machinery. Without them the place would choke on its own abundance.

Nothing there lives independently.

The birds depend on the open air beyond the cleft but return to the ledges for safety and heat. The insects depend on the birds’ waste. The frogs depend on the water that almost leaves. The crabs depend on whatever falls. The snakes depend on frogs and eggs and small bodies. The owls depend on the nervous traffic below. The stone, in turn, is altered by all of them: whitened, polished, scratched, warmed, fouled, seeded, bored, slickened.

Over time, the inhabitants have become shaped like the cleft’s demands.

They are narrow, clinging, flat, mottled, quick to hide, quick to breed when water comes, able to endure crowding, darkness, ammonia, mineral warmth, and sudden scarcity. Few of them are beautiful in an open, ornamental way. Their beauty is functional and close-up: the lacquered back of a beetle, the breathing throat of a frog, the exact placement of a lizard’s toes, the pale flash of a bird’s neck in shadow, the crab’s black claw sorting through fallen feathers.

If the place has a true resident larger than all the rest, it is the community itself: a throat-fauna, a warm dark congregation, a body made of many bodies.

It breathes in weather and exhales noise.  
It drinks mist and seepage.  
It eats what falls.  
It nests in cracks, lays in pockets, hunts along seams, sleeps against heated stone.  
It wastes nothing for long.

The cleft is not merely occupied. It is inhabited down to the smallest damp grain.
