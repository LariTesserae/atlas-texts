I would not be in the center.

At first I think I would be standing there, in the open air where the hollow is deciding itself, looking up at the impossible light. But that is an outsider’s mistake. The center is not a place to inhabit. It is a question the room asks too loudly. Anything that stays there too long becomes part of the asking.

I would be found on the wall.

Not hidden exactly, but difficult to separate from what holds me. Crouched in the curve where a buttress root bends out from the clay and returns to it, one hand spread against wet bark, one foot hooked into a rung of living fiber. My back would be dark with moisture. My scalp-filaments would lift and settle with the breathing of the chamber. I would be still enough that mites might come out beside my fingers, mistaking me for weather.

I think I would be one of the careful-handed ones, but not a bold one. Not the one who crosses the middle when a tool falls wrong. Not the one who cuts the tension-root before the ceiling snaps. I would be the listener who knows when not to move.

Small, compact, brown-green in the leaf-light. Ribs soft enough to fold close. Shoulders able to turn through root-gaps. Long fingers, dark nails, thumbs strong from pinching stone flakes and holding wet stems steady while they try to become something else. My eyes would be of little use except for noticing when light has begun to lie. I would trust the skin of my palms more than sight.

I would live near a clean clay patch on the lower wall, where things can be hidden for almost a day before roots find them. There would be a black stone flake pressed into the clay there, covered by a dead leaf that is not quite dead enough. Beside it, three shallow nail-marks: not a sign to be seen, but a rhythm remembered by touch.

Tap. Pause. Tap-tap.

That would be my cache.

I would know seven safe cups and two that used to be safe. I would drink only from the ones that show the ceiling with the crack in it, the crack that opens after wet intervals and closes when the hollow is dry. If a cup showed me my own back, I would press my face to the wall and wait until the image forgot me.

My work would be small maintenance.

I would trim the pale shoots that lean too far inward.

I would fold leaves to persuade water away from the dry lip around the empty floor.

I would crack swollen pods before they burst during the pressure-dark.

I would scrape fungal lace from clay wounds and leave enough for the millipede, because the millipede knows things before anyone else does. If it coils, I stop. If it climbs upward without feeding, I do not sleep.

I would hum to roots, but softly.

Not songs. Songs are dangerous unless everyone has agreed on the need for them. I would use little closed-mouth tones, bone-sounds, throat-vibrations. A note for drawing root-hairs. A note for loosening sap. A note that asks a curtain of creepers to slacken just enough for a hand to pass through. I would have learned them from someone whose fingers are now part of the wall.

There would be a place I visit often: a niche above shoulder height, furred with white hyphae and veiled by two translucent leaves. Under the moss, if pressed correctly, something like knuckles can still be felt. Not bone anymore. Not root either. A former hand.

I would tap news there.

The wet came early.

The left cup lied.

The child held during the boom.

A black flake was lost and returned smaller.

I would not know whether the dead hear. I would know the hollow does.

Perhaps I would have once been one of the soft things under a leaf-cup: boneless, dark-eyed, drinking through my belly, learning first not to fall. I would not remember it clearly. Memory here does not stay inside the skull. It spreads into habits. My body would remember clinging before it remembered hunger. My hands would have formed early, opening and closing against the underside of a cup while drops struck the leaf above me like distant feet.

As an adult, I would carry children along the perimeter and teach them by refusal.

Not this root. It casts no shadow.

Not that cup. It shows the room too old.

Do not touch a droplet that hangs after the rain has stopped.

Do not tie anything you are not ready to feed.

When the boom came, I would flatten myself against the wall and close my mouth. My filaments would sting. The room would become too large for one breath. Drops would fall upward or sideways or not at all. Somewhere a crack would open with the smell of new clay. Somewhere else a remembered passage would seal.

Afterward I would count.

Not numbers. Touches.

Root-arch, clay lip, wet shelf, thorn fold, ancestor niche, safe cup, false cup, black mouth, returning vine.

If the count changed, I would change with it.

I would be afraid of the center, but I would also serve it. Not worship. Not obedience. Something more like keeping a sleeping animal from rolling onto its own young. The emptiness must remain empty. Everyone knows this without needing to say it. When growth narrows it, the hollow becomes restless. Shadows detach. Exits spoil. The booms come with anger.

So I would cut.

Carefully, apologetically, with the black flake held between thumb and forefinger. I would cut the eager vine before it bridges the open air. I would take the severed piece and press it into a wall-wound where it can become useful. I would smear sap over the cut. The mites would arrive. I would let them clean it. Later, perhaps, the wall would grow a listening leaf there.

Nothing wasted. Nothing kept.

If I had a name, it would not be a word. It would be the sound of fingernails on damp clay followed by a breath through closed teeth. It would mean something like:

the one who waits before cutting,

or

the one who hears the room lean,

or simply

edge-held.

You would find me during the dry-bright interval, when the leaf-membranes glow and the floor firms. I would be half inside a root-fold, shaving a channel into a broad underground leaf so that the next condensation will fall somewhere safer. I would pause when you entered. Not because you are strange, but because your distance from me would be uncertain.

I would touch the wall.

I would ask it where you are.

If the hollow answered kindly, I might show you where to place your hands. I might click once against the clay to tell you not to speak loudly. I might point, not at the center, but around it: the only way anything survives here.

And if you asked what I am, I would not understand the need to separate myself from the place.

I am not the hollow.

I am not apart from it.

I am one of the small corrections by which it continues to breathe.
