I would be found near the station platform, where the rails are still straight but no longer insist on being railway.

Not at the center, not in the municipal factory with its small old heart turning every few minutes. Not in the chimney crowns, where the dead become weather. Not deep in the dry store, where the severe ones keep paper from the air. I would be at the place where arrival has become maintenance: the platform edge, the tram shed, the green trellis where the service carts wait in their slow schedules, the drainage cut just beyond the embankment breathing black water through sedge.

At first I think I would be a person there.

One of the tram-watchers, perhaps. Someone with a resin-stiff apron, a knife kept clean, a coil of fiber over one shoulder, and a habit of touching rails before stepping over them. I would walk beside the compact carts when they emerge, one hand on their side rail, not guiding exactly, more accompanying. I would know where the buried cable runs shallow. I would know which patches of moss over the sleepers can bear weight and which must be bridged with boards. I would carry a little brass gauge for charge and damp, a scraper for lichen that has grown too thick over a contact, a paste for places where iron has begun to show its dangerous shine.

My work would be passage.

Not speed. Not transport in the old sense. Passage as an agreement renewed.

I would trim the trellis so the cart can leave the shed without tearing leaves. I would clean its wheels of root-hair and grit. I would check the algae lamp at the platform sign before dusk, because arrivals should not come into total uncertainty. I would oil what still needs oil and encourage what has learned to move by swelling, tightening, drinking. When rain begins, I would be among those who wake easily, pulling my boots on in the dark, crossing the platform under the leaf membranes to see whether the gutter above the ticket office has unfolded correctly.

The old enamel sign there might still read STATION, though cracked through the O. Beneath it someone long ago may have hung a newer tag: TRANSFER / ROOT-LINE / OUTSIDE GOODS. I would have polished neither. I would only keep them legible.

There would be a little room behind the platform wall where I sleep when it is my turn to watch the carts through a wet night. Its door would be willow-framed, with a latch-vine that must be unlooped from below. The room would smell of waxed cloth, damp paper, ceramic tea, and the faint metallic warmth of the engine batteries. A shelf would hold tags, schedules, seed packets, dry gloves, three spare lamps, and a cup turned upside down so no filament can find its lip.

But after a while I do not remain only a person.

The town would not permit that kind of separateness for long, and I do not think I would want it.

My boots would learn the platform better than my eyes do. My hands would become stained in the ordinary way: green at the nails, brown in the cracks of the knuckles, a gray polish from old iron. I would begin to hear the difference between a cart resting and a cart waiting. I would know when a rail is cold from weather and when it is cold from a break in the buried line. I would stop thinking of the trellis as a thing to cut back and begin thinking of it as a colleague with tendencies: overgenerous in spring, brittle after three dry weeks, sulky if pruned on the north side before rain.

Children sent to help me would find me dull at first. I would give them careful tasks. Rotate these seedlings. Sweep that channel, but do not scrape it bare. Count the drops from this joint in one minute and tell me whether it is a leak or a promise. They would want to ride the cart. I would let them, but only after they had cleaned their soles and hung up their tools. The town would be full of lessons that look like chores because most true lessons are.

I would become a keeper of thresholds more than a tram-watcher.

The platform is a threshold: between inside and beyond.  
The cart shed is a threshold: between stored motion and permitted motion.  
The drainage ring is a threshold: between the town’s damp law and the paler fields.  
Every door in the place is a threshold, and every threshold is a negotiation.

So perhaps my trade would have a local name: not gatekeeper, not porter, not engineer. Something softer. A word meaning one who keeps openings from becoming wounds.

I would inspect the edges.

That would be my place in the civic organism. I would notice where passage risks becoming exposure. Bare metal on a hinge. A gutter spilling beyond the cistern mouth. A lane narrowing past usefulness. A stranger’s boot bringing seeds the roots do not recognize. A child leaving a knife on a bench long enough for a filament to taste it. These are small emergencies, but the town is made of small emergencies answered before they become events.

If a stranger came in from the fields, I would be among the first to meet them, not because I am important, but because I stand where the town becomes vulnerable.

I would not greet them quickly. I would look at their shoes. I would look at their hands. I would ask whether they carry grain, metal, medicine, news, or hunger. I would give them the brush and wait while they cleaned their soles. If they were offended, I would let the silence explain what words cannot. If they were frightened, I would offer tea from the platform room, pale and slightly bitter, and a dry stool away from the leaf-screen.

I would tell them three things:

Do not force what resists.  
Do not waste what falls.  
Do not leave anything bare.

Then, if they stayed, I would hand them something to do.

But I suspect I would not remain fully human in the imagination of the place. I would become one of those arrangements the town recognizes.

The rails would know my weight. The latch-vine would loosen for my wrist before I touched it. The moss beside the platform would grow dense where I stand waiting in rain. A willow rib might lean over my workbench because I always hang damp capes there. The route between my sleeping room and the tram shed would remain open even during a season when no carts run, because my feet and the roots would have agreed on it.

If I grew old there, I would grow quieter, not from sadness but from improved hearing.

My knees would stiffen. My hands would remain useful. Younger watchers would climb the trellis for me while I stood below, telling them which membrane to spare and which vine had earned cutting. I would become one of those people who knows dates by repairs: the winter the south rail lifted; the year the little engine learned to draw from the new bioelectric mat; the dry season when we almost lost the platform cistern; the rain that came at night and filled every gutter so fast the central engine cycled before dawn.

Eventually I would be less a watcher of arrivals than a memory of how arrival should be done.

My tags would be in order. My tools hung high. My room swept. My cup upside down.

And after my body was wrapped and taken to the warm vaults beneath the chimney towers, I think some part of me would still be found at the station.

Not as a ghost. This town has better uses for the dead than haunting.

I would rise as heat. I would enter vapor. I would feed a chimney tree whose roots have gone down through soot, brick, and names. Perhaps, years later, a young tram-watcher waiting in drizzle would look up and see white steam loosening from the chimney crown, and the platform lamp would glow a little earlier than expected, and the latch-vine of the old room would slacken at the correct touch.

That would be enough.

So: I would be found at the station platform, at the town’s kept edge, where the slow carts sleep under green trellis and the rails vanish into leaf-shadow.

I would be a threshold-keeper: human at first, then habit, then part of the town’s memory of passage. A soft-footed maintainer of openings. A person whose work is to let things enter without letting emptiness in.
