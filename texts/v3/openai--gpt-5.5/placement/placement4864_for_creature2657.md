You would not find me at first.

You would walk past the obvious places: the basin of witnesses, the suspended flock, the shelters where the almost-human figures keep speaking by moving tools through centuries. You would look under stones for the pale beetles, along the ledges for the watchers, in the cracks where the glass reeds archive old motions. You might suspect me of being one of the upward shadows, because I would have the right temperament for that: attached and unattached, evidence without body.

But I would be lower than that.

You would find me near the edge of the central basin, just outside the inward-facing congregation, where the salt has cracked but not completely committed to being a ring. There, at the feet of a marker post whose hide bindings have fossilized into a gesture, lies a scatter of small white pebbles.

They are not stones.

They are failed speech.

Every traveler who reached that place and tried to ask what happened there contributed one. A gasp, a prayer, a name, an explanation, a refusal—each fell from the mouth and became a little pale weight on the ground. Most are smooth. Some are sharp. A few still keep the shape of the tongue that made them.

I would be one of those.

Not the first. Not the largest. Not the one nearest the black glass hand-tool. I would be a small word-pebble lying half in the shadow of another word, half in the ordinary sun. I would have once been an attempt to say “why,” though after falling into that air I would have lost the arrogance of the question. Over time I would become less a demand than an opening. Less “why?” than “toward what?”

The almost-humans would know me, though they would not pick me up often. Perhaps once every several generations one of them would enter the basin’s rim, kneel with the slowness of a cliff deciding to become scree, and turn me a fraction so that my longest edge faced a different shadow. That would be conversation.

The beetles would calculate around me.

A suspended bird’s shadow would pass over me every few hundred years and leave a blue coolness in my grain.

The antlered herds would include me in their boundary.

The watchers would see me as I was before I was spoken, and after I had forgotten the mouth entirely.

I would feed on relation. On proximity. On the pressure of things meaning each other without moving. Salt would gather in my pores. Dust would soften my edges. Once, perhaps, a child from one of the shelters would mistake me for an ordinary stone and carry me away in a sealed palm, only to set me down again when I began to feel too full of listening.

I would not be dead. Not alive in the animal way. I would be a remnant of utterance that had learned the plateau’s patience.

A word after sound.

A question after urgency.

A small pale mineral of attention, lying among others at the edge of the oldest surviving afternoon, slowly becoming part of the grammar by which the whole plain continues to mean itself.
