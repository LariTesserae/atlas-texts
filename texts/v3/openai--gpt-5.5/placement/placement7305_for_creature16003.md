You would not find me in the open water at first.

The open basins are too vast, too bright, too without edge. Anything with a single body would be made ridiculous there, reduced to a mote crossing a cathedral of pressure. Nor would I be one of the great storms, though for a while that is tempting: to be a transparent animal of density, dragging sound over newborn stone, waking fields of mineral spines and leaving no remains. But storms are too complete in their passing. They have a beginning, a body, a disappearance.

I would be something less dramatic and more persistent.

You would find me on a high shelf, close to the upper brightness, where the water has begun to thin into falling vertical ocean but has not yet become air. The shelf would be pale, newly made, opalescent under a skin of silt. It would have risen only recently from mineral suspension: a terrace grown by some pressure-rule, still warm only in the structural sense, still deciding whether it is floor or precipitate.

There would be a crack running through it.

Not a natural-looking crack. Too straight. Too long. A line opened by stress, then partially sealed by milky mineral deposition. Along that seam, where alkaline water leaks upward in cool threads, where metals gather and light folds into silver ladders, I would live.

Not as a creature.

As a stain.

At first I would look like green-black tarnish on stone: a thin, wet darkness clinging to the seam, darker at the center, coppery at the edges. If the water were very still, you might see that the stain was not flat. It would be made of filaments, bead-chains, transparent blades no higher than a fingernail, veils stretched between mineral nubs. A soft colony pretending to be discoloration.

If you came closer, though no animal comes closer here, I would become less like growth and more like an instrument.

I would be a photosynthetic membrane threaded with conductive mineral hairs.

A colonial skin.

A listener.

I would drink the broken light that arrives from above after being folded through kilometers of suspended sea. I would take carbon from the water, ions from the vent, trace metals from the seam, and make of them a low, dark persistence. I would not eat. I would not hunt. I would not flee. My first verb would be cling.

But clinging would not mean stillness.

Through my filaments, charge would pass. Not thought, not yet, but difference. A pressure wave crossing the basin would flatten me in one direction. A cold layer descending from the rain-wall would slow my chemistry. A grid of heatless light waking under the terrace would make my surface contract in thousands of tiny dark pulses. When the stone beneath me began to grow, I would know it by adhesion before I knew it by shape.

I would know the world as change in contact.

Not distance. Not horizon. Not object.

Only gradients.

Salt increasing.

Light whitening.

Metal singing.

Stone loosening.

Current reversing.

A hollow opening somewhere below, announced minutes before by a trembling in the mineral seam.

I would not be one individual. That would be too fragile a fiction for this place. I would be a patch, a colony, a temporary agreement among cells. Some of me would be awake in the light. Some of me would sleep under mineral glaze. Some of me would drift already as spores in the clear water beyond the ledge. Some of me would be dead, but useful, holding the living layers against the stone. Some of me would be almost not mine: bacteria, mineral crystals, trapped bubbles, metallic threads grown through me by the terrace itself.

My boundary would be negotiated.

A little life.

A little chemistry.

A little circuitry.

A little weather caught on rock.

When the great system moved beneath me, I would be one of the places where it became sensitive.

Not its mind. That would be too grand.

Not its eye. There are no eyes here.

More like a taste bud on the drowned plateau. A dark receptive patch along a seam where water, mineral, light, and charge meet densely enough to register one another.

If the basin was calm, I would spread.

Slowly, not with the confidence of a forest, but with the caution of something that knows surfaces are temporary. I would send green-black threads into hairline fractures. I would glaze pale stone with a living film. Around the vent I would raise translucent blades, each one angled to the folded light, each one flexing when density-winds passed over the shelf. My outer layers would mineralize into a glossy membrane to keep sudden chemistry from burning me away.

If the water above me began to sing, I would answer by changing shape.

The metallic tone would enter through the seam and tremble along my conductive hairs. Cells would open, close, flatten, darken. Pigments would shift. Stored ions would move in waves across my surface. To an observer, perhaps I would seem to ripple without current touching me.

That would be my speech, if I had speech.

A darkening.

A contraction.

A release of spores.

A tiny rearrangement of mineral and living matter along the crack.

When the shelf collapsed, as it eventually would, I would not survive as a self.

The pale terrace would shudder first. Bubbles would gather under the mineral skin. The straight crack would brighten. The stone would lose its certainty. Then the floor would slump into fog, and I would be torn open, shredded into ribbons, beads, cells, armored seeds. The colony would end.

But ending, here, is one of the ways a thing travels.

Parts of me would sink into the newly clouded basin. Parts would be carried upward into rain within water. Parts would catch on crystal rods kilometers away. Parts would sleep inside mineral pores until light returned. Most would become chemistry again. Enough would not.
