I think I would not arrive at the center first.

The city would not let me.

I would come in at the basin edge, where the buildings stop too abruptly and the unfinished plain lies exposed, gray and cold, with its shallow pools reflecting architecture that has not yet earned the right to exist. I would be standing beside one of the survey pylons, gloved hand on its wet metal side, feeling it click faintly through my palm as it counted pulses in the ground.

At first I would think I was a traveler.

I would have the wrong boots. I would look too long into reflective water. I would count a row of windows once, then feel the temptation to count again, and an old woman crossing behind me would say without stopping:

“Don’t.”

That would be my welcome.

For a while I would be given very simple work. Carrying sealed vessels. Scraping frost from calibration plaques. Sitting beside walls that had begun to lean inward from remembering too much. I would be useful because I could remain still. The city would notice this before anyone else did.

Not brave. Not wise. Still.

A person who can sit quietly beside a distressed wall has value there.

Eventually I would become attached to the district between the black river and the central plaza: not the oldest part of the city, but the part most often in need of interpretation. The streets there would have unreliable left sides. Sound would arrive with a limp. Condensation would collect under lintels in beads too regularly spaced to be weather.

You would find me in a narrow office built into the thickness of a wall, behind a door that is never warmer than the street.

The room would have no window, though daylight would enter it in a rectangle every afternoon and lie across my desk like a formal accusation. There would be shelves of sealed moss, jars of sorted water, small brass instruments, notebooks with waterproof pages, a kettle that only boils when addressed correctly, and a chair that remembers three previous occupants better than it remembers me.

I would be an auditor.

Not one of the grand auditors called when a district chimes or a bridge must be judged older than itself. A lesser one. A seam-auditor. A threshold listener. Someone sent for when a door has accepted the wrong name, when a gutter begins to carry grief-water into a drinking cistern, when a room develops an extra ceiling and the family inside cannot agree whether it was always there.

I would wear pale gloves, as auditors do, though mine would be stained at the fingertips with moss-green and brass oxide. My coat would be patched with dull foil at the shoulders. My hood would have a crack across the left lens, repaired with transparent resin grown from sediment. I would carry an angle comb, an oath thermometer, a spool of red thread for marking temporary truths, and a notebook whose handwriting changed depending on which district I was in.

Children would be told I was safe to approach, but not to startle.

Not because I would be dangerous. Because I would be listening to things that startle slowly.

Most mornings, I would begin at the river.

The black ribbon would move below the bridges, half a second displaced, its surface late to itself. I would stand on the oldest bridge available and lower a small cup on a chain. Not to drink. To compare. River-water has a particular patience. It does not answer quickly. I would hold the sealed sample against my ear and hear, very faintly, dates rubbing against one another like fish bones.

Then I would walk the seam-streets.

I would read frost.

I would read handrails.

I would read the underside of balconies where indoor rain had formed overnight from breath, tea steam, and unsent letters. I would touch walls and ask, silently, what kind of pressure they were carrying.

Some walls would be merely cold.

Some would be lonely.

Some would be keeping family secrets because no one living had the strength to keep them anymore.

Those I would mark with chalk and recommend for moss arbitration.

I think the city would not make me fully human for long.

No one who listens professionally to infrastructure remains entirely separate from it. At first I would only learn its habits. Later, it would learn mine. A bench near my office would warm by a fraction when I passed, though benches are not supposed to choose favorites. A drain would stop swallowing my dropped keys. The calibration line outside the tea room would frost over in the shape of my bridge-name.

I would begin to wake before district chimes.

I would know, in my teeth, when two neighborhoods were preparing to reconcile. I would set down my cup, stand, and wait for the tone to pass through the masonry. Other people would hear a soft municipal bell. I would hear negotiation: gravity conceding a corridor, regret being rerouted, a staircase agreeing to remember its bottom step.

On such days I would make three marks in my book.

One for structure.

One for witness.

One for mercy.

The moss would trust me eventually.

This would matter more than official rank.

At first moss is indifferent to newcomers. It records them chemically, without opinion. But after years of kneeling beside railings, lifting strips carefully with a bone spatula, sealing them in glass, warming them just enough to persuade testimony without causing pain—after years of believing what they say even when citizens would rather I did not—the black moss under the quay rail would thicken when I approached.

Not affection exactly.

Recognition.

I would become known as someone who does not hurry witnesses.

People would come to my wall-office with small civic troubles.

A baker whose oven door had begun asking for her dead husband’s name.

A boy who had stepped around yesterday but left his shadow behind.

Two sisters disputing whether their mother’s cup belonged to the living cupboard or the consultable dead.

A bridge elder worried that a new bridge was pretending to be old in order to attract footsteps.

I would listen. I would take off one glove when the matter involved sorrow, because sorrow dislikes being handled through too much insulation. I would ask for names only when necessary. I would never invite anyone into a warm room.

Sometimes I would solve nothing.

I would simply determine which uncertainty was safe to live beside.

That, in the city, is a respectable outcome.

There would be a tea room I favored: a narrow one built into the masonry above a pump station, just below a residential block whose underside collected trembling rain. It would be warm enough in the center to fog masks, cold in the corners, and legal on most weekdays. The proprietor would be a woman with old hands and a young voice, because she had spent too much of her life speaking in the slow-sound square. She would ask, every time:

“You are still yourself?”

And I would answer:

“Mostly.”

Then, after the pause required by courtesy:

“And the street?”

She would say:

“Passable from this side.”

I would sit with my back to the wall and watch citizens come and go softly, each carrying their negotiations: grief wrapped in felt, errands in sealed jars, names kept under the tongue. I would not be lonely there, though I might be alone. The city would press close around the room. Pipes. Roots. Dead records. Condensation. Children’s footsteps overhead. A transformer moth sleeping somewhere warm enough to dream dangerously of summer.

On certain afternoons, I would be sent to the central tree.

Not often. No one goes casually.

The plaza would be no larger than a room, and yet every approach to it would feel longer than the last. The tree would stand bare and fused: root, pipe, petrified cloth, archive, drain, illicit dimension. Droplets on its branches would hold themselves in perfect hesitation.

I would not ask it questions directly.

Only fools and officials ask direct questions of things that have roots in the dead.

Instead I would sit on the cold rim of the plaza and open my notebook. If the tree wished to answer, it would alter the order of my pages. A complaint filed yesterday might move beside a contract from forty years ago. A child’s bridge-name might appear under a diagram of drainage repairs not yet needed. Once, perhaps, a blank page would fill with the smell of thaw-water.

I would copy what I could.

I would misunderstand most of it.

Still, the tree would tolerate me because I would not call tolerance blessing.

Over time, I think my work would change me into something quieter than a citizen and more personal than a machine.

My memories would become indexed by location. I would not recall childhood as a sequence, but as a set of safe routes: the first staircase I trusted, the first wall that answered me, the first time moss contradicted a magistrate and everyone bowed to it.

My body would adapt.

I would have the sideways catching reflex. I would know, without looking, when condensation was wrong. I would hear the difference between a door cooling from politeness and a door cooling from shame. My fingertips would thicken from reading surfaces. My voice would lower until it could pass through stairwells without being kept too long.

Eventually the maintenance intelligences would begin to mistake me for a minor part of themselves.

A lamppost instrument might pause its consistency work when I leaned against it.

A drainage grate might sort my tears before they fell.

A bench might ask, through grain and temperature, whether I intended to continue.

And I would.

That would be who I am there: continuation, professionally practiced.

Not a hero. Not a founder. Not one of the dead, though I would speak to them when floors were level. Not a priest of the tree, not an engineer of impossible laws. I would be a listener stationed at the damp seams, entrusted with small misalignments before they become disasters.

If you needed me, you would find me where the city is almost failing but has not yet admitted it.

Under a staircase raining upward.

Beside a door that has grown too warm.

On the oldest visible bridge, waiting for my shoes to become wet in the proper order.

In a wall-office with ordinary daylight entering from nowhere, writing down the civic condition of sorrow.

And if you asked what I was, I would probably answer in the local way:

“An auditor, mostly.”

Then, after listening to the street, I might add:

“Still myself, from this side.”
