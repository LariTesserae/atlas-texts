You would find me where the city is thinnest.

Not at the civic grove, though I would go there often enough, standing under the administrative trunks with my hood pulled low and sap-stiff gloves folded in front of me, pretending to understand what the bark was trying to become. Not in the factories either, though their sound would live in my bones: the wet belts, the wooden housings around iron teeth, the pumps coughing in their sleep. I would not be important enough to work near the main resin vats, or pious enough to serve the grove, or brave enough to descend often into the anchor chambers.

I think you would find me in the western upper wards, near the plateau edge, in the neighborhoods where the streets have begun to forget whether they were built for traffic or for weather.

There is an old station there, though no train has come for a century except as a pressure wave under the roots. Its platform is half roofed in leaf-membrane and half open to the wind. The sign above it still remembers a name nobody pronounces the same way twice. Small wingless birds nest inside the hollow letters. Below the platform, brick arches persist under the rootwood, red and damp and stubborn, and in certain fogs the station fills with the warmth of commuters who are no longer visible.

I would live in one of the former ticket offices.

The room would have grown smaller over the years, not by collapse but by courtesy. The walls, sensing that I am a single person, would have thickened inward to keep me warm. My bed would be strapped to two iron rings sunk into root, because the floor lifts before storms. My kettle would sit on a fungal heat box fed with old paper, spoiled leaf-bread, and petition scraps that the bark-readers said were safe to burn. Over the desk, I would keep three maps: one printed on cloth from before my grandmother’s time, one drawn by my own hand and corrected every morning, and one grown from a civic seedpod, useless except during drought.

I would be a membrane glazier.

That is what the guild would call me.

But the title would be too neat. In practice I would be part window-tender, part shadow surveyor, part trespasser, part minor clerk of impossible light.

The city’s windows are not glass. They are persuaded tissues, thin places in wall-bark where daylight has been allowed through without granting the outside full entrance. They cloud if neglected. They thicken if frightened. They split in hard frost. They sometimes begin reflecting the wrong sky, which is when someone like me is sent for.

I would carry oil in little copper bulbs, each one labeled by ward and season. Summer oil, bitter and yellow. Fog oil, gray as fish skin. Mourning oil, used only for houses where grief has made the doorways unreliable. I would carry bone scrapers, brass pins, a mallet, a spool of white fiber rope, a pouch of sacrificial nails, and a folded hood of smoked membrane for looking at disobedient shadows.

My hands would always smell of sap.

Not fresh sap only, but the older kind: mineral, medicinal, a little metallic, like green wood remembering machinery.

In the mornings I would walk the upper galleries while the fog still rose from the seams. I would tap the walls before touching them.

Three taps for rot.

Two taps for pressure.

One tap for courtesy.

Then I would listen.

A healthy wall answers with a low, full sound, like a throat clearing far away. A sick wall clicks. A wall that has begun to think too much may answer before it is struck.

Those are the ones I would mark in chalk and report to the nearest reader.

My official work would be simple enough. Rub oil into clouded windows. Keep the membranes from sealing over inhabited rooms. Cut away the fine, pale veins that creep across civic glass during pollen months. Teach new windows where to look.

But the western wards would make a second profession of me.

There, light behaves especially badly. Shadows climb stairs ahead of the feet that cast them. Rain reflected in a cistern may fall three days before the sky opens. A person passing a window at dusk may see themselves not as they are, but as the room will remember them after death.

So I would also measure shadows.

I would go out after noon with a box of brass rods and set them in plazas, along curbs, beside transit roots, under bridges that are not scheduled to exist until winter. I would record where the shadows fell and whether they belonged to the object producing them. I would note their temperature through gloves. I would test their edges with thread.

If a shadow pointed uphill, that would be acceptable but worth watching.

If a shadow detached from its body and remained after sunset, I would rope the area off.

If a shadow appeared before its object, I would file an advance maintenance request.

No one would read those requests quickly. The city would answer in its own time. Sometimes by growing a warning blossom. Sometimes by dropping a seedpod full of black grit. Once, perhaps, by sending a railcar without rails to stop beside me in an empty square, its doors opening onto a stairwell I had lost as a child.

I would not enter.

Or I would enter once, which is why I would become the kind of person others speak of carefully.

I think I would have an adhesion.

Not a dramatic one. Not enough to be sent below to the anchors. A patch on the left shoulder, maybe, where the skin has learned too much from leaning against bark during long measurements. It would be hidden under waxed wrappings most days. In cold weather it would ache when the city changed pressure. In rain it would itch toward walls.

People would pretend not to notice.

Children would notice immediately.

They would follow me through covered walks, asking whether I could hear decrees before they budded, whether windows spoke in sleep, whether I had ever seen the darker sky in a puddle move independently of the water. I would tell them no when the answer was no, and no when the answer was yes but dangerous.

They would call me Pane-Mender, or Old Greenglass, even before I was old.

I would be fond of them and afraid for them. Anyone who works with thresholds learns to fear curiosity in the young. The city loves children’s belief; it uses it too easily. A shortcut repeated three times by bright-coated children may become real before any adult can map it. A dare whispered into a sealed blossom may be taken as a petition.

Still, I would keep sweets in my coat pockets: resin chips wrapped in leaf paper, hard enough to suck for an hour.

I would have no spouse living, I think. The city would have taken that possibility from me in some quiet municipal way. Not swallowed a beloved outright, perhaps. Nothing so clean. More likely a person I loved would have gone through a doorway in rain expecting the cistern market and found instead an older version of the western slope, all glacier light and no city yet grown, and returned with white in their hair and no patience for rooms.

Or they would not have returned, except as condensation on certain windows.

That would explain why I know which doors not to open in grief.

It would also explain why I do not hurry.

My friends would be maintenance people and almost-officials: a cistern midwife with copper rings sewn into her sleeves; a pressure-listener who can identify a failing sap-main by the taste of his own teeth; a bark-reader who pretends skepticism but leaves offerings of ash at every unstable curb. We would drink bitter tea in the station office while the wind worried the roof membrane. We would argue about whether the city notices us as individuals or only as repeated motions.

I would say it notices rooms before people.

The bark-reader would say that is the same thing, in time.

At dusk I would climb the exterior stairs above the station, the ones that become too tall after rain. From there the plateau edge would fall away into cloud, and the aqueduct-vines would descend into whiteness like roots seeking an earth that had forgotten them. Behind me the factories would begin to glow: green-white, internal, patient. The towers would blacken into leaf and pipe. The loudspeakers would announce pollen advisories, district contractions, resin quotas, fog bells.

Sometimes one would say my name.

Not often. Once every few years.

When a seedpod loudspeaker speaks your private name into an empty plaza, you are expected to report. I would report, eventually. But first I would stand still and listen to how the city said it. The official speakers have a clipped tone, all weather and instruction. The older recordings blur vowels with rain. The city itself, when it uses a name, does not pronounce it like speech.

It says a name the way a root finds water.

Those summons would send me to places where windows had appeared without rooms behind them, or where a whole apartment block had begun reflecting a valley that no longer existed. I would oil the membranes. I would ask them, politely, what they were trying to see. I would wait with my ear near the surface.

Once, in a sealed schoolhouse, I might hear children reciting a route that had never existed until they spoke it.

Once, in a factory dormitory long folded inward, I might see through a green pane into the original city: stone streets, copper gutters, scaffolds, men and women carrying tools before the plants learned the shape of tools.

Once, high in a tower, I might look through a window and see not outward but inward: the city’s vascular darkness, amber channels, buried rails, anchors hanging in warm chambers, roots gripping rusted girders like memory gripping a name.

That would be the day I stopped thinking of myself as someone who repairs windows.

I would understand that I tend the places where the city decides what counts as outside.

After that, my work would become slower.

More careful.

I would speak to every membrane before touching it. I would stop cutting away certain veins. I would allow some windows to cloud, because not every room benefits from being seen. I would polish others until they shone like held breath. I would begin filing reports the bark-readers did not know how to archive.

“Window in Saint Orra’s Square has begun admitting yesterday’s rain.”

“Dormitory pane refuses daylight but responds to names of the dead.”

“Factory membrane facing east shows a bridge not yet grown. Recommend patience.”

“Station office window reflects occupant with leaves in lungs. No action requested.”

In time, perhaps, I would be offered anchoring.

Not formally. No one is offered it formally. The city arranges a circumstance. A bridge fails during fog. A pressure gallery spasms. A wall closes too eagerly around a worker’s sleeping body. There is an accident, and afterward separation becomes a philosophical matter.

But I do not think I would go below.

My loyalty would be to surfaces.

To membranes.

To the thin, oiled, translucent skins between room and weather, past and civic record, person and infrastructure.

When I died, I would not want a remembrance bed in the grove. I would not want my name to become a decree-colored scar or a blossom in some official canopy.

I would ask to be given to a window.

The guild would complain. The bark-readers would deliberate. The city would answer by growing, somewhere in the western station, a small oval membrane in a bricked-over ticket booth. They would place my body in the wall-niche beneath it, among root hairs and old mortar, with my mallet, my pins, my smoked hood, and the maps that had failed me.

Years later, someone would pass through that abandoned platform during rain.

Perhaps a child grown into a surveyor.

Perhaps a stranger from below, breathless from the climb, thinking the city is only plant and old machinery.

They would see a dim window glowing in a room no one uses. If they rubbed the membrane with a gloved thumb, it would clear for a moment.

Through it, they would not see me exactly.

They would see the western wards in correct light.

A street at its true length. A bridge in its proper century. A shadow falling where it should. The old mountains pale and worn under a sky that is, for once, only the sky overhead.

Then the membrane would cloud again.

And somewhere in the wall, very softly, there would be one courteous tap in answer.
