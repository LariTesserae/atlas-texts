At first I would think I belonged in the open water, among the fleas, in the visible drift—one of those bright, trembling commas by which the city’s currents reveal themselves.

But I do not think I would be that free.

Then I would think: perhaps a mussel, half-buried and necessary, a gate with a pulse, one of the old households through which the whole drowned room quietly passes. There is dignity in that. There is a kind of civic patience. But a mussel is too settled for me, too certain of its place in the architecture.

Nor would I be the salamander. The salamander is too complete an event. It enters and the city understands danger. It has a mouth like a law. I would not be so singular.

I would be found in the middle districts, where the sponge towers make the current hesitate.

Not at the ceiling, not in the deepest mud, but in the dense working quarter between them: below the blind rootlets, above the coldest silt, in the lee of a mussel ridge where the water brings food but does not tear. There, among worm chimneys and egg-strings and the slow porous shoulders of sponge, I would move with my house around me.

I would be a caddis larva.

Soft-bodied, hidden, unfinished.

A small wet intention inside a case built from whatever the city has given up.

My house would not be beautiful from a distance. Nothing here is made for distance. It would be a narrow, crooked tube, just large enough for my body to withdraw into, weighted carefully so the current cannot take me but not so heavy that I cannot drag myself forward. I would have made it grain by grain: a chip of old mussel shell, a black seed husk, three mica flakes, a curved splinter of fishbone, clay pellets hardened by my own secretion, one translucent scale from something eaten long before I found it.

I would not know that I was making architecture.

I would know only: this fits. This holds. This edge must be turned outward. This bright grain is too exposed. This hollow piece will trap air and must be rejected. This heavier shard will keep me from rolling when the cold pulse enters.

My mind, if it could be called a mind, would be all touch and correction.

I would taste the world with the front of myself. I would test surfaces. I would know fresh silt from old silt, shell from bone, living film from dead film, the tremor of amphipods from the heavier silence of the salamander. I would know when the mussels closed before I understood why. I would feel pressure change as weather, warning, command.

My streets would be the edges of things.

I would creep along the side of a sponge tower where the surface is furred with edible haze. I would cross snail-glazed shell and pause where the glaze is thinnest. I would descend into a shallow canyon between worm-tube neighborhoods, careful not to cloud the water too badly with my dragging. I would anchor myself when the current feeds well, extend my head and legs, gather, scrape, choose. At danger I would vanish backward into my little made tunnel, becoming for a moment not an animal but debris.

This would be my safety: to resemble what the city is made of.

A predator would see shell chip, seed husk, mineral accident. A fish-mouth might taste me and misjudge. A flatworm might pass over me like a shadow over a stone. The salamander’s intake might pull at the water around me, and I would hold, hold, hold with all the hooks and bristles of my small body, sealed inside my own arrangement of refuse.

I would be a citizen of salvage.

The city would enter me as fragments. I would return it as order.

Not grand order. Not plan. Not law. A larval order: local, blind, exact. A curve here to turn the flow. A heavier grain there to brace the entrance. A corridor pressed between two cases because many of us have chosen the same sheltering current. Over time, our private protections would become pavement. Our discarded houses would become ridges. Our ridges would slow water. Slowed water would drop silt. Silt would invite worms. Worms would ventilate the new ground. Mussels would settle against it. Sponge would rise.

I would not know I was helping build a city.

I would only be trying not to be eaten.

That seems right.

I would live in a body that expects transformation but trusts postponement. Somewhere inside me there would be the old instruction to become winged, to leave water, to enter air. But there is no air here except trapped silver under shells, no dry reed, no night sky, no swarm above the flood. My future would be written for a world sealed away from me.

Perhaps I would still prepare.

Perhaps, after enough feeding, enough building, enough surviving the pressure tremors and the clay slumps, I would seal myself into my case and begin the change. Inside my little mineral room, the larval mouth would be unmade. New limbs would dream themselves. Wings, thin as failed leaves, would form in darkness.

And then?

Then the city would keep me.

Maybe I would emerge into water as something meant for air, beating useless wings in a chamber that has never heard flight. Maybe I would drown at the threshold of my own becoming. Maybe I would be eaten by a fish before I had time to understand that the upper world promised by my body was absent.

But even that would not be waste.

My drowned adult body would soften. Bacteria would silver me. A snail would graze my wing. Amphipods would take what loosened. My old case would remain longer than I did. A larva not unlike me would find it lodged beside a mussel shell and use one of its mica grains. My attempted departure would become material.

So I think I would be found there:  
in the middle cold,  
under the dim root-hung ceiling,  
beside a sponge tower where the current bends,  
inside a portable hallway made of the dead.

I would be small enough to mistake for rubble.

I would be an animal wearing the city.

I would be one of the masons who does not know there is a city, carrying a private wall through public water, adding to the drowned metropolis by wanting only a narrow safety for my soft body.

And if there is any human part of me left, any memory of hands, it would live in that choosing:

this grain,  
not that one;

this shard turned inward,  
this husk sealed over;

build because the water moves,  
build because the mouth may come,  
build because softness needs a room.
