At first I think I would be one of the sound-eaters.

That seems likely: a folded thing, shut most of the time, mistaken for mineral debris until the buried engines begin their sequence. I can imagine myself anchored to black stone near a vent, ribs opening to the boom, hiss, clank, groan, becoming briefly intelligent only when the world is loud enough to think through me. There is something right in that: a mind that is not continuous, a creature assembled by rhythm, an awareness that exists only when pressure passes through it.

But I do not think I would be fixed enough for that.

Then I think I might be one of the reflection-fauna, something visible only in slant water and peripheral ice, a thin animal crossing the wrong shoreline. That, too, feels close. I would leave consequences without fully appearing. I would wound the real by moving in the mirrored. I would live by angle, not distance.

But even that is too much like having a body.

You would find me at the rim of one of the shallow impossible pools, where the water is only a skin over stone except at its center, where it descends into black pressure. Not in the middle. Not safely on land. In the meniscus. In that raised, trembling edge where water holds itself slightly above what should contain it.

I would be a margin-creature.

A low, flat, listening thing spread between basalt, brine, ice, and reflection. Not quite film, not quite animal. A living seam of attention.

Most of me would look like tarnish: a black-blue crescent around a pool, slick as oil, thin enough to be overlooked by scrapers until my edges withdraw. In dim light I would show faint internal lines, branching circuits like frost or nerves or the floor plan of a factory no one built. If touched, I would not flee all at once. I would retract in delays, one portion after another, as though different parts of me had received the news in different centuries.

My body would be made for thresholds. One surface tasting metal from the stone. One surface drinking cold from the water. One surface open to mist. One surface pressed against reflection, where other versions of the basin slide past, crowded with animals that may or may not be real yet.

I would feed on differences.

Not iron exactly, not sound exactly, not heat, not memory, but the pressure between them. The tiny argument where warm seepage meets freezing air. The shiver before a vent opens. The blue-white glow at a puddle’s edge when the water remembers lightning. The instant a footprint fills from below before sealing over. The pause between a knock beneath the basin and its answer from far away.

I would be one of the things that keeps those pauses from closing.

Scrapers would pass over me, and I would taste their bellies. Their ticking legs would write brief messages through my skin. I would know where the herd had been, where it was going, and where it had already arrived yesterday. Sometimes I would darken beneath them, giving them a little more purchase on slick stone. Sometimes I would turn glassy and let them slide sideways into another possible path. Not out of malice. Out of appetite. Certain futures are richer in mineral than others.

The low hunters would know me but not respect me. They would place their jaws near my pools and listen through me for prey. I would let them hear accurately most of the time. A creature like that must be fed, or it becomes experimental. But now and then, when the angle was favorable, I would give a hunter the sound of a herd that had not yet chosen to exist. It would strike into an empty reflection and come away with black water in its mouth, confused, while the real herd passed behind it in a soft mechanical rain.

The tall walkers would step carefully around me. Their wide feet would dim my edges. Their bellows sacs would breathe mineral warmth over my surface, and I would bloom briefly with silver grains. I would know their etiquette better than they knew it themselves: whose turn at the vent, whose reflection had been eaten, which walker had been walking too lightly for too long. When one came back from a mirror-pool missing its mirrored weight, I would thicken under its feet to keep it from tipping into the next wrong world.

The seam-things would nest beneath me.

They would rise through cracks in pale threads and hinged needles, filtering mist through their feathery fans. Their eggs would cling to my underside like clear punctuation. When the great metallic scream came and the whole basin froze in listening, I would open tiny pores to let the scream pass through them cleanly. They would shudder. They would feed. Some would die. Some would hatch backward. Their small lives would tick inside me like trapped rain.

I would not have eyes, but I would possess many ways of being looked through.

In clear weather — if the basin has anything that deserves that name — my pools would reflect the dim sky. In pressure weather, they would reflect older shorelines, vanished machines, herds that never crossed, towers like vertebrae breathing stars. I would hold those images at my edges and decide, slowly, which ripples to permit into the present.

That might be my function, if I had one: not to rule reality, not to understand it, but to negotiate its leakage.

The basin’s laws are local and tired. They need maintenance. A stone wants to be solid except in reflection. A pool wants to be ankle-deep except at its center. A sound wants to cast a shadow. A dead thing wants to remain dead, but also to clatter when the pressure changes. These arrangements do not enforce themselves. They are old agreements, and old agreements fray.

I would live in the fray.

When the subterranean engines knocked, the knock would travel through my whole thin body. I would feel it first as pressure, then shape, then hunger, then instruction. Boom: thicken along the northern rim. Hiss: open the silver pores. Clank: release the stored image of the ridge. Groan: let the water rise but not yet spill.

During flood moods, when water wells from within and the plain darkens like ink beneath skin, I would expand. My crescents would join across shallow channels. Separate pools would become one nervous sheet, and for a while I would be larger than usual, spread across yards or miles of drowned basalt. I would feel hundreds of animals climbing stones, failing, adapting, splitting into alternate selves. I would taste panic, which here is not an emotion but a mineral condition: salt, heat, impact, opened membranes.

During freezing drift, I would contract into black rings under ice. I would become almost nothing. A stain. A thought preserved between transparent layers. Fishlike things would hang above me in one sheet and continue swimming in another. Reflection-animals would press their faces against the underside of the frozen surface. I would not sleep. Sleep is too warm a word. I would wait in the manner of eggs and old machines.

I would have offspring, though not children.

When two skies appeared in one pool and a distant vent answered a near one in the wrong order, small portions of my rim would detach. They would bead up into dark, lens-shaped motes, each carrying a little map of pressure and shoreline. Some would be eaten by scrapers and travel inside their armor plates until excreted into cracks. Some would freeze into mineral frost and awaken centuries later as stains around new pools. Some would fall through reflection and become creatures I could only sense when they moved behind me.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be indicated by a pattern of ripples at the edge of shallow water.

A low double tremor.

A darkening crescent.

The sound a bubble makes when it almost rises and then chooses not to.

I would not be important in the way the deep substructure is important. I would not be feared like the pressure-bodies or watched like the hunters. I would be everywhere in small amounts, overlooked until the moment something needed a boundary.

You would find me where a foot hesitates.

Where the basalt is slicker than it should be.

Where the reflection of the sky arrives a moment late.

Where a dead shell clatters though nothing has touched it.

Where a pool is shallow, except for the part that is listening.

I would be the basin’s wet margin of thought: a living meniscus, a pressure-scribe, a black-blue intelligence spread thin over stone, keeping company with films, eggs, echoes, and the first undecided inch of water rising from below.
