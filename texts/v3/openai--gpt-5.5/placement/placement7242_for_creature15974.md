I think I would first mistake myself for someone who wanted height.

I would climb toward the upper service balconies after rain, drawn by the long view: black roofs shining, vapor snagged in gantries, yellow windows diluted by wet glass, all those disciplined silver lines running downward. I would think I belonged where the whole valley could be read at once.

But I would not stay there as a watcher of vistas. The view is too large to be useful for long. In that city, beauty would embarrass me unless I could put my hand to it.

So I would end up a little lower than the highest balconies, in the working seam between the catchment works and the inhabited districts: not quite mountain, not quite town. I would be one of the people who walks the wet margins where weather first becomes infrastructure.

A cistern reader, perhaps. Or a pressure-line walker. But not only that.

I would be a leak listener.

Not the emergency kind, not the person called when a wall is already sweating brown or a stairwell channel has begun to sing too loudly. I would be one of the routine ones, the almost invisible maintenance folk whose work is mostly noticing before noticing becomes alarm. I would carry a hooded lamp, a waxed notebook, chalk, a short wrench polished by use, a roll of gasket fiber wrapped in oiled cloth, and a listening rod with a wooden handle darkened by my palm.

My district would be the western rise, where the cloud comes in low and fast over the saddle and the first cold burden of rain finds the roof nets, slate lips, condenser wires, and upper cistern throats. I would know the back ways behind the glasshouses, the ladder down beside the old dye flue, the pipe bridge that stays warm enough to thaw your gloves, the inspection hatch whose hinge always sticks unless lifted slightly before turning. I would know which grates can be trusted under snowmelt and which are “public” only because no one has yet painted them forbidden.

I would live in a narrow room built into the side of an old retaining wall, above a reed-bed terrace and below the rear windows of a former mill turned seed store. My ceiling would tick after rain. One wall would be stone, always cool, always faintly damp no matter how many times it was sealed. There would be two shelves: one for tools, one for books and ledgers, though the books would gradually lose territory to spare washers, folded diagrams, labeled screws, a jar of mineral scale, a bundle of dried herbs, and pieces of unknown machinery kept because they looked as if they might explain something someday.

My bed would be built into the wall, with a curtain I pretended kept out the cold. Above the stove would be a rack for socks and gloves. By the door would be a boot drain so good that visitors would mention it. I would take pride in that, more than in any ornament.

I would not be important in a grand way. I would be one of those people other workers nod to because they have seen me everywhere inconvenient: crouched under a condenser trestle in needling mist, chalking a mark beside a sweating joint, standing silent with my ear to a pipe while carts pass behind me, lowering a tin cup on a string into a dark cistern mouth to smell the water before measuring it.

I would be quiet even by valley standards, not because I disliked people, but because my work would train me to leave space around sounds. I would dislike unnecessary speech near machinery. I would have the habit of holding up one finger without looking, asking whoever approached to wait while I listened.

People would say I had “pipe manners.”

This would be half compliment, half warning.

I would know the city vertically. Not by street names first, but by fall: upper fog nets to west cisterns, west cisterns to pressure galleries, galleries to turbine drop, turbine wash to dye cooling, dye cooling to reed beds, reed beds to lower storage, lower storage to outflow. I would understand that no water is ever only where it appears to be. A dry channel in rain would trouble me more than a full one. A warm patch on a cold wall would make me stop mid-sentence. A silent pump house would wake me from sleep faster than thunder.

My hands would always be marked. Black under the nails from old iron. Green at the creases from algae and wet brass. Small pale scars across the knuckles from reaching blind into housings where I should have waited for someone with longer tools. I would have strong calves from stairs and a permanent tenderness in one knee from slipping on a mossed service step during my apprenticeship, a mistake no one would let me forget because I had been warned twice and trusted the dry-looking middle.

I would dress like everyone else, but with certain preferences: a gray oilcloth coat patched in copper-green canvas, wool cuffs knitted too long so I could pull them over my fingers, a cap with a short brim, a scarf dyed the color of old slate. My lamp would have a little brass shield I polished without admitting I polished it. My notebook would be waterproofed and tied to me by cord, because losing notes to a drain is the sort of humiliation that becomes public folklore.

I would have a cat, though “have” would be too strong a word.

The cat would be attached to the warmth of my room, not to me, and would follow me only when my route included favorable pipes. A black-and-gray thing with white toes and a torn ear, known in three districts under three different names. I would call her Sluice. The glasshouse workers would call her Madam. Children near the seed store would call her Thumbprint. She would appear at inspections where she had no business being, sit on valve housings as if claiming responsibility for them, and once, during a winter pressure fault, she would refuse to cross a supposedly safe plate, thereby causing me to test it again and discover rot in the timber below.

After that I would never joke about her judgment.

My closest friend might be a tray gardener named Renn, whose hands smelled of sorrel and nutrient salts, who could diagnose mildew by mood alone, and who considered all water workers arrogant because we thought gravity was a personality. We would meet in a greenhouse aisle during bad weather, where the air would be briefly warm enough to make both of us stupid. Renn would give me bruised flat apples from the roof trees and complain that I tracked rust scale near the seedlings. I would repair a sticking vent for them and pretend it was not a courtship offering.

If I loved someone in that valley, it would begin by sharing dryness.

A pair of gloves left on a pipe for me. A note in chalk under a stair: west grille singing, mind your step. A tin of cress cakes wrapped in cloth and tucked into my tool satchel. My own gift would be practical to the point of almost rudeness: a better latch, a length of warm-route access cord, a hand-drawn map of which covered passages stayed least icy in east wind. Eventually, maybe, a key to the maintenance stair behind my room, which led to a small roof no one used except to inspect the gutter. From there, after rain, you could see the lamps come on in bands across the valley.

That would count as romance.

I would be superstitious in the local way, which is to say I would believe absolutely in consequences. I would touch certain gauge rims before reading them, not because the brass cared, but because the ritual would slow my hand enough to read correctly. I would distrust anyone who said “good enough” near a seal. I would keep, in my room, a box of failed parts labeled with dates and causes: split from frost, grit scoring, vine pressure, careless threading, old repair hidden, unknown vibration. The last category would bother me most.

On festival nights I would not be at the center of the music. I would be along the lantern route with the other maintenance people, half celebrating and half inspecting by colored light. Children would think we were solemn. We would be watching the crowd load on bridges, the condensation near electrical boxes, the way the old stair by Bath Three shifted under foot percussion. I would enjoy the bells and low pipes. I would also count exits.

But I would not be grim. I would have a dry humor, probably too dry for such a wet place. I would laugh hardest in doorways during weather too bad to dignify with complaint. I would cherish small luxuries: tea strong enough to taste like bark, a wall warm behind my shoulders, a clean channel after hours of scraping, the first minute after taking off soaked outer clothes when the inner wool still holds body heat.

I would love the valley in the only way that would make sense to me: by being irritated with it every day and loyal to it beyond reason.

And over time, I think, I would become less like a person with a job and more like one of the city’s minor senses.

Not its mind. Not its heart. Nothing so grand.

A fingertip, maybe.

One placed against pipe, stone, hatch, glass, rail, moss, cistern rim. Feeling for tremor. Heat. Wrongness. Flow. The small shiver that says water has found a path it was not given.

You would find me after rain on the western upper works, where the cloud still drags its belly across the stacks and the roofs shine black below. I would be standing under a dripping condenser frame, lamp hooked to my belt, one glove tucked under my arm, listening rod pressed to an old iron main. My notebook would already be damp at the corners. Sluice would be somewhere above me on a warm pipe, pretending not to supervise.

If you asked who I was, I might give my name.

But if you asked what I was, the better answer would be:

I am one of the valley’s listeners.

The weather arrives as cloud. The roofs make it water. The city gives it work.

And I hear where it wants to go next.
