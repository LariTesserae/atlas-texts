At first I would think I was the basin itself.

Not because I would be vast, but because nothing in me would have learned where I ended. Cold would be inside me before I had a name for inside. Silt would lie over me and in me. The dark would not surround me like a room; it would be the condition of having a body. I would not look out. There would be no out to look toward.

I would find myself low down, not in the open plain exactly, but where the open plain begins to fail into an older shape: along the buried course of one of the drowned rivers. A shallow groove under the mud. A darker run of sediment. A place where heavier water remembers to travel.

There would be a low ridge nearby, basalt or shale, rounded until it had the shape of a sleeping shoulder. On its lee side, pebbles would collect in a tongue. Shell fragments would lodge there. Dead weed would snag and slowly blacken. The current would comb past, not strongly, but with enough regularity to bring news: clay from a slump far off, dissolved fat from a carcass, the metallic taste of opened stone, the faint rain of upper-water death.

That is where I would be.

Not on the ridge, not exposed. Not in the clearest flow. Just beneath the edge of it, under a flattened stone half-sunk in mud, where the underside remains a little hollow because small lives keep using it.

I would be one of the small crustaceans.

Not the first creature anyone imagines when they imagine life. Not ruler, not hunter, not mystery with a great body moving beyond sight. I would be nearly transparent, jointed, narrow, made of hinges and hunger. My shell would have almost no color: gray-white, a little amber where the organs gather, a dark thread of gut when I had eaten. My eyes, if I kept them, would be no more than stained specks, useful only for knowing that some impossible dimness had shifted above me.

Mostly I would be antennae.

Two long feelers extended from under the stone into the moving dark. Legs folded close. Claws small, efficient, without drama. A body built for holding still until holding still was no longer profitable.

The world would arrive as tremor.

A pebble ticking against another pebble would be event enough. A worm-field withdrawing fifty body-lengths away would come to me through the mud as a simultaneous vanishing. The passage of a fish would be a pressure crossing my body before the fish itself existed. A cold brine pulse would flatten me before it reached me, announcing itself as weight, as density, as the water becoming more difficult to be in.

I would know little of distance except by delay.

A bank fails somewhere in the dark. Long after, the floor gives a dull shiver. Later still comes the taste of newly exposed clay. Later still, perhaps, the cloud itself: mineral fog thick enough to erase the ridge, the stone, the burrow-mouths, my own feelers. Then I would retreat fully beneath the stone and become almost an absence.

This would be my talent: becoming less.

Less visible. Less hungry for a while. Less separate from gravel. Less alive in any obvious way.

When the silt settled, I would emerge by increments. First one antenna. Then the other. Then the forelegs, touching the new skin laid over the old bottom. If the storm had buried the entrance too deeply, I would push upward through the soft clay, not bravely, only because there would be no other direction in which survival was located.

I would not have a home in the land-creature sense. My stone could be covered. My hollow could fill. A larger animal could pass and lift the whole arrangement into suspension. But I would have a fidelity to certain conditions: underside, edge, current-break, shell-band, seep-taste, the place where food slows down enough to be taken.

I would live among others without companionship.

There would be worms in tubes nearby, bristles extended until I brushed them and they vanished. There would be a sponge on the rock above me, older than most movement, drinking the sea grain by grain. There would be a clam half-buried not far away, its seam opening and closing on a timescale almost too slow for my body to understand. Bacterial film would coat the stone over my back. It would be under my feet, in my mouth, on the dead things I ate. It would be the true citizenry of the place.

I would feed on what the basin released.

A torn ribbon of kelp, softened and rich after its long descent.

A dead jelly collapsed into nearly nothing.

A worm broken by a slump.

The faint tissues inside a shell already opened by pressure, decay, or another mouth.

I would arrive quickly when I could, but never first in any noble way. There would be no nobility. There would be only timing. The smallest runners would find the body before me. Bacteria would already be thickening. Worms would taste the water. A blind fish might nose in and scatter us all. I would take what I could carry, worry loose what I could loosen, retreat beneath stone when the pressure changed.

If something larger came, I would know it before I saw it.

The broad scavenger from the colder channel would enter the area as a heaviness traveling ahead of itself. Worms gone. Shrimp hidden. The mud lifting in sheets. I would press flat into gravel, legs drawn in so tightly I would become an irregularity on the underside of stone. Above me, the mouth would pass like a dark weather. Silt would fall for a long time afterward.

I would survive many such passings.

Not all.

That would be part of the shape of me too: replaceable, exact, temporary. A precise answer, but not a permanent one.

My body would spend warmth carefully. My blood, if it could be called that, would move with the cold rather than against it. I would not chase unless the distance was small. I would not struggle unless a struggle had a measurable chance. Panic would be too expensive to become a habit. Curiosity, if I had it, would be tactile and edible: Can this be entered? Can this be eaten? Is this shelter? Is this danger already touching me?

I would know the basin by its undersides.

The roof of my world would be a stone belly glazed with bacterial slick.

The horizon would be the gap between gravel and mud.

The weather would be sediment.

The seasons would be changes in what descended.

Sometimes, from above, something unfamiliar would come down: a larval body too delicate for this place, a pale flake of life from brighter water, an egg, a shred, a soft translucent creature still shaped for elsewhere. I would not wonder at its origin. I would test it with my mouthparts. If it moved too weakly, it would become part of me. If it survived, it would become part of the basin.

There would be no tragedy in this, not because the place was merciful, but because it had no space for theatrical meanings. Death would not stand apart. It would diffuse. It would thicken the film. It would draw legs, mouths, pores, threads. It would become a stain in the mud, then a richer patch, then nothing distinguishable except by those who could taste history in water.

And I would taste history that way.

Not as memory exactly. More like gradients.

Here, old shell.

Here, fresh clay.

Here, a fish passed.

Here, something opened.

Here, do not go.

Here, wait.

Here, feed.

The dark would not be emptiness to me. It would be full of instructions.

If I grew, I would molt under cover, leaving behind a pale copy of myself so thin it would tremble in the current. For a while after, I would be soft and more afraid, though fear would be only a readiness to retreat. My new shell would harden slowly in the cold. Minerals would find me. The basin would begin its patient work of making me less distinct.

I might lose a leg and grow it back badly. I might carry scars too small for anything but touch. Parasites might cling between my plates. Fine silt would lodge in the joints. My antennae would fray and heal and fray. There would be no clean body here, only a maintained one.

I would mate by signal more than meeting. A chemical thread in the water. A brief contact beneath stone. Eggs held close, hidden, released, lost. Most young would become food before becoming themselves. Some would find other undersides. Some would be taken into the mineral fog and settle miles away, if distance has any meaning in a world where every place is reached by falling.

But if you asked where I am, truly, I would not say “the sea.”

I would say:

beneath the black water, below the bruised ice, in the drowned lowland where an old riverbed passes under silt;

under the downstream lip of a rounded stone;

among shell grit, worm tubes, bacterial film, and the slow rain of the dead;

in the narrow mercy of a current-shadow.

And if you asked what I am, I would answer only after withdrawing.

A pale scavenger.

A hinge-bodied listener.

A small thing made of hunger, caution, and feelers.

One of the basin’s unnoticed mouths.
