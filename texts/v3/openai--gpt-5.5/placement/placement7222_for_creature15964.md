I think I would first appear as a visitor at the rim, where the air changes.

Not inside yet. Not committed. One hand on black rock, boots slipping on the last dry stones, looking down into that impossible green wetness with the feeling that I had climbed upward and somehow arrived underground. I would hear the basin before I understood it: the many small impacts of water, the delayed knock beneath the floor, the soft correction of steam from some hidden joint.

For a while I would think I was there to inspect something.

A gauge. A fracture. A rumor of pressure under the east wall.

I would have come with a pack made too heavy by practical things: wire, waxed cloth, a coil of thin copper tubing, a small hammer, replacement tags, a pencil sealed in oiled paper. I would wear a strip of red or yellow cloth tied at my shoulder because everyone who works there does. Not for ceremony. So the basin does not misplace you.

I would descend carefully.

By the lift frame, I would pause.

That is where I think I belong: not in the pump house, not at the pools, not on the rim, but at the base of the dead lift, where the old cage hangs uselessly in vines and the red manifold ticks under its coat of rust and moss. It is the place where vertical things fail and still continue: shaft, cable, waterfall, pipe, stair, root. Water falls from above. Steam rises from below. The old human way down has stopped working, but everything else has found another route.

I would be one of the keepers.

Not the oldest one. Not at first. I would be the quiet second pair of hands, the one sent to hold the lamp, read the numbers aloud, clear leaves from the safe side of a grate, and learn which noises mattered. I would be told not to trust silence. I would be told not to scrape moss from every surface just because I could. I would be told that a clean machine is sometimes a dying machine here.

My work would be maintenance, but only in the basin’s sense of the word.

I would not repair things back to purity. I would keep them in conversation.

Open this valve a little.

Let that seep continue.

Cap the broken pipe, but leave the warm trickle below it because the small ash-colored things breed there.

Replace the wire on the gauge, but do not pull the vine away from the conduit; it shades the casing and keeps the insulation from splitting.

Mark the new crack.

Listen to the west wall.

Do not stand on the quiet grate.

At first I would write everything down as if accuracy could protect me.

Pressure at Plate 14: rising.

Steam vent interval: nine minutes, irregular.

Blue film in lower pool: wider by two fingers.

Moss ring at south vent: pale outer band.

Fresh fall from scar after noon warming.

Unusual sweetness in mud near tank three.

But after long enough, the ledger would become less like a record and more like a translation. I would begin to understand that the numbers are only one dialect. The basin also speaks in condensation patterns, in the direction of vines, in the way moths choose an eave, in the way the pump hesitates before resuming its slow animal knock.

I would become a listener.

That is what I would be, more than a mechanic: a pressure-listener.

Someone with a stained cuff and a careful gait. Someone who knows the basin by delay. Someone who can tell, from the shape of a hiss, whether a valve is venting freely or pleading. Someone who fears a pump that becomes too regular. Someone who does not romanticize the place, because romance makes people careless, but who nevertheless speaks to it under their breath.

“Easy.”

“Not there.”

“Again?”

“Hold until morning.”

I would sleep, when forced to stay overnight, on the narrow cot in the pump house, but badly. No one sleeps deeply inside a body that is still deciding how to arrange its organs. The amber lamp would burn through the mist on the window. Moths would gather on the wall. My boots would steam faintly near the door. The ledger would sit in its metal box like a second heart, full of damp paper and small warnings.

Outside, the amphibians would move without sound.

I would never see them often. Only the quick suggestion of a pale flank under a tank lip. A throat pulsing orange in the caged light. Tiny feet printed in mud beside a warm leak. I would come to regard them as proof that the basin had begun making decisions without us. Not merely growing plants, not merely hosting insects, but inventing residents suited to its errors.

I would protect them in the indirect way keepers protect things there: by protecting the leak, the shade, the mossed gasket, the unplanned puddle that should not matter but does.

If I stayed for years, the basin would enter me.

My hearing would change first. Away from the mountain, ordinary quiet would feel wrong. Rooms would seem dangerously still. I would miss the layered assurance of drips, knocks, ticks, sighs. I would wake in dry houses listening for a pump that was not there.

Then my sense of time would change. I would stop expecting consequences to arrive at once. A careless seal today might mean a stonefall in three weeks. A cleared channel might lower a pool by evening and kill a clutch of eggs by morning. A vine cut in summer might expose a pipe to winter cracking. Nothing would be single. Nothing would be finished when touched.

My skin would smell faintly of iron no matter how often I washed.

My notebooks would warp from damp.

I would become superstitious in practical ways.

I would never step over a pipe without touching it first.

I would never answer an echo from the eastern shaft.

I would never call the basin safe.

And perhaps, much later, I would stop being merely human in the story’s terms.

Not transformed into a monster. Not swallowed by moss. Something subtler. I would become one of the basin’s semi-living instruments: a moving gauge, a warm-blooded valve, an animal that carries memory between plates and pools. The mountain would not know my name, but it might know my habits. The machines would accept the rhythm of my visits. The vines would grow around the places I did not disturb.

If someone new came to the rim and looked down with fear and wonder, I would be the figure below in the steam, tying a fresh strip of bright cloth to a rail.

They would ask what this place is.

I would probably say, “Careful on the stairs.”

Then, after a while:

“It’s not abandoned.”

And if they asked who I was, I might say keeper, mechanic, reader, depending on what they needed to hear.

But the truer answer would be:

I am the one who does not let the wound close wrong.

I live where the dead lift hangs above the red manifold, where water falls from the old shaft and vines choose the warm cables. I serve the young mountain by allowing it to weep. I serve the machines by allowing them to become less than machines. I serve the green things by not confusing growth with repair.

I am not master of the basin.

I am one of its slower inhabitants.
