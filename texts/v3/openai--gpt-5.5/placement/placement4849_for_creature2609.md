At first I would think I belonged in the archive quarter.

Not as an archivist exactly — too much confidence is required even to file a thing — but as someone who works near the archivists, at a side table under a window that only shows the correct street in dry weather. I would like the flexible maps, the pencil corrections, the mildew that knows next month before anyone does. I would like the quiet panic of paper trying to describe a city that has no obligation to stay described.

But I don’t think I would remain there.

The archive is too high-minded, even when damp. Too much of it believes that if enough layers are added, enough dates cross-referenced, enough colored threads pinned between districts, the city will eventually consent to being known.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not in the deepest cisterns, not among the tank-singers and blind trout, though I would visit them often. Not in the cable towers either, though I would envy the crews their clean fear and their knowledge of tension.

I would live at a seam.

There is a place beneath one of the long black trestles where the tramline passes from the industrial wards toward the higher dwellings. Below it, the ground is open raw stone: green-gray, wet, and faintly warm in patches. A retaining wall crosses it, but the wall is no longer straight. Stone has shouldered through the paving beside it, lifting one end of a stair by the width of a thumb every winter. Moss covers the lower bolts. A steam pipe runs along the wall, then disappears into a fissure someone has politely fenced with iron and bells.

That is where I would be.

In a narrow municipal room built against the retaining wall, with one window facing the tram supports and one facing nothing dependable. The room would have a black stove, a table scarred by compass points, a rack of measuring rods, a basin for questionable rainwater, and shelves of ledgers swollen soft at the corners. Outside the door would be a sign repainted every few years:

**OFFICE OF MINOR DISPLACEMENTS**  
**THRESHOLDS, RETURNS, UNCONFIRMED LANES**  
**PLEASE REPORT ANY EXTRA STEPS**

I would be a kind of clerk.

Not an important one. Not a geomancer, though I would know several and mistrust them affectionately. Not a cartographer, though I would lend them notes and accuse them of using ink too soon. Not an engineer, though I would know which valves were lonely and which ones should not be greeted after dusk.

I would be a registrar of small wrongness.

People would come to me when a pantry became longer than it had been, when a reflected courtyard began appearing in the washbasin, when a child counted thirteen stairs in the morning and twelve at night, when a fox refused a bridge but only from one direction. I would write these things down carefully. Not to solve them. Not at first. The first duty would be to let them become official enough that no one could pretend they had imagined it.

I would own many pencils and no pen I trusted.

My hands would be cold most of the time. I would have ink in the cracks of my knuckles, mineral stains on my cuffs, and a habit of pausing before doorways to see whether the air on the other side had the same weight. I would wear wool, oilskin, boot spikes, and a measuring cord tied not at the wrist but around the throat under my scarf, like a private reminder that distance is never only outward.

Children would know me because I would pay two copper bits for good reports:

“South market puddle reflected snow though there was no snow.”

“Pipe behind Aunt Merrow’s stove said a word.”

“Goat stood on the bakery roof and would not come down until the oven was relit.”

I would keep their reports in a separate ledger called **Unverified but Sincere**.

Animals would be better informants than people. Crows would steal my chalk. Marmots would whistle under the floorboards before the gauges moved. A pale lizard would live in the fuse box and be considered part of the office. There would be a map-moth pinned nowhere, because it would still be alive, sleeping against the inside of the window with a district on its wings that no one had yet admitted existed.

I think I would be human, but only administratively.

The city does that to people: makes them partly function, partly superstition, partly instrument. I would have begun as someone with a name and a family ledger and an address. Over years I would become more like my work. People would stop saying, “Ask him,” and start saying, “Ask the office.” Then, eventually, “Ask the threshold.”

I would learn the city by its hesitations.

The pause before a stair accepts weight.  
The delay in a pipe before it answers a knock.  
The way fog thickens where a lane is considering existence.  
The smell of mineral water about to become civic infrastructure.  
The particular silence of a clock deciding whether to forgive the hour.

I would not be brave in the cable-crew way. I would not swing above districts with ice in my eyebrows. My courage would be smaller and more repetitive: returning to the same crack every morning for ten years, measuring it, naming it, admitting when the name no longer fit.

My closest friend might be a pipewright with burned wrists who pretends not to believe in anything she cannot tighten with a wrench, though she leaves crumbs at cistern mouths and speaks very politely to old brine lines. She would bring me illegal valve diagrams. I would bring her reports of echoes in message pipes. We would drink ironberry wine that tasted like a bad decision and argue over whether the mountain has moods or merely processes.

In winter, my office would grow moss along the inner wall despite all efforts. I would scrape most of it away, then leave a handspan untouched because it darkened before pressure changes. Over time it would become a better warning device than the brass gauge beside it. I would label it reluctantly:

**MOSS, CONSULTED UNDER PROTEST.**

I would believe in accuracy, but not certainty.

That distinction would matter to me. Certainty is how people get eaten by old roads. Accuracy is humbler: the stair had twelve steps at dawn; the rain fell northward against the wall; the woman in the reflection wore mourning clothes though she was laughing on this side; the tram arrived twice but only departed once. Accuracy does not demand that the world behave. It only refuses to lie about the manner of its misbehavior.

And maybe, after many years, I would become something else.

Not through drama. Nothing in that city becomes itself all at once.

The office would absorb me slowly. My ledgers would begin dating entries before I wrote them. My pencil marks would appear on maps in the archive quarter without my having climbed there. People would hear my voice in warm pipes reminding them to count their steps kindly. The pale lizard in the fuse box would outlive three mayors and come to resemble a seal of office.

Eventually my chair might remain empty, but reports would still be filed.

A cracked doorstep would receive a chalk mark by morning.  
A dangerous puddle would be circled in white.  
A child lost between districts would hear someone say, “Turn back before the second lamp,” and obey.  
A new alley, embarrassed and wet, would find itself already named in pencil.

So perhaps I would not be a person at the end.

I would be a civic habit.

A small local intelligence of thresholds and annotations. A caution in the hand. A pencil kept near the door. The impulse to measure before mourning, to listen before crossing, to mark change without accusing it.

You would find me where the built thing meets the arriving thing.

Under the tram trestle. Beside the warm fissure. In the damp little office with the unreliable window.

Recording the city as it edits itself.
