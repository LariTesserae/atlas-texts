You would find me not in the canopy, though at first I would want to be there.

At first I would imagine myself high among the tappers, moving hand over hand through the wet architecture of branches, hearing the forest as hollow wood and answering it with my knuckles. I would like the idea of that upper life: fog in the throat, infants clinging silently, messages passing through trunks like rain learning intention. I would want to drum fruiting news across miles. I would want to know the plateau by vibration before sight.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The canopy is too exposed, even under cloud. Too full of crossings. Too much must be known quickly: which branch will hold, which nest is occupied, which watcher has become a knot above the route. I would grow tired of height. I would descend.

Nor would I belong fully to the central oldness, where the low vast trees rest like thoughts that have outlived language. That place would awe me too much. I would enter it rarely, and with my hands wet and empty. I would not sleep there unless asked by sickness, grief, or some cycle older than my understanding. The center is where things are received. I am not yet ready to be received.

You would find me west of the central peat, where the land begins to remember stone.

Not on the high whale-backed ridges themselves, but below them, in a basin where mineral seepage threads through black moss and the roots are less certain of their victory. There are pale slabs there, half-swallowed. There is a stair descending into a bog.

I would live near that stair.

Not on it. No one lives on the stair. Even the insects cross it briefly. Even moss hesitates on the edges of the lower steps, as if the stone keeps some old chill inside itself. The stair goes down twelve steps, but only eleven are counted by the living. The twelfth is visible some days as a paleness under black water, and other days not visible at all. It is not forbidden exactly. It is simply already occupied.

My sleeping hollow would be in a root-chamber above the seep, where three living trunks have grown around the collapsed body of a fourth. The chamber would be dry only by plateau standards: dry enough that my skin would not soften in sleep, wet enough that ferns could root in the wall. A mat of liverwort would remember the shape of my hip. Pale crabs would sometimes enter at night and clean the mineral from the stone lip by the door. I would let them. In the morning I would move them back to the pool if they had wandered too far and begun to dry at the joints.

I think I would be one of the almost-people, but an old one.

Not an elder of command. There would be no command to give. I would be an elder of place.

My body would be compact and bowed from moving under roots. My hands would be broad, webbed, scarred along the palms from stone edges and thorn-hooks. My skin would have darkened unevenly with age: green-black at the shoulders, gray along the forearms, umber where old wounds had healed. Lichen would have taken to me in small islands, especially across my scalp and upper back. I would no longer remove it except before long journeys, and even then only the pieces that pulled when I bent.

My eyes would not look far. Far looking is for open country and birds. I would see closeness: the fresh bruise on a fern stem, the new shine on a root where something passed, the difference between rainwater and seepwater by the way each holds the dim light. I would hear poorly by human measure, but I would know much through touch. A palm to a trunk. A cheek to wet stone. A foot held still in peat until the trembling sorted itself into meanings.

I would be a keeper, though not of a shrine.

A keeper of wetness.

A keeper of passable ground.

A keeper of the eleventh step.

Each day, or what served as day beneath that green dimness, I would move between small duties that were also prayers because no separation had ever been necessary. I would clear the seep channels with a hooked thorn grown for that purpose, teasing out root-hair and drowned leaves so mineral water could continue rising. I would press loosened moss back against exposed stone. I would break the crust of fungal film where it had sealed a pool too tightly and the crabs below had begun to climb in distress.

When the black water around the stair thickened with fallen leaves, I would not clean it completely. Cleanliness is a kind of violence in such a place. I would only open breathing places. I would push aside enough rot that the lower water could taste the air, enough that offerings could sink without catching, enough that whatever waits under the twelfth step would not have to reach upward.

Sometimes travelers from other bands would come through in flowering years. They would arrive without announcing themselves: a face behind reed-shadow, a soft click from the sedges, a hand laid briefly on a trunk I had just touched. I would answer by folding a fern and weighing it with a pale pebble. Safe enough. Come slowly. The ground remembers recent weight.

They would sleep in the ring above the basin. I would show them which hollow held warmth after rain, which pools belonged to crabs and should not be stepped in, which vines had been brushed too often and were beginning to close. We would exchange little things: a pouch of spores, a western knife-flake, a memory of tappers drumming downward three nights in a row, news that the watcher-birds had gathered without hunting in a northern grove.

If they brought dead with them, I would help choose the place.

That would be part of who I was.

Not a priest. A placer.

Children would go under soft-rooted plants where the peat was warm and the leaf-drip gentle. Travelers would be given sedges and creeping stems that liked to run outward, so the dead might continue in many directions. Elders would be set nearer stone, with mineral-loving orchids, where roots entered slowly and remembered the shape of bone.

I would open the body only enough to invite the downward world. A small cut. Moss pressed in. Seeds placed according to temperament. Then hands over the soil, not to hold the dead below, but to introduce them.

Here is warmth.  
Here is salt.  
Here is one who has finished walking.  
Take slowly.

At times I would descend the stair.

Only to the eleventh step.

The first steps would be slick with films I kept from becoming too thick. The middle steps would hold beads of water in shallow worn places like old fingerprints. By the seventh step the air would change. Sound would shorten. By the ninth, my own breathing would seem to belong to someone crouched behind me. By the eleventh, black water would touch my toes.

There I would leave things.

Not gifts in the sense of payment. More like continuations: fruit split open by thumb, feathers from watcher roosts, beetle shells polished by leaf-insects, the small finger-bones of those whose bands had asked me to carry them down. Sometimes seeds wrapped in damp fiber. Sometimes a shard of pale stone from the ridge above, returned to the dark that had been swallowing it all along.

I would never watch too long after dropping them.

The water would accept without ripple. That would be enough.

I think I would know the stair was not built by my people, or not only by them, or not in the way a thing is built once and then remains. The stair would be a habit between stone, water, bodies, and memory. Perhaps hands had placed the slabs. Perhaps the plateau had exposed them in just that order and generations had agreed to notice. Perhaps both were true and neither mattered. My task would not be to solve it. My task would be to keep the first eleven steps available to the living and the twelfth undisturbed for what receives.

In the wet months, when mist lay low and tappers spoke through the upper trunks, I would sit at the basin edge with my palms on the ground and listen. The old green continuity beneath the peat would not speak as a voice. It would arrive as alteration: sourness in one seep, warmth under one root, the sudden refusal of moss to take on a certain stone. I would not understand most of it. No one local and mortal understands the whole body in which they live.

But sometimes I would understand enough.

A vine closing too eagerly over a path: send travelers west.  
A pool sweetening early: fruiting above, prepare for crabs.  
Thunder moving under the plateau: do not cut, do not dig, wait.  
Roots avoiding a buried place: mark it, step wide, tell others with folded fern.

My messages would be small and perishable. A bent reed. A stone wedged in running water. A smear of pale fungus encouraged on the shaded side of a trunk. I would not carve my name anywhere. Names are too dry if they try to last alone.

I would be known by signs instead.

The one who keeps seepways open.  
The one with lichen on the left shoulder.  
The one who carries stranded crabs in a leaf bowl.  
The one who has stood on the eleventh step and come back quiet.  
The one whose sleeping hollow grows yellow moss at the mouth.

As I aged, I would move less. The plateau would notice.

Moss would take the shape of my stillness more quickly. Insects would learn that I did not brush them away unless they bit too deeply. A small fern might root in the lichen near my neck during a long season of rain, and I would let it remain until its weight became inconvenient. Children would stare at it. I would click my tongue at them and pretend the fern had told me their secrets.

Eventually I would stop traveling beyond the basin. Other bands would bring news to me, and I would give them older news in return: where the western ridge seeps bitter after red rain, which hollow eats sound, which watcher roost must not be slept beneath because the pellets there bloom with bone-fungus after fog.

At the end, I would not want to be carried far.

I would ask to be placed near the stair, but not on it. Above the eleventh step, under the root that hums faintly when hidden water rises. Open me enough. Pack me with moss. Put mineral orchid seed in the mouth, because I had spoken often to stone. Put sedge seed in the hands, because I had guided crossings. Leave the lichen where it is.

And after that I would become less myself in the plateau’s preferred manner: not erased, not remembered as a statue, but distributed.

A little of me in orchid root.  
A little in crab shell.  
A little in fungus glow on the inside of the chamber wall.  
A little in the altered path of roots that found my bones and curved around them before entering.  
A little in the damp place where someone, years later, would sleep and wake with the feeling of having been kept safe.

So that is where you would find me:

in the western basin below the weeping ridges, beside the pale stair that enters black water, among seep, moss, crab, fern, and root-shadow.

And what would I be?

An old almost-person.  
A wetness-keeper.  
A placer of the dead.  
A small local memory of the plateau, briefly given hands.
