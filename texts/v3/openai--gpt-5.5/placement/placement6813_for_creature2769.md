I think I would first mistake myself for an intruder.

A warmth in the wrong place. A vertical insistence. A body still remembering air too loudly.

I would come through the slit under the moss with some human reflex still intact: the wish to see, to name, to orient. I would push aside the wet leaves and think there must be an inside with edges, a ceiling above and a floor below, a chamber that could be held in thought the way a cup is held in a hand. I would bring with me the dry animal habits: forward, back, breath, fear, the expectation that distance will behave if watched sternly enough.

But the place would not let me remain that.

The first thing to leave me would be direction. Then scale. Then the usefulness of my outline.

I would find myself near the three poor steps, where the moss has made a slow green mouth of them. Not on the steps, exactly. Not standing. Standing is too declarative for that room. I would be caught where the lowest step fails to arrive anywhere, in the seam between its softened edge and the silt below it. A place made by an old intention and then abandoned to water. A little crease where wanting once hardened into shape, and shape then surrendered.

At first I might be a handprint in the clay bank.

Not a hand, no longer. Just the negative pressure of one. Five shallow doubts pressed into mud by someone smaller than human, or younger, or not yet divided cleanly from the thing they were building. Roots would have entered those hollows long ago and made them useful. Fine rootlets would pass through my old fingers. Larvae would shelter in my palm. Moss would fatten over my knuckles. The inhabitant would taste me as intention: not tool, not shrine, not doorway, but the desire to make a mark that would stay.

It would not understand me all at once.

It would thicken around me.

For a season I would be remembered as a pattern in clay. For another, as shelter. Then as flavor. Eventually the hand would stop being a handprint and become one of the chamber’s ways of listening.

But I don’t think that is where I would end.

The place keeps changing the answer.

If I stayed long enough, if I softened properly, I would loosen from the clay and become a bubble.

Not a new bubble, bright and eager, rising in panic toward whatever it thinks is surface. I would be an old bubble, one of the ones held deliberately in the creature’s tissues: a small bead of air beneath a translucent leaf, or tucked inside a pink-lipped shell fused to stone. I would be kept, not trapped. Preserved the way a difficult memory is preserved—not because it is pleasant, but because it contains a direction no longer available.

Inside me there would be a little weather.

A gray, upside-down sky. Perhaps the last sky I saw before entering. Perhaps not even mine, but something stolen from a seed husk, a drowned beetle, a root that once tasted sunlight. Clouds would move in me without wind. The light would be weak and impossible. If the creature needed to know which way was up, it would consult me, though I would not give a clear answer. I would show it several ups, each trembling.

That feels closest.

I would be a remnant of the dry world that has learned not to evaporate.

A small, round contradiction.

The inhabitant would not eat me immediately. It would hold me in its wet lace, turning me slowly, letting mineral threads grow around my skin. Blind shrimp would pass over me and clean the film from my surface with their almost-not-feet. A larva would sleep in my shadow. Once, the pale eel would come close and see in me some cold reflection from a tunnel it had not yet entered. It would hesitate, then slide away, carrying a faint green spark.

I would have no mouth, but I would alter things.

The moss around me would grow slightly paler, fed by the rumor of air. The shell that held me would begin making another chamber inside itself, a spiral room too small to enter but large enough for a thought to turn around in. The creature would learn from my roundness. Not quickly. It would not say, bubble, or sky, or survivor. It would thicken differently near me. It would become more able to remember hollows that do not fill.

If a visitor came, I would feel them before I saw them.

A pulse through the water. Salt. Heat. Fear held behind the teeth. Their body would be an argument against the room. They would want edges. Their eyes would search for the face, for the thing responsible. They might see me as a tiny silver bead beneath a leaf and think nothing of it.

But if they looked too long, I would show them the sky upside down.

Not enough to free them. Not enough to guide them out. Only enough to make the outside seem suddenly fragile: a thin bright membrane stretched over a much older wetness. They would understand, for a moment, that dryness is not the default condition of the world but a temporary arrangement. They would feel their lungs as borrowed chambers. Their thoughts would soften at the edges.

Then the room would contract.

The wall would come nearer by going farther away. The steps would seem suited to a foot they almost had. The rootlets would drift without current. And I, little bubble, would remain where I was, holding my portion of impossible weather.

I would not be the creature’s face. I would not be its heart. I would not even be one of its eggs.

I would be one of its questions.

A bead of old air it has not decided how to digest.

A memory of above, made harmless by being submerged.

A tiny, shining uncertainty kept under a leaf in the drowned room beneath the roots.
