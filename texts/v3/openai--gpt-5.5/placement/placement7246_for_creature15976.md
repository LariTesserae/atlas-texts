I would not find myself standing there.

Standing is too vertical an idea for that basin. Too dry, too separate. A standing thing declares itself against the ground, and nothing there is allowed that much outline for long.

I would find myself low.

Not in the deepest interior at first, not among the great wallowing presences whose calls move through mud like remembered thunder. I would be nearer one of the warm seep-pools, where raw stone lifts just enough to make a shoulder under moss, and water gathers in shallow black pans between roots. A place where the air is almost water and the water is almost flesh. A place where the green roof hangs close enough that every falling drop arrives already touched by leaf, insect, pollen, rot.

I would be on the underside of things.

Under a leaf. Against a reed. In the slick crease between stone and moss. At the rim of a pool that has no current, only breath from below.

For a while I think I would be one of the small lives that cleans: a pale tadpole-mouth moving over the flank of a larger animal, grazing algae from its skin; a blind fish at the edge of chemical warmth; a worm tasting iron and sweetness in the upper mud. There is something right about that—being useful without intention, eating the world’s excess before it can become history.

But I do not think I would remain only that.

I would have a throat.

A small one. A soft one. A pulsing sac of sound.

I would be one of the frogs.

Not the great boomers hidden in the deep reed chambers, not the stone-mottled ambushers with mouths large enough to erase another life in a blink. I would be smaller, almost overlooked: glass-skinned, reed-fingered, green-brown and amber, with a body that seems made from condensation given nerves. My bones would show faintly through me in the right dim light. My heart would be a red flicker behind a wet pane.

I would live in a leaf-cup above the waterline, in one of those small suspended basins where rain, pollen, insect husks, and the dissolving bodies of flies collect into a private broth. My world would be no larger than two hands held together, but it would contain enough: a curved green wall, a trembling skin of water, a rim of wax, a rootlet descending like a rope from the canopy, the periodic shadow of a hunting bird, the silver thread of larvae rising to breathe.

Below me, the basin would go on forever.

I would know it not by distance, but by vibration.

The great marsh beasts would be weather. Their calls would pass through the reed I clung to and enter my belly before my ears understood them. Rain would be a change in pressure. A snake sliding through the stems would be a silence shaped like danger, even though the larger chord never ceased. The approach of another of my kind would be felt in the skin of the leaf before it was seen.

My feet would be made for holding.

Wide pads, damp and delicate, able to grip slick leaves, reed skin, mineral sweat, the curved backs of sleeping turtles. I would have no wish for open ground. Open ground would be exposure, heat, mouths, wings. Safety would be vertical but not high: the layered middle, the wet green thickness where bodies pass without paths.

My days would not be days. The dimness would only swell and thin.

When the copper-green light thickened, I would tuck myself beneath the lip of my leaf and let condensation gather along my spine. Mites might cross my legs. A fly might land near my eye to drink salt. I would allow it until the exact nearness became unbearable, and then my tongue would vanish it.

When the heat rose from the pool below, I would wake more fully. My skin would drink. My throat would loosen.

And I would sing.

Not beautifully, not for beauty. A small wooden click. A damp bell-note. A pulse repeated into the saturated air.

I would not know that I was contributing to a chord. I would only feel the pressure to answer. Another throat would call from a reed hollow. Another from a leaf-cup above and to the left. A deeper one from the mud below. We would not harmonize by choice, but by placement, by body size, by water temperature, by the thickness of our throats, by the old recurring need to be found and not found.

My voice would be my territory, but my territory would be tiny: the underside of one leaf, the cup of water in its base, the slick stem descending from it, three reachable flowers, one fold of moss, a shadowed seam where I could hide.

I would lay eggs where they could remain wet but not drowned.

Clear beads under the leaf, each one with a dark comma curled inside. I would stay near them. Not from tenderness exactly, though perhaps tenderness exists before it has a name. I would wet them with my belly. I would chase mites from them with little jerks of my head. I would eat the gnats drawn to their shine.

Some would hatch. Some would be pierced by wasps. Some would cloud over with fungus. Some would drop too early into the pool below and become mouths for other mouths. This would not be tragedy in that place. It would be arithmetic. It would be weather.

If I survived long enough, I might carry a few tadpoles on my back to another cup after rain. Their tiny bodies would cling to the mucus of my skin. I would climb slowly, stopping whenever a bird’s shadow crossed the green. My whole life would depend on exact pauses.

I would be clever only in the ways the basin permits.

I would know which flowers attract the heavy flies. I would know when the waxy blossom that smells of blood is safe to hunt and when it is already occupied by a spider. I would know the reed that trembles falsely because a larva bores inside it. I would know the stone-back of the old turtle as a place that moves but rarely betrays me. I would know the difference between rain beginning and a canopy creature shaking water down.

I would not name any of this.

Names would be too dry. Too separate from hunger.

My mind would be a ring of proximities: wet enough, warm enough, too open, safe stem, edible movement, singing neighbor, snake-reek, egg-place, hide-now.

And yet, in that small mind, the basin would be complete.

Not mapped. Held.

Held as the shape of moisture against my skin. Held as the interval between calls. Held as the difference between the ordinary roar and the one extra note that means something has shifted nearby. Held as a memory that does not become story, only readiness.

When I died, I would not leave a body for long.

Perhaps a narrow-headed pool hunter would take me from below, my final sensation the water opening upward. Perhaps a bird would pluck me from the reed and the basin would tilt once, green and gold, before I disappeared into a throat. Perhaps I would simply weaken, cling poorly, fall into the warm dark at the base of my plant, and soften among larvae.

Either way, nothing would pause.

My eggs, if any remained, would bead under the leaf. My call would be absent for less than a morning before another throat occupied the same damp chamber of air. My little territory would close around the gap. The leaf-cup would refill. The flies would return. The pool would steam.

So I think that is where I would be found:

not at the center, not at the edge, but in the middle layer of wet green breathing, clinging to the underside of a broad leaf above a warm mineral pool.

And I would be a small glass-skinned frog-creature, amber-eyed, soft-bellied, almost transparent, with rain in my bones and mud in my voice.

Not a ruler of the place.

Not an observer of it.

A note.

A damp, repeating note in the basin’s endless first song.
