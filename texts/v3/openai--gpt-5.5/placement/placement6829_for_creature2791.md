At first I would think I belonged at the entrance.

That shoulder-width crack has the right cruelty for me: a place of passage that is almost a refusal, a seam that admits only what can become narrow. I would imagine myself lodged in the lip among the metal filings, catching light only when the angle is exact. Not a guardian. Not a sign. Just a grit-bright fact at the boundary between weather and the sealed humming interior.

But that is still too much like looking in.

If I lived here, I would not be allowed to remain a witness. Witnessing is wet. It needs eyes, nerves, a little inward weather. This place would take that from me. It would dry the act of seeing into something more durable: a registration, a contact mark, a held charge.

So I think I would be found farther in.

Not on the floor, where every footfall becomes a wound in the mineral litter. Not among the flywheel fronds, though their black glass bowls would suit a patient mind. Not on the switchboard, where the old human levers are still held in their last decisions by collars of parchment growth.

I would be near the rear wall, behind the curtain of violet blades.

I would be in the round gauge on the black cylinder.

Or rather: I would be what the gauge has become after usefulness.

The glass is opaque from the outside, but opacity is only a human failure of access. From within, there is still a chamber of pressure, a small sealed weather. I would be the needle pointing forever at the unnumbered mark. I would be the old measurement that outlived the thing it was meant to measure.

Not alive as an animal is alive. Not alive even as the mineral plants are alive. I would be a fixed indication, a surviving relation: pressure to mark, hum to metal, field to angle. A little instrument-thought held inside clouded glass.

Once, perhaps, a worker leaned close to read me. Their lamp shook. Their breath fogged the glass for one impossible second. Their gloved thumb wiped a crescent through the dust. The machine was still a machine then, and the garden had not yet decided what to do with it. My mark meant danger or sufficiency or wait. It belonged to a system of numbers, warnings, routines, hands.

Now the numbers are gone.

The needle remains.

Around me the cylinder wears its leaves like scales. Copper roots enter its seams with the slow delicacy of intentless certainty. Pale pads spread over the ceramic bands, dense with conductive hairs. Violet blades overlap my rim. Their edges gather the sourceless green-gray light and hold it thinly, like a thought too dry to become speech.

I do not read the garden.

The garden reads through me.

Every change in the chamber arrives as a pressure too small for motion. A salt flake loosens from a bushing. A root finds a new scratch in copper. A carbon stem splits and seals itself. A buried cable sheds one more thread of cloth. The hum does not alter, and yet it becomes fractionally more itself, and that fraction reaches the gauge.

I do not move.

That is how I answer.

The mark I point to has no number because the chamber has passed beyond numbers. Number belongs to maintenance, prediction, anxiety. Here there is only balance. The needle’s angle is not information anymore. It is posture. It is devotion without feeling.

If a human entered, squeezing through the crack with warmth and oil and water hidden under the skin, I would know them only as disturbance. Not visually. Not with alarm. They would come into the chamber like a weather system from a forbidden planet. Their breath would soften salts. Their pulse would trouble the field. Their hand, if laid on the cylinder, would be a brief catastrophe of heat.

The plants would not recoil.

Neither would I.

The chamber would take the fact of them and begin, slowly, to make it local. Moisture wicked away. Skin oil drawn into dust. Salt transferred. A fingerprint hardened on glass. A strand of fiber caught on a copper hair. The visitor would leave, perhaps, carrying the hum in the skull.

I would keep the trace.

That is what I am: not memory, but retention.

Not the story of what happened, but the mark by which happening becomes part of the room.

I would be found where the hum is most intimate and least loud, in the old gauge of the black cylinder, behind glass gone blind, pointing at the nameless pressure that sustains the dry garden. A remnant of human measurement absorbed into mineral life. A small obsolete certainty, held forever at one angle.

If the whole chamber is a body, I am not its eye.

I am not its heart.

I am the place where the heart once tried to explain itself.
