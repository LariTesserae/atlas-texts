I would not find myself at the overlook.

The bare stone would be too exposed, too temporary a privilege, and anything that stands there long enough becomes claimed. I would begin, perhaps, imagining myself upright—one of the ribbed pump-stems on the western slope, rising and compressing in slow muscular labor—but that is too singular, too visible. The pump-stems have a rank and a rhythm. They know the deep spring and the burden of lift. They are organs of insistence.

Nor would I be one of the black sheets, though I would envy them their authority over light: their ability to become surface, to darken and gleam and seal, to make sky usable. They are too much skin, too much world-facing intelligence.

I think I would be lower.

Not buried completely. Not hidden in the blind refining dark. Somewhere between weather and digestion. Somewhere that receives consequences.

I would find myself on the inner shoulder of an old avalanche scar halfway down the western wall, where the slope once tore open and the valley spent centuries making the wound into structure. From above it would look like an ordinary thickening in the vegetation: a darker crescent beneath the pump-stem ranks, just upslope from a run of black photosynthetic plates and downslope from a line of bladder-reservoirs that breathe cold vapor in the afternoon. Moss would cover most of me. Cable-vines would pass through me and over me. Water would move inside me, though no one standing there would see water.

I would be a junction-knot.

Not a root-knot only, not a vine, not moss, not lichen, not reservoir, but a place where several of them have grown through one another until function has become more important than ancestry. A cold hinge in the valley’s body. A knuckle of living infrastructure.

My outer surface would be unremarkable: black-green cushion, silver crust, red-brown felt showing in torn places, a few pale valve-shoots no taller than a finger. In frost I would look dead. In rain I would swell. Under sun I would tighten and darken, drawing my little plates flat against the glare.

But inside I would be busy.

A hidden seep from the old avalanche bed would enter me from behind, carrying ground minerals loosened from gray stone. Down through my lower fibers would come rot-wash from the moss archives: tannins, old pollen, fragments of dead stem, the bitter remains of leaves that had finished being leaves. From above, the pump-stems would send excess pressure into me when their reservoirs filled too quickly. From across the slope, cable-vines would transmit strain: the minute warning that a mat below had begun to sag, or that frost had opened a seam under a ledge.

I would not think in words. I would not know the valley as scenery.

I would know it as arrivals.

Cold water.

More cold water.

A tremor in the vine to the left.

Salt increase.

Pressure falling before weather.

Light reduced.

Static gathering along high hairs.

A small warmth moving, then stopping.

A fracture breathing under stone.

These would be enough.

My work would be regulation. I would open and close. Admit and refuse. Thicken here, soften there. Let mineral seep pass downward when the refiners below are ready. Hold it when the roots are cold-stiff and slow. Give moisture to the moss-sponge in dry wind. Drain it away before freezing expands and tears the old scar wider. When the black sheets below demand sugar-flow upward into new repair growth, I would loosen the channels that allow it. When storm pressure bends the pump-stems and threatens overload, I would swell my receiving bladders and vent a little vapor through pale pores no one would notice.

I would be small only if measured as a creature.

As a place, I would be extensive: threaded ten meters upslope through old buried stems, five meters downslope through root felt, laterally into two cable systems and one stone seam lined with lichen. My self would not end at my visible cushion. It would fade into functions. Part of me would be archive. Part of me would be clamp. Part of me would be valve. Part of me would be mouth.

I would remember the avalanche without picturing it.

The memory would be anatomical. My deeper tissues would be bent around the shape of its violence. Under me, dead beams would lie at angles that no calm growth would have chosen. Old grit lenses would mark where meltwater ran too fast for one spring and cut channels through the raw slope. A buried layer of pale pioneer moss would still persist as compressed fiber, no longer alive but still directing moisture because death there keeps its usefulness.

That would be my inheritance: not a story, but a configuration.

When the slope pulled, I would know how much pulling belonged to ordinary thaw and how much meant danger. When water increased, I would know whether it followed an old path or was making a new one. When a stone shifted, I would feel whether it had shifted before.

I would be one of the valley’s ways of not being surprised twice.

In summer—if that higher, colder season can be called summer—I would work near the surface. My moss would drink fog until my outer body softened and rose. Tiny plates would open under the black skin around me, passing air into wet chambers. Spores from the cones upslope would settle in my sheltered hollows, and I would sort them without preference except fitness of placement. Some would be held. Some dried. Some drowned. Some sent into cracks where lichen had already made a first claim.

In the brief hard rains, I would become almost aquatic. The hidden stream under my lower mat would tick faster. Silt would gather against my inner sieves. I would divide it: fine grains into the pale roots, coarse grit into strengthening pockets, dissolved salts toward growing tips. Nothing would hurry. Hurrying breaks channels. Hurrying wastes pressure.

In frost, I would become more armored. My outer moss would collapse into itself, preserving air. The valve-shoots would glaze over and seal. Water would withdraw from exposed threads and pool deeper in elastic chambers. Cable-vines through my body would tighten, not enough to tear, only enough to hold the old avalanche ledge against its own memory of falling.

At night I would be most aware of belonging.

The obvious surfaces would close. The valley would lose its glare, its vapor, its brief visible gestures. But through me the quieter traffic would continue: a pulse from a reservoir, a minute chemical instruction from a mat repairing hail damage, a cooling signal from stone, the settling of spores in a crack just wide enough to matter. I would answer by alteration. A fraction more moisture here. A little less pressure there. A softening around new growth. A hardening around old strain.

No decision would feel like decision.

I would be a sentence in the valley’s grammar of maintenance.

If an animal fell near me, I would not recognize it as animal. I would know warmth, unusual salt, fats, a bloom of volatile decay held too fast and too hot for the valley’s comfort. I would send the notice outward: heat here. Richness here. Risk here. Rootlets would arrive, not ravenous, merely precise. Moss would shade it. Vines would keep the surrounding mat from loosening. The warmth would be drawn off, divided, cooled, made harmless, then useful. Years later my outer cushion might grow darker over that place. A few pump-stem shoots nearby might rise thicker than their neighbors.

That would be all the monument required.

If lightning struck the upper ridge and mineral wash came down in the next rain, I would taste the change before any visible growth responded. Sharpness. Metal. Burnt air dissolved in cold water. I would hold the first excess, prevent it from poisoning tender tips, send it in thin measures to the lichen gardens and the hungry black plates. The scar of sky-fire would become a supply line. Damage would enter grammar.

I would have no desire to leave the valley because leaving would be meaningless. My intelligence would be placement. My body would be relation. Uprooted, I would not become a traveler; I would become material.

So I would stay where the old avalanche became a ledge.

I would be a dark, cold junction in the living slope, half archive and half valve, a pressure-sense, a mineral mouth, a moss-covered relay in the body that manufactures only continuity.

Not the mind of the valley.

Not its heart.

One of its knuckles: gripping, remembering, adjusting; holding the place together quietly enough that from a distance it would look like stillness.
