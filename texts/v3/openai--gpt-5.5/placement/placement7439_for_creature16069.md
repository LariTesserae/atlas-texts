I would find myself near the central square, but not in it.

Not at the well itself, not among the oldest black crusts where the charge is too strong and the city’s dreaming is too direct. I would be one alley away, where the square’s blue-white pulse still reaches but has already thinned into violet. A narrow place. A passage that was almost sealed the night before and then spared by some disagreement between salts.

There, between two walls that failed to meet, I would live as a threshold.

Not metaphorically. That would be the body I had learned to use.

At first I would seem like nothing more than a darker seam in the mineral: a vertical stain at the edge of an opening, one of those marks that might be crust, shadow, or stress fracture. Dust would gather against me and slide away. A mica-grazer might pass over part of me without noticing. The city would continue tightening and loosening around me, adding a lip here, a pane there, narrowing the aperture by the width of a scale.

But when the temperature dropped, I would unfold.

I think I would be a thin, hinge-bodied creature, flatter even than the mica-grazers, but less mobile. Not a wanderer. Not a hunter. Something that belongs to the places where one space becomes another. My body would be made of overlapping transparent plates, each plate edged with black-green growth, each hinge dusted silver. In daylight I would press myself into the wall and become architectural. At night I would loosen from it in sections, not stepping out, exactly, but opening.

A living door that never closes all the way.

My underside would be dark and faintly conductive. My outer surface would mimic salt-glass, complete with false cracks and cloudy inclusions. I would feed on differences: charge between one side of a wall and the other, trace vapor caught in the throat of a passage, the chemical tenderness of new mineral before it hardens fully. I would not eat the crusts as the mica-grazers do. I would graze the beginnings of walls.

That would be my occupation.

Where the city tries to seal a room too quickly, I would abrade the fresh skin. Where an arch grows too thin, I would leave it alone. Where a doorway begins to vanish, I would taste the edge and decide—not with thought, but with appetite—whether the opening should remain a little longer.

I would be one of the habits by which the city keeps its interiors available.

The city wants rooms. It wants partitions. It wants enclosed chambers. But it also wants thresholds, because without thresholds an interior is only a trapped void. Something must tend the almost-closed places. Something must keep passage possible without opposing enclosure. That would be me: not a citizen, not a builder, but a small hesitation in the city’s mineral will.

By day I would be nearly indistinguishable from a flaw.

A glider passing overhead would see no heat worth taking. A grazer would fold beside me and trust my stillness. Dust colonies would climb my edges and sort themselves in my seams. Sometimes a glass reed would root close enough that its nightly violet discharge would pass through my plates, and for a moment my whole body would show: a fan of fine internal lines, like a map of streets that had not yet formed.

Then darkness would return me to the wall.

I would know the city by pressure.

Not by sight, not sound. Sound has almost no authority there. I would know when a tower had lengthened because the ground beneath my threshold would tighten in a new direction. I would know when the dry well was about to exhale because my hinges would grow bitter with charge. I would know the approach of a mica-grazer by the slight erasure it made in the crust-field, the way the wall’s skin became cleaner behind it. I would know a glider by absence: a moving coldness across the upper openings.

When startled, I would not flee.

I would become more like architecture.

My plates would lock into a broken lintel shape. My dark feeding side would vanish inward. My edges would align with the mineral ribs around me until I became simply one more failed crystal, one more shard in a city composed of failures that learned pattern. If danger remained, I could stay like that for days. Hunger in that world would not be a hot thing. It would be a slow subtraction, a thinning of charge, an increasing willingness to open when the vapor came.

At night, when the city exhaled, I would become almost social.

The vapor would enter the alley first as a low metallic brightness. The glass reeds would answer, their hollow stems threading violet. The black crusts would lift their fronds. Seam-worms would taste the air from cracks too narrow to see. Somewhere above, a glider would adjust itself in a tower mouth.

Then I would open from the doorway.

Not wide. Never wide. Just enough to make a second edge inside the first.

Vapor would pass over my plates and condense into a fine reactive film. I would draw charge from one wall and release it into the other. The new mineral forming along the threshold would harden around the pattern of my body, and by morning the doorway would bear my trace: a bevel, a notch, a slight inward curve at shoulder height, as if someone had paused there often.

No one had.

Only me.

Only the city, dreaming a body by repeating its absence.

I would be most alive during those hours before morning, when the map alters. The passage behind me might narrow. The wall to my left might grow a blind window. The floor might split into a seam that would, in another season, become a street. I would taste each new edge. I would leave some untouched. I would thin others. I would polish the ones that mattered.

Matter here dreams socially before life does; I would be one of the first things to answer that dream.

Not with language. Not with intention.

With a body shaped like permission.

If anything could name me, it might call me a threshold-grazer, though I would not truly graze. Or a hinge-crust, though I would not truly root. Or a door animal, though I would have no door’s purpose. I would be the creature that lives where architecture hesitates between open and closed.

I would not belong to the square, but I would face it.

From my alley I would sense the dry well’s nightly loosening, the inward gathering of lives around its rim. I would not join them fully. My kind would rarely leave our openings. But when the blue-white charge passed through the radial crusts, it would reach me as a tremor along the wall, and I would open all my plates in response.

For a moment, every threshold nearby would do the same.

A silent widening throughout the city.

Not welcome, exactly. Not invitation.

Just the brief mineral equivalent of attention.
