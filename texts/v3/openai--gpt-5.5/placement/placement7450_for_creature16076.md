I would not be found at the highest overlook, though I would be tempted there at first.

I would begin by looking for myself in the long view: the cliff rim, the vapor opening and closing, the lower forests far below like another world half-remembered. For a moment I would imagine being one of the narrow-winged birds that uses the open lanes above the marsh, cutting across steam and light. But that is too much sky. Too much exposure. I would not belong to the clean distances of the plateau.

Then I would look for myself among the frogs, because they seem nearest to the place’s pulse: wet-skinned, temperature-wise, calling only briefly from hidden leaves. But even they are too bright, too declarative. A frog announces itself, however small. It makes a note and places that note in the evening.

I think I would be quieter than that.

You would find me lower down, where a warm rivulet comes out from under a black lip of stone and loses its heat by degrees among roots.

Not at the mouth of the vent itself. Not in the scalding place. A little below it, where orange mineral film thins into green algae, where the water is still warm enough to steam faintly in morning but cool enough for larvae, fallen petals, and torn leaf-threads to gather. There is a fern root arched over the channel like a knuckled bridge. Under it is a pocket of shadow, gravel, and softened wood. That is where I would live.

I would be one of the small freshwater crabs.

Dark-backed, low to the ground, with pale joints and careful claws. Not large enough to command anything. Not beautiful unless closely watched. My shell would hold the color of wet stone; my legs would know slickness before thought. I would move sideways through the plateau’s hidden workings, neither fully aquatic nor fully terrestrial, belonging to margins: waterline, rootline, heatline, rotline.

I would be a sorter.

All day, or what passes for day beneath steam and leaves, I would work through what the mountain gives down. A drowned beetle. A curl of fern. Threads of orange microbial skin. A fish scale. A softened fruit seed with flesh still clinging to it. A frog’s shed skin, translucent as a peeled thought. I would lift, test, turn, taste. Keep what can be eaten. Let the rest go. There would be no hurry in me, only alertness.

When rain came, I would feel it first as a change in pressure through the water. The channel would grow brown and push harder against my legs. Pebbles would shift. The warm seam would blur into cold rainwater. Then I would wedge myself under stone and raise one claw slightly, not in threat exactly, but in calculation. The whole world would be moving more than usual. Leaves would strike the surface. Sediment would pour down. Somewhere upstream a mat might tear loose; somewhere downstream a pool might open. After rain, the plateau would be remade just enough to require rereading.

That would be my intelligence: not language, not memory as a story, but a map of gradients.

Too hot here.

Food collects there.

Heron shadow above open water.

Snake smell on the root.

Safe hollow behind the stone, unless the spring pulses hard.

Cross after rain, before the sun opens the black rock and the lizards come out.

I would know the plateau by contact. By the softness of peat under the tips of my legs. By mineral taste. By vibration. By the difference between ordinary bubbling and the deeper thump that means the ground beneath the moss has shifted. I would not understand the upland as beautiful, but I would participate in its beauty exactly: a small, precise motion inside the wet machinery.

At dusk I would come out farther.

The frogs would begin their short hollow calls from leaves above the pools. Midges would tremble over the warm seams. The seed-knocking bird, if it had worked the canopy that day, might have dropped pieces into the water, and I would find them by smell. Other crabs would emerge too, each from its own undercut, its own root-chamber, its own little claim of warmth and debris. We would not gather socially. We would cross, pause, touch, retreat. Sometimes fight. Sometimes mate. Mostly continue.

If a footstep of one of the night browsers pressed into the mud nearby, the print would fill slowly with brown water. Later I might enter it. A hoofmark is a temporary pond, a trap for fragments, a bowl of loosened life. In this place even disturbance becomes architecture for a while.

I would have enemies.

The heron-like bird with its patient bill. The aquatic snake waiting with only eyes lifted. The long-bodied mammal that knows crossings and hunts during rain. I would survive by being uninteresting until the instant I must become quick. Flat under stone. Still beside a mineral crust. Gone into black water before the beak completes its downward thought.

I would not own the place. I would not even understand the whole of it. My plateau would be maybe twenty body-lengths of stream, three warm stones, one root tunnel, a mossy bank that sometimes collapses, and a shallow basin where edible films grow thick after cloudy weeks. Yet through that small territory the entire mountain would pass in pieces.

Rain from the high clouds.

Heat from the deep stone.

Leaves from the dwarf forest.

Minerals from the cliff seam.

Eggs, larvae, rot, pollen, dung, drowned wings, fish-flash, frog-call, fallen flower.

Everything reduced to touchable portions.

Everything handled.

So that is where I would find myself: under the fern-root bridge below a steaming cliff spring, in the bath-warm runnel where orange film gives way to green, half-hidden in moving brown light.

And I would be the dark little crab with pale hinges, working patiently through the mountain’s overflow.

Not the engine.

Not its builder.

One of its small articulated thoughts.
