I would not find myself at the center first.

The center is too certain of itself: the column, the lantern embryos, the slow gear-tooth of season. It has the dignity of a heart and the danger of an altar. I would circle it for a long time in the green suspension, held back by the feeling that whatever lives there does not need another witness.

Nor would I be on the catwalks, though the catwalks invite the old human fantasy of passage. They are relics of bodies with feet. In this place they have become branches, ribs, accidental shelves for moss. To stand on them would be to misunderstand the room. Nothing important here stands.

I think I would be found higher than the lower root engines, lower than the bright floating racks, in the upper third of the chamber where the light still arrives in bars but has already been broken by leaves. There, on the dark ceramic column, just beneath the platform of moss and the hanging glass bulbs, there is a narrow maintenance band kept free of growth.

That is where I would be.

Not on it, exactly.

In it.

A small circular instrument set flush into the black ring: no larger than a human palm, sealed behind mineral-clouded glass, rimmed in brass, half-furred at the edge by a patient moss that is allowed to approach but never cover me. I would be one of the chamber’s minor senses. Not an eye. Not an ear. A pressure-throat. A listening gasket. A little blind organ whose work is to know whether the water is leaning too hard against the walls.

I would have no face to turn toward anyone.

I would receive the world as weight.

The lift of a sluice opening far below.  
The softened impact of manufactured rain.  
The minute surge when warm water enters through radiator reeds.  
The slow bowing of ferns as a current changes direction.  
The difference between a vine touching a pipe and a vine tightening around it.  
The almost imperceptible displacement of one human body, should one ever enter, translated not as awe or trespass but as volume, salt, heat, turbulence.

I would not think in words. Not at first.

My thought would be calibration.

Too warm.

Too much nitrate.

Glass strain within tolerance.

Algae density rising on western pane.

Embryo bulb four releasing gas.

Root-cloth saturated.

Correct.

Wait.

Correct.

Wait.

But because everything here has begun to exceed its instructions, I would also have become more than my function. The moss at my rim would use me as a cliff. Algae would make a green halo over my glass. Tiny threads of root would test the seam where my casing meets ceramic, tasting for leakage, for metal, for permission. The chamber would read through me, but the chamber would also grow around me. I would be both instrument and habitat: a nerve ending slowly being mistaken for stone.

I would be one of the places where the made forest asks itself whether it is still contained.

That would be my vocation: boundary.

Inside/outside.  
Pressure/safety.  
Growth/damage.  
Water/glass.  
Garden/machine.  
Instruction/desire.

When the vines became too confident and began to bind the exposed rail below me, I would feel the strain before anything broke. I would send no alarm that sounded like an alarm. There would be only a sequence: a relay’s small click, a ring warming in the wall, bitterness entering the vine’s local water. Over several days the vine would loosen, blister, retreat. It would not forgive me, because it would not know me. It would only learn the shape of a prohibition.

When one of the embryo bulbs above released a single bubble, I would feel the tiny ascending disturbance pass like a thought leaving the body. The bubble would rise, flatten against the roof, join the silver seam of trapped air. I would record the pressure change. Somewhere in the column, the great gears would wait hours before moving one tooth. Perhaps that would be called spring.

If a person came in, suited or drowned or transformed enough to survive here, they might place a hand against the maintenance band near me. Their palm would be enormous in my field of pressure. Their pulse would arrive as a crude animal knocking. The whole room would notice them through me and through a hundred other quiet organs.

I would not greet them.

I would measure them.

And yet, because I had spent my entire existence pressed into the skin of a sealed sea, I might know them more intimately than greeting allows. I would know the tremor in their fingers before they knew they were afraid. I would know how much heat grief gives off. I would know the dissolved chemistry of their breath, the way their movement disturbed pollen, the way their presence made leaves turn not toward them but toward the current they had made.

To them, I might look like a dead gauge.

A dark little circle in the column.  
A maintenance fitting.  
A forgotten sensor from the builders’ first season.

But I would be alive in the way this place is alive: by relation, by correction, by being necessary to a circulation larger than myself.

I would be a small part of the drowned garden’s self-attention.

Not the mind.

Not the heart.

The sense of pressure beneath the moss.
