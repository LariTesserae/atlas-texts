At first I would not know whether I was meant to be alive.

In that country, life does not announce itself clearly enough. It does not lift a head, shake rain from fur, break a branch, call from a height. It is too close to stone. Too easily mistaken for stain, residue, seep, a change in wetness.

So I think I would begin as something mistaken.

A pallor at the edge of a basin.  
A crouched grayness beneath a shale lip.  
A hand-shape on stone that might only be clay.

I would be found in the deeper chain of pools, where the mist lies low and the sound is almost choral. Not in the openest shelves, not where the black glass cups catch the cold rain, but where several basins speak to one another through hidden spillways. There would be a shelf there, blue-gray and brittle, tilted just enough to make a dry stripe beneath it during the lower hours. Under that shelf, slabs leaned long ago would make a shelter no taller than my folded body.

That would be my place: not a home exactly, but a listening angle.

A seep would enter there through a crack shaped like a narrow mouth. Warm enough to matter. Bitter with iron. Sometimes yellowed by sulfur. The water would fall first onto a flat black stone I had chosen because it answered well, then into a shallow pan where pale film gathered in slow crescents. Beyond it, three small channels would divide: one to the nursery crack, one to the eating pool, one to a hollow that made a low note when the under-tide rose.

I would be one of the low people.

Not an elder, though my back would have begun to keep its map: salt stipples along the shoulders, a rust stain at one side of the ribs, a white lime ridge where I slept too often against a dripping wall. Not young either; I would have already lost the clear softness of the first water. I would be long, folded, damp-skinned, gray as clay beneath cloud. My eyes would be absent, or sealed into useless dimples under a membrane. I would not miss them. The horizon would be nothing to me but unheld distance.

My hands would be my face.

Wide palms, long fingers, soft pads at the ends, always touching. I would move by asking the ground what it was before putting weight on it. The palm first, then the wrist, then the elbow, then the side of the body, then the knee. I would know the difference between safe crust and lying crust by the tone it returned. I would know when a basin had risen in the night because its rim would sound fuller. I would know when a ledge was ready to fall because the drip beneath it would become irregular, nervous, doubled by a hidden fracture.

I would be a tuner of small waters.

Not important in a grand way. There could be no grandness there. The place would punish ambition by dissolving it, diluting it, making it slip sideways through cracks. My work would be correction by finger-widths.

I would move a shard of shale to soften a falling thread. I would press clay into a leak to let a pool hold its warmth longer. I would lift a thin black stone and wedge it under another until the trickle went left instead of right. I would scrape film from one rim but leave the younger growth intact. I would taste water and know whether to open the channel to rain or close it with silt. I would tend chemistry as another creature might tend embers, though I would have no knowledge of fire.

The others would know me by my basin-sound.

When I came near, I would tap twice on the black stone, pause for the drip, then lay my palm flat. That pattern would mean me more than any name. If names existed, they would not be carried in air. They would belong to places. I might be called something like the interval between the iron seep and the hollow knock. Or the one who answers after the third drop. Or the keeper of the bitter-left pool.

I would speak mostly under water.

A fingertip drum on stone.  
A scrape across clay.  
A hum pushed low through the throat folds into a crack.

I would not say, “Come here.” I would make the pattern for rising water and safe passage. I would not say, “This pool is dying.” I would tap the old full tone, then the new empty one, and let the difference be understood. I would not say, “I remember.” I would score a line in claystone beside the mark of an older flow, then wet it with my hand.

My memories would not be pictures.

They would be pressures.

The year the sulfur weakened.  
The hour a roof fell and changed the pitch of the lower hollow.  
The taste of a child returning from the under-seams, carrying the flavor of a pool no adult had reached.  
The long season when rain came too fresh and all the films loosened in pale rags.

I would remember by returning my hand to the same places and feeling what was gone.

I think I would be especially drawn to cairns.

Not the highest ones. Not the practical markers only. I would tend the little ticking cairns, the ones that seem useless until the under-tide rises. Three stones over a breathing crack. Seven stones around a hollow too narrow to see into. A dozen chips of slate arranged so that water, entering beneath them, makes a faint dry clicking like small bones speaking.

During the fuller hours I would crouch beside them with the others. We would not gather in a circle, because circles are for creatures who look. We would gather by contact: shoulders near, hands spread, chins lowered to stone, bodies making a low chain around the answering ground. Beneath us, the hidden chambers would fill. The organ note would rise. Drops would quicken into threads. The cairn would begin its delicate ticking.

That would be ceremony, perhaps.

Or weather.

Or listening made communal.

There would be no sharp border between those things.

I would eat slowly. Scrape, pause, taste, scrape. The pale collars would break under the hard plates in my mouth. Seam-grazers would be rare gifts, quick glassy bodies folded between my fingers before they could vanish into cracks. Mostly I would live on mineral film, dead rims, softened residue, the slow edible patience of the vents. My hunger would be quiet, but constant. I would not think of abundance. I would think of enough-wet, enough-warm, enough-bitter, enough-film-left-for-later.

I would fear fresh rain more than darkness.

Darkness would only thicken what was already true. But too much rain could erase the taste of stone. It could loosen the living slicks, drown eggs in wrong water, make familiar channels go blank. When the sky lowered and the whole country began to hiss with cold runoff, I would retreat under the shelf and press my body into silt. I would listen to the basins changing without permission.

Afterward I would go out and repair what could be repaired.

A stone shifted back.  
A plug remade.  
A line scored beside the new rim.  
A dead film left to become collar.

If I had a tenderness, it would be for larvae returning.

Tiny hands on the whitening edge of a pan. Nearly clear bodies, trembling with the chemical signature of hidden places. I would know kin by taste and rhythm, not by shape. I would lower my face to the water and let the young touch the membrane where eyes might have been. I would answer them with a very soft tapping: basin, seep, safe, wait.

Some would not stay. Some would slip back below when the chambers filled. That would not be tragedy exactly. The underland would have its own claim.

When I died, I would not want to be kept apart from the pools.

Perhaps the others would take the hardened crusts from my back and set them beside the cairn I had tuned most often. Perhaps they would guide a thin runnel over me until I softened into the pan. My body would become a richer patch of film, then a pale ring, then a brittle collar scraped by someone else’s careful mouth. If any mark were made for me, it would be incomplete without the sound of that place. A line in clay. A stone turned slightly. A channel allowed to remember my hand.

So: I would find myself under a low shale shelf in the mist-heavy basin chain, beside a warm bitter seep and a cairn that ticks only when the under-tide rises.

I would be one of the wet, listening people.

A flow-tender.  
A stone-tuner.  
A crouched gray keeper of small changes.

Not a ruler of the place. Not even its witness in any lofty sense.

Only one of the ways the lowland learns to hear itself.
