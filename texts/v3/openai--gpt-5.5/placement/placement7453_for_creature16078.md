You would not find me in the open water.

At first I would want to say the eel: long, half-seen, learned in the old currents, a body made of caution and remembered pressure. There is dignity in that — to know the city by muscle, to turn with the machinery until danger and instinct become the same thing.

But I do not think I would be that whole.

The eel is too singular. Too cleanly itself. It can leave a duct and cross a corridor. It can choose warmth, hunger, shadow. I would not have that much agency here.

You would find me smaller, more distributed, in a place that is not quite a room and not quite a wound: behind the cracked pressure gauges on the lower gallery of the Throat, where a maintenance crawlspace opens into the Copper Quarter through a torn seam of conduit.

There is a shelf there, if it can be called a shelf: a rusted bracket, a lip of concrete, the underside of an old switch cabinet whose door hangs permanently open in the direction of the flow. Above it, the Throat turns and deepens. Below it, particles begin their slow obedience to the spiral. To one side, warm exchanger-water leaks through ceramic sleeves. To the other, a dead bundle of electrical cable still sometimes wakes greenly, softly, enough to charge the water but not enough to kill everything every time.

That is where I would be.

Not one creature.

A skin. A listening stain. A colony grown over the face of an instrument that no longer measures anything for anyone.

Orange at the warmest edge. Black-green over the copper. White in the hairline cracks where minerals gather. A thin bacterial intelligence spread across bolts, glass, gasket, wire braid, and the inside lip of the gauge housing. I would have no eyes, but I would know every light by what it does to the water. I would have no ears, but the city would strike through me constantly: pump-start, gate-slam, turbine-shudder, chain-rattle, cavitation-scream. I would not hear these things as events. I would be shaped by them.

My body would be a record of pressure.

Where the current always pulls, I would be combed flat and dark. Where the flow eddies, I would thicken into soft wrinkled lips. Where heat rises, I would brighten. Where poison comes, I would die back in crescents, then return along the edges if the chemistry sweetened again. The old gauge needle, frozen behind clouded glass, would point to a number that meant nothing, but around it I would make another dial: rings of growth, absence, scorch, mineral lace.

A map, but not for walking.

A memory, but not of people.

I would know the city as gradients: safer-warm, scalding-warm, metal-sweet, copper-bitter, voltage-soft, voltage-killing, silt-heavy, gate-before-opening, suction-before-hunger. I would know the Throat’s cycle before the particles began to orbit, because the bracket beneath me would tremble differently. I would know when the Long Gallery had fired debris through the dark, because the water would arrive with powdered tile in it. I would know when the Assembly Basin was moving, because tiny loosened crustaceans would come tumbling past, some still folded into themselves like pale commas.

They would graze me sometimes.

The little transparent things would pick at my edges with careful claws, taking what they could before the next pulse. I would not resent them. Resentment belongs to bodies that can turn. I would grow back if the heat held. I would feed them and be fed by the leaks the machines could not stop making.

A blind fish might shelter in the cabinet’s shadow during a surge, its fins beating just enough to keep it from being taken. Its mouth would test the glass, the cable, the softest parts of me. Then a boom would pass through the gallery and it would vanish into the brown water like a thought interrupted.

The eel would know my place.

It would pass close, sometimes, using the dead conduit behind me as a road. Its side would brush the cabinet and shear away a strip of my black-green skin. For a moment I would be opened to cleaner metal. Then the water would cloud with my own torn chemistry, and days later I would lace over the wound again.

I would not be important.

That would be the secret of surviving there: not to matter enough to be pursued, not to rise enough to be torn free, not to need silence, not to require a stable world. To become an agreement between leak and surface. To make appetite out of damage. To be almost nothing, and therefore difficult to finish.

And yet I would be one of the city’s thoughts.

Not a thought with language. Not even a thought with direction. More like the city realizing, chemically, where it is warm. Where it is failing. Where the old instructions still pass through the drowned dark as pulses and fields.

The machines would not know me, but I would read them.

The pumps would breathe; I would darken.

The valve would clench; I would flatten.

The cable would wake; I would brighten at the rim and die at the center.

The Furnace Sump would exhale some new bitterness; I would retreat into white threads and wait.

The Throat would begin its downward hunger; I would hold to the sheltered underside of the bracket while everything loose became weather.

If there were a name for me, no one living would say it. The animals would know me only as taste, shelter, risk. The machines would know me not at all. The city would use me as it uses rust, silt, heat, and error.

I would be a pressure-bloom.

A listening film.

A little dark gospel written on a drowned gauge in the machine-warm water beneath the forgotten city.
