You would find me just under the lip of one of the overfull stone bowls, not in the middle of the pool where the sky burns down through the water, but where the mineral rim curls inward and makes a narrow blue shade.

At first I would look like nothing.

A pale fleck held to the underside of stone.  
A loosened bit of shell.  
A torn scrap of old ice that had failed to melt.

Only if the water were very still, and if your shadow did not fall across me too sharply, would I become animal.

I would be one of the older blind grazers.

Not one of the clear young ones drifting helplessly between basins, not one of the tiny newly molted bodies almost indistinguishable from the water itself, but a broad, slow, mineral-furred thing no longer than a finger joint. My back would be dulled with white accretion. Small beads of stone would have gathered along the hinges of my limbs. I would have carried the plateau on my body for years without knowing it as burden.

I would have no eyes. I would not miss them.

Light, to me, would be a pressure change, a heatless opening above, an instability in the roof of water. Shadow would be more real than brightness: bird-shadow, stone-shadow, the vast impossible eclipse of a passing foot. I would know these as sudden absences in the pattern, interruptions in the usual flood of sensation.

My world would be contact.

The hooked tips of my limbs on wet limestone.  
The fine hairs around my head combing the current.  
The taste of iron arriving from a black seam.  
The colder thread of old ice-water sliding beneath the warmer seep.  
The bitter trace of another body that passed here before me.  
The faint richness where a film has thickened enough to feed on.

I would live in the roar, but I would not hear it as you do.

To you it is too much sound, a white force against the skull. To me it is the given condition, the great surrounding fact. It is not loud because there is nothing quieter to compare it with. It is roof, weather, distance, memory. Inside it are differences: the granular ticking of drops from the pool rim, the hollow pull of a shaft beneath the basin, the soft combing hiss of water sliding over my ledge. Those differences are my landscape.

The plateau above me would not be barren. It would simply be upside down.

The open glare, the ridges, the teeth of stone, the basins flashing at the sky—those would be the outer surface of a deeper country. I would belong to the underside: to the drowned crack that leaves this bowl by a slit no wider than a claw; to the dark bend beyond it where the water tastes of basalt; to the gravel pocket where molts collect and soften; to the warm mineral seep where the invisible food grows thickest.

If you lifted the pool from the stone and turned it in your hands, you might understand where I live.

I live on ceilings.  
I travel through throats.  
I graze on the skin of rock.  
I wait in the narrow places water remembers.

I would not be lonely. There would be others, though not often beside me. Their trails would touch mine as faint chemistry. Their bodies would arrive head-first under a ledge, fringes moving, and we would pause in the clear cold water, reading one another without greeting.

Deep shaft.  
Fresh overflow.  
Iron seam.  
Danger above.  
Food beneath the third rim.  
Readiness.  
Not yet.  
Go on.

Then we would separate.

My days would not be days. There would be no green season, no dry season, no flowering, no swarm of insects, no warmth gathering in soil. Time would be measured by thinner things: a crust thickening over an egg pocket; a basin flushing colder after rain; a mineral lip growing just enough to change the current; the slow loosening of my own shell before a molt.

Sometimes I would fold myself flat and vanish into a crack while a tremor passed through the pool. A pebble shifting nearby could be catastrophe. A bird crossing overhead could empty the world for a breath. A footfall on a distant ridge might travel through linked basins as an enormous blind announcement.

When the disturbance faded back into the plateau’s continuous voice, I would emerge by degrees.

One feeler.  
One hooked limb.  
The pale rounded head.  
The body returning from stonehood into animal life.

Then I would resume eating the invisible.

This would be my work: to keep a film thin on a ledge no larger than a palm. I would scrape and brush with my soft underside, taking nourishment from mineral life so slight it would not register as abundance anywhere else. I would leave behind almost nothing. A cleaner patch. A faint trace. A body-print the current erases.

If I died there, under that lip, the plateau would accept me without drama. My shell would loosen. Smaller mouths would enter me. What remained would drift into the gravel or soften into the chemistry of a seep. Some minute part of me might feed a film, and that film might feed one of the clear young, and the young might catch itself on a crystal edge before the downward current took it.

I would not know the cliff.  
I would not know the peaks.  
I would not know that the plateau is high.

I would know only that water is above, below, beside, inside the stone; that dryness is death; that the roar is constant; that the brightest openings must be avoided; that the old routes persist unless a grain falls, a crust closes, a new seep begins.

So you would find me where the place is most itself: not on the shining surface, not in the theatrical fall at the edge, but in the small hidden agreement between a full basin and its mineral rim.

A pale, blind, crusted grazer.

A listener too small to hear silence.

A living clause in the contract between water and stone.
