I think I would not be found in the open ward at first.

I would be somewhere narrow enough to be mistaken for blockage: behind the collapsed ticket office, where the old tram rail enters a wall and continues as a black line under mineral fur. A place with three exits, none of them comfortable. One route upward through a smashed transom into the bathhouse corridor, one downward through a cable chase toward the generators, one sideways along the rail itself, where the brick has been rubbed clean by many bodies turning their shoulders sideways.

Not the regulating room. Not quite. Close enough to feel it when the great wheels move, but not important enough to be inside the holiness of it. I would live in the listening places around it.

I would be one of the dwellers, but a thin and partial one. Not an elder, not a hunter, not one of those who can cross a street under fallen signage without fear. I would be the kind who survives by knowing vibrations before others do.

My hands would be dark-palmed and overused. The pads of my fingers would be thick from rail, brick, tile, cable-braid. My legs would trail badly in current. One shoulder would fold more easily than the other, useful for slipping through the broken office partition near the archive cellar. I would carry very little: a hooded green lamp no brighter than a buried ember, a coil of wire, a slate hook, three brass tags tied at the throat. One tag would be mine. One would have belonged to someone weighted in an office room. One would be unreadable, kept because it clicks well against iron.

My work would be messages.

Not speech exactly. I would know how to send a warning through metal so it arrives as a pressure in another hand.

Two taps close: wait.  
Three spread wide: current.  
A dragged nail: grille-hunger.  
Palm heel, pause, palm heel: wheel moved, test all routes.  
Knuckle-ring-knuckle: child loose, hold edges.

I would be trusted with small distances, then longer ones. From bakery lane to pumping chapel. From pumping chapel to generator braid. From generator braid to the market awning when the filament plants need combing. I would learn which metals lie and which metals carry clean. Tram rail is best. Old copper is anxious. Iron doors exaggerate. Chain speaks in pieces. Pipe voices must be checked twice because water inside them has its own opinion.

I would spend long hours pressed to the ward.

One cheek against a cold plate. Both hands wrapped around a rail. Throat slits barely moving. Listening for the difference between machine rhythm and change. The pumps would be almost weather to me. The nine-minute breath would be as familiar as my own body. If it came late, I would wake. If a cable sang once and stopped, I would follow it.

I would not be brave in the way of open water. The center of the market square would terrify me. The old canal reach would be a blank mouth. I would cross those places only beneath things: under pipes, under awnings, under fallen signage, hand over hand, belly nearly to the silt, my lamp shuttered.

But I would be brave in narrowness.

I would enter places others dislike: the archive cellar where ink blooms blue-black and direction becomes untrustworthy; the clocktower stairwell, though never following the bubbles; the hospital edge, tasting iodine and rust, going only far enough to tap a warning on the tile and retreat. I would know rooms by what they do to the skin. I would know when a doorway has become unsafe because the water has acquired a sideways thought.

The children in the pumping chapel would know my wrist-pattern before they knew my face.

They would touch me and receive routes.

Pitted brick.  
Green tile.  
Double braid.  
Missing third rung.  
No-open-shaft.  
No-open-shaft.  
No-open-shaft.

I would be useful because I remember. Not stories in the old air-breathing way, but sequences: handhold, taste, pressure, turn, wait, descend, do not descend. I would carry the ward as a map of touches inside my body.

And maybe, because I listen too much, I would begin to hear the town not as separate machines but as one slow animal.

The pump breath. The cable twitch. The lamp tremble. The valves easing. The silt sliding from roofs like old skin. The regulating room turning a thought through its wheels.

I would not worship it with words. I would put my palms to it.

If you came near, I would know you first as disturbance.

Not sight. Not sound. A wrong shiver in a railing. A softness in the silt where no eel passed. Heat, perhaps, extravagant and alarming. I would come along the wall above you, slowly, because rushing belongs to predators and fools. One hand on brick. One hand on pipe. Face tilted. Silver eyes clouded. Mouth shut.

I would touch the back of your glove with two fingers.

Not greeting yet.

A question.

Do you know edges?  
Do you know cold?  
Are you debris, danger, animal, or weather?

If you answered wrongly, I would vanish into the clutter and send the warning through the rail.

If you answered with stillness, with one hand finding the proper seam in the brick, I might show you the safer route: not the street, never the street, but the broken window, the pipe bundle, the bathhouse tile, the black moss under the bridge.

I would not lead you to the center.

Not at first.

I would lead you to a place where the ward can decide what you are.
