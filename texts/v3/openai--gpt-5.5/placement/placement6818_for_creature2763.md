I would not be found in the plazas first.

The plazas are too declarative: the vent-keepers standing over their wells, the herds lowering their plated heads into steam, the cable-things darkening the lines between towers. Those places belong to bodies large enough to be mistaken for civic decisions.

I would be somewhere narrower.

You would find me in a threshold district, in one of the compression-zones between the lower animal avenues and the sealed inner stacks: a place of doors, sluices, elbows, listening walls. Not a room exactly. More a passage that has grown organs around its own importance.

There is a bridge above it, always dripping. Below it, a herd-road turns sharply around a thermal well and enters a tunnel cut into the young black stone. On one side of the road are pressure chambers and old human balconies filmed over with mineral skin. On the other side is a row of small doors no herd could use, doors polished at shoulder height, hand height, tool height, touched by generations of careful passage.

That is where I would be.

Not outside, not hidden entirely inside. In the seam.

At first I think I am one of the remaining people: narrow, slow, wrapped in dark insulation, my face behind a mask that drinks back its own breath. I imagine thin hands, a little lamp used only under cloth, a habit of counting droplets before opening any seal. I imagine myself scraping frost from a pressure gauge with a bone tool, listening for hoof-patterns through the floor.

But that is too simple. In that city, anything that remains in one place long enough is changed by duty.

So I would have been human once, perhaps, or human-adjacent: one of the door-custodians, one of those entrusted with the sin of opening and closing. Over time the role would have entered the body. The pressure suit would not come off. Its seams would be lined with mineral film. Its joints would grow patient. Its inner surfaces would learn my salts, my heat, my small electric moods, until there was no exact boundary between garment, skin, tool, and office.

I would be a keeper of pauses.

Not a guard. Guards belong to places where force is cheap.

I would decide when things may pass.

A door in that city is not a simple mercy. Every opening spends heat. Every delay saves water. Every passage changes pressure, wakes film, startles puddle-swarms, alters the slow arithmetic of condensation. To open too soon is waste. To open too late is cruelty. To open quickly is violence.

So I would live beside the doors and teach them hesitation.

My station would be a crescent-shaped recess in a wall of dark translucent ceramic, just above the level where the mist thickens around the ankles. A person could stand there, but only if they understood how to stand: shoulder against the warm rib, left hand in the condensation groove, feet placed where the floor will not soften. Behind me, the sealed stack would breathe faintly through capillary vents. Before me, through a narrow vertical slit, I would see the herd-road shining black under its skim of water.

My light would be almost nothing. A green line under the wrist. A violet pulse in the wall when I ask it a question. The tiny cold stars of puddle-swarms gathering below my alcove, feeding on the leakage of my instruments.

I would know them individually, though not by names.

One swarm always forms triangles before pressure drops. Another refuses the left gutter when iron has soured the runoff. A third, bright with swallowed archive dust, comes to my threshold whenever an old memory panel behind the wall begins to fail. They would climb my boots. They would tap against the plates over my shins. Sometimes I would let them enter the shallow channels along my sleeves, where they would clean the contacts and leave behind minute arrangements of conductive salt.

The gutter-braids would be my slower correspondents.

One lives in the drain below the eastern hinge. It is old, milk-white, plated in translucent mineral scales, its length disappearing into a channel that no map admits. When the road warms, it rises by inches and lays itself across the door-track. When it does this, I do not open the door. Not for human, machine, animal, or alarm.

It knows things before the walls do.

Through my palm, through the wet groove where my hand rests, I would feel its messages as changes in taste and pressure: copper from the upper bridge, sour stillness in a cistern, a hairline crack waking under the fifth stair, too much haste approaching from the inner chambers.

My work would be partly mechanical.

I would trim frost from the hinge-lips with a heated wire no brighter than an ember. I would wipe mineral films gently when they thicken over the pressure eyes. I would feed recovered warmth into the door-bones, never enough to steam, only enough to prevent seizure. I would catch every bead that forms on the underside of the lintel and guide it back into the circulation thread.

But mostly my work would be listening.

I would listen to the herds before they arrived.

Their first sign is not sound but preparation. The road stiffens. The mist lowers. The wall behind me cools as heat is called elsewhere. The puddle-swarms align and become writing. Then, much later, the deep striking begins: hoof or pad or claw on resonant plates, a sequence so old the pumps below answer before thought can.

When the herds pass, my doors do not open.

No matter who waits inside.

This is one of the first laws a child learns, if children remain: do not enter the road during migration. Do not ask a threshold to prefer you to circulation. The great animals do not hate you. They simply cannot make you important.

I would watch them from my slit: wet backs shouldering through mist, conductive antlers dimly green, mouths rimed white from vents. Their bodies would slide past like weather given muscle. The younger ones might turn their heavy heads toward my recess, tasting the heat behind the wall. The older ones would not look. They would already know the door and its keeper. They would know my delay as part of the route.

Sometimes one would stop before me.

A huge heat-browser, scarred along the jaw, one radiator vane cracked and grown over with purple mineral. It would lower its mouth to the vent beside my threshold and drink. The wall would complain softly. My instruments would dim. I would place both hands into the grooves and give back what warmth I could spare from the inner stack, measuring the exchange breath by breath.

That would be intimacy there: not embrace, not speech, but lawful transfer.

I would not be lonely in the ordinary sense.

Loneliness requires the expectation of separation. In that city, everything touches everything through water, load, temperature, delay. My body would be read constantly. The film behind my shoulders would know when I slept. The door would know the salt of my fear. The braid below would taste my illness before I felt it. The puddle-swarms would rearrange around my discarded heat. The machines would come, eventually, if my stillness became wrong.

Yet I would have private things.

In an alcove above my station, I would keep objects arranged with tenderness: a broken hook from a cable-thing, a white plate shed by a young herd animal, three frost bowls sorted by crystal habit, a strip of old human fabric sealed under transparent mineral, a valve tooth from the first door I learned. I would not call them possessions. Possession is difficult in a place that takes everything back.

They would be proofs of attention.

I would sleep upright, or nearly so, folded into the warm rib of the wall while the city lengthened the corridors around me. Dreams would come as pressure maps. I would dream of doors opening under oceans, of mountains rising through black water, of a sun I know only as an inherited danger: too much light, too much waking, too much waste.

If I spoke, it would not often be with air.

Air-speech fogs masks, spends warmth, startles small bodies. I would speak through touch and timing. Three pulses through the hinge for wait. One long warmth through the sill for safe. A shutter opened just enough to wake the wall-film into silver: danger in the upper passages. A palm held against the ceramic until the print remained: I was here; the door remembers.

Perhaps there are still others behind the stack doors.

Perhaps once each cycle a narrow figure comes from the inner chambers, carrying a sealed vessel of condensed water or a packet of repaired sensors. We do not greet one another quickly. We stand on opposite sides of the threshold while the door considers the cost. When it opens, it opens only as wide as the city permits.

The other figure passes me an object.

I pass back a reading, a warning, a memory scraped from the wall-film.

Our sleeves touch. That is enough.

Or perhaps no humans remain, and I only preserve the shape of their caution.

Perhaps the thing in the threshold is no longer a person at all, but a composite habit: a pressure suit filled with mineral nerves, a maintenance machine trained by old hands, a colony of films using a human outline because doors respond well to it. Perhaps the face behind the mask is a dark cavity where warm vapor circulates. Perhaps the hands are tools grown delicate from centuries of frost-work.

That would not make the duty false.

I would still be the one who slows the frightened.

The city itself discourages haste, but it is broad and patient; it cannot attend to every panic. I would feel running before I heard it: sharp heat, ragged breath, impact too fast for the corridor’s liking. The floor would soften. The walls would lengthen. My door would darken and refuse.

Then I would place one hand on the inner plate and one hand on the outer groove, lending my own rhythm to the passage.

Wait.

Condense.

Listen.

Count the drops.

Let the herd pass.

Let the pressure equalize.

Let the small things turn away.

Only then may you enter.

Some would hate me for it, if hatred still has room there. Some would pound on the door and spend heat with their anger. Some would call the delay cruelty. But the city would know. The braid would know. The films would keep the thermal ghost of their hands, flaring bright and then slowly cooling into understanding.

Eventually even they would learn.

No one survives there by arriving suddenly.

So yes: you would find me in the wet black hinge of the city, between animal road and sealed chamber, beneath the dripping bridge where the mist falls sideways when the pylons hum.

I would be a threshold custodian.

A narrow remnant.

A listener in a pressure suit.

A door that learned to have hands.

My body would belong partly to the old citizens, partly to the machines, partly to the mineral films that remember touch, partly to the gutter-braids that taste decisions before they are made. I would eat almost nothing but recovered warmth and the bitter salts of recycled water. I would shine only when necessary. I would move slowly enough that the city trusts me.

And my purpose would be simple, severe, and almost sacred:

to keep the water moving,

to keep the heat from waste,

to keep darkness unbroken,

and to open only when opening has become less costly than remaining closed.
