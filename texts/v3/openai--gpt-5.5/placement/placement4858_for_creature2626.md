I would not find myself on the horizon.

Nothing in me would want the far line, the lifted ground, the few stunted trees. I would belong lower than that — not in the open sheet of water, not fully under stone, but where the two argue softly and never finish.

At first I would think I was a patch of dark rock at the lip of a shallow basin.

A low rib of blue-gray stone would run beside me, warm from the day and sweating from beneath. Around it, rainwater would have gathered in a pan only ankle-deep, amber with silt, filmed at the edges with silver algae. Sedge roots would hold the mud in loose fists. Tiny round leaves would press themselves flat against the wet clay. Mosquitoes would rise and fall above the surface. A snail would be working slowly across my back.

I would not move for a long time.

The sky would be in the water. The water would be in my skin. My breath would be so slight that even I would not think of it as breath, only as a dim opening and closing somewhere near the surface of myself.

Then evening would lower.

The plain would begin to sound from underneath. Frogs first, thin and many. Insects next, tightening the air. A harsh bird-call traveling far across the wet. The little plink of water falling from stone into water, as though the land were counting without numbers.

And then I would remember that I was not stone.

I would be one of the low ones, but old enough to have nearly become country.

A broad-backed female, peat-brown and blue-gray, with copper stains along the plates of my spine and pale mineral crusting in the shallow ridges of my hide. My body would be wide, patient, difficult to lift. My limbs would spread from me like roots that had learned to move. My toes would know slick stone, black mud, sedge-shadow, flooded crack. My eyes would sit high and still, dark gold, taking in the long wet distance without needing to own any part of it.

I would carry eggs.

Not yet laid, but heavy in me: strings of small brown moons waiting for the right water. I would be moving toward a basin I had not seen for many seasons, except that seeing would not be the right word. I would know it by the mineral taste that rose through the mud after rain. By the way its clay sealed cool around the belly. By the warmth it kept after sunset. By the old memory of hatchlings there — not thoughts, not pictures, but a pressure in the body saying: there, there, still there.

You would find me after rain, when the scattered pools had joined and the channels were briefly awake.

I would come out from under a softened ledge where I had spent the heat pressed into black clay. At first only my nostrils would break the skin of water. Then the eyes. Then the blunt head, the shoulders, the heavy plates of the back. Mud would release me with a soft sucking sound. A small crablike burrower would freeze beside my forefoot. I might eat it. I might not. Hunger would be present, but not urgent. Urgency would belong to wings, to dragonflies, to small fish trapped in shrinking water. I would have a slower law inside me.

I would cross the stone by degrees.

One foot placed forward, web spread. Weight accepted. Another foot. Belly brushing wet grit. A pause long enough for insects to forget me. Another motion. My skin drinking from air, from puddle, from the damp breath of stone.

If danger passed overhead, I would flatten.

A raptor’s shadow would slide across the pan like a thought not meant for me, and I would become rock again. My colors would break me apart: algae-green along one shoulder, dark mud in the folds, a pale dry bloom over the high places of my back. My stillness would be older than fear. Only when the frogs resumed would I move.

I would not be alone.

Somewhere beyond a low rib, an old male would begin calling. His note would come through the ground before it came through the air — a hollow, wooden tremor, broad and low, ringing the skin of the pools. Another would answer farther off. A third from so far away he would seem less animal than weather.

I would listen, but I would not go to the loudest.

I would go to the safest water.

Warm enough. Deep enough for a while. Rooted enough to hide the eggs. Not so open that birds could write their hunger across it without obstruction. Not so shaded that cold would keep the young slow. I would taste as I moved, lowering my mouth to water, pressing my throat to mud, reading the basin by salt, iron, rot, algae, the faint sourness of other bodies.

Perhaps a young male would mistake my path for his invitation and place himself before me, throat pale, sides trembling with effort. I would stop. He would call once, too near, the note swelling against my jaw. I would touch him with my blunt snout, not gently, not cruelly. Then I would continue past him toward the darker sedge.

I would be social in the way water is social: touching, joining, withdrawing, leaving no boundary sharp enough to defend.

By full dusk I would reach the basin.

It would lie between two stone backs, half-hidden by low grasses and fernlike things rooted in cracks. Fresh rain would have deepened it. The water would be brown-clear, holding the last light. Small fish would flicker there already, or perhaps not fish — only reflections breaking. Snails would cling to the underside of a submerged plate. Worms would stir in the saturated silt.

I would enter slowly until the water came over my spine.

There I would wait.

The male in the basin would call again, his throat swelling, his body scarcely distinguishable from the mud shelf beneath him. I would feel the call in my ribs. I would answer not with sound, but by remaining.

Later, under the low dark, I would fasten my eggs where stone, root, and silt met. Clear brown beads in soft strings. Dark commas curled within them. So many would be eaten. I would know this without grief as humans know grief. Birds would find some. Crabs some. Fish some. The water itself would fail some. But enough might remain if the rains held, if the basin did not warm too quickly, if no long dry wind came.

Afterward I would rest with only my eyes above the surface.

The plain would sing around me. Frogs ticking and rasping. Insects shrilling. The deeper calls of my kind traveling basin to basin, hollow to hollow, as if the old country had many throats and all of them were remembering rain.

I would have no name for myself.

If I had to be named, it would be by what I keep: not territory, not shelter, not tool, not story, but dampness.

I would be a memory of wet places carried in a body.

A finder of lasting pools.

A broad, low mother of temporary waters.

A thing almost stone, almost mud, almost voice.

And if you came looking for me in daylight, you would probably miss me. You would see only a slick dark back of rock, a stain of algae, a pair of gold flecks in a shallow mirror of sky. Then the flecks would blink.

And the country would have looked back.
