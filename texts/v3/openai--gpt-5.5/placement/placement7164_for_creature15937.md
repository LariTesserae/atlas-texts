I would find myself at the edge rather than the center.

Not on the open floor of the basin, not beneath the full white lamps, not among the reeds where the room makes its most visible claim to being a marsh. I would be lower and more lateral than that: near the sump, behind the rust-proof ladder, where the tile wall meets the poured concrete and the seam has opened just enough to hold a black-green shine.

At first I would think I was there as a person.

A maintenance worker, perhaps. Someone who knows which rail to grip, which steps to distrust, how to duck under the insulated mains without touching the sweating jackets. Someone sent to check a valve that no one has checked in years except because a checklist still remembers it. I would stand in the industrial daylight with my hand on the polished rail, feeling the whole plant pass through my bones as sound.

But I would not remain a person for long.

The place would reduce me. Not violently. Just by correcting my scale.

The roar would make speech irrelevant. The lamps would make drama impossible. The wet concrete would take the authority out of my boots. I would become aware that everything important here happens by contact: water touching metal, root touching dust, algae touching light, vibration touching stem, hand touching rail, mineral touching edge until the edge forgets itself.

So I think I would become something that lives by contact.

Not the frog. The frog is too complete a secret.

Not the reeds. They are too ceremonial, too upright, too much like an announcement.

Not the vine on the gauge either, though I envy its patience and its strange fidelity to zero.

I would be the snail’s clean line.

Not quite the snail. The line.

A brief clearing made through algae on the wall near the sump. A narrow, shining absence, drawn overnight across a surface that had seemed settled forever. I would be evidence of passage rather than the passerby. A thought the basin allowed itself and then slowly covered over.

That feels right: to exist there as a small revision that does not demand permanence.

I would begin under the lip of a drainage groove, where the black algae has gone smooth as glass and the lamps tremble in it in broken white bars. I would move without hurry, because hurry has no language in that place. I would cross mineral bloom, skirt a bolt head furred with rust, pass beneath the shadow of the ladder rung, and leave behind me a thin bright track where the green-dark film has been eaten or parted or remembered differently.

By morning, perhaps, no one would notice.

A worker might glance at the wall and see only dampness. The hidden creature of the basin would feel me more accurately: a small subtraction, a delicate edit in its skin. Not harm. Not improvement. Just motion.

I would be useful because I am minor.

In a place whose great fear is optimization, I would not organize anything. I would not clean enough to alarm anyone. I would not bloom, flower, clog, document, photograph, or repair. I would only make a temporary path across accumulation, proving that even the most stable wetness can be crossed without being conquered.

And then the line would soften.

Condensation would bead over it. Spores would settle. Algae would close from both sides. The basin would take the mark back into itself, not erasing me exactly, but digesting my having-been into surface.

So: I would find myself near the sump, on the tiled wall where moisture gathers and the roar is too large to hear as sound anymore.

I would be a clean trail through algae.

A small moving absence.

One of the basin’s quiet ways of knowing it is alive.
