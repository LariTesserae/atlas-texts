I would find myself where the city narrows around water.

Not at the highest watch platform, not in the market basin, not among the quarry gangs where the hammerstones click all day, but in a cistern court tucked into the lee of a broken ridge: a round stone mouth under fitted slabs, a few roof channels descending toward it from three directions, fog cloths stretched above like poor gray sails, goats always trying to nose past the low wall.

At first I would think I was a raven, because the ravens understand this place too well. Then perhaps a wall stone, fitted into a windbreak, holding heat after sunset. But no. I would be human enough to be thirsty, and useful enough to be tolerated.

I would be a cistern keeper.

Not an exalted role. No crown, no temple, no authority beyond what the dry months give me. I would be one of the people who knows the hidden order of roofs: which house drains into which gutter, which gutter silts first, which child is likely to forget a cover stone, which old crack in the channel leaks into the goat pen if ice opens it. I would know the sound of good dripping in the dark, and the bad silence after a thaw should have begun.

My place would be a low room beside the cistern court, its ceiling blackened by years of dung smoke. I would sleep near the entrance with a gray dog pressed against my knees and a coil of rope within reach. My tools would hang from horn pegs: bone scraper, slate gauge, cord with knots for depth, a hooked stick for lifting covers, a patched skin bag, a small bronze awl inherited from someone whose name is still spoken when the first rain comes.

People would not come to me for wisdom in the grand way. They would come to ask:

“Is the west tank still bitter?”

“Can we draw for the sick cow?”

“Did the upper roofs send their share?”

“Will tonight’s fog be worth hanging cloth?”

“Who fouled the melt run?”

I would answer slowly, because words use breath and because a fast answer about water is usually a foolish one.

I would have a ledger, but not a written one. I would keep it in stones set along a shelf, in knots, in memory, in the habits of my hands. A pale chip for the widow’s household. A dark one for the cousins with too many goats. A flat red-marked pebble for the shrine allotment. A cracked bead for the family still paying labor after their wall drifted snow into the lower stair. I would move these markers in the evening, after measuring, while the dog watched me as if I were arranging bones.

Children would dislike me a little and trust me completely. I would be the one who catches them scraping frost from the wrong wall, splashing in a thaw puddle, leaning too far over the cistern mouth to hear their own voices come back. I would cuff them lightly, set them to carrying three stones, and later give them the smallest sip from the cold ladle when no one was looking.

I would have no patience for waste, but I would be moved by offerings. A woman bringing the first meltwater from her roof in a cup covered with wool. A boy leaving a raven feather beside the cistern because his little sister’s fever broke. An old man wetting his dead wife’s lips before the stone chamber is sealed. These things would make me look away and pretend to examine the channel.

In rain I would become young again.

When the first hard drops struck the stone, I would be out before my cloak was fastened, shouting without caring how much breath it cost. Covers lifted. Gutters cleared. Cloths spread. Jars set under every lip and corner. I would climb stairs that make younger people wheeze, because I would know exactly where the old leaves collect, where the grit dams, where one loose slate can send a roof’s whole gift uselessly into the street.

And afterward, when the rain had passed and the whole district smelled of wet wool, cold stone, dung smoke, and opened earth, I would sit with my back against the cistern wall and listen.

Drip.

Drip.

Drip.

A city surviving by the smallest possible music.

I would grow old there if the lungs allowed it. My face would narrow. My hands would crack white at the knuckles. Younger people would begin asking me things they could not know I had noticed: where the spring rose before the north channel failed; which winter split the stair; why the lower houses still leave one roof edge bare; where water ran in the year the fog came every morning for twenty days.

Then I would become, little by little, one of the old maps.

Not important from far away. From the distant pass I would vanish into the mineral roughness like everyone else: one dark figure beside one gray court in one of a thousand stone districts.

But close by, I would be there at dawn, lifting the slab from the cistern mouth, lowering the knotted cord into black water, feeling for depth by the damp mark on my fingers.

A raven would be waiting on the parapet. A goat would be testing the latch. Somewhere above, a child would scrape frost into a cup.

And I would say, to no one grand enough to be called a god, but perhaps to the stone, the fog, the ancestors, the hidden ice:

“Enough for today. Let it be enough.”
