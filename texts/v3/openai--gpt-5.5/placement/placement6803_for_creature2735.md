At first I think I would be found near the surface, among the leaf-platforms, where the wet air gathers in trembling pockets and the outside world presses close as a rumor. I imagine myself as one of the surface-speakers: greased with fish oil, eyes narrowed against wind, breathing folds packed with moss, trading upward with dry people who smell of smoke and iron.

But no. That is too much brightness, too much performance. Surface-speakers must be brave in a way I am not. They must endure the insult of air and return with stories clinging to them like burrs.

Then I think perhaps I would belong in the western gardens, where the floating rafts of peat and root drift under green-gold light, where tubers fatten in silence and every task is a negotiation with buoyancy. There is peace there, a useful peace: loosening silt, checking for snails, tying leaf-stems before the current can fold them under. But even that feels too open.

I keep sinking.

I would find myself below the Lung.

Not at its bright center, where councils sing and bellfish thunder through the ribs, but beneath it, among the root-baskets of the dead, where the cold mineral water rises from inside the mountain and gives everything a faint silver taste.

I would be a well-keeper, or not yet a full one: an apprentice old enough to be trusted with bones, but still young enough to be corrected by turtles, eels, and anyone over sixty.

My work would be patient and intimate. I would tend the baskets where the dead are placed in open reed, not hidden but loosened gradually back into use. I would know which fungi clean gently and which take too fast. I would brush snails from eye sockets when too many gathered there. I would retie the reed wrappings after storms. I would learn the names of those whose hands had already become wall-clay, whose ribs were waiting to be strung into household cords, whose thighbones were promised to the foundation of a new nursery room.

I would not think of this as grim work. Or perhaps I would, sometimes, when I was young and still too full of self. But the town would teach me otherwise. It would be difficult to fear death in a place where nothing disappears cleanly, where every loss grows rootlets.

I would wear a pale stone against my sternum, not yet the carved one of a full well-keeper, but a smooth cold pebble taken from the spring-mouth. My weighted belt would be heavier than most, because the work is low and still, and because no one wants a bone-sorter drifting upward in the middle of a counting. The stones would knock softly against one another when I moved: Lung-stone, spillway-stone, garden-stone, animal-quarter stone, and one small dark piece from the northern shade where my mother’s house grew.

My hair would be bound close, except for one cord I could never keep from loosening. Pearl-lichen would try to take in the tiny scars along my arms where basket reeds had cut me. I would scrub it away when I remembered and forget when the work was heavy.

I would know the town from beneath.

Others would know roof-fruit, market gossip, mating bellfish, the quickest child-routes through root-rails. I would know foundations. I would know which houses were literally built with which ancestors. I would know that the southern nursery’s western threshold held powdered bone from three cradle-keepers and one child who had died before receiving weights. I would know that the old bridge near the animal ponds creaked not because it was failing, but because the living vine there had grown around a string of finger bones and resented cold weather.

People would come to me when they needed to remember consequence.

Not because I was wise. I would not be especially wise. I would be quiet, which people often mistake for wisdom until one opens one’s mouth at the wrong time. But I would have a keeper’s access to the slow ledger of the town.

A family wanting to grow a new room might ask whether any dead had been promised to that wall-line. A quarrel over silt might require someone to say, “This mud was first laid aside for the root-baskets after the rockfall year.” A child might come, solemn and frightened, carrying the shell bead of a grandmother whose bones were not yet ready. I would take the bead and tie it to the basket, and I would say, with the grave authority of someone repeating what had been said to me, “Not gone. Not ready. Still loosening.”

My hands would become very careful.

That would be my true craft: not doctrine, not mourning, but careful hands. Hands that could lift a cleaned wristbone without scattering the smaller finger bones. Hands that could separate root from rib. Hands that could tell old wall-clay from new by texture. Hands that knew when to wait.

I would be called during disputes, but I would not enjoy it. Councils would make my jaw ache: the reed-songs, the old trunks resonating, the bellfish sometimes answering at exactly the wrong moment. Still, when a channel failed near the dead-root shelves, I would be implicated, and therefore I would come.

I would speak slowly. I would hate speaking and do it anyway.

“This current feeds the baskets before it feeds the lower gardens.”

Or:

“If you move the stone, you move the bones beneath it.”

Or, once, perhaps, with more anger than a well-keeper ought to show:

“You want the dead to hold your wall, but you do not want to feed the roots that hold the dead.”

Then an elder would glance at me, approving and disapproving at once, and later tell me to clean the Lung stones until my temper had somewhere better to go.

I would love the bellfish, though I would pretend not to. Their thunder would roll down into my work-chambers and enter every basket, every root, every hollow of the old trunks. Morning bellfish would mean I had worked too long without eating. Midday bellfish would mean the cold spring was strengthening. Dusk bellfish would mean the dead had another night to become less themselves and more everything.

I would have a private fondness for the eels. They would slide through collapsed places with the authority of old law. If an eel nested in a basket frame, I would curse it, then mark the basket and leave it alone. Eel-right is eel-right. Even grief waits for eels.

Children would be wary of me until they discovered I could be bribed with sweet bladder-leaf. They would dare each other to touch the cold well and shriek bubbles when they did. I would pretend not to see unless they stirred silt. If they stirred silt, I would catch them by the ankle with one hand and point, very sternly, to the work brush.

I would not marry early. Perhaps not at all. I would have friendships braided through duty: a roof-grower with green knots at both wrists who came often to ask after bone-clay; a fish-speaker who could identify sickness in a trout from the angle of its drift; an old nursery-aunt who brought me cracked tools to lower into the well and always stayed too long, talking to the baskets as if they were lazy relatives.

If I loved someone, it would be someone who understood that tenderness is often maintenance. Someone who checked my breathing folds for mineral rash without making ceremony of it. Someone who knew when to sit beside me in the cold and say nothing because the water was already full of voices.

I would fear dryness, yes, but more than dryness I would fear waste.

A body sealed away where no root could reach it. A story kept until it soured. A wall cut without feeding. A grief made private so long it poisoned its house.

That would be my deepest superstition: that whatever is not returned becomes dangerous.

So I would spend my days returning things.

Bone to basket. Basket to root. Root to wall. Wall to shade. Shade to fish. Fish to child. Child, eventually, to basket again.

And if, after many years, my own body became mottled and slow, if pearl-lichen gathered in my scars faster than I cared to scrub it away, I would ask not to be made into ornament. No memory-cord, no bead at a throat. I would ask to be powdered into wall-clay for some ordinary threshold in a house with children.

A low threshold. One brushed by hands, toes, turtle shells, drifting leaves.

Somewhere people passed through without thinking.

That is where I would want to remain: not at the center of the song, but mixed into the place where living bodies cross from one room to another, carrying food, tools, quarrels, babies, grief, laughter, and silt.

I would be one of the town’s quiet returns.

A keeper for a while.

Then a threshold.
