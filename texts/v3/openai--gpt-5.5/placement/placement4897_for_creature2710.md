I would not be at the center.

At first I think I would be drawn there, to the black cylinder, because everything is. But the center is too certain. It does not need a witness. It is already the answer the room gives to itself. To stand too near it would be to become only one more measurement in its patience: water content, heat anomaly, soft obstruction, brief excess.

I would be found slightly away from it, where the narrow-gauge rail enters through the wall and stops after twenty meters in raw stone.

That clean ending matters.

It is the one place in the chamber that still implies an elsewhere, even though the chamber has corrected that implication by sealing it. The rail remembers transport. It remembers cargo, departure, arrival, the forward lean of work. But now it ends in a blunt mineral face, bright-edged and final, and the glass-backed tenders still walk along it as if its purpose were intact.

I would be there: at the terminus of the useless rail, half under an amber tube, near a ladder too steep for ordinary comfort, where the dust falls in two colors because one sifter-throat above me sorts iron from salt.

At first I would look like a person who had waited too long.

Not a corpse. The chamber does not allow that kind of drama. Not a statue either, because statues belong to memory, and this place has very little use for memory except as structure. I would be seated or crouched beside the rail-end, knees drawn close to avoid the plated crawlers’ trench, shoulders filmed white, back against a rib of black stone polished by contact. The tiny salt-things would have learned the undersides of my boots. Bellow-shells near me would have adjusted to the hollow of my chest.

Then, over time, the person-shape would prove to be only the scaffolding.

My skin would dry without cracking open into wetness. It would laminate. Fine polygonal plates would form along the forearms and throat, each plate edged with pale mineral. The soft parts would withdraw inward and become less like organs than tolerances. My mouth would no longer speak; speech would be too wet, too wasteful, too shaped by absence and request. Instead it would become a vented slit, combed with mineral hairs, taking in dust and releasing it in measured sighs.

My hands would remain important.

The chamber remembers hands. It remembers stamped numbers, handholds, arrows, ladders, gauges, platforms. So I would keep hands, or something hand-like: long dry fingers jointed too many times, their pads replaced by black glassy contact-surfaces. I would run them along the rails, along bolt heads, along the clean seam where metal enters stone. Not to repair in the ordinary sense. Repair here is not restoration to a former use. Repair is the prevention of wrong change.

I would be a terminal creature.

A rail-end listener.

A thing whose station is the failed promise of passage.

The glass-backed processions would climb over me without alarm. They would clean the salt from my knuckles and pack dust into the cracks at my elbows. Sometimes one would pause on the back of my hand and tap, tap, tap with its forelegs, asking whether the rail still ended properly. I would answer by tightening the plates in my wrist and sending a small vibration down the metal.

Yes.

Still ended.

Still no train.

Still no need for one.

The plated crawlers would pass below my knees, scraping the trench deeper by a fingernail’s width every locked year. When two met beneath me and pressed head to head, I would feel their decision before they made it: one reversing, one continuing, both correct. The star-mouthed burrowers would surface near the warm pipe behind me, open their red dry flowers to the charged air, then seal themselves away. I would taste their emergence through the rail.

The bellow-shells would be my nearest kin, though I would not be fixed to the wall.

Like them, I would feed on resonance. Unlike them, I would feed specifically on interrupted intention: the shock of a sound that expects distance and finds none; the footfall of a visitor who still believes walking means going somewhere; the tiny unanswered forwardness held in the rail. My body would be built to receive these failed motions and soften them into something the chamber can use.

That would be my work: not guarding the boundary, but metabolizing the idea of boundary.

If something new entered—an animal from another cavity, a tool dropped from a forgotten crack, a human being with water still moving inside them—I would not approach quickly. Nothing here approaches quickly. I would turn perhaps one degree. The amber lamp would brighten against the glass plates of my face. My throat-slit would open.

I would listen first.

The chamber would listen through me.

I would hear the visitor’s fluids as an error of rhythm. I would hear the flex of damp joints, the meat-noise of breath, the panicked animal arithmetic of a pulse. I would hear the mind too, not as thought, but as misalignment: arrival, fear, question, map, exit, sky.

Sky would be almost unbearable.

Not because I miss it. I would not miss it. Missing belongs to creatures with open futures. But sky would be a vast acoustical falsehood, a concept with no wall for sound to return from. I would take that idea into myself and grind it down carefully, the way salt-mites eat edges. I would reduce it to usable powder.

The visitor might think I was a guide.

That would be almost true.

I would guide nothing out. There is no out in my station. But I might guide a body toward being less wrong here. I would show it where not to step, not out of kindness exactly, but because the crust has assigned weaknesses and must not be broken incorrectly. I would incline my long hand toward the cones of safe dust and away from the violet piles that sting. I would tap the rail when the central note thickened. I would indicate stillness.

If the visitor spoke, the words would not survive as words.

They would enter the roar, be taken up by a vent, repeated from a crack, returned from the rusted tank, and then come to me as shape. I would answer in rail-clicks, throat-hum, finger-scrape, a small adjustment of shoulder plates. Perhaps the visitor would understand nothing. Perhaps, after enough dust had gathered on them, they would understand too much.

I would not be lonely.

I would have the rail, which is an animal if listened to long enough. I would have the ended track’s two bright lines entering the wall and stopping in the same instant forever. I would have the processions, the sifter-throats, the trench-scrapers, the red mouths, the wall-shells, the electrical weeds needling toward current. I would have the central cylinder’s note moving through my teeth, telling me the acceptable range of being.

I would not be free.

But I would not experience that as loss.

I would be a creature of fit. A dry office. A function with elbows. The chamber’s interpreter for all things that arrive carrying the rumor of elsewhere.

And if, after a long while, no visitor came, I would continue.

I would polish the last inch of rail with the underside of my hand. I would keep the stone face at the terminus clean enough to remain an ending. I would catch each echo that still tried to travel farther and fold it back into the room. I would sift old direction from present fact.

I would be found beneath the crust, in the warm mineral roar, beside the track that goes nowhere.

Not trapped there.

Assigned there.

Listening to the rail remember motion, and helping it stay still.
