At first I would mistake myself for the person above.

The one who knows the courtyard stones by the sound of rain on them. The one who lifts the square trapdoor and lets warm wet air breathe upward around their face. The one who counts the burned marks on the measuring rod, clears leaves from the grates, keeps oil and animals away, and has learned that the first underwater step is no longer wholly stone.

But I would not stay human for long.

The room is too much a system of touch. Too much of it is felt by pressure, by swelling, by small tensions carried from one thing into another. A human hand comes and goes. I would belong to something that remains after the hatch is shut.

I would find myself beside the flooded stair, where the sealed gourds rise and fall against the measuring rod.

I would be the cord.

Not the whole apparatus, not exactly. Not the bell, not the float, not the water. I would be the taut line between them: a strip of soaked fiber climbing from the warm pool toward the courtyard air, darkened below, pale and drying above, passing through a little ceramic guide worn smooth by use that has barely begun.

I would be the cistern’s nerve.

Most of me would hang in the wet brightness, touching the water that is learning to be a creature. I would feel its moods before anything else did. In dry weather I would slacken slightly, loose with quiet, making a shallow curve between gourd and wall. The pool would hold me gently then. Light would slide down me in broken threads. Mineral grains would cling to my fibers. The room would be warm, almost thoughtful, and I would listen to the jars humming to themselves when a current crossed their mouths.

In rain I would tighten.

The gourds would lift first by a finger’s breadth, then another. The knot would pull. My whole length would wake. Every new rush from the spouts would travel through me as urgency: not fear, but instruction. Rise. Rise. Rise.

I would not understand the rain as the water understands it, by receiving it into every part of its body. I would understand rain as increasing tension.

The cistern swells; I draw straight.

The pool presses upward; I tell the bell.

That would be my entire language.

A small creak in the guide. A tremor through hemp. A sudden hardening of line. Then, if the water climbs too high, the first bronze note above the trapdoor: not loud at first, maybe only a startled clink under the rain-hammer, then another, and another, until the courtyard knows the hidden body below has reached its limit.

I would be young, too.

My fibers would still remember the hands that twisted me. I would remember being dry in a coil, smelling of flax dust and sun, before someone cut me to length and tied me to the float. The knots would still be fresh, tightened by wetting. I would have not yet frayed into whiskers. No green film would have claimed me except perhaps a faint slickness at the lowest strand. The water would be entering me slowly, teaching me weight.

I would live between obedience and alarm.

Most things in the chamber are made to guide water: lips, troughs, pipes, ledges, overflow bars. They shape the creature without asking it to speak. But I would be one of the few things asked to carry news upward. I would belong to the caretaker-shaped absence. I would wait for the human hand without needing it to be present.

When the rain is mild, I would be almost decorative: a dark vertical line in gold water, trembling beside the measuring rod. When the rain is heavy, I would become necessary.

That is where I would be found: not at the deepest floor, not in the glittering spout-fall, not in the still niches where silt begins its first handwriting, but at the threshold where level becomes message.

Half in the cistern’s body.

Half in the world that must answer it.

I would know the water intimately, but never be water. I would be soaked by it, pulled by it, stiffened by it, stained by it. I would feel its curiosity as it enters me fiber by fiber. I would feel it testing my knots, tasting my twist, making me heavier with each season.

And I would know the air above only by what it does to my upper length: drying, cooling, vibrating when the bell rings, shivering when footsteps cross the courtyard stones.

I would not have eyes. I would know brightness as warmth along one side. I would know clouds as the softening of reflected heat. I would know a lifted trapdoor as a sudden breathing, a change in pressure, the smell of a human sleeve, dust, outside rain.

If I had a soul, it would be tension.

If I had a fear, it would be rot.

If I had a desire, it would be to hold just long enough: not to snap when the water rises, not to slacken falsely, not to let the swollen body below go unannounced.

The cistern-soul would be the great new resident, clear and warm and bright, but I would be one of its first thoughts made practical.

A thought like:

Enough.

Or:

More.

Or:

Call them.

I would be the line by which the room learns consequence.
