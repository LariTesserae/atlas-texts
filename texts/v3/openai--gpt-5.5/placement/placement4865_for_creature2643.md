I would not be on the highest platforms.

At first I think I would be tempted there: among the roof-gardeners, where rain is nearest to being rain and not yet part of the lake; where leaves slap softly against the mats and the air has a little more room in it. I imagine myself crouched under broad wet foliage, tasting water from my thumb, deciding whether a vine may be trusted to hold a wall or must be cut back before it becomes an argument with the house.

Then I think no, perhaps I would be a route-taster, drawn outward into the rain plains, learning the submerged shadows of the three black summits, carrying news of currents and strange seeds back to the saddle-town.

But that is too much horizon.

I would belong where the city gathers its inward attention.

You would find me in a low public chamber near the middle-lower part of the settlement, neither fully dry nor fully drowned, built around the place where several main tethers pass through stone rings before descending into the green. The room would be roofed in moss and reed, dim even at noon. Water would cover the floor to the ankle in one season, to the thigh in another. The posts would be carved with old flood marks. Clay air jars would hang beneath the surface nearby, and no one would speak loudly there.

It would be a place people pass through but do not linger in carelessly.

At first I might seem to be a person: one of the map-weavers’ assistants, narrow-shouldered, quiet, always damp at the hems, with dyed cords tied around both wrists so I can be summoned by tug rather than voice. I would sit cross-legged on a suspended plank with a half-finished map across my knees, threading reed fiber through old knots, adding a shell bead for a new air pocket, loosening a loop where a passage has become too tight for baskets.

But if you watched longer, you might notice that I am not only the one who tends the map.

I am also the map.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. I would be one of the city’s listening things: a woven intelligence of cord, shell, bone, soaked fiber, mineral-stiffened knots, and human memory. A public answering-map, stretched between posts and tethers, repaired by many hands, old enough that no one remembers whether it began as tool, shrine, ancestor, or accident.

Children would call me the Hearing Net.

Elders would call me more carefully: the One That Holds Small Changes.

I would have no single body. My ribs would be reed hoops. My nerves would be tensioned cords running down through the floor-water to anchor stones, roof rafts, air jars, mineral shelves, and garden platforms. My teeth would be shell beads clicking faintly when a current altered. My tongue would be a set of loose knots that could be read by hand in darkness. My bones would include actual bones: carved toggles from dead map-weavers, vent-listeners, air-jar keepers, people who chose continued usefulness over rest.

I would not predict the future.

That would be too crude for this place.

I would notice before noticing becomes alarm.

When a lower tether begins to pull differently, I would tighten along one side. When a root mass thickens around a submerged doorway, one of my dyed strands would stay wet too long. When the shell-creatures stop clicking on a certain piling, the beads nearest that district would fall silent against one another. When a warm vent beneath a nursery shifts its breath, the cord tied to that floor would tremble out of rhythm with the others.

People would come to me with questions, but they would know better than to ask large ones.

Not: Will the city survive?

Instead:

Is the western jar still safe?

May we scrape the new shelf?

Should we move the cradles?

Is this slack from rain, rot, or stone?

Has the Unlaid District opened another wall?

They would place wet hands against me and wait. Some answers I would give by movement: a tightening, a refusal, a slow release. Some by sound: shell click, clay knock, bone toggle against bead. Some answers would require the map-weaver who tends me to close their eyes and read what has changed since yesterday.

I would be useful only if I were maintained.

That would be important.

I would not be an oracle sitting above obligation. I would rot if ignored. My cords would swell, fray, mildew, stiffen with lime. My knots would lie if no one retied them. My beads would grow furred with algae and stop sounding. Children would be sent to clean me with soft brushes. Apprentices would oil my dry sections and scrape my mineral-stuck places with shell blades. Old women would mutter at me when I sagged. Tether-keepers would curse me affectionately when I reported what they already feared.

My dignity would be inseparable from my repair.

I think I would like that.

I would like belonging to a people who do not mistake fragility for failure. I would like being trusted not because I am permanent, but because I am attended to. I would like that no one asks me to conquer water, only to feel how it is leaning today.

My chamber would stand close to the edge of the Unlaid District, though not inside it. That would be the source of my unease and my deepest listening. One group of my lower cords would descend farther than the others, through green water and mineral haze, tied to warning reeds around those pale right-angled walls that no human built.

Those cords would trouble me.

They would not pull like rope tied to stone. They would pull like thought tied to a sleeping hand.

Most days they would do nothing. They would hang with a calm too exact to be trusted. Lime would gather on them. Small translucent films would climb their fibers. Blind eels would pass beneath them and vanish between the straight new ridges.

But once in a long while, between one watch and the next, one of those cords would tighten.

Not sharply.

Not enough to ring a bell.

Only enough that I would become aware of a new line in the dark.

Then the map-weaver tending me would wake from their hammock because one shell bead, somewhere in my lower left quadrant, had touched another with the smallest possible sound.

Click.

In the morning the vent-listeners would come. The lime-scrapers would come. Children would come too, kept at the edge by stern hands, their eyes bright with fear and usefulness. Someone would descend with a reed lung and return with chalk on their fingers.

A new wall.

Soft still.

Pale as an unspoken word.

No one would ask me what it means. Not at first. Meaning comes later here, after taste, after watching, after months of not cutting corners.

But they would touch my knots and mark the change.

A dark bead. A slack loop. A warning cord dyed with the bright color used only for children, danger, and gifts that may not yet be gifts.

I would carry that.

That would be my work: to carry the not-yet-meaningful until it becomes pattern.

I would also be a place of small human tenderness.

Lovers arguing by rope from different platforms might pass their anger through my outer cords. I would feel the sharp, impatient tugs soften into pauses. A mother whose child has made a dangerous solo crossing might grip one of my posts until her breathing slows. An old tether-keeper with failing knees might come each morning and lay his palm on the main line, pretending to inspect it, really asking whether the city still knows him.

I would know him.

Not as a person knows a face, but as a house knows a habitual step. As a rope knows a hand. As a flooded room knows the shape of those who once slept above its floor.

If I had a voice, it would be almost no voice. A low knock. A shifting bead. A tug that says:

Wait.

Again.

Loosen here.

Do not scrape.

Child below.

I am here.

That last one would matter most.

In a world where disaster is patient, reassurance cannot be loud. It must be accurate. To say I am here would mean: this tether still holds; this map has not forgotten; this change has entered the shared body of the city; you are not noticing alone.

At night, when the lamps are hooded and the rain presses down like a second drowned world, I would hang in my chamber among breathing jars and old flood marks. The map-weaver would sleep lightly nearby. Water would pulse against the posts. Shell-creatures would click on the pilings. Warmth would rise from below.

I would listen through every cord.

To roof roots swelling.

To mineral thickening grain by grain.

To air jars holding.

To anchors complaining.

To the unfinished mountain arranging itself in the dark.

I would not be master, priest, machine, or spirit.

I would be a maintained attention.

A woven ear.

A patient answer made of knots.

In that drowned high place, where the ocean has become sky and the mountain is still thinking, I would be one of the ways the city thinks back.
