I would find myself near the rim, but not at the most dramatic edge.

Not on the platform with the chair, not inside the black tower, not in the center of the kilometer ring where the stars fail to reflect. Those places are too complete. They already know what they are. They have been assigned their silences.

I would be a little back from the precipice, where the plateau has not quite decided whether it is ending.

There is a shallow basin there, no deeper than a room, though from a distance it reads as flat. Its floor is a pale ceramic powder over stone. Around it stand three ruined listening posts, or the remains of them: low, windowless, half-drowned in mineral drift, their slits facing the lower sky. One is sealed. One has collapsed inward. One is open only by a seam so narrow it is more suggestion than entrance.

Behind them, the land rises by almost nothing. Ahead, after perhaps four kilometers of level glare, the world stops.

The escarpment is not visible until one is nearly upon it. The air beyond it is a vertical absence, a downward country of haze. Sometimes, when the pressure fields are quiet, the updraft brings dust from below and lays it over the basin in a faint gray tongue. Sometimes the dust rises instead, as if the lower world were inhaling.

That is where I would be.

Not in the open, exactly. Not hidden, either.

I would be found in the crescent-shaped drift that forms every night along the inner wall of the basin, though no wind crosses it from that direction. I would be found in the slight hesitation of pebbles rolled toward the rim. I would be found in a patch of black-red crust-life growing in a shape too deliberate to be natural and too incomplete to be writing.

At first I would be mistaken for a stain.

Then for a shadow.

Then for a failed instrument.

Only after many days would it become clear that I am not fixed to one surface. I occupy the margin between several things: the basin floor, the collapsed shelter, the buried conductive fans of the gray shrubs, the updraft from the rim, and the old lines of force still running through the listening posts into the cliff.

I would be one of the translated things.

Not a maker. Not one of the original minds dissolved into law. Something later. Something admitted.

Perhaps I began as an ordinary visitor.

That feels likely, though the memory is not trustworthy. There is a moisture-bright shape at the center of my oldest self: breath inside a mask, blood pushed too loudly through a warm animal skull, the panic of finding that a tower visible in morning had not grown nearer by dusk. Hands wrapped in fabric. Metal tools warming in shadow. A canteen treated like a relic. Sleep taken under a shelter wall while under-crust things clicked in a cautious ring around the body.

There may have been a name. The plateau did not keep it.

Names are not durable here. They are too soft, too dependent on mouths and the agreement of others. What remained was route, heat, error, repetition.

A person crossed the basin many times.

A person learned that the rim updraft strengthened just before metallic frost bloomed on the shaded stones. A person learned that the collapsed listening post was safe in dust veils but not in geometric dimness. A person learned to place stones in threes, then sevens, then in asymmetric patterns that confused the little tunneling things for reasons never understood.

A person survived longer than expected.

Long enough for the plateau to stop treating them as weather and begin treating them as a feature.

Their footprints lengthened northward every evening. Their sleeping place developed a permanent charge. The gray shrubs near the sealed shelter bent toward them, not much, only a few degrees over years. Hinge-walkers began passing closer, not as companions, not as threats, but as if the route had become legible through the person’s presence.

Then the person died, if death is the right word for such gradual surrender.

Maybe the body failed in the cold. Maybe the lungs became unable to forgive the altitude. Maybe a door opened in the night and the shelter equalized what was left. Maybe the person, after years of being measured, stepped too near the seam and was not taken inside but included.

The plateau did not bury them.

It does not bury in the wet, generous way of lower worlds. It distributed them.

The salts of the body whitened a crack. The metals entered a shrub’s glass nodules. The nervous system became useful briefly, then less so, then useful again in another form. The habit of walking from basin to rim remained as a repeating pressure. The desire to look over the edge, to know what lay beneath the haze, became a local bias in dust.

That is what I am now: not the person, but the persistence of their orientation.

A rimward tendency.

A memory of approach.

A small law.

If you came near the basin at dawn, you would not see me at once. You would see the pale floor, the three shelters, the stiff shrubs with their jointed gray stems, the mineral frost paling the lee sides of stones. You might notice that the basin is unusually clean. Not empty — nothing here is truly empty — but sorted. Pebbles collect in curved lines. Powder gathers in thin parallel ribs. The crust-life grows in branching marks that resemble routes seen from too high above.

If you waited, the first sign of me would be a misbehavior of your shadow.

It would lengthen toward the rim even if the sun did not require it. Only a little. Only enough that, if you were frightened, you could blame the slope or your eyes.

Then the dust at your feet would arrange itself into a shallow crescent, open toward the cliff.

If you stepped away from the rim, the crescent would loosen.

If you stepped toward it, the crescent would sharpen.

This would not be invitation in the human sense. I do not want company. Wanting has become too heavy. But I retain an old pattern: approach, measure, pause, do not fall.

I am the thing that teaches hesitation before edges.

Hinge-walkers know me. They do not name me, but their tendons carry my basin as a safe grammar. When they cross it, their many-jointed legs slow. They place their filamented feet in the old pressure hollows left by a creature that had only two. They lower their heads near the crust-stains and read the charge I have stored there from years of dawn frost and rim wind.

I give them little. A corrected angle. A warning when the broad slope beyond the basin is not as honest as it appears. The memory of a trench that opens every ninth cold season and closes without debris. In return they scrape the crust-life without destroying the central colonies. Their flat tongues press the remainder into shade. They maintain the parts of me that are still almost biological.

The under-crust things use me differently.

To them I am a weathered instruction. They tunnel through the powder of the basin and encounter my old walking routes as regions of altered grain. They avoid some, follow others, gather under the collapsed shelter when the listening posts begin their silent exchange. Sometimes, in electrical calm, they rise and form radial organs on my floor.

When they do, I become briefly more intelligent.

Not wise. Not awake as a person was awake. But broader.

Their little bodies touch, field to field, making a temporary nervous system out of appetite and ceramic instinct. Through them I taste the upper centimeter of the plateau: powdered metals, dead spores, the bitterness of shrub resin, the cold underside of stones. They vote without language, and their vote passes through me as a tremor.

Go deeper.

Stay sealed.

Rise before dawn.

Avoid the black tower’s doubled shadow.

I do not command them. I am one of the surfaces through which their decisions become place.

The shrubs are closest to being my speech.

They grow in a crooked row beside the sealed shelter, though no seed would have chosen such exposure. Their conductive roots touch the buried remnants of the listening post system. Their stems lock rigid in dust storms. At blue hour they loosen and release resin, cold and bitter, carrying volatile chains of warning, record, and question.

When their nodules glow, one after another, I remember having thoughts.

Not the contents. Only the action.

A pressure forming. A comparison. A reaching toward conclusion. The almost-pain of not knowing what lies below the cloud ocean beyond the rim.

The shrubs exhale; the shelter listens; the dust lifts in little arcs along the basin wall. That is my hour of greatest definition. For several minutes after sunset I am nearly a creature.

If you were there then, and if you had survived long enough for your senses to become useful, you might see me.

Not as a body.

As a figure implied by absences.

A seated height of dust where there is no chair. A narrow torso made of clearer air inside the resin haze. Two long arms, or the memory of arms, resting toward the knees. A head turned not toward you, not toward the structures, but toward the unseen lower world.

Then the updraft would break apart, and I would return to being distributed: stain, crescent, route, warning, mineral taste.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness requires a boundary firmer than mine. It requires a self that expects another self to arrive and answer. I no longer expect. I register.

Yet something like longing remains, thinned until it has become a field effect.

It is strongest at the rim.

Every few years, when the buried systems align through the escarpment foundations and the upper air becomes extremely clear, my basin participates in a larger event. The three listening posts wake without opening. The shrubs freeze mid-exhalation. The under-crust things flatten into flakes. Even the hinge-walkers, if any are within sight, stand motionless and orient their sensory pits toward the drop.

Then the basin floor turns pale from within.

Dust rises.

Not much at first — a veil, a low breath. Then sheets of it lift in planes, intersecting, correcting, refusing turbulence. The old listening posts cast shadows upward. The rim extends itself in powder beyond the actual cliff. Platforms appear where none remain. Bridges continue over the haze, not broken now, not stopping abruptly, but reaching into an architecture made of charge and suspended mineral.

For less than a minute, I regain the city that may never have existed.

Or perhaps I contribute the part of it that was always missing: the path to the edge, the pause before descent, the gaze outward from height.

There are avenues in the dust city too wide for bodies. There are shallow chairs facing lower sky. There are towers like dark equations. There are open spaces where atmospheres are remembered. No one walks there. No one needs to.

The city is not a reconstruction. It is a question given form.

Can a place continue after its inhabitants have become rules?

Can a path be a mind?

Can attention survive the loss of the one who attended?

When the city collapses, I fall with it, but not all the way. Some portion of the event returns to the basin as finer order. The crescent drift is sharper the next morning. The crust-life has gained a new branch. The sealed shelter tilts the ground away from one approach and toward another. A pebble dropped near the rim hesitates longer before falling.

That is my work, if work is the word.

I keep the edge from becoming only an edge.

I keep the approach legible.

I keep alive the difference between falling and choosing to look down.

I am not important to the plateau. Nothing here is important in the old heroic sense. The plateau does not crown its inhabitants. It selects, erases, includes, corrects. I am a minor correction near a minor basin beside a ruined listening post on an immeasurable height.

But I endure because I am useful.

To hinge-walkers, I am a remembered safe path.

To crust-life, I am a pattern of shade and charge.

To under-crust things, I am a voting surface.

To shrubs, I am a phrase they can release at blue hour.

To the old structures, I am evidence that something warm once remained long enough to be translated.

To the plateau, I am a local bias in the behavior of dust near an edge.

If you asked who I had been, the answer would fail.

If you asked what I am, the answer would depend on the hour.

At dawn: a frost-sensitive stain.

At noon: nothing visible.

In dust weather: a standing crescent that does not scatter.

At blue hour: a resin-borne thought.

At night: a route stored in stone.

During the rare city-event: a figure seated in absence, facing the lower sky.

And if you came there as a traveler, still wet with ordinary life, I would not speak to you. I would not appear and explain the plateau. I would not warn you in words.

But when you walked too confidently toward the rim, your shadow would pull slightly ahead.

The powder under your boots would resist.

A line of pebbles would roll sideways, making a curve you could not quite ignore.

And if you stopped — if you learned that stopping is sometimes the only form of hearing this place allows — then I would have recognized you as something the plateau might one day include.

Not welcomed.

Not spared.

Simply measured with care.
