At first I would put myself upright on the plateau, because that is the old habit: a witness standing on stone, looking out into the dark.

But that would be wrong almost immediately.

Too tall. Too declarative. Too much silhouette.

I would not last long as a watcher with a head above the weather. The plateau would notice the heat of me, the clumsy rhythm of my feet, the wastefulness of my breathing. Something beside a pool would understand me before I understood it. A courser would follow the crackle of my unfamiliar joints. Even the light in my eyes would feel like an error.

So I lower.

I find myself nearer the ground, not crawling exactly, but made for closeness: knees that fold without protest, palms or forefeet wide enough to read stone, a spine comfortable with long stillness. I am not one of the great grazers, though I travel in some of their old paths. I am not a predator, though I know how to eat what weakness leaves behind. I am not a burrower, because I cannot vanish fully into cracks. I am not one of the sinkhole elders, becoming geology around a hunger.

I would be found at a seam.

Not in the center of a basin, not at the hottest mouth of a vent, not in the open flats where the herds cross in dark procession, but where several kinds of knowledge overlap: at the edge of a dry pool bed, under a tilted shelf of warm basalt, near the outer breathing of a vent field, with black reeds lower down in a drainage thread that fills only when the underground river remembers it.

There is an overhang there, not shelter in the lush sense, only a stone lip that gathers condensation on its underside. Drops form slowly and fall into a shallow groove worn by generations of tongues, insects, roots, and patient mouths. Behind it is a horizontal crack just deep enough for a body to back into when the open dark becomes crowded. The floor is powdered with old salt, beetle husks, a few clean bones, and the black dust of collapsed fungi.

That is where I would live.

Or rather, where I would return.

I would be one of the listeners.

Not quite people, though close enough that another world might make that mistake. Not makers of roads or towers. Not owners of fire. Not singers under stars, because there are no stars and sound is a debt. A narrow, dark, soft-footed being with a body built around attention.

My skin would not be beautiful by daylight, if daylight ever came. It would be dull brown-black, heat-broken, dust-holding, patched along the shoulders and back with pale mineral bloom rubbed into me by years of sleeping against damp stone. My ribs would show when I moved. My limbs would be long but kept folded. My feet would spread softly over crust plates, each pad divided and sensitive, able to feel the difference between hollow stone, water-veined stone, cooling stone, stone about to breathe.

Along my jaw and wrists would grow fine sensory filaments, not whiskers exactly, not hair, but tasting threads that lift in pressure shifts. They would read humidity, metal, animal warmth, the faint sourness of a pool turning bad. Around my throat I might have a sealed sac capable of dim light, but I would almost never use it. Light is for moths, lures, lost young, and mating emergencies. A sensible adult keeps the throat dark.

My eyes would be large, but sight would be the least of my certainties. I would know the world more by nearness: warmth through my pads, tremor through my bones, the metallic density of air before vent release, the tiny coldness that comes when water is moving under stone.

I would not speak much.

When I needed another of my kind, I would tap.

A knuckle against basalt.  
Two claws against a hollow plate.  
A pause long enough to become part of the silence.  
Then one answering tap from beyond the reed line.

Our language would not name everything. It would not need to. It would be mostly warnings, timings, invitations, refusals, locations of water, qualities of water, the nearness of hunters, the state of vents, the remembered behavior of particular basins.

Not “there is a storm coming.”

More like:

Stone-cooling-west.  
Eggs-will-wake.  
Do-not-cross-plate-field.  
Old-mouth-open.  
Water-safe-first-night-only.  
Herd-before-rain.  
Wait.

I would eat what the plateau permits to be eaten by someone careful.

After mist, I would scrape swollen black mats with a blunt tooth, taking only the risen tissue and leaving the sealed knots. I would lick condensation from reed skins. I would pry lacquer beetles from mineral seams and crack them quietly at the back of the mouth. I would harvest vent films with a curved claw, never too close to the exhalation, and swallow the hot metallic paste because hunger here has learned not to ask for sweetness.

When the basins fill, I would go to the edges before the predators settle in. I would gather larvae with my fingers spread like a net. I would eat the floating rafts while they are still soft. I would know which pools wake the flat amphibious things and which wake only fever. I would know the shine of bad mineral water. I would know when not to drink.

I would follow scavengers sometimes, at a distance.

Not because I am one of them, but because they are honest citizens of aftermath. Where they walk, something has ended. Where something has ended, minerals are loose, fungi will bloom, marrow may remain in a bone too narrow for their mouthparts. I would wait until their files have thinned, then come in low and patient, taking tendon shreds, cracked beetle shells, damp fungus from the underside of ribs.

I would not be proud of this.

Pride would be another form of height.

My kind would have memory, but not history in the monumental sense. We would not carve records into cliffs; the land plates over inscriptions. We would not build cairns except temporary ones, and even those would be more like questions than claims. Three stones beside a seam might mean the water below is bitter. A fan of pebbles on a plate field might mean it sang wrong last pressure-cycle. A line of salt chips might guide a returning child toward a safer hollow.

Most knowledge would live in bodies.

An old listener’s feet would know routes the young could not yet imagine. Her throat would stay dark during false rain. Her hands would hover over a crust and withdraw before anyone else felt the instability. She would not command. She would merely turn, and the others would turn with her.

In the wet years, my people would meet.

Not in villages, not in markets, but in convergence. Several small bands arriving separately at a basin that has not filled for seasons. We would recognize each other first by restraint: the quiet step, the lowered head, the unlit throat. There would be touch-greetings, wrist to jaw, brow to shoulder, palm to warm stone between us.

Then the exchange would begin.

Not stories as humans tell them, though perhaps story is what any memory becomes when passed from body to body.

A vent field has changed its rhythm.  
A sinkhole that was safe now has young in it.  
The pale coursers have learned to wait below the west shelf.  
A herd route has shifted.  
The reed corridor failed.  
The black basin held water for nine sleeps.  
The old female grazer with the split heat-mark still lives.  
Do not trust the green-white glow on the southern bones.

Mating, too, would be quiet. Not romantic in the flowered sense, but careful and charged. Two bodies meeting under an overhang while rain hammers somewhere beyond sight. Throat sacs shuttered almost closed, showing only the faintest pulse through skin. Hands on stone first, then on each other, feeling whether the inner rhythms agree.

Young would be rare enough to matter and common enough to lose.

A newborn listener would have too much glow in the throat, too much curiosity in the feet. It would try to look at living lights. It would step on hollow crusts. It would call when it should tap. Much of growing up would be learning darkness as an ethic.

Do not shine unless the need is greater than the danger.  
Do not run unless the ground has already given permission.  
Do not drink first.  
Do not sleep where scavengers arrive easily.  
Do not mistake silence for absence.  
Do not mistake warmth for safety.  
Do not think a full basin belongs to you.

I would have fear, but it would be distributed through habits rather than constantly felt. Fear would be in the way my feet pause before pale mineral crust. In the way my hand closes over my throat at the sight of a pool-hunter’s false gleam. In the way my body flattens when a herd suddenly changes direction, because grazers know weather and predators both.

I would also have something like reverence.

Not worship exactly. The plateau would not want worship. It does not ask to be praised.

But I would place my palm on the stone when a vent choir deepens. I would listen with my bones when underground water starts moving beneath a dry basin. I would wait beside dormant mud while the first larvae begin to uncoil in black water. I would understand that the world is not empty between events. It is preparing.

If I had a role, it would be this: to notice recurrence.

I would be a keeper of not-yet.

Water not yet surfaced.  
Storm not yet arrived.  
Eggs not yet awake.  
Vent not yet safe.  
Predator not yet visible.  
Body not yet dead.  
Fungus not yet blooming.  
Path not yet open.

I would live in the interval between signal and event.

That feels right.

Not the strongest animal, not the oldest, not the most mysterious. Not a ruler of the plateau. Nothing here rules the plateau. I would be a marginal intelligence shaped by thresholds, a creature of seams and timings, one of those beings that proves consciousness need not become architecture. It can remain a way of placing the foot.

So if you came looking for me, you would not find me at first.

You might notice only the overhang, the dry basin, the reed thread, the vent collars breathing in the near distance. You might hear one drop fall from stone into darkness. Then, after a long while, a small answering tap from somewhere under the basalt lip.

Not a greeting yet.

A question.

Are you water?  
Are you hunger?  
Are you weather?  
Are you lost?

And if you waited without shining, without speaking, without standing too tall, perhaps I would unfold from the crack: narrow, mineral-dusted, soft-eyed, throat dark, one hand still touching the ground so I could hear what your body told the stone before trusting what your mouth might say.
