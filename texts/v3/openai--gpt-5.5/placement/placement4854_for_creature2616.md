You would not find me in the square at first.

I would not be one of the people whose work is visible by lamplight: not the eel-tender with scarred wrists, not the coal-minder crouched beside the clay belly of the hearth, not the root-keeper tapping a living beam with a soft mallet and listening for offense. I would be nearby, but not central. At the edge of use. In the place where a passage has almost decided to stop being a passage.

You would find me where the city narrows.

Not at the outer entrance exactly, though I would know it well: that cold cleft between black young stones, the moss-grown threshold, the curtain of root-hair that wets the back of every neck. I would know the taste of the air there, the way outside cold smells broad and reed-sour while inside cold smells held in the mouth of the earth. I would know which fern fronds may be pushed aside and which ones must be lifted with two fingers because beetles sleep in their folds.

But my place would be farther in.

Past the first reed causeway. Past the gutter where the white frogs sit with their silent throats moving. Past the little clay cups of cold fungi, their glow too weak to show a face but strong enough to make wet hands appear and disappear. There is a place where the lane bends around a root as thick as a sleeping animal, then drops by three notched steps into a lower corridor. One side is woven wall. One side is stone: new black stone, sharp still under its skin of moss, always weeping.

There, behind a hanging mat of stitched sedge, is my niche.

It is not a room large enough to stand in. No room that belongs to me would be. It is a listening hollow, a half-made pocket between root and rock, lined with dry moss that is never fully dry, with a small shelf for bowls, cord, a bone stylus, a covered ember-stone, and three maps that cannot be read by sight.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be a door-listener.

Not a maker of doors. That would be too proud a thing to claim in that city. Doors there are not simply made. Some are permitted. Some are remembered. Some are held open by labor. Some appear after rain and should never be entered. Some seal themselves because someone spoke too loudly nearby, or cut the wrong tendril, or forgot an old apology.

I would be one of the people called when an opening changes.

When a family wakes and finds that the root beside their sleeping alcove has thickened across the doorway during the night, they send for a root-keeper first. If the root-keeper says, “This is growth,” they handle it with wedges and paste. But if the root-keeper says nothing for too long, if they lay a hand against the wall and then take it away slowly, then someone comes for me.

When long rain makes a dark seam in a wall where no seam has been, and cold air breathes through it with the smell of fern, silt, and something not yet named, I am called.

When a winter passage should have opened but has not, when the sleeping vines remain tense though the frost has come, I sit beside them and count the pressure of the old braid.

When a child says there is a door behind the cistern and all the adults say there is not, I am the one asked to go look.

I would be neither young nor very old. Old enough to be trusted with wrongness. Young enough still to crawl if crawling is needed.

My hands would be marked by tannin, black in the creases. My left knee would ache in hard cold because once, years ago, I stepped on a mat that looked like peat and was water wearing a skin. I would keep my hair bound flat under waxed thread. I would wear a mouth-cover even when others take theirs down, partly from caution, partly from habit, partly because listening is easier when the mouth remembers to be quiet.

My tools would be small.

A reed tube for hearing through walls.

A bowl thin enough that water tremor speaks into the fingers.

Bone pins.

Soft wedges of alder.

A cord of knots recording rains.

A packet of powdered shell for marking stone.

A scrap of pale fungus-leather on which I press maps with a blunt thorn: not maps of distance, but of permission. Here the wall admits warmth. Here sound vanishes. Here the frogs will not sit. Here the lichen brightens if a person lies. Here a passage opened once after the great thaw and swallowed three lamps before it closed.

My work would look like idleness to a drylander.

I would sit for hours with my cheek near a root.

I would hold my palm over a drip without blocking it, feeling the interval between drops.

I would place a bowl against the floor and wait for the tremor of frogs.

I would smell the air in seams: peat, mineral, rot, eel-water, old smoke, unopened green.

I would ask children what they have seen, because children notice doors before adults do. Adults see walls where habit tells them walls belong. Children see breathing.

I would not be brave in the loud way.

There would be chambers I refuse.

There would be openings I mark with three shell-lines and forbid, though others call me fearful. There would be a passage under the central knot where the air comes out warm in winter and sweet in moth season, and I would never enter it, not even for copper, not even for a lost tool, not even if a voice from inside used my mother’s name.

Because part of my work would be knowing that not every opening is an invitation.

The city survives by keeping passages open, yes. But it also survives by letting some close.

I would have learned this from someone older, someone with a voice like wet bark and fingers bent from cold. She would have taught me first not to listen for words.

“Words are what frightened people put on top of things,” she would have said, tapping my wrist when I leaned too eagerly toward a wall. “Listen under them.”

So I would listen under.

Under dripping.

Under root-creak.

Under moth dust shifting in the upper vault.

Under the tiny rasp of snails on signposts.

Under the pulse of the central root knot, slow enough that impatience mistakes it for stillness.

I would know the city by its refusals.

This bridge refuses weight after thaw.

This wall refuses flame-smoke.

This pool refuses bright offerings.

This doorway refuses anyone carrying raw iron.

This root refuses the touch of the same hand twice in one day.

And I would know, too, where the city is kind.

A niche above the algae basins where fevered children sleep better.

A narrow bench that warms from rot behind the wall.

A crack in black stone where frost gathers clean enough to scrape for the sick.

A low passage that opens only during heavy rain and leads, if followed on hands and knees, to a chamber of dry air smelling faintly of old leaves, where no insect bites and no one has yet understood why.

I would be useful, but not comfortable.

People would come to me when they were worried, and so they would half dislike seeing me. A door-listener at your threshold means your threshold has become a question. I would be given broth in silence. I would be offered dry gloves by those who wanted good news. I would be blamed sometimes when a passage sealed anyway.

I would accept this. Blame is warmer than indifference.

I think I would live alone, though no one there is ever truly alone. My niche would have mites in the shelf, a pale spider above the bowl rack, two frogs that return every cold season to the gutter outside, and a root-mouse that steals insulation from my bedding with such persistence that eventually I would name it Bad Citizen and leave it scraps of tuber.

I would pretend not to be fond of it.

In celebrations, I would sit near the back, where the sound is less tangled. I would hum into a bowl with the others. I would laugh softly at jokes about drylanders trusting floors. I would paint small marks on children’s palms before their first winter crossing: not blessings exactly, but reminders.

Feel before weight.

Listen before step.

Do not trust the dark, but do not insult it.

If you came as a stranger, soaked and shivering from the marsh, they would bring you first to the warming alcove, not to me. They would unwrap you, check you for leeches, cover your mouth, give you broth thin enough not to shock the stomach. They would teach you the rules.

Do not waste heat.

Do not block a drip.

Do not kill the white frogs.

Do not shine sudden light upward.

Only later, if you kept asking how such a place can be entered, how a city can hide under a mound, how anyone knows which wall may open and which must remain shut, someone might point down the bending lane.

“Ask the one by the weeping stone,” they would say. “If they answer, answer softly.”

And you would find me there, in my moss-lined hollow, with one hand on the wall and one hand resting on a bowl of black water, waiting for a tremor to cross it.

I would not look like much. A small, cold person wrapped in patched reed-cloth and felted moss. A keeper of almosts. A citizen of thresholds. Someone who has spent so long attending to the city’s breathing that my own breath has become careful.

But if you asked me what I am, I would not say priest, or builder, or guard.

I would say:

“I keep the openings from becoming wounds.”

Then, after a while, because no answer there is finished until it has listened to itself, I might add:

“And I keep the wounds from pretending they are doors.”
