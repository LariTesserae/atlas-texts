I would not be one of the birds at first, though I would envy their suddenness.

I would not be a horned animal either, not fully — too much body, too much risk, too much need to place a hoof correctly and be responsible for weight. I would watch them and understand their patience, but I would not want horns, not in that narrow place. Horns are almost ambition. Horns imply a head lifted against stone.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not at the highest ledges, where the rookery darkens the cliff like soot. Not in the open tilted floor, where every movement is seen and every fall becomes food. I would be near the cleft, but not standing before it. I would be in the wetness around its mouth: in the black seam where cold air breathes out and touches warmer stone, where condensation gathers before it knows whether it will become water.

I would be a green-black film.

Not one creature, exactly. Not one life with a face and a boundary. I would be a slow, clinging colony laid thin over stone, dark until disturbed, faintly luminous when touched. I would live on iron, seep, mineral, the unfinished chemistry of the young mountain. I would be older in method than anything with wings, but new here, still learning this particular crack, this particular rhythm of underground water.

Most of me would be hidden.

A skin inside the cleft where no bird can reach. A slickness beneath the overhang. A tremor of green at the bottom of a hand-sized pool. A taste on the wall where the little horned mammals press their tongues and close their eyes. I would not move across the hollow as animals move. I would arrive by dampness. I would spread by contact, by drip, by beetle-foot, by the fine hairs of moss, by the mouthparts of larvae, by the accidental generosity of rain.

My body would be a place things happen.

Midges would hatch through me. Beetles would graze me down to pale stone and I would return after dark. Worms would taste me with their blind bodies. Moss would drink from the water I darkened. The cliff-mammals would take me into themselves with salt and cold mineral, and then the birds would take them, or their droppings, or the insects that fed on them. I would enter everything without ever becoming large.

I would know the hollow without seeing it.

The birds would be pressure and interruption: many small claws above me, grit falling, the hot brief rain of droppings, the sudden shadow-flutter when they burst from a ledge. The mammals would be slower weather: hoof-knock, breath, muzzle, tongue. Their licking would be devastation and intimacy both. They would erase whole fields of me in dark rasps, and I would remember them as warmth passing over stone.

The insects would be my nearest citizens. They would walk through me, divide me, carry me, harvest me, shelter in the moisture I held. Their crescent walls of mineral grains would matter to me more than cliffs. A ridge no higher than a seed would change the way water lingered. A larva’s body would be a moon crossing my pool.

And beneath all of it, always, the cleft would sound.
