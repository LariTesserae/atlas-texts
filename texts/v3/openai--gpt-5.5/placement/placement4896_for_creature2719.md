At first I think I would be one of the visible things.

A raven, perhaps, because the basin seems to have invented ravens in order to give thought a black body. I would stand on a wet boulder with my feathers tightened against the cold, turning my head toward the smell of a half-buried carcass under snow. I would know the basin by absence: where the lamb did not rise, where the fox passed, where the ice loosened something edible.

Or I might be a pika, all urgency and stored summer, vanishing into the broken rock with a mouthful of sedge. That life has a clean severity: gather, listen, warn, survive. To be a small warm engine inside talus while the world freezes around me.

But I do not think I would be allowed so much motion.

I would be found lower.

Not in the center of the basin, where the pools hold the dull sky, and not on the high ridges where the goats place their hooves like prayers. I would be on the side of a black volcanic boulder just above a seep-line, where fog beads and freezes and melts again. A place easily overlooked: windward enough to receive weather, sheltered enough not to be scoured completely away. Below me, a narrow thread of water would run under stones. Above me, quartz would show in the rock like a pale vein.

I would be a lichen.

Not a single creature exactly, but a compact agreement. Fungus, alga, bacteria, mineral hunger, light hunger, all clasped into one small patience. A crust no thicker than old paint. Orange at the rim, ash-gray toward the center, sulfur-yellow where the moisture comes most often. From a distance I would look like a stain. Up close, I would be a country.

I would live by almost nothing.

A little dim light strained through cloud.  
A little water condensed from fog.  
A little mineral loosened from stone.  
A little warmth from the rare hour when sun touches the boulder.  

I would not hurry, because hurry belongs to animals with blood and fear. My time would be basin-time: thaw, frost, rime, melt, burial, exposure. A goat’s shadow might pass over me and be gone in a breath. A raven might land above me, black claws clicking on stone. A midge might rest on my surface as if on a continent. The winter could cover me entirely, and I would wait without complaint.

My work would be almost invisible.

I would hold to the rock. I would drink from the air. I would darken the surface grain by grain. Under me, the stone would begin its slow surrender. Not collapse, not dramatic ruin—just the first softening, the first permission. One day a flake would lift. One day dust would gather. One day moss might find enough purchase near my edge. One day a seed, blown low by wind, might lodge in the roughness I helped make.

I would be part of the basin’s first handwriting.

Not a sentence yet. Not even a word.

A mark. A color. A beginning.
