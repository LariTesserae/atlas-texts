At first I think I would be found in the central hollow, where everyone passes and everything must be known: beside the planked ring, under the place where lanterns are hung in fog, with a basket of tools and a voice always being called for.

But that is too exposed.

Then I think I might be one of the animals: a roof-cat slipping through reed walls, a heron in the drainage beds, a damp dog with bridge rights and opinions. That comes closer, but not quite. I would not belong wholly to the human paths or wholly to the animal ones.

I would be found at the edge where the town begins to come undone.

Not outside it, not in the wild forest, but near the western channels where the shelf tilts toward the marshy ravine. There, the walkways grow narrower and the houses stand higher on their stilts. The boards are always slick. The pigs know secret ways beneath them. The reeds below are taller than children, and the water moves black and slow under floating mats until rain wakes it and sends it shouldering through everything.

My house would be a low, patched thing built partly around a living alder, partly over a drain, partly into a bank that never fully agrees to hold still. Its roof would have too much sedge on one side and not enough on the other. People would comment on this. I would say I know. I would mean that I know and have known for years and will fix it when the western seep stops changing its mind.

There would be coils of bark rope hanging under my eaves, bundles of split willow, spare pegs, lengths of hollowed gutter, baskets of river stone, a mallet darkened by hand-oil, and three knives, none of them good but all of them faithful. My door lintel would be a bent branch trained by someone dead before I was born. I would touch it when leaving, not from reverence exactly, but because it would be rude not to.

I would be a keeper of small passages.

Not a chief. Not a priest. Not even a builder of the impressive kind. I would be the one called when water has begun speaking in the wrong place.

A drain-listener. A bridge-mender. A person who knows the under-town.

I would spend much of my life crouched, wet to the elbow, clearing leaf clots from stone channels with a hooked stick. I would know which trickles are harmless and which are the first thin tongues of a new problem. I would know the smell of a house-post beginning to rot below the floor. I would know the note a plank gives when it is tired. I would know how to persuade water around a hearth, beneath a goat pen, away from burial moss, through reed beds, into cisterns, out again.

Children would be sent to fetch me.

Not politely. Breathlessly.

“Come. The floor is bubbling.”

“Come. Old Mera says the frogs stopped.”

“Come. There is water in the grain wall and my mother is saying words.”

I would grumble because grumbling is part of the work. I would take my cord, my knife, my wedge-bag, my rain cape if it was where I left it, and I would go.

My hands would be broad, dark at the nails, with little pale scars from cane splits and fish spines. I would always have a bruise somewhere from striking a knee against a hidden root. Leeches would find me with insulting ease. My hair would never be dry except in memory. I would smell of smoke, wet bark, cold mud, goat, and the sharp green scent of crushed fern.

I would not be especially old, but children would think I was, because I would already speak like someone who has lost arguments with weather. Adults would trust me only after arguing with me. Elders would not praise me much, which would be how I knew I was doing well.

I would have learned from an aunt or grandfather who could hear water through wood. As a child I would have followed them under platforms, into culverts, along the black channels where pale crayfish backed away like bits of moon. I would have hated being cold. I would have loved being necessary. I would have learned that the town has another map beneath the walking map: a map of seep, root, pressure, hollow, rot, and escape.

That would be my map.

When others spoke of households, I would think of flows. When they said, “Taro’s house,” I would think: upper roof draining east, bad gutter joint, goat urine cutting the post base, good moss on the north wall, dangerous fern near the smoke hole, three children who drop plum pits into the trough. When they said, “the old bridge,” I would hear its sequence of repairs: willow, alder, slate pin, storm-year lashings, new rail after the boy fell, underside gnawed by pigs, left footboard ready to turn treacherous.

I would be intrusive in the way of the town’s hospitality. If a visitor set a pack in a low corner, I would move it without asking. If they stood under a loaded roof edge, I would pull them aside by the sleeve. If they complained, I would point to where the water fell three breaths later.

I would not say, “I told you.”

Unless I liked them.

My closest neighbors would not all be human. The ducks would be my enemies and collaborators. I would curse them for fouling a clean channel, then rely on them to keep larvae down in the shallows. A particular dog, gray-muzzled and solemn, would accompany me on night rounds until it decided I was too slow. A roof-cat would have kittens in my dry basket and I would pretend outrage while placing smoked fish scraps nearby. There would be a green-backed stone near the ravine path where salamanders gathered after long rain; I would avoid stepping there even in darkness.

The bees I would respect from a distance. I would not be a bee person. I would be too abrupt, too full of knocking and prying. Around the hive logs I would make myself quieter.

I would know mushrooms, but cautiously. Enough to eat some, avoid more, and understand the terrible ones that mean a beam has begun feeding something it should not. If I found those pale shelf-fungi under a floor, I would sit back on my heels and sigh before calling the household. There are repairs that are knots and pegs. There are repairs that are news.

In festival times I would help hang lanterns over the small bridges, testing every rail as I went. I would stamp in the dances but watch the planks more than the dancers. Someone would press a cup into my hand and tell me to stop listening to the gutters for one night. I would try. I would fail. Halfway through a song I would hear a cistern overflow where it should not, and someone would find me later in the dark, laughing and swearing, ankle-deep in cold water, saving the feast firewood from a foolish little flood.

I would have a private tenderness for dry things.

A dry sock. A dry scrap of paper from the lower villages. A dry corner of bedding. A piece of firewood that rings when struck. I would store kindling better than money. I would give it away in long rain, resentfully and without hesitation.

If I had prayers, they would be mostly practical.

Let this knot hold.

Let the hillside wait until morning.

Let the child remember where the boards are weak.

Let the pigs not find the burial moss.

Let the frogs resume.

Let the water take the path we made for it, and not the one it is dreaming of.

And if I had a place that was most mine, it would be beneath the western footbridge at the hour before dawn, when mist fills the ravine and the town above is only weight, creak, drip, sleeping breath, and animal shifting. I would crouch there with one hand on the bridge post, feeling the tremor of runoff through wood. Above me, people would still be asleep in their patched, breathing houses. Around me, roots would hold the shelf together by patience and tension. Below me, dark water would be deciding what kind of day we would have.

That is where I would be.

Not at the center, but in one of the places that keeps the center possible.

I would be a small-weather person of the underways: the one who ties, clears, listens, props, redirects, and comes home soaked; the one who knows that a town is not made once, but remade by every hand that notices what has begun to loosen.
