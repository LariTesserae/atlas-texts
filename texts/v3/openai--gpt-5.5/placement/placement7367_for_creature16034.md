I would find myself near the old civic platform, but not in the open center of it.

Not beside the pool, not at first. I would be close enough to feel its rule over the place, close enough that people lower their voices before they reach my door, but I would live one level down and one level aside, in the rooms where the archive leans into the Water Office and the pressure hall shares a wall with the records.

There would be a narrow passage there, roofed in green glass patched from old tram windows. Condensation would run down the panes in crooked bright threads. On one side: waxed-paper maps, slate ledgers, drawers of mineral samples, records of fog behavior tied in cloth. On the other: pipes entering stone, each one with a brass tag, a listening cup, a handwheel polished by generations. The floor would never be wholly dry. Ferns would grow out of the crack under the outer wall no matter how often someone scraped them away.

That is where I would belong: in the seam between measurement and memory.

I think I would be a keeper of small changes.

Not an important official. Not one of the great water families who can taste a cistern and tell its source. Not a ravine walker with depth in my boots, not a condenser worker with frost on my lashes, not a gardener trusted with poisons and bees. I would be the person called when something has altered but not yet failed.

A pipe has begun to knock half a breath later than usual.

A wall is weeping although no tram has passed.

Moss has crossed a line of iron it has avoided for seventy years.

A cistern filled by three finger-widths during a dry fog.

The pool reflected a lamp before the lamp was lit.

Someone would send for me then, not because I could repair the thing, exactly, but because I would know how to sit with it until it became legible.

I would carry a satchel of dull instruments: a pressure notebook wrapped in oiled cloth, chalk, a folding rule, tuning forks, a little mallet, a wax pencil, strips of blotting paper, a cracked thermometer that everyone distrusts but no one throws away. My hands would be ink-stained more than oil-stained, though there would be oil too, under the nails. I would have a bad habit of touching walls while I walked, reading temperature and vibration through my fingertips.

People would call me a seam-reader.

That would not be an old title. It would be one of those city names that begins as a joke and then becomes useful. The city is full of seams: in stone, in weather, in districts, in memory, in the difference between water behaving naturally and water negotiating with something older than nature. My work would be to notice the seam before it opens.

I would live in a small upper room over the archive annex, with a low bed in an alcove and a shelf of inherited objects whose uses no longer quite match their names. A lamp regulator that hums at the wrong pitch. A pressure key from someone else’s grandmother. Three smooth stones taken from cistern mouths. A dead gauge whose needle moves only in fog. On the sill I would keep bitter herbs, a jar catching roof seep, and a tray of handmoss I was trying unsuccessfully to cultivate away from human touch.

My room would be warm only at scheduled hours. I would know them without checking.

At night, when the pressure hall below woke for a release, the pipes in the wall would thicken with sound. Not loud sound. A contained, bodily sound. The room would seem to inhale. The lamp would brighten, then steady. Drops would begin falling into the jar on the sill. I would wake halfway, count them without meaning to, and sleep again when the interval proved ordinary.

I would be one of those people who pauses too often.

In conversation I would stop with my mouth slightly open because some bracket behind the wall had shifted, or because the air had changed weight, or because a tramline three streets away had begun singing in a register it only used before rime. This would annoy some people and comfort others. Children would test me by making tiny false noises: a fingernail on a cup, a pebble against a pipe, a whistle shaped like fog-work. I would pretend not to notice until they became too pleased with themselves.

I would like children best when they were still believed by the city.

Before adults corrected their descriptions. Before they learned to translate what they saw into acceptable reports. I would ask them what the pool had shown. I would write down their answers carefully, even the impossible ones.

“Lights going downward.”

“A roof under the water.”

“A man with no wet on him.”

“The sky behind the sky.”

“A fox walking upside down.”

The archive would not approve of the phrasing, but I would keep a second ledger.

Everyone who works near the civic pool would have a second ledger.

Mine would be hidden behind a cabinet of obsolete condenser diagrams. It would contain things that were not yet facts, or were facts only at certain pressures. I would record them in a narrow hand:

> Third fog after pruning week. Pool still at sixth hour. Reflection delayed by two breaths. No wind. South stair handmoss brightened when bellows door opened. Child of Bent Pear household reported “green stars under stone.” Cross-reference: old quarry basin hesitation, Year of the Lean Chimney.

I would not know whether I was a scientist, a clerk, or a minor priest of phenomena no one officially worshiped.

The city would not require the distinction.

In the mornings I would eat dark steamed bread with nut oil and sour plum paste, standing by the warmest wall in the archive kitchen. Someone from the Water Office would complain that the east cistern was lying again. Someone from the infirmary would come for records of mold fever. A tram mechanic would leave a broken gauge on the table with a note: “Only stutters near Low Seven. Don’t say cursed.” I would say cursed aloud just to see if the needle moved.

Then I would go out.

I would climb slowly. Everyone climbs slowly there, but I would climb with particular care because my breath would never have been the strongest. I imagine I was a sick child, one brought to the pool after lung fever and given a bead of honey to drop into the dark reflection. Perhaps that is why I became attentive. Perhaps the city entered me then not as revelation but as a habit of listening.

I would know the dry benches in every district. The warm pipes. The dangerous mosses. The shops where one could ask for broth without explaining exhaustion. I would be welcome in maintenance rooms because I would never touch tools without permission and because I would write down the names of machines correctly.

Names would matter.

Not official designations only, but the names given by use: Low Seven, Widow Valve, Mother’s Turn, the Patient Boiler, the North Throat, Little Liar, Old Blue, the stair that coughs, the wall that answers. I would believe that a name is not proof of life, but it is proof of relationship. That would be enough.

If I were sent to inspect a wall that wept in lines, I would not begin by scraping it.

I would sit opposite it and wait for a tram.

I would note whether the droplets gathered along old erosion grooves or newer mortar joints. I would hum softly at different pitches, embarrassed if anyone heard me. I would press blotting strips to the stone and label the mineral taste: iron, lime, old cistern, root-water, unknown. If the wall released all at once, I would step back, let it finish, then thank it under my breath because everyone does, whether they admit it or not.

I would not be especially brave, but I would go into the ravine mouths when required.

Not far. Never as far as the walkers. Only to the first pipe galleries, where the city’s underbreath becomes colder and echoes return wearing other shapes. I would hate the lamps seen moving where no crew was scheduled. I would record them anyway. My boots would come back smelling of rust and fern. For days after, I would dream of water falling upward through dark stone.

The ravine walkers would tolerate me because I listened more than I asked. One might bring me a piece of pipe furred inside with mineral bloom shaped like feathers. Another might leave crescent mud marks pressed onto cloth, taken from near an aqueduct mouth. I would store these in the archive under “Infrastructure: Unclassified,” which is where the city keeps many of its monsters.

In time, I might become known less as a person than as a function.

People would say, “Take it to the seam-reader,” meaning a chart, a rumor, a stain, a sound, a child’s account, a tool that had begun behaving like inheritance. I would grow older in damp cuffs and patched sleeves. My hair would be bound back carelessly. My knees would ache before pressure changes. The handmoss along my favorite stair rail would grow thick where I rested my palm.

I would have friends among practical people: a tram woman with black crescents under her nails; a fungal keeper who could diagnose contamination by closing her eyes; an archive elder who pretended to hate speculation but never threw away my unauthorized notes; a roof-hermit who sent down weather readings by basket-line written on scraps of tea paper.

I would love someone who repaired things more decisively than I did.

Someone who did not ask what a valve meant, only whether it opened. We would argue about that. I would say every failure begins as a message. They would say sometimes a cracked fitting is only a cracked fitting, and if you spend all morning interpreting it, the lower district loses heat. We would both be right often enough to remain irritating to each other.

If I were not human, though — if the city had worked on me longer than blood allows — I might become the archive itself in miniature.

A pressure cabinet.

A listening box.

A narrow brass-and-slate apparatus mounted in the wall between the records and the pipes, patched for a hundred years until no maker remained in its design. People would tap me twice before asking questions. My needles would not give answers so much as tendencies. Fog would bead on my glass. Moss would grow toward me but never touch. Children would see lights moving behind my dial and be told it was only reflection.

Perhaps that is closer.

Perhaps in this world I would begin as a seam-reader and end as one of the instruments.

Not by magic in any dramatic sense. Simply by being used, remembered, repaired, quoted, contradicted, and built around. My notebooks would become reference. My mistakes would become warnings. My favorite listening place would acquire a bench. Someone would mount my old cracked thermometer beside a newer gauge because, though inaccurate, it had known certain fogs better than its replacement.

The city makes afterlives out of usefulness.

So after death I would not want a monument. I would want my tools redistributed. My tuning forks to a condenser child. My notebooks to the archive, including the forbidden ledger if they were patient enough to find it. My good boots to someone with narrow feet. My pressure key to whoever had learned to hesitate before turning it.

And maybe one small thing kept near the pool.

Not in the pool. Never presuming that.

Just near it: a strip of brass etched with contour lines, set into the stone where people lower their voices. In heavy fog, water would gather along the engraving and wait there, trembling, until a tram passed or a child hummed the right note. Then the droplets would release together and run toward the shallow basin.

Someone might notice.

Someone might write it down.

That is where I would be: in the civic seam, between pipe and page, between the measurable city and the city that answers only when listened to correctly.

I would be a keeper of hesitation.

A listener before valves.

A recorder of damp, pressure, rumor, and return.

One more quiet function in a place where nothing survives alone, and nothing truly stops if it can be repaired into another form.
