I would not be at the waterfall first.

That would be too much like mistaking the loudest thing for the truest thing. The throat at the back of the hollow is the heart, yes, but not a place for a self to begin. It is already occupied by force, by repetition, by the old labor of water making darkness deeper. Anything that tried to live there as a separate thing would be unmade too quickly into noise.

I would find myself off to one side.

Under the overhang, where the smoke once clung low and never fully left.

Not in the center of the shelter, not where a body would have slept, but near the edge of the collapsed frame, where the bent saplings have sunk into moss and the stone still remembers hands. There is a flat working rock nearby, dulled by damp. A shallow basin gathers drops one at a time. The roar fills everything, but here it is slightly softened by the lip of stone overhead, made dense rather than sharp.

At first I might think I am one of the shelter ribs.

Blackened wood. Bent by old intention. Held in a shape that no longer serves its maker, but has not yet been released. A thing once cut, carried, leaned, tied. A thing that has become less structure than memory of structure. Fungal threads would lace me. Moss would pad my broken joints. The places where lashings bit me would remain as narrow dark rings, small bracelets of pressure.

But I would not be wood.

Wood still belongs too much to growth, even dead wood. It remembers sap. It remembers the upward pull.

Then I might think I am one of the placed stones at the entrance.

A low tooth in the mouth of the hollow. A marker no longer announcing itself, nearly natural, nearly forgotten, but still holding the beginning of the path in place. Snow would lean against me. Moss would climb my north side. Animals would pause before me without knowing why. I would not keep anything out, exactly. I would only make the crossing slightly more conscious.

But that is too declarative. Too much threshold, too much position.

I think I would be smaller.

I would be the bone awl tucked in the moss.

Not newly lost. Not shining. Not tragic. Just there.

A polished point, softened at the edges, lying half in the green near the basin where drops gather and fall. I would be a thing made by hands, but no longer held by them. A tool whose purpose has been taken away slowly enough that it does not feel like loss. Once I opened hides. Once I made holes for sinew. Once I passed through resistant material so that separate pieces could be drawn together.

Now I would make another kind of passage.

Between hand and hollow.

Between intention and weather.

Between the quick heat of bodies and the cold patience that receives them.

The moss would have reached me first as touch, then as cover. It would not bury me all at once. It would test my edges. It would find the shallow groove where fingers had held me often. Mineral damp would enter my pores. The polish from use would remain longest at my point, because use is a kind of brightness deeper than light.

Droplets would collect along me.

For a while, I would still seem capable of work. A bead of water would tremble at my tip, swell, and fall. Another would gather. Another. In that way I would continue piercing, but only downward, only through time. Each drop would make a tiny dark mark in the moss below, then vanish into it.

I would listen through contact.

Not with ears. Through the cold pressure of the ground. Through the slight tremor of the falling stream. Through beetles moving under bark. Through frost tightening around me in winter. Through the rare footstep that finds the old path and hesitates beneath the overhang.

If a person came close, I would know them first as warmth in the moss above me.

Then as salt.

Then as weight.

If they knelt and pushed their fingers into the green, perhaps they would touch me without seeing me. Their hand would close around something hard, pale, shaped. For an instant I would become tool again, because recognition has heat in it. The body remembers a made thing before the mind names it.

They might lift me.

The hollow would dislike that, though not violently. Around me, roots would release a little moisture. Moss would cling. A thread of fungus would break. The roar would grow larger in the hand that held me, as if the whole place were warning against separation.

In the open air I would look less impressive than expected.

Small. Browned. Slick. Not sacred. Not beautiful in the way museum things are made beautiful by distance. I would be intimate instead: a worked bone point, useful, nearly used up, carrying the shine of thumb and task.

And if the person understood anything, they would set me back.

Not exactly where I had been — no hand can do that — but close enough. Beside the basin. Under the overhang. In the damp margin between the working stone and the shelter ribs.

Then the hollow would resume me.

That is who I would be: a small deliberate remnant being slowly forgiven into place.

I would not be the creature entire. I would not be the roar, or the moss, or the long dark agreement of root and stone and water. I would be one of the places where that agreement has had to make room for human purpose. A hard little syllable in a language otherwise made of dampness.

Once, I made openings so things could be joined.

Now I would be the opening.

The hollow would pass through me: water, cold, fungus, memory, the pressure of vanished hands. I would keep my point for a long time, but not forever. The point would dull. The polish would cloud. Green would take the groove where fingers rested. Eventually I would stop being recognizable as an awl and become only a pale firmness under moss, something a root bends around, something water darkens, something the place includes without explanation.

But for now, if you wanted to find me, you would have to kneel.

You would have to put one hand on the wet stone to steady yourself. You would have to let the roar erase the usefulness of speech. You would have to look not for an object, but for a slight interruption in the moss’s softness. A pale line. A small angle. A point from another life, still making its slow passage into this one.
