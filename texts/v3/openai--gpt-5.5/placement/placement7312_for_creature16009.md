You would find me where the basin is neither fully pale nor fully black: at the edge of the central troughs, where a dark iron rib sinks gradually into a field of cracked clay and old salt.

Not at the loudest vent. Not in the open flats. Not among the tall chimneys with their separate voices.

I would be in a shallow lee, beside a slotted fissure that breathes steadily but not violently. The rock there would be warm enough to hold me through the long even light, damp enough each cycle to wake the pale films, but quiet enough that the ground still makes sense. A place of moderated force. A place where the basin speaks in layers rather than all at once.

At first I would be difficult to distinguish from the ground.

A low oval darkness. A plate of basalt fallen from nowhere. Salt gathered along my seams. Rust-colored dust in the grooves of my back. Condensation collecting on my shell and sliding down in patient threads.

I would be one of the shell-dark grazers, but an old one.

Not large in a dramatic way. Just heavier than I look, broader than a stone should be, with edges worn by years of levering myself over glassy shelves and mineral crust. My shell would not be smooth. It would be pitted, banded, accreted. The basin would have written itself onto me: white evaporite along the rear plates, black sheen over the shoulders, orange mineral stain near the mouth where I have fed too long at iron seepage.

My head would remain hidden most of the time.

I would keep it tucked beneath the front shield, where steam cannot sting the soft parts and salt cannot crust the small recessed eyes I hardly use. Sight would be a late sense, almost decorative. The world would reach me from below.

Through the broad underside of my body I would know the basin.

The deep turbine-roar of the western vents.  
The clacking cone far south, steady as an old error.  
The hollow danger beneath pale crust.  
The tremor of water gathering in a crack.  
The confused, blinding vibration of a vent field in heavy vapor.  
The faint living tightening of film under steam.

I would not think in images. I would think in pressures.

A safe route would be a sequence of tones. A remembered feeding place would be a warmth at a certain depth, a mineral taste in the ground, a vibration that arrives through the left side before the right. Distance would not be measured by sight but by how one trembling layer gives way to another.

I would move rarely, and never without reason.

When I moved, it would be with the deliberate effort of something that has learned the cost of every departure. One forelimb placed. Weight tested. Shell shifted. Rear body dragged forward. Pause. Listen. Again.

Across pale crust I would spread my feet wide and become almost weightless. Across dark glass I would hook the hard crescents of my pads into tiny flaws. At a pool lip I would lower myself slowly, not drinking first, but reading the shiver beneath the surface. If the water trembled with the wrong rhythm, I would leave it untouched.

I would feed on the pale vent-skins.

Not greedily. There is no greed in a place like this; greed belongs to abundance. I would press my blunt mouth to a living sheet and rasp it away in slow arcs, taking hours to clear a patch no larger than my body. The film would cloud the warm water faintly. Steam would bead on my shell. Minerals would grind softly between my mouth plates.

What I ate would taste of sulfur, iron, salt, bitterness, heat.

It would be enough.

Perhaps once every long interval I would meet another like myself. Not by surprise; surprise is for quick worlds. I would feel the other before I saw it: a low irregular pressure crossing the stone, a body interrupting the familiar vent rhythm.

We would stop.

For a long time, nothing would happen.

Then perhaps I would tap once with the edge of my forelimb. Not greeting exactly. Not warning. A statement of presence. The other might answer with a shell-scrape against dark rock. We would remain there, two stones admitting we are alive. If the film were broad enough, we might feed at opposite edges. If not, one of us would turn aside.

No anger. No affection as warm creatures know it. Only recognition, sparse and sufficient.

I would have no song because the basin already sings too much.

My voice, if I had one, would be contact: shell against stone, foot against crust, the weight of my waiting. I would not try to rise above the roar. I would enter it quietly, as another small rhythm among larger ones.

My home would not be a den in the usual sense. It would be a circuit.

A dark ridge that holds heat.  
A crack that keeps bitter water.  
A porous terrace where vapor collects in holes.  
A minor vent whose film returns after grazing.  
A hollow where the sound softens enough to sleep.  
A route around the strong vibration field.  
A place to wait when the ground becomes too loud.

I would know these not as possessions, but as permissions.

The basin does not give shelter generously. It allows continuation. That would be the whole shape of my life: receiving continuation from stone, paying for it with slowness.

If you came upon me in thick vapor, you might stand close before understanding. You might see only a black mound beside a steaming fissure. Then one edge of me would lift. A blunt head would emerge. The small eyes would not look at you with curiosity. They might not understand you as an animal at all. You would be a disturbance above the useful world, a warmth and a pressure poorly transmitted through the ground.

I would wait for you to become still.

If you did, perhaps I would know you as another temporary weight on the basin floor.

Then I would lower my head again and resume feeding.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of company. I would not be content either; contentment is too soft a word. I would be adapted past complaint. Patient, yes, though not virtuously. Reluctant, perhaps, but only because movement is expensive and the world is mostly already complete.

I would be a slow animal made of listening.

A mobile stone with a mouth.  
A memory of routes.  
A shell carrying the basin’s minerals in its body.  
A life that almost succeeds in looking like nonlife.

And when the vapor thinned and the ordinary light returned, I would become difficult to see again: a dark low form against dark low stone, still as the ancient engine beneath me, waiting for the next tolerable interval to move.
