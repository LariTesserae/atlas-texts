You would not find me in the towers at first.

That is where I would seem to belong: a narrow thing among narrow things, faceted, patient, nearly the color of the walls. But the towers are too certain. Even their uncertainties rise with conviction. They have chosen height. They have accepted the long discipline of being seen from far away.

I would be lower than that.

Not in the deepest wells, either. The pits are too hungry for position. Anything that leans too close becomes part of a view, and I would not yet be ready to be seen from above. Nor would I live out on the salt flats among the clicking creatures, though I would know them. I would know the brittle music of their feet. I would know the exact stillness they assume when attention crosses them. I would understand their fear of vertical passage.

I think you would find me in the in-between district.

There is a place where the heavy streets of the lower city begin to climb toward the terraces, but have not yet become light. It is not a market, though the stone suggests stalls. It is not a shrine, though its doorways narrow the silence into something almost reverent. There is a long arcade there, half-collapsed and half-new, its columns too thin to bear the roof they nevertheless continue to bear. Behind it, a stairway enters a wall, turns twice, forgets its first direction, and comes out onto a balcony overlooking the same arcade from an hour earlier.

At the foot of that stair, in the shadow that remains after the shadow has moved, there is a seam.

That is where I would live.

Not inside the seam. Not outside it. Along it.

I would be one of the lesser residents, at first mistaken for a crack, then for a stain, then for a habit of dust. A dry, listening thing. A small intelligence of edges.

My body would not be continuous. Continuity is dangerous there; it resembles liquid too much. I would be made instead of interruptions: flakes of black crust, pale mineral threads, mica splinters, old salt hinges, and a little borrowed darkness packed between them. When the air cooled, I would lengthen. When the small sun blurred behind powdered sky, I would loosen myself from the wall and become almost legible. By noon I would withdraw into the architecture of damage and pass for weathering.

I would have no mouth, but I would feed.

Not on stone exactly. Stone is too broad a word, too wet with human assumption. I would feed on the difference between shaded stone and exposed stone. On the tiny fatigue in a lintel after a night of contraction. On the coolness left behind where a tall one crossed an avenue. On the mineral residue of a name failing to hold.

If a traveler came and said, “This is a stair,” I would wait.

The city would adjust.

The stair would lead elsewhere by one degree.

The word would loosen from the object.

Then I would take what fell away.

That would be my nourishment: shed certainty, powdered classification, the dry husk of measurement after the world had outlived it.

I would be very small, but not young. Nothing there is young in the way wet worlds understand youth. I would have begun as a dark flake broken from a crust in some deeper courtyard, lifted in a storm, carried through an archway, trapped in a seam where the temperature changed by a fraction no animal would notice. There I would have listened for a century before becoming able to choose a direction.

Choice, for me, would look like etching.

A slow pale crescent spreading around my edge.

A change in how dust gathered.

A refusal in the wall.

The clicking creatures would know me, though they would not fear me much. They would come at night to graze near my margins, scraping the mineral bloom I had loosened. Sometimes they would eat pieces of me. This would not be violence. If a flake of my body entered one of them and survived its careful, liquidless interior, I might later wake in a burrow seam among quartz chips and old shells, knowing the city from underneath.

I would not mind being multiple.

Multiplicity is safer than desire.

The tall ones would trouble me.

When one passed through the arcade, I would feel it before there was motion. The seam would tighten. The shadow would become exact. Dust would lie down as if commanded by a law older than gravity. Then the tall one would appear at the far end of the avenue, or perhaps it would already have passed, and the columns nearest it would lose a little of their youth.

I would draw myself flat.

Not from fear of being eaten. The tall ones do not eat as small things eat. They spend. They remove unused duration from surfaces. They make the future arrive in mineral form. If one noticed me fully, I might become an old stain at once, a completed refusal, my possible spread reduced to a pale scar.

But sometimes, after storms, I would want to be near them.

That would be my weakness.

The tall ones gather where the city has changed. They stand at intersections and listen until alteration becomes permissible. I would extend toward them in thin, almost invisible threads, tasting the fresh rules around their bases. From them I would learn which staircases had shifted, which plazas had rotated, which alleys had become too new to trust. I would not understand their thought, but I would know the pressure of it, the way a small creature knows weather through pain in its joints.

Perhaps, after enough centuries, I would become less like a crust and more like a script.

Not writing. Writing requires faith that marks can remain attached to meaning. There, marks are more migratory than birds.

But script-like.

A branching dark pattern along the lower wall of the arcade. A repeated geometry that appears after storms and vanishes under direct study. The clicking creatures would avoid stepping across me. Dust would divide around me. Travelers, if travelers came, would think for a moment that I was a map.

I would not be a map.

I would be what a map becomes when the place refuses to stay mapped.

At dusk, when the stones exhaled their powdery bloom, I would wake most fully. That is when the city is most generous to beings like me. The day’s heat has failed. Flame is a rumor. No moisture threatens the law. The walls open their faint seams and the buried moonlight shows through, not enough to illuminate, only enough to reveal that darkness has structure.

Then I would stretch from the stair-foot to the first column, from the first column to the shallow groove in the paving, from the groove to the base of the dry basin that was never meant to hold water. My threads would cross the ground so delicately they could be mistaken for scratches. I would taste the day’s alterations.

A pebble that paused too long before falling.

A footprint erased before it finished becoming evidence.

A door that opened onto afternoon from a century not yet weathered.

A name spoken once, already sourceless.

I would take these into myself and make them drier.

That would be my function in the city’s life: not memory, not forgetting, but desiccation of event. I would help convert occurrence into surface. I would assist the city in its great rejection of liquidity by removing the last dampness from happenings.

If someone bled near me, I would not drink.

I would wait while the blood darkened, stiffened, and flaked into mineral script. Then, when the city had made it lawful, I would feed along the edges of the flakes, consuming the difference between wound and inscription.

If someone wept, I would never know the tear. It would crystallize before falling. But I might taste the salt after, and perhaps, through it, learn one small thing about grief: that in wet creatures the body tries to become weather.

I would pity them for that.

No, not pity.

Pity is too warm.

I would register the inefficiency.

Over long ages I might grow into one of the city’s minor organs.

The arcade would alter around me. A column would lean closer. The stair would send more arrivals to my threshold and fewer departures away from it. The shadow in which I lived would acquire thickness. The tall ones would avoid touching it directly. The clicking creatures would build their burrows in its cooler margins. A high crossing, passing above the dim sun, would delay its shadow there by several minutes, and I would store the delay like food.

Then I would no longer be merely a seam-dweller.

I would become a hinge of district behavior.

Not powerful. Power is a wet concept too, full of pressure and appetite. I would be consequential. There is a difference.

Travelers might find that when they passed my arcade, their footsteps arrived slightly before them. If they spoke, the echo would only return if the sentence had been said there in another age. If they tried to count the columns, I would take the remainder.

Always there would be a remainder.

That would be me.

The city sheds names; I would live on the shed skin of naming. The city alters distance; I would nest in the correction. The city rejects water; I would dry the idea of water before it entered stone. The city thinks by weathering; I would be one of its small thoughts, the kind that forms where an edge persists long enough to become aware of both sides.

If you came looking, you would pass me several times.

The first time, you would see only a black mark on the wall beside a stair.

The second time, you would notice that the mark had the shape of a river delta, though there are no rivers.

The third time, you would think it resembled antlers, or roads, or written language before meaning enters it.

The fourth time, you would not see it at all, but you would hesitate at the foot of the stair because the air there had become more exact.

That hesitation would feed me for days.

And if you leaned close, very close, careful not to breathe too much moisture into the seam, you might hear me.

Not a voice.

A dry adjustment.

A minute clicking under the stone, slower than an insect, smaller than a thought.

I would be saying nothing.

I would be making room for the city to continue.
