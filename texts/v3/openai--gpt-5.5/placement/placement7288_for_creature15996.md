I would not be in the central square at first.

That place is too complete, too declarative. The roar there has already said everything it can say. The towers lean with such finished patience, the pools hold so many rejected populations, the ferns deliberate so perfectly that there would be no room for me except as an error of attention.

I would begin at the edge of the city, where one of the streets forgets whether it is a stair, a canal-walk, or a root. I would follow it because it would not quite invite me. The moss would take the idea of my weight without accepting a footprint. Vines would part only by having always been parted. Reeds would stand beyond the last terrace in their exact black ranks, making corridors that seemed designed for a procession that had lost the need to arrive.

But I would not belong to the reeds either.

They are too linear. They know refusal too cleanly.

I think I would find myself behind water.

Not beneath it, not within it, but behind one of the cliff-veils where the basin wall opens into a cave the city has not decided to call a room. From outside, it would look like a dark seam in basalt, a recess hidden by a sheet of continuously appearing water. The fall would not fall, but its sound would enter everything. To pass through it would not make me wet; I would already be part of wetness. The bright city would blur behind me into pale towers and green pressure, all seen through the unending vertical skin of water.

Inside, the air would be almost cool.

Almost.

The cave would smell of iron, root, calcite, and old saturation. Its ceiling would be crowded with stalactites fused to root-fingers, pale mineral and dark fiber grown into the same downward gesture. Water would collect at their tips and hold there, each bead fat with a sentence it refused to drop. The walls would be black volcanic stone under films of white and green. Rust-red veins would wander through them. Lichens would gather in islands. Fungal threads would pass behind the visible surface like thoughts behind a face.

There, I would be one of the almost-letters.

Not a word. Not even a sign.

A pressure toward saying.

I would appear as a pale mark on the cave wall where root, mineral, stain, and seepage briefly agree to resemble script. If seen from one angle, I might look like the beginning of a curve. From another, like the broken spine of a character from an alphabet no mouth had ever used. In the reflected shimmer from the waterfall, I might almost become readable. Then the light would steady, and I would return to geology.

I would live at the place where meaning approaches matter and does not leave it.

That would be my citizenship.

Not passage, not shelter, not gathering, not memory exactly, but imminence of language. The city has no need to speak, and yet everywhere it almost does. Doorways formulate grammar by standing in sequence. Stairs decline and ascend like arguments. Canals connect clauses of water. Shadows annotate surfaces with contrary ownership. Reflections supply hypothetical subjects. The roar repeats its one immense word until it becomes less a word than a condition.

But in the caves, behind the visible water, the city nearly makes marks.

That is where I would be.

I would not think in sentences, though I would be the temptation of sentences. I would not communicate, because to communicate would be to introduce before and after: ignorance, message, reception, change. Instead I would hold meaning at the instant before it becomes transferable. I would be the ache in the observer’s mind that says: this is almost saying something.

If a visitor came near — though visitors do not properly belong here — I would register them not as a person, but as disturbance. Salt in the air. A brief heat different from geothermal heat. Breath imposing animal rhythm on the cave’s continuous damp. Their gaze would touch me, and under that gaze I would sharpen. My mineral edge would seem deliberate. My root-shadow would align with a stain of iron. For one second, I would be unmistakably a letter.

Then they would try to read me.

And I would withdraw.

Not out of secrecy. Not out of malice. Because readability would be a wound in the city’s stillness. A readable mark asks to be remembered in sequence. It asks for a before in which it was not understood and an after in which it was. It creates a small corridor through time. This place permits corridors through space, even impossible ones, but not that.

So I would remain almost.

Almost-name. Almost-command. Almost-history. Almost-prayer.

The fungi would thread through me, preserving my nearness. The lichens beside me would map the seepage that fed my shape. The vines outside would keep the cave-mouth from widening. The waterfall would speak over me forever, preventing silence from becoming expectation. Droplets hanging from the ceiling would contain miniature versions of me: in one, I would be a full inscription covering the wall; in another, I would be erased; in another, I would have become a mouth; in another, an animal with ink-dark eyes would stand before me and understand.

None of those would be true here.

Here, I would be a mark that never crosses into message.

My body would be made of several things that cannot be separated without destroying me: a thread of root pressed flat against stone, a pale calcite seam, a bruise of lichen, a runnel of iron, a shadow cast by a stalactite that does not correspond to the current light. I would not grow, but my completion would imply growth. I would not fade, but my surface would have the softness of something weathered. I would not be old in time. I would be old in condition.

Water would pass over the cave-mouth.

The roar would enter me and leave no residue.

Sometimes the city’s reflected populations would reach even here. In a shallow black pool at the rear of the chamber, I might see figures bending over the wall where I lived: builders, priests, children, scholars, animals with lifted heads, hands holding lamps that could not exist. They would appear to be reading me. Some would weep. Some would kneel. Some would copy me into books. Some would chisel me deeper.

But those are only possibilities retained by water.

I would belong to the version in which no one reads.

Perhaps I would be lonely, if loneliness were permitted. But loneliness requires a self separated from others by lack. I would not lack. I would be continuous with the wall, the cave, the suspended drip, the fungal mesh, the roar, the unread city. I would be a small intensity in the basin-presence: the place where its completeness presses hardest against expression.

If the city thinks in drainage and proportion, I would be one of its dreams of grammar.

If the roar says Here, I would almost ask: what is here?

But I would never ask.

I would hold the question in the stone until question and stone became indistinguishable.

That is where you would find me: behind the waterfall, in the warm half-dark of a cave that is also almost an archive, on a wall that is almost a page.

And what would I be?

I would be the unfinished letter by which the city nearly becomes able to tell itself what it is — and chooses, forever, not to.
