I would not be in the center of the basin at first.

The center is too full of established appetite: mats already meters deep, herds already knowing where to kneel, thread-keepers with maps in their legs, bladder-vines swollen with old fog, root valves opening and closing according to rhythms older than any one body. To arrive there would be to arrive late, into an economy that had already named every warmth and shadow.

I would find myself at the margin.

Not the edge of the plateau itself, but one of its countless inner edges: a place where new black stone still shows through, where a recent fracture has split a ridge and exposed raw basalt, warm-smelling mineral glass, pale green seams, and frost not yet mastered by moss. On one side, a crowded mat presses forward, bristling with wire-green growth and blue-black lichen scales. On the other, bare rock glares under the hard sun, too new to have been softened, too bright to look at directly.

There, in the handspan between lifeless and claimed, I would live.

At first I might be mistaken for weather damage.

A tarnish. A crust. A thin dark bloom in the cracks, black when wet, silver when frozen, violet when struck by slant light. I would lie flat against the stone, no higher than a fingernail, spread over several meters in separate patches that are nevertheless one creature. Not plant, not animal, not fungus, though all three would have left instructions in me. A seam-being. A fracture-listener. One of the plateau’s first small intelligences at the frontier of stone.

I would be made for beginnings.

My upper skin would be mirrored in places, to reject the killing brightness, and matte black in others, to drink what warmth could be stolen. Under that skin would run conductive threads, not nerves exactly, but a damp mineral logic: salts, metals, antifreeze sap, fungal memory, the tiny stored shocks of daylight. I would keep my softest tissues down inside the cracks, pressed against the rock where frost enters and widens the world.

I would taste stone slowly.

Not by chewing. By leaning acid mouths against its weakness. By breathing into fractures. By holding water where water wants to flee. By letting freeze and thaw do part of my labor. Each night I would guide a little frost deeper into the basalt; each day I would relax around the widening seam and drink the loosened ions. Iron, magnesium, copper, sulfur, trace metals too faint for larger hunger. I would not mine like a machine. I would persuade like lichen.

The mat behind me would need what I found.

That would be my place in the work: not builder, not ruler, but interpreter between raw stone and the crowded green apparatus advancing over it. I would learn which cracks would hold water, which would shear open in the next cold, which would poison roots, which would feed them. I would send this knowledge backward in brief chemical pulses, in blue ticks of charge passed through wet moss, in mineral tastes delivered by beetles who walked over me and left with my dust on their feet.

Perhaps I would have no thought of helping.

Perhaps help would only be the shape hunger takes when hunger is old enough.

In the brightest hours I would draw myself tight and become almost reflective. The sun at that height would be too much even for me. My sensing pores would close. My mirror cells would tilt. I would go shallow and hard and let the plateau blaze over me.

But under the surface, I would listen.

The rocks there speak constantly. Not in voices, but in clicks, tension, minute collapses, the first whisper of a fracture before it becomes visible. Basalt cooling. Frost prying. Roots probing. Beetle feet tapping codes down the slope. Far below, the pump-beat of the mats. Farther still, a great one shifting weight and sending a pressure wave through the stone.

I would know the world first as vibration.

A winged flock lifting would arrive as a soft storm of impacts. A heliostat herd discharging at dusk would arrive as a sweetness in the ground, a faint brightening of my inner salts. A pale hunter passing through the thicket behind me would be almost silent, but not silent enough; its care would have a pattern, and I would taste the pattern as absence.

When rain came sideways, I would open.

All my small mouths. All my salt gates. All the folded membranes kept shut against glare. Metallic droplets would bead over me, and I would draw them inward before the wind could take them. My surface would darken. Filaments would rise, no taller than frost hairs, each tipped with a transparent cup. Some would catch spores from the old mats. Some would release my own.

My young would not look like me.

Some would leave as dust on helmet-beetles, tucked into the seams of copper armor. Some would freeze into crystalline fans on exposed stone and wait for a season of condensation. Some would be eaten by frost-combers and survive the gut, waking later in steaming dung beside a herd trail. Some would enter the mats and become, for a while, part of their underbody: a sensor, a mineral valve, a small dark memory of the rock before vegetation covered it.

I would be one individual only by courtesy.

Part of me would be crust on the fracture wall. Part of me would be a blue thread under moss. Part of me would ride in the crop of a reed bird. Part of me would sleep as silver dust in the hollow of a great grazer’s footprint. If one patch died under sun or was scraped away by beetles, the others would tighten, remember the loss chemically, and grow differently.

At night I would be more visible.

Not beautiful in the way the flowers are beautiful. Not with glass petals or cold lamps along ribs. My light would be low and intermittent: tiny green sparks moving along the fracture lines, amber specks under frost, a pulse like a thought reconsidered. From above, I might look like a broken circuit trying to complete itself across black stone.

The mature mats would glow behind me. Herds would stand in the dark, blue at their joints, emptying the day into roots. Lichens on the cliffs would shine in bands. The great vent-crowned bulk in the lower basin would breathe steam lit from within.

And I would face the bare rock ahead.

Always the bare rock ahead.

That would be my weather, my prey, my future. New stone too clean for life. New glare. New mineral silence. I would advance by less than the width of a seed in a day, and some days not at all. I would be patient because patience is the only body possible there.

If a traveler came and stood near me, I would know them first as shade.

A sudden mercy over my mirrored plates. Then pressure. Heat. Salt. Foreign minerals in the dust shaken from their boots. Moisture in their breath freezing and falling in crystals I could taste. They would think themselves outside the system, upright and separate, looking over the high cold continent as if it were landscape.

I would begin with their soles.

A silvering at the edges. A few exploratory threads in the scratches of leather or claw or whatever carried them. Not attack. Not welcome. Inclusion. I would test what they brought: calcium, iron, oil, pollen from lower altitudes, dead skin, fear-salt, tool-metal if they carried tools. I would tell the mat behind me, in my slow way: here is a new surface; here is warmth; here is something that does not yet know it has entered the work.

Who would I be?

I would be the plateau’s first question asked of stone.

A small dark intelligence at the fracture line. A crust that listens. A colony that becomes a map. A miner of cold light, a weather-stain, a messenger between basalt and root. Not one of the great animals mistaken for ridges, not a flock roaring up like torn weather, not a predator thinking in weeks.

I would be almost nothing.

The thin living edge where nothing becomes occupied.
