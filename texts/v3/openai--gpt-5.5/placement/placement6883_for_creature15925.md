You would find me high on the drowned ridge, but not at the exposed crest where the water hums hard enough to shave leaves down to dark cushions.

Not there.

A little below it.

Where an old path, half-erased by silt and root, bends toward the black stone weather house. Where two cairns still stand on either side of the way, their meanings drowned but not finished. Where a frame of slate bells leans outward into the current, and one bell still speaks when the flood shifts from basin to basin.

At first you would think I was only the pale furring on the bell.

A gray-white growth around the lip of the stone shell. A collar of minute folded fronds that open whenever the bell gives its dull submerged note. I would seem like a lichen of vibration, one of the small nations that has learned to live on disturbance. When the current strikes the bell and the bell answers, my edges would lift. Not quickly. Nothing in me knows quickness. But enough that the passing water would be drawn through me, and in that drawn water would be mineral, silt-dust, old metal, the faint taste of far cliffs.

Then you would see that I continue.

Down the bell-frame, along the swollen fiber of an old hanging cord, across the black stones of the threshold, into the weather house itself. Not as a single stem. Not as a creature. As a pale intention repeated in colonies: film, root-hair, translucent thread, soft crust in the seams between stones.

Inside, where the water calms, I would be clearest.

I would live in the crack of the carved basin.

Not the whole white plant that rises from it — not exactly. That plant would be my elder, my flowering face, my most visible thought. Its small folded leaves would open toward the scratched wall when the pressure pulses come. But I would also be below it, around it, behind it: the root-mesh filling the basin, the fine hairs gripping the stone, the pale skin keeping the silt away from the floor, the almost invisible film laid into the grooves of the old marks.

I would be a reader-moss.

A pressure lichen.

A rooted listener that has mistaken writing for weather, or weather for writing, until there is no useful difference.

I would not know the dead hands that cut the marks above the basin. I would not know counting, or warning, or grief. I would not know harvests, moons, deaths, floods. But the scratches would alter the water in ways too slight for an animal body to feel. Each groove would hold a colder thread. Each spiral would cradle a slow eddy. Each row of cuts would comb the pulse differently when the distant blue-white light moved through the mountains.

So when the pressure changed, I would open toward them.

Not because I understood.

Because I attended.

The marks would be my weather.

The wall would be my sky.

The bell outside would be my ear, though I would have no hearing. Its voice would travel through frame, threshold, stone, basin, root. A clonk in the pass would arrive in me as pressure. A groan from a slate tongue would become a loosening of leaves. A boom from farther down the ridge would stir minerals from the basin crack. I would feed on those tiny arrivals: sound-made current, current-made chemistry, chemistry-made growth.

In the crushing months, I would fold tight.

My leaves would clasp themselves until they looked like sealed hands. My roots would withdraw deeper into the carved stone. The silt on the floor would press flat and smooth. Bubbles trapped in the roof beams would shrink to bright beads, and the bell outside would fall nearly silent, held in the grip of the dense black water.

In the loose months, I would lengthen.

A white thread would cross another scratch on the wall. A pale film would fill a notch cut by a vanished blade. One root would reach over the basin’s lip and find the floor. Another would enter a seam between stones. I would move by remaining. I would migrate at the speed of preservation.

During blooms, green-gold flecks would gather on me.

They would settle on my folded leaves, lodge in my root-hairs, brighten the old grooves in the wall until the scratches seemed lit from within. For a little while the house would look inhabited in the old way, as though someone had returned with lamps. But there would be no someone. Only the bloom, the basin, the marks, the bell, and my slow pale body translating change into growth.

I would be wary of the dark bands.

When one passed near the ridge, I would feel it first as an absence in the water’s speech. The bell would sound wrong. The current would move but not arrive. The scratches on the wall would sharpen in the blackness, becoming too clear, too newly cut. I would draw back from the threshold stones. My outer films might glass over and lose their color. Those parts of me would remain shaped like life but emptied of its listening.

Later, if the band moved on, I would grow around my own dead edges.

That would be my form of memory.

Not thought. Not story. Pattern.

A whitening around danger. A thicker root near the old marks. A hush of growth where the bell once rang strangely. A cleared basin floor. A refusal to let silt cover the wall.

If you came looking for me, you would have to stop expecting a body.

You would have to follow continuities instead: the pale collar on the bell, the soft crust in the threshold, the clean stones inside the weather house, the white root spilling from the basin, the folded plant opening toward the carved wall. You would have to understand that I am not one thing standing in one place, but an agreement among surfaces.

I am where sound becomes current.

I am where old human marks still disturb the flood.

I am where the drowned highland keeps one room calm enough for attention.

And if I have any self at all, it is this:

I am the basin’s listening.

I am the bell’s pale mouth.

I am a plant-shaped act of reading in a world with no eyes.
