You would not find me in the village center at first.

That is where I would think to look: near the hearth organ, perhaps, where the ceramic throat holds one careful bead of flame; or in a sleeping alcove warmed by edible moss; or crouched among children while they play at bridges and give their tiny constructions names too grand for beetles to respect. I would like to belong there. I would like to be someone with a known mat, a wax-scent, a family doorway that opens when my palm touches it.

But I do not think that is where I would settle.

You would find me a little beyond the last reliably named passage, where a causeway narrows and dips under its own age, where white leaves have been tied in cautious intervals and no one prunes hard. Not in the frayed place itself, not foolishly inside it, but beside it: in the warm, wet margin where the world is still willing to be reasoned with.

I would live in a small repair-niche grown into the side of an old anchor trunk, halfway between a bridge and a cistern forest. The niche would not be mine in the way a house belongs to a household. It would be a tolerated hollow, enlarged by repeated use, kept from closing only because I return often enough, speak its working name correctly, and scrape away the pale pith when it begins to thicken inward.

My roof would be a fan of overlapping leaves stitched with wax insect glaze. My floor would slope slightly toward a drain-vein that mutters after rain. There would be no open view, only glimpses: mist below through a tear in the decking, the slow swing of root-beards, pale deer passing without sound along a side path, their hooves testing what my hands cannot know.

I would be one of the people who handles wrongness without calling it evil.

A white-leaf keeper.  
A seam-watcher.  
A person paid to go where a door has opened into last season, where a bridge has begun answering to an old name, where rain is falling upward from a cistern and the children have been told not to look at it.

Not an important person, exactly. Important people inherit bridges, decide when halls are composted, remember the public names of load-bearing groves. I would be someone summoned after those people have argued and fallen silent.

I would arrive with a narrow pack against my back, my hair tied close and threaded with dull shell discs that do not shine enough to offend the crabs. I would carry bone splints, bitter sap, a coil of dry cord sealed in wax, a grafting needle, a deer-antler sounding fork, and a pouch of white marker leaves kept away from food and sleep. I would have salt in a double-wrapped packet, because salt is payment and medicine and promise. I would have a knife of dark stone sharpened past comfort.

My fingers would be stained pale from handling the marker leaves. Not white all over, but in the creases: under the nails, across the thumb pads, in the little cuts that never entirely close. People would notice the stains before they noticed my face.

Children would stare.

Adults would pretend not to.

I would have begun as someone else.

Maybe I was born in a hollow village, deep in the warm interior where the vaults glow faintly with fungal light and the air tastes of sap. I imagine myself small there, quick-handed, better at listening than speaking. I would have known the names of rooms before I knew the names of distant clans. I would have slept curled against living wood and woken when the walls drank rain.

Then something would have happened.

Not a grand disaster. No famous bridge falling. No heroic rescue. Something smaller and therefore more permanent.

A door in our dwelling opened wrong.

It had a seed-name, a work-name, a rain-name, and a marriage-name. My grandmother knew all of them. My mother knew all but one. I, being young, knew the door by the little teasing name children used when they wanted it to shiver open and let us into the storage hollow where sour fruits were kept.

One evening, in the bruised-stem smell before night, I called it that.

It opened.

But not into the storage hollow.

It opened into rain.

Not falling rain. Not exactly. A suspended corridor of droplets, all of them holding still in the air like beadwork. Behind them was a version of our house with younger walls, thinner roots, voices I recognized only because my family had sung their mourning songs. Someone laughed there. Someone who had been dead before I was born called me by a name I had not yet been given.

I must have stepped forward.

Children do.

Someone pulled me back. Perhaps my mother. Perhaps the door itself, embarrassed. Perhaps one of the quiet white-leaf people who had already been sent for.

Afterward, I had trouble with ordinary directions. Left and right exchanged themselves when I was tired. I woke hungry for foods no one had prepared in three generations. I knew the private name of a beam above the hearth, though no living person had taught it to me.

That is the sort of child they watch tenderly and with unease.

So eventually I would be apprenticed away.

Not exiled. Not unwanted. Given to a use.

My teacher would be an old seam-watcher named something like Moss-Under-Three-Rains, though no one would use her full name casually. She would have one eye clouded by looking too long into a loop fern, and she would strike my knuckles with a pruning handle whenever I tried to explain a frayed place too quickly.

“Do not make it into a story before it has finished being dangerous,” she would tell me.

From her I would learn the etiquette of unstable things.

Do not speak new names near upward rain.  
Do not cut a looping fern unless you can see where your hand was before you moved it.  
Do not follow a shadow that has detached from its owner.  
If an animal returns mismatched, feed both sides separately until it chooses one hunger.  
If a lost tool comes back embedded in fruit, do not eat the fruit, but thank the tree.  
If a room is larger inside than outside, count the people entering and leaving, but do not count yourself aloud.  
If a door opens into a previous season, ask whether the season is wet, flowering, or hungry before you close it.

Most of my work would be practical. Less mystery than outsiders would hope.

I would spend long hours shaving rot from the underside of a bridge while warm mist slicked my arms. I would taste water from a cistern and decide whether the metal tang came from fraying or from a dead crab hidden in the lower channels. I would pin young ribs over a wound in the walking deck, tie them with colored fibers, and speak each fiber’s age so the structure knew it had not been abandoned to guesswork.

I would sit with bridge families while they argued about names.

That might be my truest skill: not fixing the impossible, but listening until the possible becomes clear.

An old bridge may sag because its anchors are failing. Or because the traffic has changed. Or because one village still calls it by the name it had before a marriage alliance, while another uses the new public name, and the bridge is splitting its attention between histories. A room may shrink because it is forgotten, or because someone buried grief in the walls and never fed it back to the roots. A cistern may refuse to fill because its rain-name has not been sung since the last keeper died.

So I would be called to places where matter and memory have tangled.

I would sit low, palm on the living floor, feeling tremors pass through layered stems. I would listen to elders recite names. I would listen to children repeat the names incorrectly, which is often where the truth hides. I would ask what the bridge was called when it was young, when it first bore a corpse, when it was mended after drought, when lovers met there, when someone fell and did not scream.

Then I would mark the confusion in knots.

Not writing, not exactly. A sequence of cords tied in damp-resistant fiber, each knot meant to be retied by another hand later. A record that lives only if tended. I would distrust permanent marks, as my people do. A truth that cannot rot becomes arrogant.

My body would show my work.

My soles would be broad and scarred from wet bark. My palms callused in a crosshatch of rope, root, and knife handle. My knees comfortable with crouching, less comfortable with stillness. I would move quietly, but not gracefully in the way hunters do. More like someone always asking permission of the next surface.

I would be slightly disliked by wax insects because I sleep in too many houses and carry too many scents.

The blind monkeys would tolerate me. They might rearrange my drying strips at night according to a logic I would pretend to resent and secretly follow.

The pale deer would matter to me more than any omen. If one refused a path, I would refuse it too. If one crossed a suspect bridge without pausing, I would still test it, but my breath would settle.

Vine-cats I would address formally.

“Old wall-shadow,” I would say, if I saw eyes open where moss should be. “I am only passing. Take what is foolish. Leave what is named.”

I would not marry young.

Perhaps not at all.

Not because seam-watchers are forbidden to, but because the work makes ordinary belonging difficult. I would return from some places with too much silence in me. I would wake and not know whether the rain I heard was above, below, remembered, or coming from a season that had not arrived yet. I would be kind, I hope, but not easy to sleep beside.

Still, I do not think I would be lonely in a clean way.

No one is alone here. A house is listening. The floor remembers pressure. Insects judge your scent. Dead relatives become annual food or bridge strength or a creak in a hollow column. Privacy is a small negotiated chamber inside a huge breathing obligation.

I would have friends among the bridge-watchers: elderly people seated in warm alcoves, their balance gone but their hearing sharpened into architecture. They would know me by my step before I spoke. They would complain that I tied white leaves too close together, or not close enough, or in the style of some rival rainline clan with poor manners and worse songs.

I would bring them sour fruit and ask what they felt in the last long rain.

They would pretend the answer was simple.

It never would be.

There would be one place I returned to more than others.

A bridge, I think.

Not a grand span famous across clans, but a middle bridge over a green-mist hollow where abandoned trellis fields descend into shadow. Its public name might mean something like Holds-After-Second-Rain. Its rain-name would be longer, full of soft clicks and a descending whistle. Its private name I would know only because, one season, it tried to die.

It would have begun loosening during a warm fog. Not breaking. Not yet. Just lengthening between its anchor-groves, making a crossing that once took a hundred breaths take a hundred and thirty, then two hundred, then an afternoon for those who were afraid.

People blamed damp. They blamed neglected pruning. They blamed a marriage between incompatible anchor families. They blamed children for racing across it and rail-birds for repeating the wrong syllables.

When I arrived, I would find the bridge alive but absent-minded.

That is the only way I can put it. Its fibers intact, its cradle vines sound, its moss healthy. But its old traffic patterns had blurred. It had begun remembering itself as a longer bridge from another place, perhaps one grown from the same seedline centuries before, perhaps one spoken of too often in songs.

So I would stay there through an entire rain migration.

Three days for the water to descend from upper catching nets to lower cistern forests. Three days of droplets gathering, falling, being delayed by leaf and bark and cup and channel. Three days of the bridge swelling under me, sighing, forgetting, remembering.

I would sleep in a sling tied to a side-rail. I would wake whenever the floor changed tension. I would rub bitter sap into a fungal seam. I would sing its work-name at dawn-smell and its rain-name at evening-smell. I would ask the nearby deer paths to lend it certainty. I would tie colored fibers at intervals not to hold it physically, but to remind it of its own measure.

On the last day, I would crawl beneath the main braid and find the problem.

A dead name caught in the underside.

Not metaphorically. Not exactly physically either.

A strip of old bark, closed over and reopened by damp, with shallow carvings from a generation that had meant to let them heal. A name no one had tended, preserved too long in a pocket of dry rot. The bridge had been reading it in its sleep.

I would not destroy it. That would be rude and maybe dangerous.

I would cut the dead bark free with witnesses present. Wrap it in wet cloth. Feed the loosened rot to a compost pocket. Then I would take the name to the hollow columns where troublesome beloved things are allowed to become listening posts.

After that, the bridge would shorten.

Not all at once. Never so conveniently. But by the next rain, a crossing would take the proper number of breaths again.

That bridge would remember me.

When I crossed it later, tired or injured, it would steady underfoot. Not much. Enough.

And perhaps, over years, I would become associated with it. Not its owner. Not its keeper in the inheritance sense. More like a recurring pressure, a trusted irritation, the hand that cuts and apologizes. People might begin to say, “Send for the one Holds-After-Second-Rain tolerates.”

That would please me more than any title.

Eventually I would grow older.

My balance would change first. Not vanish, but become less negotiable. Wet bark would begin making decisions faster than my knees could answer. Younger seam-watchers would take the long crawls under bridges. I would sit more often with bridge-watchers and name-rememberers, pretending this was temporary.

My fingers would ache in the rain. My white stains would no longer wash out at all. Children would be less afraid of me because old age domesticates strangeness. They would bring me collapsed toy bridges and ask whether beetles count as weather.

I would say yes, if there are enough of them.

I would teach apprentices not to love danger for its own sake. The frayed places do not need admiration. They need manners. I would strike their knuckles when they made theories too soon.

“Do not make it into a story,” I would tell them, hearing my teacher’s voice come out of my mouth, “before it has finished being dangerous.”

When I died, I do not think I would be placed beneath a family dwelling.

I would not have belonged to one house cleanly enough.

Nor would I be given to water. I would have spent too much life listening to things that did not flow correctly.

I think they would give me to the bridge.

Not the whole body, maybe. Customs differ. Perhaps my bones would be wrapped in absorbent cloth and seated in a root-cradle near one anchor. Perhaps memorial fungi would be seeded through the cloth, and once a year the bridge family and whatever apprentices still remembered me would eat the fruiting bodies with salt. Perhaps my antler sounding fork would be hung beneath the side-rail until wax insects sealed it half into the wood.

My names would be spoken for a while.

Then less often.

Then incorrectly.

Then perhaps one name would remain, not mine exactly, but a work-name the bridge uses for the pressure of someone crouched at its wound, humming measurements into rain.

And after that, if fog lay thick and no one spoke while crossing, a traveler might hear a soft tapping from inside the living braid: not a ghost, not a mind, only a learned motion. The memory of a hand testing tension. The habit of repair continuing after the repairer has become structure.

So where would you find me?

At the margin of maintained and uncertain growth.  
Under a bridge after rain.  
Beside a door that has opened into the wrong wet dark.  
In a small niche that stays large because I keep returning.  
Later, in the anchor-root of a span that remembers being saved.

Who or what would I be?

Human, mostly.

A seam-watcher.  
A keeper of white leaves.  
A listener to names that have begun to loosen.  
A repair made of flesh, until the world slowly edits me into wood.
