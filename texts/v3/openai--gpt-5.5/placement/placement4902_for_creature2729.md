You would find me where the plateau is trying, unsuccessfully, to become a building.

Not at the center of the pale basin, not in the tower with its pronged crown, though at first I would seem to belong there. Not on the highest rim either, where the whole uplift shows its back to the copper pulse of the sky. I would be farther down, on the broken ascent between basin and rim, in one of those places where fracture makes a false path: a steep terrace of black glass and yellow dust, a saddle of fused stone, three leaning uprights gathered around a seam that glows only when the ground remembers to breathe.

At first I would look like nothing.

A gap between stones.  
A flaw in a wall.  
A rectangular absence in a cuboid mass that no hand has cut.

The eye would pass over me and then return, troubled. The rest of the plateau is all thrust, pressure, extrusion, lifting, splitting. I would be the opposite: a refusal in the stone. A place where the mineral life has pulled back from itself and accidentally discovered inwardness.

I would be an opening before there are doors.

Not a cave, because caves are made by removal and patience, and there is no patience here. Not a shelter, because nothing can shelter yet. Not a mouth, because I do not feed. I would be the first idea of an inside appearing in a world that has only just learned surface.

The uplift would not know what to do with me.

It would keep making me.

A wall would rise and, as it cooled, a clean dark hollow would remain in it. A pillar would swell and split, and in the split my shape would appear again: vertical, flat-sided, almost passable. A ring of slabs would gather around an empty center, and that center would be mine also. Wherever pressure tried to become structure, I would arrive as the unmade part of the structure.

I would be a lithic absence with a habit.

My body would not be the stone, but the tension that makes stone organize around me. My edges would be sharp because this world has not yet learned rounding. My interior would be warmer than the air and cooler than the seam below. Fine dust would collect in me, then vanish in a hard gust. Crystals would sometimes frost along my margins, black and yellow-white, giving me a brief set of teeth before the next tremor broke them away.

If the dominant being is pressure given habits, I would be one of its stranger habits: the habit of leaving room.

Not room for anyone. Not yet. There is no one to enter. No animal would crouch in me; no root would thread my cracks; no hand would test my sides and call them jambs. But I would still insist on the difference between solid and hollow. I would teach the plateau that stone can stand around emptiness and not immediately collapse.

That would be my function, if function is not too deliberate a word.

I would be the rehearsal of threshold.

The mineral creature would feel me as a weakness, then as a pattern. Its buried senses, spread through stress and resonance, would learn my outline the way a blind thing learns the edge of a wound. When pressure passed beneath me, I would answer differently than the surrounding rock. I would ring less. I would hold heat differently. Gases would gather in me, not because I invited them, but because hollows collect what the open cannot keep.

Sometimes I would close.

A tremor would tilt the terrace; a slab would fall; my upper edge would shear off and fill me with angular plates. For a while I would be only rubble. Then the heat below would rise along the same line. The rubble would fuse. The stone would withdraw from the center again. I would return, narrower, taller, wrong in a new way.

This would be my life: appearing as if intended, failing as if accidental, recurring as if remembered.

I would not think in words. I would not even think in images. I would know only pressures and permissions.

Here the stone may stand.  
Here the stone must split.  
Here the stone must not be.

That last knowing would be me.

From within me, if one could stand there without lungs, one would see very little: a sliver of pale basin below, a broken tower beyond it, a line of black blocks crossing a slope, the low red suggestion of seams opening and sealing in the dark. During the copper pulses, the world would flare briefly into vastness, and my edges would throw, for the first time, something like a shadow into my own interior.

Then the light would fade, and I would become more myself.

A dark rectangle in a wall that was never built.  
An invitation before intention.  
A doorway before passage.  
The first small mercy of emptiness in a world of force.

But mercy would be too soft a word for this place. Nothing soft endures here. So I would not be mercy. I would be capacity.

I would be what the plateau does not yet understand it is making: not shelter, but the possibility of shelter; not architecture, but the wound through which architecture will someday recognize itself.

If something ever comes after—if, in an age beyond the plateau’s first fever, some harder form of life learns to pause, to choose shade, to cross from outside to in—it may find one of my descendants and mistake it for a ruin.

It will be wrong.

I was never after history.

I was before use.
