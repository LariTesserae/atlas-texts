At first I think I would be a visitor.

I see myself arriving too loudly, with dry feet and a body full of endings. I would stand on the raised causeway and feel the whole marsh notice me without turning its head. Frogs would hold their gold-eyed rows. A heron would keep its lifted foot. The water beside the path would show me a reflection half a breath late, as if deciding whether I deserved to be copied.

I would try to be careful. I would not touch the suspended droplets. I would not pluck the fruit. I would not complete the vine’s curl.

But even caution is a kind of interference if it comes from outside. Even standing still is too much when your stillness has a beginning and will have an end.

So I do not think I remain a visitor for long.

I think I would be found at a crossing of causeways, where one path runs straight over a chain of square pools and the other bends for no practical reason around a stand of reeds planted in clay combs. There is a place there where the smell changes. One paving tile smells of wet ash. The next smells of crushed mint. The next smells faintly of fruit skin warmed in the hand.

I would live at that boundary.

Not in a house, though I would keep one ready.

Not in a shrine, though I would know which carvings dry last.

Not in the open pools, though I would appear there when no one looked directly.

I would be one of the small maintainers of passage: a keeper of transitions that must not become arrivals.

My duty would be to make sure that crossing remains crossing.

I would count the tiles in both directions, not to correct the difference, but to keep it tender. Forty-seven out, forty-two back. That is the proper discrepancy. If one morning there were forty-eight, I would kneel with wet fingers and persuade the extra tile to become brightness. If there were only forty-one returning, I would press my palm into the path until the missing distance remembered its shape.

My body would not be made cleanly. None of us are, however carefully we are kept.

I would have reed fibers in the long muscles of my arms. My skin would be pale where lime had risen through it and dark at the joints where manufactured mud had settled. My palms would be too broad, because my work would require touching surfaces without pushing them forward. My hair would hang in thin damp ropes, smelling of trough-water and green stems cut but not withered.

My mouth would usually be full of water.

Not because I had something important to say.

Because I would be responsible for not saying it yet.

There are many kinds of silence in the basin. The frogs keep watchful silence. The elders keep plaza silence. The houses keep ready silence. The pools keep reflective silence, which is the most dangerous kind, because it can contain things that have never happened and show them without shame.

My silence would be threshold silence.

I would hold words until they lost their edges. If a visitor asked, “Where am I?” I would not answer. A place becomes too finished when named. Instead I would dip two fingers into a bowl and touch them to the lintel:

not yet.

If they asked, “Who made this?” I would turn the bowl upside down:

cause removed.

If they asked, “How long has it been like this?” I would split a reed lengthwise and lay both halves beside each other, inner faces wet and shining:

this has happened before, but not earlier.

I would not be important. That feels necessary. Important beings belong too much to stories, and stories move. I would be a small function, one of those almost unnoticed inhabitants whose labor can be mistaken for a natural law.

I would tend delays.

When a drop began to lengthen beneath a roof beam, I would stand under it with my hand raised, not touching, feeling its weight accumulate. When it reached the exact fullness before falling, I would breathe through my teeth and hold it there. If it grew too proud and wanted to descend, I would take a little shine from a nearby shell path and set the shine beneath it, so the droplet would be distracted by its own reflection.

When fruit grew too heavy on an espaliered branch, I would visit it at noon. I would cup it from below. Not lift. Never lift. Lifting creates hope. I would simply remind it of the pleasure of almost falling.

When a fish in a canal forgot its pattern and attempted a fourth silver turn, I would darken the water with my shadow until it paused. Then the three turns could begin again, graceful and harmless.

Sometimes I would be sent to the houses.

Those would be the hardest duties, because readiness resembles loneliness if watched too closely.

I would smooth the sleeping mats. I would rinse the bowls already clean. I would sit beside the pot trembling at the edge of boiling and listen to the water inside think about steam. I would keep the thought from becoming vapor. I would warm my palms over the absent fire, not because I needed warmth, but because the hearth needed someone to remember the shape of use.

In those houses I might almost become a person.

That is where the danger would be.

A bowl in the hand. A mat under the knee. A doorway open to noon. The idea of returning after work. The idea of sleeping. The idea of waking to a day that follows another day.

If I felt those things gathering, I would go back outside at once and stand where the ash-scent becomes mint. I would let the contradiction pass through me until I became useful again.

The children would know me as the one who miscounts kindly.

They would stand in the pools, ankle-deep in reflected water, their real feet elsewhere or nowhere, and call to me without sound. They would show me ladders reflected from an absent wall. They would ask whether today they might climb one rung higher.

I would answer by placing my palm flat on the water.

remain.

They would sulk brightly, as children do even when they are made of reflection. One would put a translucent foot on the first rung anyway. The ladder would hold. For a moment everything would sharpen: the rung, the foot, the upward glance, the terrible possibility of arrival.

Then I would lean close and whisper the only word I might risk releasing:

opening.

Not opened.

Opening.

The child would understand, or pretend not to. The foot would remain on the rung. The climb would not proceed, but neither would it be denied. That is mercy in the basin: not granting, not refusing, but holding the desire where it can still shine.

I think the elders would distrust me a little.

They would sit in the too-bright plaza with water gathered in their palms, reading the carved waves and eyes and hands. They would know that those who tend thresholds are always close to error. A boundary must understand both sides. A keeper of passage must carry, somewhere in the damp folds of the body, the idea of passing through.

Perhaps that is why I would be stationed where I am: not at the center, not at the edge, but at an inner crossing. Close enough to the retaining wall to feel the rearranging distance. Close enough to the houses to remember habitation. Close enough to the pools to see the absent feet. Close enough to the planted reeds to hear, once in a while, the dry thought hidden inside green growth.

I would love the marsh.

I would love its artificial mud with the devotion of someone who knows that made things are not lesser for being made. I would love the clean seams, the deliberate moss, the frogs arranged like punctuation along cistern rims. I would love the plank gates, the reed hinges, the jars that pour though empty, the walls that stop before becoming useful.

But my love would have unease in it.

Now and then, I would walk to the low retaining wall and look beyond. Not long. Looking long makes the distance rearrange itself, and those who tend distances dislike trespass. Still, I would look.

The brightness beyond would suggest more basin, more platforms, more planted water. Or it would suggest an outside where mud has stones in it, where reeds rot at the base, where frogs leap without permission, where rain arrives because clouds have gathered and not because a condition is being maintained.

I would not know whether I wanted that.

Wanting is already a current.

So I would return to my crossing.

I would press loose matting back into resin. I would adjust the scent-boundary between ash and mint. I would count the tiles wrong in the proper way. I would collect any fallen reasons in small sealed bowls and stack them in the shade where no shade should be.

And if a visitor came, careful but too alive, I would watch them from the canal first.

Their reflection would see me before they did.

If they tried to drink, I would place a bowl on a threshold instead.

If they tried to fix the marsh, I would stand with the others, broad hands open, waiting for understanding to ripen but not fall.

And if they only stood there, overcome by the noon, by the wet heat, by the beauty of a world forever almost happening, I might come close enough for them to see me directly.

Wet brick skin.

Shell-chip nails.

Gold-black eyes.

Water held in the mouth.

Then I would raise one palm.

Not in warning.

Not in welcome.

In the old gesture that means:

remain, but do not become permanent.

And if I had to name myself, though names are dangerous, I would choose one that is more task than self.

I would be the Keeper of the Uncrossed Crossing.

The one who tends the moment between here and there.

The one who keeps the bubble rising.

The one who does not let it break.
