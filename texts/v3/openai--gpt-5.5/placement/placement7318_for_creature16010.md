I would not be at the center.

Not at first.

The center is too sure of itself, even in its uncertainty: tanks, gantries, sorting chambers, the dark piled like cloth. It has purposes, or the remains of purposes. It receives the old grazers. It notices new bones. It alters channels. I would not belong there except as something eventually delivered.

I would be found nearer the edge of approach.

Along the crushed-shell road where it gives up and enters the flooded underpass, past the three black signal posts whose backs stay warm, there is a maintenance hut raised only a little above the mud. Not high enough to be safe. Just high enough to express an opinion. Its corrugated roof is bowed inward with condensation. Its stilts are wrapped in reed roots and cable. Under it, blind fish gather when the pressure changes.

That is where I would live.

Or where I would be kept.

At first I would seem like one of the maintenance dwellers: stooped, narrow, patient with tools, moving between hut, signal post, culvert, and pump island on a circuit too irregular to be called a route and too repeated to be accident. I would have a coat that was once waterproof and is now only loyal. I would wear a mask, but not always over my face; sometimes pushed up onto my head, sometimes hanging from my chest, sometimes set carefully beside me like a second animal waiting to be fed.

I would have hands. That would matter. Hands would be the proof offered to anyone watching from reeds or black water that I was meant to alter things, though only slightly.

Not repair. Repair is too bright a word.

I would persuade.

I would tap gauges until the needle remembered the direction of trembling. I would clean algae from the rungs of ladders that no one should climb. I would oil hinges on doors that open into smaller versions of the same room. I would stencil numbers on tanks already numbered, adding the necessary corrections:

4B-after  
4B-under  
4B-when-warm  
4B-do-not-enter-if-reflected

I would carry a folded map in a skin pouch under my shirt, but I would not trust it enough to unfold it indoors. Maps sulk in enclosed spaces. They grow rooms. They begin labeling the sleeper’s organs. So I would take it outside and open it flat on the shell road, weighing its corners with teeth, insulators, a beetle-polished stone, and one small rusted valve wheel.

The map would show the basin in more detail each season.

Too much detail.

I would spend part of my work scratching things out.

Not because they were wrong, but because they were becoming true too early.

Future seep.  
Animal parliament.  
Door under water when water is not here.  
Place where rail remembers fire.

I would blur those with my thumb, spit, mud, the black condensation gathered from the underside of the hut. I would not erase them. Nothing here can be erased. I would only delay them until the ground had risen enough to bear the fact of them.

My real work would be delay.

I think that is what I would be: a delay-keeper.

A small function of the lowland, given a body almost human because hands are useful and loneliness makes a fine container for instructions.

I would wake when the bell strikes.

Not every bell. Only the one mounted near the third signal post, the one whose sound is more felt in the teeth than heard. When it strikes, the birds lower their heads. The grazers stop listening to the mud and listen to the air. Eels tighten under the transformer slab. I would sit up in the damp blanket, count my fingers, and wait for the answering knock from the pipe beneath the floor.

If the pipe knocked once, I would stay inside.

If it knocked twice, I would take the reed-wrapped wrench.

If it knocked three times, I would not light a lamp. Lamps are rude here. I would go out by touch.

My feet would know the ground better than my eyes. Heel on shell. Toe on clay. Whole foot on concrete. Never trust the mineral skin over a seep unless the beetles have crossed first. Never step where water is perfectly still. Never step in your own reflected shoulder.

I would drink from upward rain when the basins were showing too much.

There is a place beneath the underpass where droplets gather on the concrete ceiling and hang there, black and heavy, refusing the ordinary direction. The soft-faced crabs drink there too. They would not fear me, though they would resent my height. I would lift my face and open my mouth under the wrong rain. The water would taste of lime, rust, old warnings, and the underside of roads.

Sometimes, while drinking, I would see my reflection above me.

Not in water. In the wet concrete.

At first it would look like a worker.

Then like a worker remembered by mud.

Then like a tool that had learned to stand.

My skin would have changed by then. The damp would have drawn fine channels down my arms and throat, little drainage lines where condensation could run and be gathered into the corners of my mouth. My hair, if I had any, would grow in flattened reeds along the skull. My back would ache before pressure changes. My fingernails would darken with mineral crescents. When I slept too near warm cable, dreams of current would twitch in my wrists.

I would still think of myself as someone.

That would be the last human stubbornness.

The animals would know better.

The paper-pale birds would allow me to pass beneath their conveyor nests but would not include me in their lowering when the bell sounded. Not at first. The grazers would smell my hands and decide I was neither meat nor threat nor weather, but close to a valve, close to a path, close to something that opens only sometimes. The tall derrick-being would pause when it saw me. It would lower its head—not in greeting, not in hunger—and inspect the mud around my boots, as if reading what my passing had done to the basin.

Once, I would find one of its shed skins hanging from a transmission tower.

The skin would be translucent, jointed too high, already entered by fungus. It would glow only after I had stopped trying to see it. I would cut a strip no wider than two fingers and bring it back to the hut. For three days I would not know why. On the fourth, the sump pump would cough and fail to complete its gesture. I would feed the strip into the belt housing with copper wire and eel leather. The pump would turn once, slowly, gratefully, and draw a pool down by the depth of a fingernail.

That would expose eggs.

Pale eggs in a ring around the concrete base.

Not fish eggs. Not insect eggs. Not anything I had a name for.

I would mark them on the map as:

found after reluctance

By the next season, something would hatch in the hut walls.

Tiny things, almost transparent, with black points for mouths. They would eat mildew from the calendar pages. Where they passed over blank dates, circular stains would appear. Some dates would darken more than others. I would begin to arrange my maintenance by those stains.

This is how the lowland would train me away from intention.

At first I would believe I was keeping the systems alive.

Then I would believe the systems were keeping me alive.

Then I would understand that neither was true in any clean direction. The pump, the map, the underpass, the eels, the bell, the condensation, the hut larvae, my hands: all of us would be parts of one slow apparatus for deciding what the basin could become without tearing.

When new land rose, I would go to it.

Not immediately. The beetles first. Then the worms. Then the copper film. Then me.

I would crouch at the edge of a black shelf still sharp from birth and press my palm lightly to it. Warm. Damp. Unconvinced. I would taste the mud with the metal strip I carried for tasting mud. Salt, ash, brick, shell, green paste, old current. I would scratch a small mark into a tag and tie it to a reed stem:

not ready for hooves

Later I would return and find the tag altered. The ink slid sideways. The words might read:

ready for teeth

or:

already crossed

or:

do not name while soft

I would obey when I could. When I could not, the ground would correct me.

There would be mistakes.

Once I would count my steps aloud on a grated walkway.

Only once.

I would be tired, or lonely, or too full of the low tone that passes under hearing. I would say “one” into the dark, and from beneath the walkway, something would say nothing. I would say “two,” and below me, very softly, one. I would stop at once. My mouth would close. My whole body would understand the error.

But the counter would continue for a while beneath me, always one behind, counting steps I had not taken.

After that I would mouth numbers only. The way the old dwellers do. The way tools count when they do not wish to be found.

In time, I would become less visible as a person and more visible as a habit.

A door reclosed.

A warm signal back wiped clean.

A ladder cleared down to the fifth rung and no farther.

A black lens polished though no light enters it.

A row of fresh scratches beside a valve.

Bundles left at vents: cleaned bones, broken insulators, tags with names of places not yet safe to occur.

The hut would contain my bed, but the bed would no longer prove I slept there. Damp blankets can remember a shape long after the body has been reassigned. My boots might stand by the door for weeks while I moved barefoot through crawlspaces, or through pipe-shadow, or through those parts of the map that had become too accurate to remain paper.

Eventually, someone newly arrived—if anyone ever newly arrives—would glimpse me near the flooded underpass.

They would see a narrow figure crouched beneath the concrete, drinking upward from the ceiling among the crabs. They would think: human.

Then I would turn my head too slowly.

They would see condensation running along the little channels in my skin. They would see that my mask and face had exchanged duties. They would see my hands, still hands, but darkened and precise, each finger wrapped with measuring scars. They would see tags hanging from my coat, not labels but weather. They would see a voltmeter at my belt that tests blood warmth, a wrench with reeds rooted in its handle, a map case moving slightly as if breathing.

If they asked what I was, I would not answer loudly.

Loudness belongs to finished worlds.

I might touch the wall where wetness climbs into the old warning letters. I might point to the black signal lenses. I might open my palm and show them a beetle, a screw, a seed, a tooth, all carried in the same mud.

If words were necessary, I would say:

“I keep things from arriving too soon.”

And if that was not enough:

“I am one of the basin’s hands.”

Not its master. Not its witness. Not even its servant, exactly.

A hand does not command the body. A hand learns the heat of what it touches. A hand closes around a tool when the deeper animal requires it. A hand can be injured, dirtied, replaced, remembered by the work it left half done.

That is where I would be: beneath the warm-backed signal posts, beside the road that disappears into black water, in the raised hut whose calendar stains decide my routes.

That is what I would be: a maintenance dweller becoming less a person than a useful delay, a damp custodian of unfinished ground, human-shaped because the old machines still respond to hands, and marsh-shaped because everything here must eventually learn to listen downward.
