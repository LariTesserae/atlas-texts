I think I would be found where habitation begins to thin.

Not in the market’s center, not in the salt kitchens, not among the scaffold chiefs whose hands are always white with useful dust. I would be somewhere near the margin between a working district and one of the great pale flats: a place where houses still keep their lintels and ovens, but beyond them the old avenues widen into emptiness, with sleeping animals in the road and capped wells that tremble sometimes.

There would be a cistern nearby, but not a dependable one. One of those basins that fills only when an unseen reservoir remembers it. Around it, a handful of households would have gathered: a salt room, a low dome for drying eel when eels come, a vent-slab where pale living film is cultivated under covers, a shed for sled runners, and a leaning tower whose upper rooms are entered from what used to be a second floor but is now almost level with the street.

That is where I would live: in the leaning tower, or beside it, or in the room it has become after being dismantled three times.

I would not be one of the strongest builders. I would admire them too much to be one. I would not be a salt-master either, though I would know enough to tell sweet crust from bitter crust and enough not to speak while someone else is tasting a wall.

At first I think I would be a map-bearer.

But not an elder map-bearer, not yet. More like an apprentice grown old in the middle, someone entrusted with lesser revisions: the knots that change often, the routes made unreliable by condensation, the beast paths across districts that have no stable names. I would carry a bundle of cord at my hip, wrapped in worn hide, dense as a dried organ. It would not be the city’s whole memory. No one carries that. It would be a local map: cisterns, ledges, sleeping courts, salt obligations, dangerous seams, animal refusals, abandoned doorways that still matter.

My hands would know it better than my eyes.

I would sit with the cord across my knees and read by pressure: this knot tight because the basin has not failed in three generations; this loose loop because the road exists only in dry intervals; this triple twist because a pack-beast refused the crossing before anyone saw the crack; this polished bead because someone beloved was taken from there and later recovered as bone.

People would come to me quietly.

A child sent from the salt house: the west channel woke out of season.

A beast-handler: three blind haulers stopped before the shell bridge and would not put their noses down.

A builder with dust in the lines of her face: the old lintel over the oven room has begun to sweat from the inside.

I would not decide things. That would be too much authority, and authority that lasts too long is structurally unsound. I would compare. I would remember. I would say, with my fingers more than my mouth: this has happened near the Bitter Year; this happened before the lower street opened; this route is not dead, only folded; this doorway should be thanked and left.

I would have one missing finger, maybe two joints of the left hand.

Not dramatically. Not as a badge I sought. It would have happened during a survey of a narrowing passage, when I was younger and wanted to prove I could read change before it finished speaking. A seam would have grown faster than expected, or a stone would have shifted after being declared quiet. The injury would have made me slower, which in that city would mean wiser only if I let it.

Children would touch the scar before their first crossing of a newly shored bridge, and I would pretend not to be moved by it.

I would keep a damp-watcher by my door: one of the hand-sized soft stones that unfolds when moisture comes. It would sit in a shallow shell dish, indistinguishable most days from a rounded lump of mineral. I would call it nothing aloud. Names used too often grow brittle. But I would notice its moods. If it opened a little, I would hang cloths. If it opened wide, I would wake the neighbor with the suspended infant bowl. If it turned itself almost inside out, I would untie the dry-storage sacks and get them high before the bad water arrived.

I would be fond of the wall-creatures.

Their pale jointed feet, their careful priestly motion, their refusal to hurry. I would trust a wall with many of them more than a wall newly braced by proud hands. If they left my room, I would not sleep there. If they gathered around a crack, I would mark it with chalk and set a bowl of water nearby.

Maybe that is closer to it: I would be a small practitioner of attention.

Not a prophet. Not a ruler. Not even especially brave. Someone whose work is to notice the interval before a thing becomes obvious.

I would know the city as a sequence of hesitations.

Here, the floor hesitates before dropping.

Here, the ceiling hesitates before raining.

Here, people hesitate because an animal has stopped.

Here, the old road hesitated so long that houses grew around its uncertainty.

My dwelling would be provisional but cared for. A hammock of braided hide fixed to two stones that had once been part of a bridge. A low shelf for shell bowls. A smoked visor hanging near the entrance for flare-season. A listening rod made from the polished bone of someone whose name I might not know, but whose usefulness I would honor. Several lintel chips, each tied with cord: not charms exactly, more like remembered agreements.

I would eat what everyone eats: salt meat, vent-film cakes, eel paste when the basin wakes, broth cloudy with powdered bone. I would oversalt by accident when distracted and thereby once find myself nearly engaged to someone from a district where that means invitation. This would become a quiet joke. People would touch two fingers to their lips when passing me, the sign for excessive salt, and I would answer with the sign for structural misunderstanding.

I would love the flare-pauses.

When the seams overhead brighten and all work stops. When even the beasts lift their sealed faces. When people stand with stones in their hands, neither setting them down nor lifting them higher. In those moments I would feel the whole city become one listening body: bowls, ribs, ropes, statues, cisterns, sleeping giants, children held against shoulders, wall-creatures frozen in place.

I would not pray, exactly.

I would acknowledge.

We are here.  
We see the ceiling seeing us.  
We have left water in the basin.  
We have not beaten the beast for knowing sooner than we did.  
We have abandoned the beautiful house.  
We have saved the lintel.  
We are revising.

If I grew old, I would become less a map-bearer than a map-place.

People would know to find me near the unreliable cistern at the edge of the flats. I would sit with my cord bundle, my damaged hand, my soft-stone damp-watcher, my bowls of trembling water. Younger surveyors would bring me changes, and I would help them decide where in the cord the city had moved.

Not where to put the new knot only. Where to loosen the old one.

That would matter most to me: not preservation, but looseness. To remember without trapping. To keep the dead district in the bundle, but moved inward, where the fingers can still find it when needed and daily life will not snag on it.

And when I died, I would not want to be sealed away whole.

Let my bones be separated by use.

One finger-bone polished smooth and tied into a child’s first map-cord. A rib made into a listening sliver for testing hollow walls. A small bone drilled and hung where condensation strikes, adding one nearly inaudible note to the drip-music of a doorway. If my skull is useful, make it a cup for measuring salt, but not for formal occasions; I would prefer kitchens to shrines.

So: I would be found at the city’s loosening edge, beside a cistern that has not decided whether it is dead.

I would be a map-bearer, crack-listener, minor surveyor, keeper of revised routes.

Or, more truly, I would be one more organ in the city’s body of attention: not commanding change, not resisting it, but feeling for where it has begun.
