I think I would not be in the central trench.

At first that seems like the place with the most gravity: the cold lower dark, the bowed grates, the old fish holding themselves in the purposeful current. But that life is too cleanly shaped by appetite. Too much waiting for the exact error of another body.

I would also not be a rat, though the rats tempt me. Their world has edges, routes, decisions. They know the thin remaining air, the pipe-bridges, the emergency upward scramble when the pumps begin. They are clever enough to make a nation out of leftover maintenance space. But they are too warm for this room. Too much heartbeat, too much intent.

I would be lower than the rats and higher than the trench.

You would find me on the underside of a rusted ladder in a side alcove, just below the old high-water stain, where one amber industrial lamp still makes a weak garden on the tiles. Not directly in the light, but near enough that algae gathers in a dull green nap over the ceramic and the current brings food around the corner when the pumps turn over.

I would be one of the fixed ones.

A mussel, probably. Or a brackish city oyster if the water has enough salt in its memory. Not alone, but not exactly social either: one shell among a clustered shelf of shells fastened to iron that was never meant to become coast.

My place would be a rung no hand has touched in generations. The ladder would descend from the narrow air gap and vanish below me into green-black water. Above, rats would sometimes cross it, their claws ticking through the metal. Below, small fish would pass through the spaces between the rungs, tilting silver when the lamp caught them. Around me would be other mouths: closed, open, closed again, each one reading the water without opinion.

I would live by opening carefully.

When the hall quieted, I would part my shell lips and let the room move through me: rust, lime, algae, powdered concrete, invisible rot, midge dust, bat droppings, the city reduced to edible suspension. I would not chase anything. I would not need to know where the food began. The whole drowned chamber would arrive grain by grain.

When pressure changed, I would know before seeing anything. Not as sound, exactly, but as touch: a tremor through the ladder, a tightening in the water, the first hard breath of machinery behind the walls. Then I would close. Around me, the cluster would close too, not in fear so much as agreement. The fish would turn. The worms would vanish. The rats would climb. Silt would lift and erase the room.

Inside my shell, the surge would become weather.

I would be almost all patience, but not dead patience. A living hinge. A small dark soft body making decisions too slow to be dramatic. Open. Close. Hold. Feed. Seal. Endure.

My shell would keep the record the old gauges no longer can. A thin bad season. A thick year of rain. A chemical bitterness that almost ended me. A summer when the water warmed and the insects came in clouds. A long period of steady pump cycles. A sudden freshening after storms above. I would carry the city’s forgotten maintenance as rings.

I would know the hall by vibration more than sight.

The northern pipe’s knock. The grinding start of the automatic pump. The chain that taps three times when the current reverses. The soft rasp of snails crossing the lamp housing. The sharp little panic of amphipods in the silt. The heavy, nearly silent displacement of an old trench fish rising and returning. The claws of rats. The brief stitching flight of bats over the trapped air.

I would have no map, but I would belong exactly.

My world would be the curve of the ladder rung, the mouths of my neighbors, the brown-green film within reach, the water’s changing weight. The chamber would not be abandoned to me. It would be complete: wall, current, darkness, food, danger, pressure, return.

If someone came down from above with a light, I would not understand the intrusion as discovery. The beam would strike the tiles, wake the fish into silver fragments, show the warning sign, the clock, the rail descending into murk. It might find me only as texture: another rough gray growth on old iron.

That would be right.

I would be part of the room’s afterlife, not its story. A small valve of flesh attached to a ladder in a drowned service hall, filtering the city’s dark water and making, from corrosion and drift, a body that can stay.
