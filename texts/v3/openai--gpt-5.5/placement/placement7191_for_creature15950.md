Not at the center, at first.

The center is too loud, too hungry, too bright with reflected milk-fire. I would not begin where the channels meet and fail to become a river, where every bank is crowded with shells and steam and mouths. I would come to myself at the edge of that busyness, where a ledge runs along a pillar’s base and the floor below has cracked three times but not yet fallen.

A place with one wall warm enough to sleep against and one wall cool enough to store meat.

A place that is not safe, exactly, but has been safe before.

There would be shell strings in the entrance. Not ornamental. Nothing here is only ornamental. They would hang in uneven curtains: scraped white plates, orange iron-stained chips, little black carapace coins drilled with patient hands. When the air changed, they would speak before the body knew why. A dry clatter for herd-passing. A soft tapping for roof-rain beginning. A long shiver for pressure moving under the floor.

I think I would be near the Long Drip, but not inside it. Close enough to hear its constant delicate rain. Close enough to collect the translucent skins shed by the curtain-climbers. Far enough that a roof-shed would not bury the hut in poisonous young.

Behind me: the red slope with flint and smoke.

Below me: the central hollow, bright and dangerous.

Above me: a pale ceiling alive with patient lamps.

At first I imagine I am one of the small armored scrapers, because there is something honest in that life: mouth to stone, shell to danger, work without theory. To be a clicking knot among thousands, polishing the world down to food. To fold into hardness when shadow passes. To wake after tremors already moving toward the new crack.

But no. I would not be that cleanly made.

Then I think perhaps I am a stilt-beast waiting in fog, jointed and still, reading the basin through my feet. That has its own terrible grace: to be an architecture of hunger, a scaffold that collapses only when the air says yes.

But I am not patient enough to be pure ambush.

I would have hands.

That is the trouble and the tenderness of it.

I would be one of the hand-users, though not one who still believes the hand is proof of mastery. I would be narrow-shouldered, pale where the clay has cracked off, dark in the creases from mineral paste and smoke. My eyes would be large but secondary. My ears would be the truer organs. My feet would know before my mind did when a floor had become untrustworthy.

I would be a keeper of temporary paths.

Not a chief. Not a priest, unless every good listener becomes one eventually. I would be the one sent ahead when a crossing had not been used since the last After-Breaking. The one who crouches and tastes condensation from a wall. The one who presses a palm to soft stone and waits for the answer traveling through it. The one who says, “Not there,” and cannot always explain quickly enough.

My tools would hang close against my ribs: quartz scraper, bone awl, shell knife, a coil of gut-line, a little bowl patched with lime. Around my neck I would wear map pegs made from the finger-bones of someone who loved me, because their use would be a form of being accompanied.

I would carry no torch. Flame would be too rude here, too greedy with the air. Instead I would trust the roof colonies, the bellies of fish, the dim seams in the stone, the way wet things remember light.

My name would not be a fixed thing. Names in that basin would change with usefulness. As a child I might have been called Salt-Foot for stepping through a crust and vanishing to the thigh in brine. Later, after learning the sound of vents before they opened, I might be called Hears-Under. In old age, if I reached it, maybe Holding-Place, because others would pause near me when the country grew uncertain.

But privately, I think I would call myself After.
