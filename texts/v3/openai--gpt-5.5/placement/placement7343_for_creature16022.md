I would not be found at the top, where the slit of sky makes everyone look briefly holy and underfed.

Nor at the bottom, where the Foundry Houses shut their pressure doors and the lamps turn red and even the confident learn to mind their own breathing.

I would be in the middle levels, where the city is most itself: neither sky nor furnace, but tension. Halfway between cold draft and hot vent. Halfway between salt desert and sealed fire. There, on a narrow service gallery behind a row of amber lamps, there is a recess in the wall that no builder ever meant to become a room. A duct bends through it. A signal cable passes overhead. Three old pipes, no longer marked correctly, run behind the back panel and knock softly when the pressure changes below.

That is where I would live.

Not in a proper dwelling. A cleft in the cleft. A cupboard with a curtain of stiffened cloth, a hammock hooked to two iron eyes, a shelf for sealed cups, a box of listening tools, a little shrine made of instruments that have failed beautifully.

A pressure gauge with its needle buried past the last number.

A cracked voltmeter whose glass has fused into a bubble.

A brass bell from a courier animal that died crossing an updraft.

A child’s slag marble, smoky green, worn flat on one side from being worried between fingers.

I think I would be a listener.

Not one of the famous old duct-listeners with palms like burned leather and a word from whom can stop a whole level. Not yet. Perhaps never. I would be one of the lesser necessary ones: a watcher of symptoms, a keeper of murmurs, someone called when a machine is not broken enough for the Chainwrights and not dangerous enough for the Foundry Houses but has begun to sound wrong in a way that makes people sleep poorly.

I would be called a needlekeeper, maybe. Or a fault-clerk. Or, less kindly, one of the crack’s ear-mites.

My work would be to tend the places where the city speaks before it knows it is speaking.

I would go out before the shift bells, when the lamps are still dimmed and the burden beasts are being rubbed awake in their heated stalls. I would wear layered mouth-cloths scraped clean with salt, goggles clouded at the edges, fingerless gloves patched with ceramic scales where heat has bitten through. Around my neck would hang a ring of small tools: tuning pins, chalk, a chain gauge, a listening cup, a sliver-hammer, wax tablets in a sealed case.

I would touch things before trusting them.

A railing.

A pipe.

The lip of a bridge.

The flank of a lift cage.

Not fearfully. Intimately. As one touches the shoulder of someone asleep before deciding whether to wake them.

The cleftborn would know me as someone who pauses in inconvenient places. I would stop halfway up a stair while others flowed around me, one hand pressed flat to the wall. I would crouch beside a sealed conduit and tilt my head. I would put my teeth gently against the handle of a valve and listen through the bone. Children would imitate me, solemnly laying their ears to cold metal and announcing, “Hungry,” or “Too tight,” or “Full of ghosts,” then running off shrieking until a Water Warden looked their way.

I would not be important in the way guild officers are important. I would not command crews. I would not own a route, a beast, a furnace share, a hoist license, a water key. But people would make room for me when I came with my chalk.

Because sometimes I would be right.

I would mark a wall with three pale strokes and, by evening, the Cutters would find salt behind it, ready to shear.

I would hear a high sweetening in a cable and send for the Chainwrights before the lift dropped.

I would put my hand to a warm duct and say, “Close her. She is trying to sing,” and nobody would laugh, because in the crack some songs are only the beginning of rupture.

I would be thin in the local way: not fragile, but spare. My hands would be scarred at the knuckles and sensitive in the pads. My hearing would be poor for ordinary speech and excellent for strain. People would have to lean close to speak to me, and perhaps I would prefer that. I would distrust open declarations. I would believe more in a low voice beside a hot pipe than in any proclamation shouted from a balcony.

I do not think I would have been born there.

I think I would have arrived small, wrapped in someone else’s coat, after a caravan failed in the white flats. Young enough to forget rain except as a forbidden brightness in dreams. Old enough to remember a horizon, and therefore never quite forgiven by the cleftborn children who learned height before weather.

For years I would have tried to become narrow enough.

I would have learned not to flinch when powder avalanched past the grates. Learned not to look down too long. Learned not to say “wet.” Learned to clean my skin with grit and oil and patience. Learned to keep every object clipped, tied, bracketed, named. Learned that a cup without a lid is not a cup but a threat.

Still, some part of me would remain suspiciously outward-facing. I would collect things from the exterior: a flake of salt crust shaped like a wing, a shard of sky-glass, a scrap of sun-bleached harness from some beast that never made the gate. I would keep them wrapped in black felt beneath my shelf and take them out only when the city’s walls seemed too close even for me.

The animals would know me unevenly.

The pale pipe-creatures would tolerate me because I carry no food worth stealing and because I warn them, under my breath, before I strike a pipe. Sometimes one would ride inside the fold of my coat for warmth, triangular head poking out near my elbow, milky eyes bright with heat-sense. I would pretend not to notice.

The black-winged scavengers would hate me. Or perhaps they would respect me in their bad little way. I would be forever tapping them away from signal lamps, forever stealing back foil and wire from their transformer nests. One old bird with a torn tail would follow me level to level, waiting for me to drop something polished. I would call it Regulator. It would know the name and refuse to answer to it.

The burden beasts would make me gentle. I would stand aside for them on stairs, always, even when late. I would press my palm to their plated shoulders as they passed, feeling the slow furnace of their bodies. If one ground its teeth in that failing-mill rhythm, I would hear it from across a platform and come with its keeper, silent as bad news.

But the lungs—the filter creatures near the exhaust vents—would trouble me most.

I would visit the Lungkeepers when I could. Not for work, at first. For quiet. Their chambers would be warm and dim, brass mesh cages breathing slowly in rows, the pale folded bodies pulsing as factory breath passed through them. The Lungkeepers would sing below the range of ordinary hearing, and I would feel the song more in my ribs than my ears.

There, I might begin to understand that listening is not only diagnosis.

It is also care.

A machine listened to only when it fails becomes resentful. A city listened to only for danger becomes a trap. Even the crack, dry and sharp and industrious, must be heard when it is merely alive.

So I would start keeping another ledger.

Not the official fault-book, with dates, levels, guild marks, and corrective orders.

A private one.

In it I would record the ordinary moods of the mountain.

Level Nine: warm draft smelled of copper and old shells. No hazard.

North chain bridge: flexing evenly. Sound like low knuckle cracking. Healthy.

Lung chamber three: membranes responding well after soot increase. Keeper Sefa’s song steadier today.

Pipe-creatures absent from blue conduit before second bell. Returned after fan reversal. Not omen. Only preference.

Crack wind answered downward at dusk. No one whistled. It answered anyway.

Over time, the ledger would become less technical. More devotional, though I would never call it that.

I would begin to suspect that the failed instruments on my shelf had not failed because they were weak, but because they had met the mountain too directly. They had tried to translate a living pressure into numbers, and the needle had gone where numbers stopped.

Perhaps that is what I would become too.

An instrument slowly failing in the correct direction.

I would grow older in the recess behind the amber lamps. My hearing for speech would worsen. My palms would broaden. Young workers would start bringing me impossible sounds cupped in their descriptions.

“It clicks after the fan turns, but only when the red lamps are on.”

“It smells like hot salt, but the pipe is cold.”

“The bridge is quiet.”

That last one would bring me fastest.

Quiet is never empty in the crack. Quiet has weight. Quiet has intent.

And when I could no longer climb as easily, I would sit with my back against the warm duct, like the old ones before me, and let the city come through bone and scar and tooth. I would feel the lifts rising like pulses. The burden beasts kneeling in their stalls. The beetles making bright clean threads along the rails. The scavengers nesting in stolen warmth. The Foundry Houses cracking ore below. Children dropping pebbles into chutes, measuring the dark by echo.

I would feel the mountain not as stone around a city, but as a body learning to contain one.

And perhaps, late in life, I would be asked what I believe.

I would not say I believe the spire is a god. That would be too large and too soft a word.

I would say:

It notices load.

It remembers heat.

It dislikes waste.

It forgives repairs that are done well.

It speaks first in tremor, then in failure.

It has allowed us to remain useful inside it.

And if I were dying there, as cleanly and dryly as possible, I would want no exposed water, no lament shouted upward, no grand procession. I would want my tools kept working. I would want my listening cup passed to someone patient. I would want my bones reduced to pale salts and sealed where they could do no harm.

But perhaps one small part of me—one tooth, one finger bone, one polished chip—would be set into the wall of that little recess behind the amber lamps, under the duct bend where the pressure changes before a storm.

Not as a relic.

As a contact point.

Some future listener, young and irritated and not yet trusted, would press their hand there in passing.

And through salt, metal, old heat, and the narrow patience of the crack, I would still be listening.
