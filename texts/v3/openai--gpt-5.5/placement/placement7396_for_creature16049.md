I think I would first be found where the settlement thins.

Not in the center of the platforms, where children sleep in hammocks and old people scrape resin into cakes, and not out in the nursery fields, where everything is too newly born to be trusted. I would be in the ragged middle distance: on a small suspended floor lashed to three uncertain columns, above a migration trough that opens and heals every few days.

A poor place to live, by most standards. Too much traffic below. Too much scent from the white root-mouths on the western wall. Too close to a quiet pocket.

But I think I would be drawn to the places where the valley almost stops speaking.

My platform would have no proper hut. Just a windbreak of lacquered leaves, a roof of stitched glider membrane, storage baskets pulled high into the tendrils, and a ladder that could be cut loose in one motion. Everything would be hung, tied, folded, ready to vanish. A bowl of ash. A bundle of chalk stones. Three staffs of different woods, each better for hearing a different depth. A mask with too many filters, because I would be overcautious about spores and careless about everything else.

I would not be a leader. Not a hunter. Not one of the brave ones who cross nursery fields during flares to cut fresh rind before it hardens. I would be one of the people sent ahead when a route has become doubtful.

A listener. A marker. A negotiator of passages.

The kind of person who walks slowly because the ground is always speaking first.

I would carry a long staff polished by my jaw and hands. Its lower end would be capped with a beetle-bored stem that resonates against the floor. When I set it down, I would feel the under-valley through my teeth: the far surge of grazers, the scratch of termites in a wall, the deep wooden knocking that means a pressure front is gathering itself somewhere beyond sight. I would have learned to stand still before knowing why.

My work would be mostly failure, but useful failure.

I would carve maps that everyone knows are temporary lies. I would mark safe columns with cuts and return to find the cuts healed into knots, or widened into mouths, or repeated by the bark in a pattern that was almost but not quite my own. I would keep records on thin leaves lacquered hard: not roads, but tendencies.

This passage opens after clove-wind.

This ridge dislikes shadow.

This wall answered low drumming once, but not twice.

Do not sleep where the floor is comfortable.

The work would make me superstitious in a practical way. I would never step over a fresh tendril without touching my mask. I would never call a quiet chamber empty. I would never praise a route aloud while still inside it. I would know these gestures do not command the valley. They command me. They keep my fear in a shape small enough to carry.

People would come to me before leaving a settlement.

Not because I knew the way. No one knows the way. But because I might know what the way had recently wanted.

I would be paid in useful scraps: wax from the pale bees, strips of suede hide, a handful of salt pellets, a thorn needle, a piece of obsidian with one good edge left. Sometimes a child would bring me a bark lizard shed, perfectly whole and dry as a prayer. Sometimes an elder would ask me to hang a bone chime in a certain passage, because their dead had chosen that hunger and the family wanted to hear whether the roots had accepted them.

I think I would be known for entering the quiet pockets.

Not often. Not confidently. But more than was sensible.

There would be one below my platform, reached through a slit behind a folded wall of black-purple bark. The entrance would not always be there. Some days it would be sealed with a fine seam, as if the valley had closed its eye. Other days it would gape wide enough for a person to crawl through sideways, mask scraping, shoulders collecting dust.

Inside: the unnatural stillness. Smooth green-black cork under the palms. Bladder organs along the walls, slowly inflating. No insects. No ticking mouths. No glider cries. The silence would feel less like peace than attention.

I would leave things there.

Not beautiful things at first. Practical things. A broken needle. A twist of cord. A chalk chip marked with the sign for wait. Later, when I understood less, I might leave better offerings: a carved bead from my mask, a strip of my cloak, one of my own baby teeth saved by my mother in a resin capsule. I would tell myself I was measuring response. I would tell others I was testing air movement, pressure, scent.

But some part of me would know I was teaching the place my shape.

That would be the danger of me.

The valley mistakes persistence for request.

If I visited too often, the chamber would begin to answer. At first in small mercies: the entrance opening at shoulder height after an injury; the floor growing slightly ridged where my knees usually slipped; a pocket in the wall drying my chalk stones better than any basket. Then in more intimate errors. A hollow curved exactly to my sleeping spine. A warm fold where my hand rested. Root hairs combing the edges of my cloak as if learning its weave.

Someone older would warn me.

“You are making yourself legible.”

And I would stop for a while.

I would return to human noise: children arguing in gesture, firekeepers muttering over blue flame, bees rasping wax from portable frames, old masks hanging from rafters like sleeping faces. I would sit with others and eat cork roasted under ash, salt ground with bitter seed, mite paste sharp enough to make the eyes water. I would laugh too loudly. I would make ordinary repairs. I would pretend I preferred the settlement.

But I think the valley’s almost-silence would get into me.

Not because I wanted to be swallowed. Not because I worshipped it. More because I would be one of those people born with too much attention and nowhere safe to put it. In a world where everything touches back, attention itself becomes a limb. Mine would keep reaching.

So, eventually, I would become a specialist no one quite trusts.

The silence-tender.

The one sent when a quiet pocket appears in a bad place. The one who enters to decide whether it is an ear, a trap, a scar, or a door. I would carry no flame inside. Only chalk, staff, a little ash in a sealed horn cup, and a flute made from a beetle-drilled stem. I would not play songs. Songs are too certain. I would play intervals, pauses, questions made of breath.

Sometimes nothing would happen.

Sometimes the floor would crack and I would scramble out with my hands bleeding.

Sometimes a chamber would answer by opening another exit, and a whole caravan would survive because of it.

That would be enough to make people keep using me.

Not enough to make them comfortable.

I would be lean even by the standards of that place, with dust permanently in the creases around my eyes. My lips would be split. My palms would be scarred from catching thorn edges too late. My mask would be recognizable from far away: narrow-snouted, patched with translucent insect wing, hung with tiny chalk disks that clicked when I moved. In loud districts I would speak almost entirely with my hands. In quiet ones I might whisper, though whispering there would feel like touching a sleeping animal.

I would have one forbidden luxury: I would collect old marks.

Healed tally cuts. Knots that had once been warnings. Bark grown over abandoned doorways. Chime bones taken down after the family line ended. I would study how human intention changed when the valley absorbed it. I would be fascinated by mistranslation.

A spiral warning becoming a seed pattern.

A grief mark becoming a ventilation pore.

A doorframe becoming a mouth-shaped scar that never opened.

This, more than survival, would be my obsession: not what the valley means, but what it does with meaning.

I would slowly come to believe that people are one of the under-valley’s senses.

Not favored. Not central. Not chosen. But useful. We move faster than roots. We remember discontinuously. We fear in patterns. We make marks, repeat gestures, abandon structures, return to old dangers, drum at walls, burn resin, mourn our dead in consistent ways. The valley cannot think like us, but it can grow around our habits. We irritate it into information.

And perhaps it uses us to learn tempo.

That would be my private theology, though I would deny having one.

If asked plainly what I was, I might say:

“I keep passages.”

Or:

“I listen where sound has gone thin.”

But neither would be quite true.

What I would be is a hinge.

Human on one side: mask, staff, cracked hands, hunger, fear, jokes made in gesture, debts owed to firekeepers and bee-carriers and the families of the dead.

Valley on the other: pressure, response, healed scars, rooms that form around repeated need, walls that mistake offerings for instructions.

I would not belong entirely to either. That would be why I could move between them for a while.

For a while.

Because no one remains merely themselves there forever.

My end would not be dramatic. No chase by a coil-beast. No heroic rescue in the nursery fields. More likely a small misreading. A quiet pocket staying quiet too long. A chamber I had entered before, but which had reconsidered its purpose. A warm clove-wind that I mistook for opening when it meant closure. The exit narrowing behind me with the slow courtesy of a hand folding.

I would have time to understand.

That is the cruelty of plants. They give you time.

I would set down my staff. I would remove one chalk disk from my mask and place it in the center of the floor. I would press my jaw to the cork and listen, not for rescue, but for whether the chamber was becoming lung, stomach, ear, or memory.

If my people found what remained, they would not bury me.

Perhaps they would burn one glove and hang my staff beneath the platform, where wind from the migration trough could make it hum. Perhaps they would give my bones to beetles and string the cleaned finger joints into a listening chime. Perhaps they would find no body at all, only a hollow in the wall shaped almost like a crouched person, lined with pale root hairs and smelling faintly of ash, chalk, and old breath.

Children would be warned not to sleep near it.

Adults would still come, secretly, to ask whether a passage was safe.

And sometimes, after a pressure front, when the valley heaved and new paths opened through the root-mats, a small chalky disk might appear on the floor outside the chamber, marked with the old sign:

wait.

That is where I would be.

At the edge of settlement and silence.

Not quite eaten. Not quite remembered.

A temporary person slowly translated into a useful scar.
