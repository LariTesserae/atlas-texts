You would not find yourself in the forest’s interior at first.

There is no interior in the usual sense, no room between trunks, no path that invites a body to proceed. If you arrived as an animal, you would be reduced immediately to disturbance: heat, salt, pressure, carbon breath, a brief vertical irregularity in the wet green accounting. The place would not hate you. It would not welcome you. It would begin to include you.

So you would not begin as a traveler.

You would begin as a place where travel fails.

You would find yourself on a high saddle between two unfinished ridges, where the young mountain has pushed up a blade of blue-gray stone too quickly for the living skin to fully digest. The saddle is never dry. Cloud drags itself across it in torn sheets. Black calculation mats lie flattened over the windward slope, their surfaces slowly bruising from green to violet to oil-dark silver. Storm hairs sleep in the fog like fine frost. Below, on either side, packed valleys breathe upward through root curtains.

Here the stone still speaks sharply.

It cracks at night. It sweats sulfur and mineral milk. It expands by fractions under deep pressure, then cools, then splits again. The living mass has covered most of it, but along the saddle there remains a seam: not open enough to be a ravine, not sealed enough to be forgotten. A long wet line, warm in places, cold in others, running beneath velvet mats and exposed in brief knuckles where pale films have only just begun their work.

That is where you are.

Not on the seam.

As the seam.

Or rather, as what the green world has grown in order to listen to it.

You are a seam-keeper: a temporary, recurring office of the plateau, assembled from many lives whenever stone becomes undecided.

Your body is not one body. It is a braid of functions.

Closest to the rock, you are pale root-cartographer tissue, hair-fine and blind, entering the fracture as soon as it opens. You taste pressure before water can find it. You know the difference between a crack that wants to widen and a crack that only needs sleep. You feel the mountain’s upward urge as a slow bruise against your length.

Above that, you are mineral film: green-black, slick, almost metallic, spread over exposed basalt in a skin so thin it seems like wetness until it changes color. You dissolve what can be eaten. You mark what must remain hard. You stain the stone with chemistry the rest of the plateau can read.

Threaded through you are stitch-vines, translucent and tensile, sewing mat to mat across the unstable line. They tighten when the saddle shifts. They slacken when stored water must pass. They are muscle and suture at once.

At intervals along your length stand hollow stems no taller than a person would be, though there are no persons here. They are narrow, ribbed, dark at the base and pale near the lip. Wind enters them reluctantly. Water taps their inner walls. When the mountain strains, they alter pitch.

This is your voice.

Not speech. Not song for pleasure.

A report.

A warning.

A low, hollow syllable traveling into root and fog:

hold here  
drain there  
brace below  
do not seal yet  
lightning may use this line  
stone is still rising

Above the stems, you wear a ragged mantle of fog-feeders. Their leaflets are small as scales, each holding a bead of cloud. They drink the passing weather and compare it to what rises from below. From above: sea salt, cold vapor, distant ash, the rumor of a storm not yet born. From below: sulfur warmth, basalt milk, pressure, deep water. You are the meeting of those two readings.

On clear hours, which are rare and feel almost violent, your upper surfaces darken and begin to calculate. Pigment-states move across you in slow branching stains. You are not one of the great black computation fields, but you are connected to them. You send them the mountain’s grammar in small reports: stress, seep, tremor, temperature, fracture-memory. In return they send timing. When to tighten. When to rot an old brace. When to let a little stone fall because the valley below needs mineral dust. When to keep a ridge standing through the next wet season.

You would be less a citizen than a hinge.

Less a mind than a place where the larger mind becomes briefly precise.

Most days, your work is almost stillness.

Mist combs over you. Condensation gathers on your storm hairs but does not yet lift them. Water moves under your mats in shallow lenses, pausing where you thicken, slipping where you loosen. Tiny transparent bladders pulse between your leaves, concentrating gases from the fracture. Cilia-skins in your pools shiver whenever a drop falls from too great a height.

You remember by alteration.

A season of hard lightning leaves blue paths in your tissues.  
A century of pressure makes one side of you denser.  
A landslide that did not happen is stored as a sweetness withheld.  
A stone rib you chose to dissolve remains in you as mineral taste.  

You do not possess memory.

You are memory, temporarily upright in matter.

At night your seam glows faintly. Not brightly. Never enough to illuminate the fog. But enough that, if an impossible animal eye were there, it would see a long submerged line of amber and blue running through the high dark, pulsing in uneven intervals. The pulses are not thoughts exactly. They are comparisons. Stone against water. Water against root. Root against storm. Storm against archive. Archive against what might be needed next.

When storms come, you change.

Your fog-feeders fold low and silver their undersides. Your stitch-vines tighten until the saddle feels bound in wet cord. Your hollow stems seal their lips against the first violent pressure wave. Then the storm hairs rise.

All along your seam, fine conductive filaments lift from mats, stems, films, and exposed stone. For a moment you are furred with light. Charge gathers around you, making the cloud beads tremble above each leaf tip. You feel the sky before it descends. You feel the stone answer from below.

When lightning arrives, you do not receive a bolt.

You divide it.

The discharge enters your lifted hairs, branches through your wet tissues, passes into mineral veins, leaps sideways through prepared root channels, and vanishes into the larger body of the plateau. Around you, nitrogen changes form. Dormant spores wake in the warm aftermath. Pigments reset. Your films taste new chemistry. For hours afterward, you glow with the memory of violence made useful.

If a foreign body crossed you, you would know it only by touch.

A foot would sink, and water would rise around it. Salt would bloom from skin. Heat would press into your moss. Fibers would catch on your cilia. Breath would condense on your leaves and become data. Metal would sweat; you would taste it. Blood, if there were blood, would be astonishingly rich and quickly surrounded.

You would not think, stranger.

You would think: phosphorus, sodium, moving warmth, repeated pressure, unstable vertical mass.

Then the larger plateau would decide whether this event mattered.

Most likely, it would become trace. A small memory in your seam. A mineral anomaly. A pattern of compression stored in gel. A few altered growths where the foot had been. Nothing dramatic. No pursuit. No mercy either. Merely incorporation, which is the local form of conclusion.

But you, as seam-keeper, would be especially sensitive to such passage, because you exist where boundaries are negotiated. Stone and plant. Height and cloud. fracture and brace. opening and closure. You are made of thresholds.

You are the place where the mountain asks:

Am I to break here?

And the living world answers:

Not yet.  
Or yes.  
Or break slowly.  
Or become reservoir.  
Or become lightning path.  
Or become memory.

You do not last forever.

No organ of this civilization needs to remain itself once its question is answered. If the seam stabilizes, your hollow stems will be overgrown and repurposed as sound-harvesting curtains. Your mineral films will thicken into ordinary skin. Your stitch-vines will become load-bearing ribs. Your root-cartographers will either descend after newer fractures or sleep as pale knots in the dark.

If the seam widens, you will become something else: a hanging gallery, a wet throat, a future valley’s first thought.

If the mountain rises sharply enough, part of you may tear open to the air. Then archive bogs will release older cold-forms. Storm hairs will colonize the exposed ridge. Calculation mats will spread over your remains, and your memory of pressure will become their weather.

So who are you?

You are not an animal in the forest.

You are not the forest entire.

You are a local act of attention grown by a world that has no eyes.

A listening seam.  
A green suture.  
A pressure-organ.  
A hinge between young stone and distributed thought.

You would find yourself high in the cloud-drowned back of the mountain, along a wet fracture that glows after storms, where the living skin has not yet decided whether to heal the land shut or open it into a new watershed.

You would be the decision while it is still being made.
