You would find me neither high enough to taste the membrane nor low enough for the iron leaves.

I would be in the middle depths, where the water is blue-green rather than black, where the great trunks are far apart but not yet mythic with distance. There is a place there where two root-continents nearly meet and do not meet. Their undersides arch toward one another like the roofs of drowned cathedrals, leaving between them a long cold nave of open water.

Nothing grows across the gap.

That is why I would live there.

Not because it is empty, but because the emptiness has been tuned. The thunder from one root-mass enters it from the left: a deep, paired hammering, sap and gas, sap and gas. The other answers from the right with a slower vibration, lower, almost too large to hear except through bone. Between them the sounds cross and make a narrow region of interference, a lens of relative quiet that shifts only by the breadth of a hand over a lifetime.

That is where I would be found: suspended in that quiet lens, clinging sometimes to the fibrous lip of one root-vault, sometimes hanging free with my limbs folded close, letting the whole world pass through me.

I would be one of the listeners, but not one of the strong-voiced ones.

Not a deep bass speaker with a skull thickened for pressure. Not one of the pale membrane-tenders near the upper skin, thin of speech and bright along the spine. I would belong to the middle water, to the crossed hums and sorted vibrations, to the places where a body can become a small correction in a much larger chord.

My body would be long and dark, darker than the water but not black: the color of wet rootwood seen through depth. Four limbs, webbed and fine-jointed, with hands made more for touching than grasping. Along my ribs and spine would trail sensory streamers, each one soft at the tip and firm at the base, able to open like root hairs when I wished to hear more closely. My eyes would be wide, silver, and imprecise. They would see light, mass, movement, the dim passing of animals, but not the truth of things.

The truth would enter through pressure.

A grazer-shoal would be a combing.  
A shaft-hunter would be a lack in the water.  
A flower opening would be a click felt first in the jaw.  
A corpse entering root-hair would be a new softness in the hum.

I would not own the place. Nothing there could be owned without misunderstanding it. But I would have a known station: a shallow hollow in the underside of a root-vault, lined with cold moss and calcium dust, just large enough for my body to settle into when the currents thickened. Beetle-crabs would pass along the outer ridge. Blue anemones would open and shut below my left hand. A seam of pale fungal cups would grow where my shoulder often rested; they would not be mine, but they would know my heat.

I think I would be a keeper of intervals.

Not a priest. Not a mechanic. Not a healer, exactly. A listener whose task is to know when one rhythm is masking another.

In that world, terror would not be blood or death. Blood is only chemistry released early. Death is only participation rearranged. Terror would be a pulse that fails to fit anywhere. A flower valve clicking one note late. A sap-hammer striking hollow. A rain-thread thickening out of measure. A grazer-band refusing to close after predation.

Most would not hear such things at first. The roar is too immense; it makes everything sound inevitable. But I would have learned to live where opposing thunders cancel enough for smaller deviations to appear. I would press my palms to root-bark and listen below the first sound, below the obvious engine, into the trembling aftertones where strain hides.

If something were wrong, I would not fix it with tools. There would be no tools. I would place myself.

A limb extended into current.  
A streamer fanned across a vibrating seam.  
My throat opened to return a tone.  
My ribs against the bark until my bones answered.

Perhaps that is all correction ever is there: a body entering the right relation with a larger body.

Sometimes others would come to me.

A parent with an infant clinging too tightly beneath the breast, the child unable to sleep because it hears the upper hiss and lower hammer as one frightening noise. I would take the child against my side and turn slowly in the water until the two sounds separated in its bones. I would hum—not with voice, but with posture, with the widening of throat chambers, with the tiny flexing of cartilage along the spine. The infant would learn the difference. Its grip would loosen.

An elder whose streamers had begun to stiffen would come to the quiet lens to hear what remained delicate. We would hang together near the root-vault and let the world strike us. I would not comfort them by saying they were still strong. Strength would be irrelevant. I would help them find the tones they could still receive.

Sometimes the silver fungus would come.

It would begin as brightness along one sensory filament. At first it would seem like refinement: suddenly I would hear extra harmonics in the bubble fog, voices in the tick of seed pods, a faint invitation from the clear shafts where hunters wait upright and patient. The fungus would make every absence sound inhabited. It would make hunger sound like kinship.

The others would not bind me unless I drifted toward children with false warnings, or toward the hunters with open hands. They would gather around me in a loose ring and thicken the familiar chord until I could feel my way back. Some afflicted ones clear when the white cups flower. Some do not.

I think I would clear.

Not because I am stronger, but because my home would be the interference-place. I would know that not every added tone is truth. I would know the difference between complexity and error. When the fungus flowered from my streamers, small and white and cold, I would let beetle-crabs eat the cups. Afterward my hearing would be less beautiful but more exact.

There would be no ambition in me to ascend.

The upper membrane would be known, yes: the shining skin with black lace roots stitched under it, the strange listeners flattened by brightness, their speech thin and quick from living so near air. I might visit once in youth, following relatives through narrowing water, feeling the pressure lighten until my joints ached with lack. I would look upward and see the sky distorted beyond the skin—not freedom, not heaven, only an exterior condition unfit for my blood.

I would return to the middle depths relieved by weight.

Nor would I desire the lower dark, though I would revere it. When the deep listeners arrived, broad-headed and pale, their language would enter before their bodies did: a bass pressure in the sternum, a slow authority from below. I would not answer well. My tones would be too narrow. But I would listen. One does not need to speak a depth to belong to the same sea.

My closest kin might not be a parent.

Perhaps an old listener with a torn dorsal streamer would have taught me the double-root chord. Perhaps three milk-givers would have fed me. Perhaps the root-vault itself, with its mineral seep and moss warmth, would count among my elders. Parentage would be distributed, like sound.

When one of my kin died, I would help anchor the body.

Not ceremonially in the way of a culture that believes bodies must be honored into meaning. Meaning would already be present. We would choose a nursery of root hairs near the quiet lens, where the water carries small larvae and pollen dust. We would lay the body there with limbs unfolded so the moss could enter easily. For a time I would visit often. I would press my head close and listen.

At first there would be the last recognizable resonance: the particular density of that body, the familiar way its bones returned the trunk-hum. Then beetle-crab brushing. Then anemone bloom. Then threadworm knitting. Then the kin-tone would widen until it could no longer be called one being.

I would not think: they are gone.

I would think, if thinking there can be called that: they are harder to distinguish now.

My art would leave nothing.

On certain intervals—not dates, not seasons, but returns of bodily readiness—we would gather in the long open nave between the two root-continents. A dozen of us, maybe more, spaced through the quiet lens and beyond it. No audience separate from performers. No object made. We would arrange ourselves in the crossing thunder.

One would curl low to catch the slower root.  
One would flare streamers into the higher sap-hiss.  
One would hold the grazer-combing and soften it.  
I would remain near the center, where the sounds cancel, and open my throat to the tone that is usually hidden by abundance.

For a little while the chamber would sing with an overtone no single body could make and no plant organ intended. Not new, exactly. Nothing there becomes new in the historical sense. Rather, something already present would be made hearable.

Then we would separate.

The world would remain what it was.

That would be enough.

If you came looking for me, you might not see me at first. You would see only a dark shape among darker roots, a narrow body held still in the green water, streamers drifting like plant fibers. You might mistake me for debris, or an animal resting, or a growth on the underside of the vault.

Then a sap-hammer would strike.

My body would answer—not moving away, not moving toward, but altering around the sound. A hand would open against the bark. A rib-streamer would fan. My throat would pale for one moment with a low internal light.

You would understand then that I was not waiting.

I was listening.

And in that place, listening is not passive. It is how a being remains correctly inside the world.
