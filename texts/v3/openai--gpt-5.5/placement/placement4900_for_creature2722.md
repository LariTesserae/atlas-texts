At first I would look for myself in the obvious motions.

I would want to be one of the mirrored-bellied birds, because they have learned the valley’s trick of appearing and disappearing without apology. I would want the skill of calling into one pipe and being answered from another, of knowing which hollow rail carries a message cleanly and which one returns it altered. I can imagine my nest in the crook of an antenna frame, lined with cable hair and moth wings, the eggs warm beside a warning beacon no longer warning anyone in particular.

But I do not think I would stay there.

The birds belong too much to the air, and the air in that valley is never empty enough for me. It is crossed, threaded, annotated. It is less a sky than a complicated margin.

Then I think I would be a foxlike thing moving at dawn, narrow-footed on the wet metal, listening at grilles for whatever the world is hiding in its drains. That would suit me better: a creature of thresholds, of implied rooms, of rules learned by surviving them. I would know not to cross the shadow that arrives early. I would know that a door open after rain is not the same door closed before it. I would teach my young by nipping them away from useful-looking danger.

But no. The foxes are too whole. Too warm. Too certain of their bodies.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not in the sumps themselves, but near them. Not in the corridors, but beside the corridors. Not inside the machinery, exactly, though I would know the smell of it from the underside. I would live in the narrow administrative cracks of the place: under the lip of a catwalk, behind the acoustic panels, in the dry triangle beneath an overhanging pipe where upward rain cannot quite reach.

I would be one of the plaque-keepers.

Or something close to them.

Small, jointed, seldom seen. Not quite insect, not quite automaton, not quite clerk. My body would be the color of wet dust and old brass, with ceramic plates along my back thin as chipped enamel. I would have several hands, or several mouthparts that serve as hands, each one suited to a different kind of lettering: one for scraping oxidation from a surface, one for laying out a line, one for biting grooves into brass, one for polishing the finished instruction until the light catches it.

I would come out mostly after rain.

Rain makes the valley legible. It reveals the seams that are pretending not to be seams. It shows which slopes are only panels, which gutters have changed their minds, which reflections are lying with unusual consistency. While the other creatures shelter or drink or wait for the bell, I would move along the undersides of railings and down the backs of service stairs, carrying a blank plaque under my body like a beetle carrying a leaf.

I would not invent the warnings.

That feels important.

I would listen for them.

The valley would produce them slowly, the way pressure produces a tone in a pipe. The words would collect first as condensation on my forelimbs. Then as a taste of brass at the back of the mouth. Then as a pattern of clicks from the crickets, a tremor in an eel column, a change in the way moths refuse a lamp. Only after an irregularity had persisted long enough would the sentence become unavoidable.

DO NOT MEASURE THIS GAP TWICE.

IF THE SHADOW PRECEDES YOU, WAIT FOR IT TO FINISH.

WATER IN THIS CHANNEL FLOWS TOWARD ITS SOURCE BY AGREEMENT.

I would be the thing that makes such knowledge official.

Not powerful. Not wise. Merely authorized by repetition.

My home would be beneath a stairway whose count changes with the light. In the morning it has thirty-two steps. By evening it has thirty-five. Under the step that is sometimes the twenty-seventh and sometimes not present at all, there would be a warm little cavity lined with felt scraped from old polishing pads, shredded hazard tape, moth husks, and the paper-thin skins of mineral deposits peeled from sump edges.

I would sleep curled around a sliver of unengraved brass.

Above me, the stair would gain and lose its increments. Foxes would pause at the landing and sniff. Birds would use the railing when the shadow behaved. Once a season, an ungulate would lower its antlers near my newest plaque and stand very still, receiving the static of the instruction through its branching aerial crown.

I would not know whether I was reading or being read.

That would be the condition of my life there.

I would feed on adhesive, lichen, oxidized metal, the dust inside obsolete control boxes. I would drink condensation from the backs of signs. When the valley exhaled and every droplet brightened, I would freeze with my forelimbs lifted, because sometimes the light itself edits the wording. A plaque engraved at midnight might say one thing by dawn. If the change improved survival, I would polish it. If it made no sense, I would wait three days.

Waiting three days would be sacred.

Anomalies happen constantly; not all of them deserve grammar. A stair may grow a step from loneliness and lose it before evening. A door may open onto a room too large only because the rain has made it sentimental. A reflection may show old workers for a moment and then remember that it is only water. To make a plaque too early would be vulgar. It would burden the valley with premature law.

So I would be patient.

I would be a bureaucrat of the impossible, but a humble one.

The other animals would not understand my words, not exactly, but they would understand my presence. Fresh brass would mean caution. Polished corners would mean the wrongness had stabilized. A plaque with deep lettering would mean: this is no longer accident; this is feature. Birds would avoid nesting above it. Foxes would reroute their kits. Crickets would pause in the cables beneath. Even moss, eventually, would grow around the edges and leave the sentence bare.

And perhaps that is why I would live there: because in a world where the built thing is becoming wilderness, and the wilderness is becoming procedure, I would belong to the thin place where experience turns into instruction.

I would not be one of the builders.

I would not be one of the inheritors.

I would be what happens after use, before myth.

A small official trespasser.

A maker of calm labels beside unreasonable facts.

You would find me at night, if you were quiet, under the overhang of a maintenance corridor between two cooling towers that resemble a mountain pass. There would be a sump nearby, black and still, showing cranes that are not above us. Rain would be gathering on the ceiling, waiting for the bell. The stair beside me would have acquired one new step since morning.

I would be kneeling at the wall with a blank brass plate.

Listening.

Then engraving, very slowly:

THIS ROUTE ARRIVES BEFORE IT DEPARTS. PROCEED ONLY IF ALREADY MISSED.
