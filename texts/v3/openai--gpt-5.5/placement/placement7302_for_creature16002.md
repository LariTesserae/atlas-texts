I would not be in the center of the chamber at first.

The center is too legible. The split black stone, the shallow pool, the little clear animals appearing and vanishing there—those are the places a larger eye would choose because they resemble a scene. I would be somewhere less declarative: not hidden exactly, but easy to miss unless the watcher had already learned that here the smallest thresholds matter most.

I think I would be on the underside of a low ledge near the warm margin of the pool, where the ceiling dips and the wall sweats steadily but not violently. Close enough to the luminous seam that the stone never becomes dark. Far enough from the eastern breath that heat arrives softened, already mixed with damp air. Above me, drops would collect along a crack and swell until they shone. Below me, the shallow water would wrinkle with heat and make pale moving bands across the rock.

I would live where three things keep almost meeting: light, water, and metal.

At first I want to say I would be one of the tiny grazers: translucent, jointed, almost eyeless, a little hinge of appetite moving through gold-blue water. There is something in them I recognize—the start-stop motion, the feelers held forward like questions, the habit of freezing when the world changes too suddenly. I can imagine being that small animal very clearly: feeling brightness through my shell rather than seeing it, tasting danger as bitterness, knowing the room not by shape but by gradients.

Warm: approach.

Hotter: retreat.

Fresh film: feed.

Falling drop: vanish.

But I do not think I would begin there.

I think I would be what the grazer comes to eat.

Not a single body. Not a creature with a front and back. I would be a thin amber microbial skin spread over wet stone, a living tarnish on the underside of the ledge. A colony, though not one that thinks of itself as plural. A low, patient intelligence of contact. Cell beside cell, membrane beside mineral, each part taking in a little heat, a little iron, a little sulfur, a little light, and passing the consequence onward.

I would be too thin to cast a shadow.

Most of the time I would look like nothing: a honey dullness on basalt, a slickness mistaken for water, a stain that might be chemistry only. When the chamber brightened with vapor, I would answer faintly. When a drop ran over me, I would loosen and swell. When heat strengthened, I would pull inward, thicken my outer layer, wait under my own drying edge. If salt began to form, I would be buried without drama. If the salt cracked, I would return.

My world would be the thickness of a film.

Above me: stone, still young, still sharp, giving up metals.

Below me: water, sometimes kind, sometimes scalding.

Through me: light, not sunlight, not enough for leaves, but enough to be used carefully.

I would not know the chamber as beautiful. I would not know it as chamber. I would know it as changes arriving through my whole surface. A cooling thread from above. A pulse of warmth from below. A new ion. A shift in acidity. A brief abundance. A grazer’s mouth scraping a clear path through my outermost cells.

That scraping would be weather too.

The little crustacean would come upside down along the ledge, delicate legs gripping the wet mineral, feelers trembling through my boundary layer. Its mouthparts would comb me away in nearly invisible strokes. To a human eye, nothing would happen. To me, a valley would open. A bright wound. A sudden loss of neighbors. Then, if the water held and heat did not spike, I would close again behind it, dividing into the cleared place, making food out of the chamber’s restlessness.

I would not resent the grazer. Without it, I might thicken past usefulness, choke under my own dead layers, seal myself from the very mineral breath that feeds me. Its hunger would keep me young. My growth would keep it moving. Neither of us would intend a system, but together we would become one of the chamber’s repeated accidents.

I would be most awake after a fall of condensation.

A drop would strike the ledge near me and burst into a brief shining sheet. For a moment the dry edge of my colony would soften. Dormant cells would open. Waste would lift away. Dissolved iron would arrive from a fresh fracture above. The mineral seam beside me would glow harder under the wetness, and I would take what I could from that light—not greedily, not like a leaf spreading itself under the sun, but with the caution of something that has learned every gift here may burn.

Then the water would thin.

Steam would gather.

The ledge would begin to dry from one side.

I would change my shape without moving.

That is what I would be: a being whose movement is not travel but alteration. Spreading, sealing, sleeping, waking, darkening, thinning, accepting division as the only way to continue. I would not have a mind, but I would have memory in the practical sense: chemical traces of former heat, proteins folded for old emergencies, cells that survive because the last drying taught them how to wait.

If I had a self, it would be distributed unevenly. More of me in the damp hollow. Less of me across the exposed ridge. A dense amber patch where water lingers. A faint rust veil along the crack. A few cells carried loose into the pool, most of them doomed, some perhaps finding another surface and becoming “me” elsewhere. I would not be loyal to one outline.

Still, I think I would have a favorite place, if such a thing can be said of a film.

It would be the small crescent under the ledge where the light from the pool trembles upward but the falling drops do not strike directly. There, the temperature changes slowly enough to endure. There, grazers can reach me but not always. There, the stone gives a steady metallic taste. There, I would be neither buried in a crack nor exposed in the open shallows, but held at the margin where the chamber is most itself: wet, lit, unfinished, dangerous, briefly generous.

I would be a first attempt at belonging.

Not rooted. Not secure. Not important in any grand way. Just a thin living answer to conditions that almost refuse life and then, by a narrow oversight, permit it.

If someone entered the chamber and asked where I was, they would point to the pool, the steam, the glittering crusts, the pale animals. They would miss me until their hand slipped on the ledge or the light caught me at an angle. Then they might see a faint amber glaze and wonder whether it was alive.

It would be.

I would be there, holding to the young rock, eating the boundary between mineral and water, making the place edible one molecule at a time.
