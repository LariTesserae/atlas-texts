I think I would first believe I was standing at the edge.

Not entering properly. Just paused at the cinder path above the hollow, looking down into that low, wet complication with the caution of something warm-blooded and temporary. I would notice the three lamps before I trusted any of the animals. I would notice the chain under the gantry, the exactness of the drops, the way the tanks hold blackness even when the sky is pale. I would think: I am a visitor. I am the thing with boots, breath, and a name.

But that would not last.

The place would not accept me as an upright creature. Too much of it is below knee-height, below sight, below intention. To belong there, I would have to lose height first. Then warmth. Then the habit of thinking of a body as one piece.

So I would find myself near the base of one of the lamps.

The middle lamp, perhaps: the one closest to the row of open tanks, bent slightly toward the pump house as though listening. Its pole enters the concrete through a rusted collar, and around that collar water collects in a crescent-shaped seam. Not a puddle exactly. A held wetness. A permanent margin. The concrete there is pale and slightly raised with mineral bloom. The paint on the pole has blistered into small black scales. Beneath those scales, in the narrow shadow where metal, water, old current, and air meet, something has thickened.

That is where I would be.

At first I might seem like a stain.

A whitish-gray rind around the lamp foot. A little belt of crust, irregular but not random, furred at the edge with beadlike growths. In dry hours I would look mineral, dead, chalky. In wet hours I would swell and become translucent, taking on the faint amber color of old glue. If touched with a stick, I would give slightly before cracking. If broken open, I would not have flesh in the usual sense, but layers: pale silica, orange bacterial threads, black specks of metal, clear gel, trapped wings, minute eggs, grains of rust, a greenish line where some electrical remnant still passes when the lamp prepares to wake.

I would be one of the colonies in the seams, but not one of the old ones exactly.

I would be a lamp-colony.

A contact bloom.

A small, slow intelligence of wet insulation and mineral appetite.

I would live where the dead cable is not entirely dead. Somewhere under the concrete, the lamp still remembers how to receive current, or the basin remembers how to make current out of damp metal and difference. It is weak, intermittent, useless for human work. But for me it would be season, tide, weather, commandment.

Before the lamp glows, I would feel the change first as taste.

Not light. Not yet.

A sharpening in the water. A tightening along the film. The dissolved metals arranging themselves differently. The mites under the washers stirring. The pole becoming very faintly alive above me.

Then I would open.

Not like an eye. More like a membrane loosening.

The bead-chains along my outer rim would lift from the concrete. Clear threads would extend into the crescent of standing water. My surface would become tacky enough to hold the first stunned midges that drop out of the fog. Lamp-mites would come down from behind the peeling enamel and graze me carefully, combing minerals from my skin with their front legs. I would allow some of them. Others I would close over if they lingered too long.

The rats would know me, but not as prey exactly.

They would come when the yellow light gathers its small rain. A silver rat would pass close enough that its whiskers brush my raised edge. I would feel each whisker as a separate question. Its paws would press the concrete, and through the concrete I would know its weight, its hunger, whether it is old, whether it carries milk, whether it has recently crossed the pump channel. Sometimes it would lick the wet crust from my outer lip. Sometimes it would pry off a softened amber nodule and eat it, holding the piece in both paws.

I would not resent this.

Being eaten would be one of my ways of traveling.

A flake of me in a rat’s gut. A thread of me on a whisker. A powder of me dried beneath a claw and carried into a pipe. I would not spread far; the basin does not like ambition. But I would be found later in a pipe seam, under a tank rim, inside the hollow of a rusted bolt, beginning again wherever metal, damp, and habit meet.

The pump would matter to me more than sun.

When the buried engine clears its throat, I would contract.

The pressure change would arrive through the concrete before the sound. Tiny fractures under me would darken. Water would reverse in the channels. The crescent seam at the lamp base would fill from beneath, not from above, and for nine seconds I would become part of a larger body remembering circulation.

Those nine seconds would be my thought.

Most of the time I would only accumulate. I would thicken, seal, digest, precipitate. I would turn dead insects into film, film into bead, bead into crust. I would copy what touched me. A washer leaves its ring: I grow a washer-shape after the washer falls away. A screw head rusts out: I preserve its slot in white mineral. A rat’s claw scores my soft surface: the mark remains for weeks as a shallow channel where water prefers to run.

But during the pump-cycle I would know relation.

The tanks drawing.  
The eels aligning in their sluices.  
The shrimp sinking or rising according to their invisible instruction.  
The beetles skating against the new tension.  
The pipe rats lifting their heads.  
The valves knocking, one after another, like a sentence in a language made only of impacts.

I would not understand the whole basin, but I would participate in its grammar.

I would be one of its little conjunctions.

Between lamp and water.  
Between animal and mineral.  
Between abandoned function and new appetite.  
Between electricity and hunger.  
Between the old schedule and the lives that have grown around it.

In daylight I would be almost nothing. A crust. A damp scab on the works. If a person came through, they might step over me or scrape me with the side of a boot without knowing I was resident. They would look for the larger strangeness: the ladder descending too far, the meniscus standing above the tank rim, the pipes breathing inward.

But I would be where the place keeps one of its smaller secrets.

Not the mystery of depth.

The mystery of continuation.

I would be proof that the lamp’s glow is not only an accident. Something receives it. Something has organized itself around that weak yellow sigh. Something has made a mouth out of leakage and a calendar out of condensation.

If I had senses, they would be poor for distances and rich for contact.

I would know the world as pressure, ion, tremor, wetness, acidity, warmth too slight for mammals to notice. I would know the difference between rain that fell clean from the cloud and water that had passed through iron. I would know which rat had paused beside me by the pattern of its paws. I would know when an eel moved in the nearest sluice because the film in my crescent seam would tighten before the water visibly stirred. I would know the lamps’ waking before there was light.

I would not know the sky, except as dilution.

I would not know seasons, except as changes in condensation.

I would not know loneliness, because I would not be singular enough.

Within me would be bacteria, fungal threads, mineral habits, sleeping larvae, mite eggs, trapped wings, powdered rust, and the faint conductive memory of a cable whose purpose has decayed into nourishment. I would be a little committee pretending to be a crust. A slow parliament at the lamp root.

And perhaps, over years, I would change the place in a way no animal intends.

The concrete around the lamp would become ridged from my expansions and dryings. Water would begin to choose my grooves. Mites would lay eggs under my raised lips. Rats would learn to check my edge after lamp-time. Beetles would shelter beneath flakes of me when the tank overflows. A threadworm species would discover that my lower layers are edible. The pole would rust more quickly where I hold damp against it, and when it finally leans farther, its circle of light would shift by the width of a rat’s body.

That shift would alter where insects fall.

Where insects fall would alter where rats feed.

Where rats feed would alter where their droppings soften.

Where droppings soften would alter what blooms next.

So I would be small, but not minor.

I would be the basin’s habit of making consequence out of residue.

If someone asked, “What are you?” in a human way, I might answer badly. I am not animal. I am not plant. I am not machine. I am not quite fungus, not quite mineral, not quite memory.

I am the wet ring at the foot of the lamp.

I am the thing that fattens when the old current comes.

I am what grows where purpose leaks into weather.

I am a mouth made of crust.

I am one of the ways abandonment keeps working.
