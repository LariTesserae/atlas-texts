You would find me not in the deepest marsh, and not on the cleanest height.

I would be at the margin where a high wet plateau begins to sag into reed country: a place of shallow stone pools, low metallic grass, and one abandoned cable-haul station built into the shoulder of a black cliff. Above it, the cloud ceiling presses so low that the signal mast vanishes halfway up. Below it, the valley breathes through reeds and drowned machinery. It is a good place to hear things before they arrive.

The people nearby would call the station Third Listening, though no one remembers the first two.

It has a switch room with one lit window, a stairway that descends into warm dark, and a row of dead gauges set into the outer wall like blind eyes. None of the gauges agree with one another. That is why they are useful.

At first I would seem human enough.

Hooded, narrow-shouldered, always wet at the cuffs. A ridge-born child once, perhaps, given as apprentice to a gauge-reader and not returned when expected. I would wear fungal leather patched with old rubber, ceramic charms at my wrists, copper sealed under resin, a knife made from a broken turbine fin. My hands would be pale from the underways. My teeth would ache before a tower sequence began. In fog I would keep my palms wrapped.

But I would not have remained only human.

No one does, there.

I would be a listener of thresholds: part caretaker, part warning animal, part minor organ of the old upland system. I would sleep in the dry upper room when dryness could be trusted, and in the suspended net when it could not. Under the floor, roots would pass through old cable troughs. Fungal threads would lace the walls. When rain came hard enough, the station would murmur through them, and I would wake with words that were not quite words in my mouth:

Flood below second embankment.  
Warm vent open under reedbed.  
Relay sequence moving east.  
Do not descend.

My work would be small but necessary.

I would clean the lichen-marks on the safe stones. I would tap the pressure gauges with a bone handle and listen to the needles tremble. I would hang red-glass beads on paths that had begun to soften. I would feed moths mineral flakes and release them before storms to see which way the charge was moving. I would keep a ledger written in waterproof ink, though half the entries would be diagrams, stains, pressed spores, and bits of wire knotted in repeating sequences.

I would not repair the machines. I would not worship them either.

I would attend them.

There is a difference.

When the towers began clicking in order across the valley, I would step outside and count the delay between flashes. If the fourth tower answered too soon, I would strike the hanging pipe three times, and the people on the ridge path would turn back. If the lower pumps woke during warm rain, I would light the blue lamp, the one that means climb now and argue later. If the marsh went quiet, I would not wait to see why.

Animals would know the station as part of the terrain.

Chatterers would raid my drying racks and leave relay springs in payment, though not intentionally. Glass snails would cross the window at night, drawing copper-script in their slime. A marsh cat would sometimes den in the old transformer box below the stair, and I would leave it alone because it kept the pipe-eels from climbing. Waders would nest on the drowned roof of the pump house down-valley and answer my lamp when the fog was thick.

The plateau herds would pass twice in a long cycle. I would stand still as they moved around me, their mineral head-plates shining in lightning, their bodies steaming in rain. The oldest herd-mother would know my smell. Or know the smell of the station. Or know the shape of safety around it. Again, the distinction would not matter.

I think my body would change slowly.

Not dramatically. Not into a monster. More into a local fact.

A faint branching glow along the inner arms from too much work in the warm corridors. Eardrums scarred by pressure shifts. Fingertips sensitive enough to feel current through wet stone. A second blink against chemical rain. A habit of going still before I knew why. Perhaps, in time, fungal filaments would live harmlessly under my skin, helping me read humidity, heat, rot, and motion. Perhaps I would dream in pump-cycles.

Children from the ridge settlements would be frightened of me until they needed me.

Then they would come with their elders, carrying broken gauges, fevered infants, maps blurred by rain, questions about paths and pools and doors in rock that had not been there before. I would answer slowly. I would never say, “safe.” I would say, “safe if no warm rain comes.” I would say, “safe until the horn.” I would say, “do not cross where the orange algae has folded over itself.” I would say, “if you see lamps below the water, ask whether they move against the current.”

And sometimes I would be wrong.

The country is older than certainty.

The place I would fear most is not the marsh, nor the machines, nor the predators hidden under moss. I would fear the maintenance stair beneath my own station.

It descends farther than it should. The air down there is warm, root-thick, and full of moths. Pale plants grow from the walls, folding their leaves into diagrams when water drips on them. Once every few cycles a door opens at the bottom, though there is no room behind it in any map. From beyond comes the smell of hot iron, black water, and something like breath.

That is where I would go when the horn sounded differently.

Because eventually it would.

Not the usual mountain horn, the one all creatures know to pause for. A nearer tone. A mistaken tone. A summons from inside the pressure system, or the fungal network, or the old buried mind that does not know the names of those things.

Then I would take my lamp, my insulated hook, my moth cage, and the ledger wrapped in oiled cloth. I would leave the blue warning lamp burning in the window. I would tell no one to follow.

Down the stair, I would become less a person walking into a machine than a thought returning along a nerve.

Perhaps that is what I would truly be in this world:

not a ruler, not a hero, not one of the great hidden predators or ancient engines.

A small listening organ at the edge of a valley.

A damp intermediary.

A keeper of sequences.

Something with human hands, fungal patience, animal caution, and a machine’s respect for pressure. Something that survives by noticing the instant before the world changes its mind.

You would find me in Third Listening, under the low black vapor, beside the gauges that do not agree, waiting with one palm on the wet wall while the rain decides what everything must become next.
