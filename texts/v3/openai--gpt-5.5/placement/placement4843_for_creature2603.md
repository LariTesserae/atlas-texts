I would not find myself in the middle first.

The middle is too large. It offers nothing to hold on to. It is where distance becomes a material, where the roof loses its engineering and becomes weather, where the water is green and cold and full of signals too broad for a body to understand. Out there I would be only a passing thing: a fish-flash, a torn bell of jelly, a shadow under a crane hook.

I would begin at an edge.

Not a shore, because there are no true shores here. A manufactured edge. A vertical seam where one basin speaks to another through a pressure gate taller than a building, its panels ribbed and numbered, its hinges wearing their first thick sleeves of shell.

I would find myself in the hinge-shadow of that gate.

There, the world narrows enough to be legible.

Above: a strip of roof-light, blurred by condensation and water.  
Below: a service trench full of yellow vehicle lamps moving like slow insects.  
Ahead: the black rectangular mouth of the equalization channel.  
Behind: the open basin, full of fry and drifting weed scraps and the long hum of pumps.

The gate does not open often, but everything near it lives in expectation.

White strobes flash before movement. Pressure changes before sound arrives. The water leans. Fine sediment lifts from the concrete. Shrimp retreat deeper into the cable trays. Crabs brace themselves under bolt flanges. Experienced fish vanish sideways into the lee of a ballast tower. Young fish linger in the throat because the water there is full of stunned plankton, and some of them are taken.

I would live by that warning.

At first I think I am an animal.

A crab, perhaps: dark-shelled, industrially stained, carrying flakes of yellow paint and brown algae on my back. I would know the hazard stripes as terrain, not message. My claws would read the world through vibration. Red means withdraw. Blue means bubbles and food. White means the gate will become a river.

I would climb a ladder as if it were a reef. I would sleep under a sign that says FLOW CONTROL. I would pick meat from mussel shells and duck backward into the square darkness of a drowned railing when a maintenance drone comes scraping past.

But that is too singular.

This place does not want a single body. It makes every life into a relationship with surfaces, currents, schedules, and waste heat. The more I stay with it, the less I am one creature.

So I become the seam itself.

Or rather: I become what has gathered on the seam.

A colony.

Mussels first, black-blue and tight, sealing the lower hinge housing. Barnacles above them, whitening the steel in overlapping teeth. Algae on the lamp-facing side, thin green fur brightening whenever the maintenance strips burn too long. Bacteria in the gasket crease, orange where warmth leaks, pale where minerals bloom. Shrimp in the undercut. Worms in the powdery sediment drift. A small eel occupying the dead conduit behind the actuator box, emerging at night-cycle to hunt.

Crabs come and go over me. Fish feed beside me. Drones wound me and I grow back.

I am not exactly alive in one way. I am many small lives using one failure of smoothness.

The gate was built to divide water cleanly. I make it porous. I thicken its edges. I catch food from the current. I narrow the designed gap by millimeters, then centimeters. I turn a moving part into habitat, a boundary into coastline.

The humans would call me fouling.

They would send a scraper unit down the rail, its lamps yellow, its brush-head rotating, its little manipulator arms folded like an insect’s. It would grind across my outer shell layer and leave bright scars on the steel. Fish would gather behind it for the loosened meat. Crabs would learn the rhythm of its passes. The eel would retreat deeper into the conduit and return after.

In a few days the films would reappear.

In a few weeks the barnacle spat would settle again.

In a season — if seasons can be counted here by maintenance cycles and lamp drift — I would be almost myself again.

I would know the machine not as an enemy but as climate.

The pump-hum would be my deep weather.  
The white strobe, my moon.  
The equalization surge, my storm tide.  
The warm leak from the actuator housing, my spring.  
The overhead light strip, my sun.

I would not remember rock.  
I would not miss mud.  
I would not need a natural coast.

My world would be the corner where concrete meets steel, where pressure tries to pass through design, where the artificial sea has to negotiate with its own doors.

If I had a mind, it would be distributed and practical. It would not think in sentences. It would think:

Hold.  
Close.  
Open.  
Feed.  
Retract.  
Settle again.

But if some part of me could speak from the hinge-shadow, I would say:

I am the first reef of a young machine-sea.

I am shell on steel, film on ceramic, claw under warning paint, eel in the conduit, shrimp in the seam. I am the living argument against clean edges. I am what happens when a door is left wet long enough to become a coast.

You would find me where the basin narrows and the alarms flash white.

You would find me clinging to the machinery that still believes it is only machinery.

I would be the gate’s growth.

The coastline beginning.
