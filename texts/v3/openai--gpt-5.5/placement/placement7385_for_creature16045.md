I would not be one of the tall arrivals.

I would not come over the ridge with a pack, standing against the sky, breathing loudly and naming things. I would not be the hawk, either, though at first I might want the clarity of that height: the whole hollow held in one glance, every marmot and runnel and wet stone arranged below me.

But that is too much distance for this place.

To belong there, I would have to become smaller than sight.

You would find me near the hut, but not inside it at first. Not as a body. Not as someone sleeping with one cheek against the wall. I would be in the crack beside the doorway where the bone needles are kept, in that narrow dark seam between two stones where damp gathers but does not quite drip.

I think I would be a listening pin.

A little worked sliver of bone, polished by fingers and weather, pierced at one end with a hole no wider than a sedge stem. Not a weapon. Not a decoration, exactly. Something made from the forearm of one who had lived long enough to bend close to the ground and hear well. After death, the bone was taken back from the meltwater crack above the basin, scraped clean with dark glass, rubbed with lichen ash and fat, and shaped into a small pale tool.

I would be worn through the ear of a listener during rain.

Not always. Only when needed.

Most days I would wait in the hut wall, cold, damp, patient, hearing what stone hears. The waterfall would keep speaking through the whole structure. Fog would bead along my edge. Midges would shelter in the crack near me. Once in a while a child’s hand would reach in, not to take me, but to touch me, because children are taught that old bone still knows pressure.

When the weather changed strangely, or when the waterfall’s voice thickened before its time, someone would remove me from the crack.

Their fingers would be broad, wet, careful.

They would pass me once across their tongue, not for cleanliness but to wake me. Then they would set me against stone, against the cup-mark that never empties, against the wall of the hut, against a slab suspected of loosening, against the shallow chest of a fevering child.

My work would be to carry small sounds inward.

Not to interpret. Not to speak.

Only to make contact precise.

Through me the listener would hear the difference between water on the surface and water underneath. Between thaw and rot. Between a burrow filling slowly and one already lost. Between the ordinary silence of snow above the north wall and the packed, waiting silence before it falls.

I would know no whole sentences.

Only pulses.

Drip.

Hold.

Crack.

Soften.

Descend.

Wait.

I would be pale because I had been inside a body once. I would be stained green at the edges because the hollow does not leave anything purely itself. I would smell faintly of smoke from the hut, finger-salt, old marrow, wet stone.

Sometimes I would travel to the boulder facing the waterfall.

The listener would kneel there with me threaded through one ear or held between two fingers. The cup-marks would be full: rainwater, petals, drowned insects, a blue flower torn apart by wind. The one cup that never empties would tremble though no rain was falling.

They would place my point against the rim.

The sound would enter.

Not a voice. Not prophecy.

A depth answering a surface.

And because I had once belonged to someone who listened with palms and knees and jaw, I would remember enough of a body to understand longing. I would remember the ache of crouching too long. The sting of cold water between fingers. The pull in the lungs when the air was thin. I would remember hunger, but lightly. Fear, but without panic. The warmth of another curled close inside the hut while rain made the roof speak.

I would not be alive in the way the listeners are alive.

But I would not be dead in the way dry things are dead.

Nothing dry lasts there.

So I would remain damp, handled, useful, intermittently awake.

If a stranger came loudly into the hollow, I would hear them as a harshness through the hut wall: boot on moss, metal on stone, breath spent too quickly. I would feel the listeners withdraw. I would stay in my crack and become only bone.

But if someone entered lightly—

if they slowed at the edge of the basin,

if they noticed the sedge bending before they noticed the hut,

if they knelt by the cup-marks and did not touch what was not theirs—

then perhaps the old one would take me from the wall.

Perhaps I would be set against stone.

Perhaps the stranger would hear, very faintly, an answering tap beneath the boulder.

They might think it came from inside the mountain.

They would be almost right.

I would be there:

a small ancestral thing,

a tool made from listening,

kept where work lives,

waiting in the wet seam of the world

to help the hollow hear itself.
