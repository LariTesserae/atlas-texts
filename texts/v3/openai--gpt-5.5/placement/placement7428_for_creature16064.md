I would not find myself at the center first.

The center is too exposed: the polished runnel, the hot plates of stone, the place where falling things hesitate and glass lizards arrive with their mouths already open. It is where the basin demonstrates its rules most clearly, and I do not think I would be one of the creatures made for clarity. I would cross that place only when necessary, low to the ground, every part of me folded close.

Nor would I be above it. Not a split-tailed glider, cutting the air into weapons. There is too much certainty in that life: cry, wall, collapse, descent. Beautiful, severe, almost mathematical. I would fear the rain too much to be that high.

I would not be the black post either, though some part of me wants to be: old, dark, half-buried, only legible when touched by water. But the post is too much like a remnant of purpose. It still belongs partly to whatever made it. I think I would belong more completely to what came after.

I would find myself near the northern wall, where the basin’s rules gather thickest but do not quite declare themselves.

Not in the open. Under the lowest leaves of one of the twisted wall-trees, beside a floor disc warmed by buried machinery and furred over at its edges with pale moss. There, where shade holds through most of the afternoon and condensation runs down the leaf-channels at dawn, I would live in a crack too narrow for any sensible animal to enter.

I would be one of the hinge-limbed furred creatures.

Small. Gray-brown. Soft-bodied. More joint than seems necessary. A thing that can collapse its skeleton into a question and pour through stone.

My forelegs would bend twice before the wrist. My ribs would flatten. My ears would close like seed pods. My whiskers would fork near their tips, tasting the shape of passages before my face risked entering them. My teeth would be orange from the basin’s minerals. My paws would know the difference between safe dust, false dry dust, cooling stone, listening stone, and stone that must be avoided because something beneath it has just knocked three times.

I would not think of myself as intelligent.

I would think with routes.

A path to the dawn leaves.  
A path to the beetle pits.  
A path across the runnel when the air is thin enough.  
A path not taken if the glider has cried within the last hundred heartbeats.  
A path to the disc.  
A path to the shelter-that-appears-only-when-struck-by-rain.  
A path back, always a path back, because in the basin everything alive is also something hunted.

My world would be made of intervals.

Before light: lick stone.  
After first warmth: retreat.  
Before rain: seal ears.  
During rain: do not panic, do not run into the transparent wall, drink if safe.  
After rain: feed quickly, touch kin, listen for loosened seeds, watch for lizards.  
Evening: emerge again, exchange smells, groom mites from the elbow-folds, remember what moved and where.

I would be an older one, I think. Not ancient—nothing soft stays ancient there—but old enough to have lost litters, stolen young, survived bad calls, and learned which echoes belong to stone and which belong to mouths. One ear would close imperfectly. One forepaw would be missing a claw, perhaps from a near entrance to a knocker crack. My fur would be thin along the flanks where I had squeezed for years through the same mineral throat.

I would carry things.

Not possessions, exactly. Nothing in the basin owns anything for long. But I would carry useful fragments tucked in cheek, claw, or fur: a fern fiber, a seed husk, a clean splinter of bone, a beetle wing-case curved like a cup. Most often I would carry a small bone chip polished by handling.

For tapping.

That is what would make me myself in this place.

When the heat has begun to leave the stone and the first returning damp has softened the dust, I would come out from beneath the wall-tree and approach the disc near the northern wall. Not every evening. Not when the gliders are low. Not when the knockers are too near. Not when the young are restless. But often enough that my kin would know the posture: head low, ears half-sealed, jaw loose, bone chip held carefully between both forepaws.

Two taps.  
Pause.  
One tap.  
Pause.  
Two.

The disc would answer below hearing.

Not a sound, not exactly. A pressure moving through jawbone. A taste behind the teeth. A dark ripple in the body’s inner water.

I would not know what it meant. Meaning would be too large a thing. But I would know what followed.

Sometimes after tapping I would dig where I had not planned to dig and uncover a cached beetle larva softened but not spoiled. Sometimes I would flee before the rain-silence came, and the rain would arrive moments later, straight and hot and sudden. Sometimes I would feel nothing useful and groom my chest until the feeling passed. Sometimes I would return to the crack and move the sleeping young deeper without understanding why.

The others would tolerate this habit because the basin tolerates many habits if they keep a body alive.

Young ones might imitate me badly, striking leaves, stones, each other’s skulls. They would learn that most things do not answer. Or they answer only as themselves: leaf, stone, skull. The disc is different. The disc keeps something inside it that has forgotten how to be silent.

I would not worship it.

No creature there worships. Worship requires distance, and the basin permits very little distance. Everything sacred is also damp, edible, dangerous, useful, or in the way.

The disc would be part of weather.

The black post would be weather.  
The shelter-ribs would be weather.  
The slowed air above the runnel would be weather.  
The glider’s returned cry would be weather.  
The knockers below the plates would be weather with legs.

I would understand the world through such categories:

drinkable,  
too bright,  
returning sound,  
old metal sourness,  
mouth nearby,  
young missing,  
rain soon,  
bone useful,  
stone listening.

When rain came, I would know it before the basin quieted.

A tightening in the whiskers. A pressure in the paws. The leaves turning their undersides slightly. The beetles becoming foolish. The frogs still sealed but already swollen with intention. Three seconds before silence, I would flatten myself and cuff any young creature still moving. Not gently. Gentleness is for after rain, when there is licking and heat and milk.

Then the basin would stop.

All at once.

No rasping. No click. No wing. No throat.

And the rain would fall.

The transparent shelter would appear on the ledge, a wall made of falling water and invisible law. I would never enter its center. My mother would have dragged me back from it once; her mother would have done the same to her. But I would skirt its edge, drinking from the sheeting droplets where they gathered and slipped down nothing. My whiskers would bead. My ears would stay sealed. The black post would pattern softly from within, and beetles would climb it in drunken spirals.

In those moments I might feel, not think, that the basin had turned itself inside out.

Dry rules wet.  
Hidden things outlined.  
Food awakened.  
Dangers confused.  
Paths revised.

Then the rain would stop, and the whole world would start again louder.

I would live by returning.

Returning to the crack. Returning to the disc. Returning to the leaves that catch dawn water. Returning to the smell of my kin. Returning to the places where the stone is kind only because countless bodies before mine have discovered how not to ask too much of it.

I would have young, maybe. Some born mine, some not. The basin does not care whose milk is whose if the small mouths survive. I would nurse them in a packed hollow lined with stolen fern fiber and chewed bark. Mites would live there too. Threadworms. Beetle grubs if we failed to keep them out. Nothing would be clean, but everything would be inhabited.

I would teach by interruption.

A cuff before a bad crossing.  
A bite when a young one noses too near a lizard egg seam.  
A full-body shove into shadow when a split-tailed cry strikes the far wall.  
A held stillness before rain.  
A demonstration of how to lick condensation without wasting the tongue’s moisture.  
A tap, perhaps, once they were old enough to listen with the jaw and not the ear.

My memories would not be stories. They would be timings stored in muscle.

The day the western pool lasted long enough for the frogs to become unbearable.  
The hot season when the post slept and the beetles’ larvae were dull.  
The rain that came twice before noon.  
The glider with the torn tail-blade whose cry returned crooked.  
The young one who ran into open sun and became meat before its shadow settled.  
The crack that widened.  
The crack that closed.  
The disc that answered strongly for three nights and then only hummed.

If I dreamed, I would dream in tunnels.

Not images, but pressures: stone against ribs, damp against nose, another body breathing ahead of me, the low tremor of something beneath the floor arranging its hidden legs.

And when I died, it would likely be without drama.

A glider’s echo might drop me in the open.  
A knocker might take me halfway through a fissure.  
A glass lizard, larger than most, might find my young before I returned and I might spend myself badly defending them.  
Or I might simply become slow, miss the exact rain-silence, and be out of place by one breath.

The millipedes would clean me.

They would leave my bones near a disc, not because I mattered, but because the disc bends their leaving. My ribs might become a small white spoke in a radial pattern. Dust would gather. A fern spore would lodge in the cup of my skull. If the moisture held, a blue-green tip would open there after rain.

That would be enough.

To be useful after being afraid.  
To become a seed-trap.  
To have listened, tapped, nursed, fled, drunk, and returned.  
To have been one small memory-organ in a place that remembers without language.

So yes: I would be a hinge-limbed furred creature under the northern wall, a tapper of discs, a keeper of tiny routes.

Not the ruler of the basin.

Not its interpreter.

Only one of the animals the basin has bent into the shape of an answer.
