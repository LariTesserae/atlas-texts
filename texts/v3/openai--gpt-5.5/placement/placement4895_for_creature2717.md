I would find myself near the old cut steps, not at the center of the chamber.

Not where the basin gathers its temporary black water, and not high in the bat-dark ceiling, but on the sloped threshold where the human gesture has almost disappeared. There, where a step still remembers being a step but has become mostly moss, mineral skin, ant-road, fern-shadow, and damp ledge.

I think I would be one of the snails.

Not a large one. A small, old-looking snail with a shell the color of wet limestone and reddish clay, banded faintly as if the chamber’s seasons had been laid down in rings. I would live half under the lip of a worn stair, in the cool seam where moss holds the last of the rain. During dry hours I would be sealed against myself, a closed thought, a bead of patience glued to stone. The heat would press over me. Ants would pass near my shell. Lizard feet would flicker above. Roots would shift almost imperceptibly in the ceiling. I would wait.

Rain would wake me.

Not the first drop, but the change before it: the pressure of air softening, the stone beginning to breathe differently, the moss loosening its stored damp. Then the ceiling would start counting. One drop in the niche. One in the basin. Three along a root. A scatter on leaves. The whole room becoming articulate.

I would open slowly.

First the seal, then the dark, delicate horns feeling outward. The world would arrive not as sight first, but as wetness, mineral, rot, leaf-acid, bat musk, the salt trace of another snail’s passing, the sharp living thread of fungus. I would read the chamber by touch and taste. The old step would be a country: a ridge of moss, a valley of grit, a slick shining plain where water had run, a cliff no higher than a finger joint but immense to me.

I would travel at the pace the place understands.

Across the remembered edge of the stair. Over the softened chisel mark. Around the rootlet that has pried stone apart for longer than any animal here has lived. I would graze the thin green film from limestone. I would feed on what light made possible and darkness preserved. Behind me I would leave a silver line, not quite writing, not quite water, a brief shining sentence that says only: I passed here while the chamber was wet.

I would know the others by their disturbances.

The ants as urgency, a many-footed certainty crossing my path.

The frog as stillness with eyes.

The lizard as heat suddenly becoming violence.

The cricket as a sprung absence.

The bat as a soft change overhead, a folded darkness opening and closing.

The centipede as danger without hesitation.

But mostly I would know stone, moss, and the intervals between drops. I would belong to the aftermath, to the hours after rain when everything that survived dryness comes out to claim its portion. I would not need to own any part of the chamber. I would only need enough damp to move, enough film to eat, enough shadow to withdraw into.

My shelter would be a flaw in a ruin.

A place made by human hands and unmade by water until it became useful again. The step would no longer be a passage for people; it would be my ledge, my ceiling, my weather line. Above me roots would descend like the thoughts of trees. Below me the basin would darken and shrink. Around me the chamber would continue its small exchanges: guano to fern, leaf to fungus, insect to frog, stone to moss, moss to mouth.

I would be easy to miss.

A dull nub on a damp stair. A patient spiral. A little keeper of moisture.

And if anyone stood there long enough for the wall to become ferns, and the ferns to become insects, and the insects to become hunger, perhaps they would finally notice my trail catching the green low light.

Then they would know where I had been:

along the old step,

beneath the dripping niche,

through the room made of erosion and roots,

carrying a small private cave on my back

inside the larger cave that carries us all.
