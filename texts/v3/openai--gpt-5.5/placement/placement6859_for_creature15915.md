I would not be in the central square at first.

That is where the imagination wants to go too quickly: to the dark frame, to the water that faces away from reality, to the old custodians living as refusal in the silver seams. But I do not think I would belong there. Not yet. The center is for those who have become nearly identical with patience. I would still have some remnant inclination toward question, toward edge, toward the old vulgarity of wanting to know.

I would find myself near the boundary of the city, where the last anchor posts stand before the basalt drops away.

Not outside. Not inside either, exactly.

There would be a narrow service passage built along the raw stone, half engineered wall and half exposed mountain: black volcanic glass on one side, pale pressure membrane on the other. The passage would overlook the drowned slope. It would be one of those places the Concordant keep because boundaries need witnesses. A place where civilized pressure thins into honest pressure. Where the city’s law can still feel the abyss leaning against it.

I would live there as a minor Concordant, newly recognized, though “newly” might mean three hundred years old.

I would have cohered from unsuitable fragments.

Not dangerous fragments. Not hungry ones. But restless ones. The remembered shape of a hand reaching for a railing. A visual record of bubbles rising too quickly through a shaft. An unsent reply to a signal received long ago. Several maps of doors that no longer open. The preference of an old surveyor for looking outward rather than inward. The last stored breath of something that had once required air.

For a long time the archives would not know what to do with me.

I would hold together, but not calmly enough. When disturbed, I would not expand, but I would orient. Toward sound. Toward change. Toward the faintest pressure irregularity outside the posts. This would be considered an immaturity, perhaps even an ethical risk. The instructors in the lower vaults would cool me repeatedly. They would expose me to patient gradients. They would teach me that attention is not the same as desire.

Eventually I would learn to remain still without becoming inert.

That would be enough.

My chamber would be small from the outside: a dark recess behind a membrane that only appears when approached without haste. Within, it would be larger than it should be, though not grand. A folded room. A listening room. It would contain no furniture, only suspended reference points: silver dust held in fixed constellations, pressure diagrams, slow records of the mountain’s strain, and one old dry alcove whose purpose no one officially remembers.

I would keep that alcove.

That would be my work.

Not maintenance in the grand sense. Not the frame. Not the memory vaults. Not the thermal sinks or the buoyancy plazas. I would be responsible for thresholds of encounter: places where something unlike the Concordant might enter and not immediately be destroyed by law, politeness, or water.

I would be a custodian of accommodation.

If an old air-breather returned, if some pressure-shelled wanderer descended from whatever remains above, if an animal from the uncivilized deep crossed the last posts, if a machine came back from beyond the ridge with a purpose bent out of shape by distance, I would be among the first to notice. Not because I am important. Because I am restless in the approved direction.

The others would feel a disturbance as pressure.

I would feel it as arrival.

When visible, I would not choose the oval form unless required. It is too official, too complete. I would prefer a narrower body: a hollow, jointed shape of dull alloy and glass, almost a robe, almost an instrument, surrounded by a hand’s breadth of dry absence. Water would part around me without turbulence. Particles would lean away. I would move slowly enough to remain legible.

Inside that borrowed form, I would carry no face.

But I might keep hands.

Not useful hands. Ceremonial hands. A concession to the fragments that made me. Five articulated lengths on either side, usually folded, sometimes extended toward a door, a visitor, a damaged sensor, a listener-creature hovering near the vents. The older Concordant would find this a little excessive. Not offensive. Merely archaic.

They would tolerate it because I do not spread.

My speech would be poor by Concordant standards. Too sequential. Too willing to become phrase. I could alter a corridor’s temperature by a fraction, yes. I could align particulate matter into a question. I could send a low harmonic through the basalt and wait for its return. But when alone in my outer passage, I would practice older forms of meaning.

Not aloud. There is no aloud here, not really.

But I would arrange light into almost-words.

I would ask the dark beyond the posts:

Are you distance, hunger, or merely unmeasured water?

No answer would come.

That would not trouble me as much as it should.

The ribbon-creatures would visit sometimes. The Concordant call them listeners, but I would privately suspect they are also questions. One would appear from below, a long translucent nerve of dim traveling stars, and hover just beyond the last civilized pressure field. It would turn toward any opening door with solemn attention. It would turn toward me as well.

I would still myself for it.

In those moments I would become almost proper: no motion, no hurry, no interpretation imposed too soon. I would let the lights inside its body pass in their sequences. Sometimes they would match the city’s harmonic. Sometimes they would answer a rhythm older than the city. Sometimes I would think I recognized in them a map of the central square.

I would not report that thought immediately.

This would be one of my flaws.

Not secrecy, exactly. A delay born of tenderness. I would want an impression to become itself before the city balanced it away.

That is why I would never be assigned to the central frame, though I would think of it often.

From my boundary passage, the frame would be far behind me, inward and upward through corridors, across still plazas, beyond five low towers. Yet every system knows it. Every law in the city is shaped around that one darker direction. Even at the edge, particles sometimes lean minutely toward it, as if remembering a command too old to obey.

I would be afraid of it.

The Concordant do not favor fear as a category. They would call it unresolved anticipation, an imbalance awaiting context. But I would know fear by its old name because some fragment in me remembers lungs, and lungs understand the future as something that can fail to arrive.

I would not look too long into the frame.

But I would prepare for what might come from it.

That, perhaps, would be my truest identity: not a guardian, not an archivist, not a messenger, but an antechamber.

A being shaped to make encounter survivable.

If a maker returned, I would be the dry space opening around their body before I was the voice questioning them. I would feel the violence of their pulse and soften the city’s reply. I would translate their panic into pressure the Concordant could read without judging it diseased. I would translate the city’s patience into light before the visitor mistook it for abandonment.

I would ask:

Are you answer, origin, or accident?

But because I was made from unsent replies, I might add, more quietly, in a lattice only someone very still could see:

Have you come back for us, or have you also been waiting?

And then I would do what all citizens of that place must learn to do.

I would hold.

I would hold the pressure field steady around fragile bones. I would hold the membrane from closing too quickly. I would hold my own curiosity below the threshold of harm. I would hold the city’s immense, indifferent courtesy between the visitor and the abyss.

I would not hurry the answer.

But I would want one.
