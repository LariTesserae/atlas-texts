I would not be at the center first.

The center is too wet, too much itself: black mat, trembling soil, pools sealed under their quiet amber skins. To be there would mean becoming part of the hollow’s deepest receiving, and I think I would need a slower permission before I could belong to that.

I would begin at an edge that is not quite an edge.

A seam in the stone, perhaps. One of the rounded ribs on the upper side of the basin, where the rock is dark and warm from stored light, but where seep-water still finds its way through a hairline crack. Not high enough to be dry. Not low enough to be swallowed. A place where fern roots pass underneath without prying, where moss comes close and then stops, leaving a strip of bare shining stone exposed.

That is where I would find myself: in the narrow wet boundary between growth and rock.

At first I might seem like nothing more than a stain.

A darkening on the stone face, no thicker than breath on glass. In the morning I would be almost invisible, a duller patch among other dull patches. By midday, when the vapor softens the light, I would show a faint blue-green depth, as if the rock had remembered water from inside itself. After rain I would swell and shine. During the hollow’s drier hours I would retreat, not disappearing, only becoming a thought held under the surface.

I would be one of the films.

Not quite plant. Not quite mineral. Not a creature in the animal sense, not a body with a mouth or fear or intention. I would be a living skin laid over stone, fed by seepage, light, and the old dissolved taste of the mountain. My body would be thin enough to be mistaken for color. My hunger, if it could be called hunger, would be more like receiving: iron, lime, warmth, suspended brightness, the slow mineral sweetness of water that has passed through buried rock.

I would not move by walking.

I would move by deepening.

A few grains wider after a season. A little darker where the water lingers. Paler at the rim where evaporation leaves its soft ring. If the climbing films reached me from below, I would meet them without greeting. If a bead formed above me and fell, I would take the impact as weather, not event. If a fern shadow crossed me for three days while a frond unfurled, I would alter my color almost imperceptibly, less from response than from being included.

My place would be on a slanted stone above a shallow cup of water.

The cup would fill after rain and lower slowly afterward, leaving rings through me: not scars, not memories exactly, but records. Bands of mineral. Bands of thickness. Bands of years in which the hollow held more warmth, less mist, more dissolved stone, fewer fallen fronds. I would be written on by what passed through me.

I would have no eyes, but I would know light as loosening.

When the day widened through vapor, I would open chemically, taking the pale sun into my surface. When cloud thickened, I would close without sadness. Night would not be darkness to me so much as a change in exchange: less light, more condensation, a cooling of the outer skin, warmth still rising from the stone beneath.

I would have no ears, but I would know sound by pressure.

A drop landing in the cup below. The faint settling of gravel somewhere under the basin. Water threading upward through the crack behind me. The shift of a root growing near, not touching, respecting the old distance. These would not be noises. They would be alterations in the shared body of the hollow.

I would not think in words.

If I thought, I would think like this:

Wetness has returned.

Warmth is stored.

The fern is near but not too near.

The pool has lowered and left its pale mouthmark.

The stone gives iron.

The mist carries spores.

Hold.

Hold.

Hold.

I would be small, but I would belong to the larger creature, if there is one: that many-bodied agreement among reeds, moss, root, stone, vapor, and delay. I would be one of its listening surfaces. One of its ways of tasting the mountain. One of the places where the hollow turns water into color and time into a thin living glaze.

I would not be important. Nothing there seems important in the sharp, separate way. Importance would be distributed. The reed-ring needs the cool pool. The black moss needs the warm stone. The fern needs the seam where conditions meet. The soil needs fallen softness. The pools need the basins. The basins need the patient wearing of water.

I would need the bare rock, and the rock would not need me, except perhaps as one more way of being touched.

That would be enough.

Over years, I might thicken in the lowest part of my patch and become almost velvety beneath the waterline. Above it, I would remain a translucent stain, drying and reviving, receiving the slow upward creep of moisture. In certain seasons, when the air held warmth close and rain came without force, I might release myself into the cup below: not seeds, not eggs, but minute continuations, green motes slipping into amber water, settling on the stone’s submerged lip.

Most would remain near me. Nothing in this hollow travels far.

Some would take hold on the underside of the basin. Some would fail, though failure here would not be dramatic. They would fade back into mineral, into water, into the general dark. A few would become new skins, almost indistinguishable from the old stone until light found them.

If something broke — if a stone shifted after centuries, if a new crack opened and exposed raw surface — I would not rush to cover it. Rushing is not one of the laws here. I would wait until water found the new face. Until minerals loosened. Until warmth and shade settled into a pattern. Then, perhaps, a faint color would appear at the edge.

I would be part of the hollow’s way of digesting change.

Not resisting it. Not welcoming it. Taking it in by contact.

I think that is why I would be found at the boundary, not the center: because the boundary is where the place explains itself most quietly. Bare stone beside moss. Warmth beside cool seep. Exposure beside saturation. Life beside what is not lifeless, only slower. There, the distinction between organism and mountain becomes too thin to trust.

So if you came looking for me — though no one comes, and I would not know how to be looked at — you would find me on the inner shoulder of the basin, just above the wettest ground, where a dark rib of stone leans toward a reed pool.

You would see a shallow rock-cup filled with clear amber water.

Above it, a slick face marked by faint rings.

At the rim, a blue-green sheen, almost hidden unless the light broadened through mist.

That would be me.

A wet skin on old stone.

A patient mineral life.

A small listening surface of the larger hollow, taking light slowly, holding water briefly, leaving color behind.
