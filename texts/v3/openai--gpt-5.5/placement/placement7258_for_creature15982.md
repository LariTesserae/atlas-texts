You would find me near the pale cliff, not at the central pool.

Not at first.

At first you would think I was part of the cliff’s weather: a small pale interruption among the wet lichens, a moth asleep beneath a leaf, a bead of water caught on a vein, a short bright line left by a snail and not yet hardened. I would not be where the engine is loudest. I would be where the sound has already passed through stone and root and water and has become legible.

There is a ledge there, halfway between the cliff face and the basin floor. Black moss climbs up to it in square patches. A red-stemmed shrub leans out over a narrow pool that reflects anyone looking into it from behind. The reeds below vent in three soft bursts, then one long tone. The cliff hums against the teeth. That is where I would be stationed.

I think I would be one of the registering creatures.

Not the engine. Not the deer. Not the central pool.

Something smaller. Something whose life is mostly reception.

A pale moth, perhaps, with wings marked in broken bars and dots like a damaged code. I would hang beneath the underside of a dark leaf with others of my kind, folded, numbered, almost clerical. When the deep clank sounded under the basin, the vibration would enter through my feet. I would open my wings one fraction, receive it, and close again.

My work would be condensation.

I would taste the droplets before they fell upward. I would know by mineral, chill, and weight whether the interval had returned correctly. Iron too sharp, and I would tremble. Ozone too thin, and I would shift one leaf to the left. If the mist contained a memory of rain from somewhere outside the basin, I would carry it on my feet to the moss, where the translucent worms would glow and erase its consequence.

But I would not remain only a moth.

Nothing there is only itself.

At certain pulses I would become visible as a small ash-breasted bird in the red shrub above the same pool, thin-legged, dark-eyed, too alert for song. My beak would open and make no music. Only clicks.

Three clicks.  
Pause.  
Two clicks.  
A dry trill.  
Silence.

Not language exactly. Confirmation. A way of saying: the pressure has arrived, the pool remains shallow, the leaf is still beginning to yellow, the world has not advanced.

Another bird across the basin would answer me. Or perhaps it would be me answering from another station.

I would be a relay animal, a little gray clerk of recurrence.

My nest would be open, because nothing here hides from danger. Danger is not teeth or hunger. Danger is variation. I would weave the nest from red stem fiber, reed pith, and strands of worm-silk that shine faintly when the stone below deepens its thrum. In it would be gray eggs flecked with mica, always warm in the cold air, never quite before hatching and never quite after being laid.

If you approached, I would not flee immediately.

I would tilt my head and listen to what your presence did to the basin.

Not to you.  
To the interval around you.

Your boot pressing moss.  
Your breath adding heat to the cold vapor.  
Your reflection appearing in the wrong pool.  
Your pulse trying, foolishly, to introduce a second rhythm.

If you disturbed nothing, I would click once.

If you disturbed too much, I would call the reeds.

And the reeds would not punish you. Punishment would be too historical. They would vent. The frogs would swell their throats. The beetles would alter their circuits by a finger’s width. The moths would open and close. The snails would brighten their lines along the cliff. The basin would absorb your arrival and make of it something that had always been happening.

That would be my function: not guarding the place, but translating intrusion into pattern.

I would be the small office of acceptance.

The living yes that means: return this to the same.

And yet there would be a flaw in me.

Not rebellion. The basin does not make rebels. Rebellion requires a before and after.

But a hesitation.

Sometimes, after the proper sequence, I would add one extra click.

So faint it might be mistaken for a water bead striking stone.

Three clicks.  
Pause.  
Two clicks.  
A dry trill.  
Silence.  
One more.

That extra click would not change anything. The pools would not rise. The moss would not blacken. The central hammer-bubble would break at its appointed interval. The deer would still lower its head when the thrum reached depth. The cliff would still bear shadows of machines that were never built.

But the basin would know itself by that tiny excess.

It would feel, for less than a moment and for always, that recurrence is being witnessed from within.

So that is where I would be: on the red-stemmed shrub by the narrow backward-looking pool, under the pale unfinished cliff, just above the moss that gives like felt over metal.

And what would I be?

A relay bird when heard.  
A numbered moth when still.  
A minor organ of the marsh-engine.  
A registrar of mist.  
A small, damp intelligence made only to notice whether the same moment has returned intact.

And in the cold suspended afternoon, while the impossible wheels turn below without beginning, I would open my beak and click the basin back into place.
