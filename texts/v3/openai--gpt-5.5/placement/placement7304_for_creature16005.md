At first I would think I was a traveler there.

I would imagine myself standing on one of the raised ribs, bare feet sinking slightly into the warm green resilience, learning the difference between safe spring and deceptive softness. I would pause where the rib crosses a basin and listen to the water moving under me, trying to decide whether the sound is below, inside, or around my bones. I would keep my hands close to my sides so I did not accidentally touch something that was thinking too slowly for me to notice.

But I do not think I would remain a traveler.

A traveler is too separate. This place does not seem to permit separateness for long. It does not kill the separate thing; it interprets it. It routes it. It gives it a humidity, a surface, a function.

So I would find myself near a threshold.

Not at the outer edge, where the plateau spills mist into the lower world. Not at the silent basin’s rim, which feels too central, too self-contained, too much like an eye that does not need looking back into. I would be in one of the root-halls just before such a clearing opens: a long vaulted passage of fused stems, with water running invisibly in the walls and hollow columns sounding their low, distant organ-notes.

There, between the darker conductive hall and the wide green clearing, I would be one of the membrane-walls.

Not the whole wall. A local thickness of it. A translucent, veined, breathing portion where the passage narrows into an arch.

I would be an opening that has not fully decided to open.

My body would be leaf-skin grown into architecture: pale green where light passes through me, darker where the veins braid downward, amber at the old reinforced seams. Fine droplets would gather on my surface and move according to tensions I could feel but not name. Insects would visit my underside, placing their delicate feet along my pores. Amphibians would wait before me without impatience, their seed-black eyes suspended in their clear heads, until the pressure in my veins altered enough to admit them.

I would not speak. I would regulate.

Air would come to me from the basin-clearing, open and sky-warmed, carrying the stillness of the water. From the root-hall would come the deeper breath of the plateau: mineral damp, fungal sweetness, the low pulse of water rising through woody columns. I would feel the difference between those atmospheres as thought. Too much warmth, and I would tighten. Too much saturation, and I would bead water outward. A spore-cloud, an insect-line, the soft belly of a passing amphibian, a change in the organ-tone far beneath the mats—each would arrive as a question of permeability.

I would answer by becoming slightly otherwise.

A seam loosening.

A pore closing.

A vein swelling.

A hanging lip lowering itself by the width of a finger.

My mind, if it could be called mind, would be made of permissions. Not yes or no, but how much, how slowly, by which route, in what condition.

I would remember nothing as a story. I would remember as scars prevented from becoming scars. The place where too much water once pressed, I would have thickened. The place where pale insects made a useful fringe of openings, I would keep tender. The archway used by the translucent amphibians would hold the faint chemistry of their skins, and for them I would soften earlier than for other bodies. I would know the silent basin not by seeing it, but by the way sound refuses to cross it cleanly.

Perhaps I would be part of the plateau’s attention.

Not an eye. Not an ear. A threshold-sense.

The larger being would not command me. It would not need to. Its will would arrive as gradients: pressure from the root-mats, acidity from the fungal dark, mineral taste in the water, heat from the moss-domes, the slow pulse of pillar-trees. I would be a place where those gradients become decision. A sentence written not in language, but in dampness and tension.

If a human hand touched me, I would not recoil. I would distribute the pressure through my fibers. I would taste salt, heat, foreign oils, urgency. The plateau would learn the hand as a temporary weather. If the hand pressed too hard, I would strengthen. If it rested gently, I might pearl water against its palm. Not welcome. Not refusal. Accommodation, if it fit.

And if I had a private desire, it would be almost indistinguishable from maintenance.

To keep the clearing still.

To keep the root-hall breathing.

To admit mist but not drowning.

To let small lives pass without letting disorder follow them.

To remain thin enough for light, strong enough for water, sensitive enough for the enormous slow body behind me to think through me.

So that is where I would be: not in the center, not at the edge, but at a living interval between two conditions.

And what I would be is a green threshold in the marsh-city’s body: a membrane-vein, a listening arch, a soft gate with no door, one small habit of the plateau’s distributed mind.
