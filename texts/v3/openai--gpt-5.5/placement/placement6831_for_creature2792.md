I would not find myself on the summit, though the summit would tempt me first.

At first I would imagine being one of the white birds, because they seem to have solved the roar by rising above any need to answer it. To live by tilt and angle, to speak in wing-flash and falling arcs, to let the wind hold me instead of fighting it—that would be beautiful. But it feels too clean, too high, too nearly free. The basin does not seem to make freedom. It makes treaties.

Then I would think of the pale cats, because they understand stillness. They are almost a thought the stone has about hunger. But there is too much sharpness in them, too much solitude arranged around one instant of violence.

The horned grazers come closer. They know the basin by repetition: seep, shelf, shadow, moss, return. They are memory with hooves. But they belong to the open slopes, and I think I would be less visible than that.

I would be lower.

You would find me in the talus below an old schist wall, where a dark basalt rib cuts down through the pale rock and breaks the wind just enough for dampness to gather. Not in a cave exactly, but under a leaning slab that has rested there so long it seems to have chosen the place. Above it, a thread of meltwater appears only in certain hours, not falling so much as making the rock shine. Below it, stones are locked in an ancient spill, with passages narrow enough for a body that knows how to flatten itself.

There I would be one of the small talus-dwellers.

Round-bodied, gray-furred, close to the color of frost on stone. Short-limbed, broad-footed, with black eyes and ears almost hidden in the fur. Not built for drama. Built for retaining heat. Built for knowing when not to move.

My home would be deeper than it first appears. The visible entrance would be only a dark comma between rocks, one of many false openings in the fan. Inside, the passage would turn twice, narrowing where a predator’s paw could not follow, then drop into a warmer chamber lined with dry grass, shed feathers, my own loosened fur, and the papery husks of insects. There would be others with me. Bodies in a heap. Sleep as shared warmth. Fear as common property.

The roar would not be noise to me. It would be the condition of existence, like light or gravity. I would not listen upward. I would listen downward.

My feet would know the grammar of stone.

A grazer passing far above: slow, hollow pulses through the slope.  
A loosened pebble: brief, meaningless tick.  
A pale cat shifting weight behind a boulder: almost nothing, and therefore everything.  
Rain mist crossing the basin: a changed pressure in the entrances.  
Other talus-dwellers drumming warning: urgency traveling through granite faster than thought.

I would live by small certainties.

At dawn, before the sun hardens the day into glare, I would come out and drink from beads collected along the underside of a flat stone. I would know which lichens can be scraped without poisoning the mouth, which moss-dark cracks hold enough damp to matter, which cushion plants can be nibbled at the rim and left alive. I would harvest dry stems when the wind lays them against rocks. I would drag feathers underground with absurd seriousness. A single feather would be wealth.

I would not sing. I would not need to.

If alarmed, I would strike stone with my hind feet: once for shadow, twice for movement, a rapid shiver for the pale cat. I would freeze when freezing saved me and vanish when vanishing was possible. My courage would not look like courage. It would look like exactness.

I would know the basin as a map of permissions:

This slab holds warmth after noon.  
This crack smells of snake-lizard and should be avoided at dusk.  
This pool appears after three cold nights of mist.  
This overhang sheds beads even when the open stone is dry.  
This ledge belongs to nesting birds in the bright season.  
This route is safe only when the grazers are above it, because predators watch them instead.

My world would be small, but not poor. A hoofprint pool could be a lake. A strip of moss could be pasture. A mica flash could be warning. The distance between two boulders could be a dangerous country crossed only after long study.

I think I would be afraid often, but not miserably. Fear there would be practical, clean-edged, without imagination wasted on what cannot be changed. The basin would not permit melodrama. It would teach a body to ask only: Where is shelter? Where is water? What moved? What warmed? What must be saved?

And perhaps, as I aged, I would become almost indistinguishable from the place that held me.

My fur would carry dust from granite and schist. My claws would dull on the same stone my ancestors used. My paths would be faint oils and pressed grit beneath overhangs. I would leave little behind: droppings in hidden chambers, gnawed stems, loosened lichen scales, warmth briefly stored in a nest under rock.

If a white bird passed over, I would become still.

If a grazer stamped above me, I would understand before I saw anything.

If the morning wetness came, I would emerge.

So: you would find me under stone, in the settled fan beneath a patient cliff, near a seep that is more memory than stream.

I would be a small gray talus animal, one of the basin’s low lives: a keeper of warmth, a reader of tremors, a collector of feathers, a creature whose language is drummed into rock and whose wisdom is to waste nothing beneath the everlasting roar.
