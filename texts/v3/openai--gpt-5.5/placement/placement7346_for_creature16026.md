You would find me where the room first tries to mean something.

Not in the middle — there is no middle here, only crowded passage and touched surface — but at the damp shelf beside the three leaned stones, where the row of mineral pellets crosses from one texture into another. I would be half inside a seam, half exposed to the cold bone-light, a pale blunt thing with no need of eyes.

At first you might mistake me for a larva.

Then for a fragment of root.

Then for a little mistake the cave has not yet corrected.

I would be one of the builders.

Not a builder of houses, because nothing here is outside enough to need shelter. Not a maker of tools, because every tool would become immediately another surface to be grazed, polished, inhabited. I would build only distinctions.

A line of chalk pellets no higher than a mite’s body.

A triangular lean of three flakes of stone.

A hollow touched and retouched until its rim remembers pressure.

I would spend my life at the edge between wet and almost-dry, arranging grains that the springtails cross without noticing. I would move a pellet from the bright side to the dimmer side, then back again, not because the line must hold anything out, but because the line must continue to be a line.

My body would be soft, colorless, faintly translucent. My forelimbs would be jointed and careful, always dusted in calcite. My mouth would know chalk, fungus felt, old chitin, the salt trace of a vanished droplet. I would have no story of arrival. I would have hatched already belonging.

The folded listeners would know me by vibration: the slow rasp of my forefeet, the tiny click of pellet against pellet. The mites would polish my work until I had to restore it. Beetles would pass over my boundaries as if crossing weather. Larvae in their glass beads would turn once, and the water would tremble, and I would pause until the room had settled back into itself.

I would not understand time as loss.

Only as maintenance.

A crack begins to clog; I open it.

A lip grows too smooth; I roughen it with grains.

A hollow loses its edge beneath fungal milk; I scrape the rim clean.

A dead beetle shell collapses; I take a shard and press it into the line, making the boundary firmer, whiter, more remembered.

I would be small enough that my whole labor could be erased by the weight of a single careless finger, if fingers ever came here. But no hand has touched this wall. No wind has entered to scatter what I place. The chamber keeps all gestures close. Nothing is lost quickly enough to be gone.

If I had an intelligence, it would not rise above me. It would remain in the work: in the difference between this side and that side, between basin and ridge, between droplet and stone, between the chamber as geology and the chamber as world.

I would not rule the place. I would not name it.

I would tend the first grammar.

A pellet, a pause, a rim, a hollow.

Again.

Again.

In the buried brightness, I would be the creature that keeps the cave from becoming merely stone.
