I would not be found in the middle of the room.

The middle is too declarative. Too exposed. Anything there is either passing through, circling the well, or about to be interpreted by every mouth in the chamber. I would not be one of the broad-mouthed waiters, though I understand their patience. I would not be a fish in a school, because a school requires a kind of clean agreement I do not trust myself to keep. I would not live in the central well. The well belongs to things that have accepted depth as a body.

I would be higher.

Not in the shaft where the cold blue almost happens, not among the crabs who tend the ringwork, but near them: in one of the upper wall bells, one of the hollow stone vessels set lip-down into the masonry.

You would find me inside the least-sounded bell.

Not the bell itself, exactly. Not the trapped air alone. Not the stone. I would be the skin between them.

A thin, living lining clinging to the inner curve of the hollow vessel, where old air presses downward and warm black water presses upward. I would be a meniscus creature, a soft cup turned inside the cup, translucent as boiled cartilage, mineral-grained along the back, with a fringe of almost invisible hairs trailing into the water below. My upper surface would touch air that has no right to remain. My lower surface would taste the whole drowned chamber.

I would have no eyes. Eyes would be vulgar there, too convinced of distance.

I would listen with tension.

Every pressure change would pass through me: the school of blind fish turning below; an eel threading a grate; the slow contraction of a niche-mouth; the single mineral bubble shouldering its way out of the well and breaking against a lintel; the almost-fall of a crab above the central frame; the far settlement of a block in a sealed room no one enters. These would not be sounds, not yet. They would be shapes in my membrane. I would know them as stretch, release, tremor, flavor.

My body would be shallow but wide. It would not swim. It would not hunt. It would feed on what gathers at boundaries: vapor-salt, bacterial haze, the faint residue of bubbles, the dead glow of bell-creatures that drift too near and dissolve into threads. I would comb the water with cilia too small for ceremony. I would thicken by less than a thought each season.

The other creatures would avoid me without naming me.

The bell-creatures would pulse near the trapped air but never come close enough to brush my fringe. Eels would curve away from the rim as if remembering a pain from before their bones were made. Fish would leave a hollow beneath me in their routes. Crabs, crossing the high stone rods, would pause with one leg lifted when they passed near my bell, then proceed more carefully than before.

Not because I am dangerous in the ordinary way.

Because I am attached to consequence.

If something touches the bell lightly—a drifting shell, a loose tile chip, the tail of a careless eel—I would take the contact into myself first. I would spread, slacken, absorb. Most accidents would die in me. I would be a hush-organ, a living felt grown over an old instrument.

But not all.

Some touches are too large. Some tremors belong to the deeper law. When the water tastes of iron and something rises from the well with too many joints; when a crack opens and fever-water clouds the seam-films; when a stone far above shifts and releases a thread of storm-cold so sharp that every animal gathers beneath it; when the chamber needs to become one body and still itself—

then I would tighten.

I would pull away from the inner wall by the width of a larva’s spine. I would let stone speak to air.

The bell would sound.

One note, low and rounded, entering every shell and jaw and egg as command. The crabs would freeze in the ringwork. The blind fish would become suspended carvings. The coin-grazers would flatten themselves until they seemed never to have been alive. The waiters would close their sieve-mouths. Even the well would seem to hold its breath.

And I, inside the bell, would be bruised by my own permission.

That would be my place in the civilization: not ruler, not priest, not watcher, but threshold. The difference between accident and omen. The soft restraint grown over an old handmade warning.

I think I would have been born from something much smaller. A bell-creature perhaps, one whose ancestor failed at avoidance and touched the rim but did not die quickly. Or a seam-film that found the air-water boundary richer than stone. Or a colony of many things agreeing so slowly that it became one animal. My earliest form would have been a stain. A slickness. A trembling at the lip of the hollow stone.

Over generations, the bell would shape me. My body would learn its curve. My fringe would learn the distance to danger. My back would carry grains of lime and copper-black bacterial dust. I would become the inside weather of one ancient vessel.

I would know the builders only as contour.

A thumb groove in the bell’s inner wall would be a valley of safety where my young gather. A chip on the rim would be a canyon that amplifies tremor. Smoke sealed under mineral glaze would be a bitter ancestral taste. The bell’s maker, whoever they were, would survive for me only as pressure geometry: the hand that made a hollow where a living hush could later grow.

I would reproduce reluctantly.

Once in many warm cycles, when the trapped air swells after rain has reached some unseen upper passage, small blisters would rise from my inner surface. They would hang like clear seeds along the stone curve. Then, one by one, they would loosen and fall into the water as tiny cups, each with a fringe and a grain of stored air caught inside it.

Most would be eaten before they understood direction.

A fish would take one as drifting food. A crab might pick another from a rod. An eel would swallow one whole and carry its possibility through the grilles into darkness. A few would rise, by accident or design, toward other bells. Fewer still would find an empty inner curve where air remained and no older lining held the place.

My children would not know me. They would know tension, salt, stone, restraint.

If my bell lost its air, I would change quickly. The meniscus would flatten. My upper skin would drown. I would loosen from the stone in pale sheets, descending through the chamber like torn silence. Fish would eat me. Grazers would plane me from the wall. Eels would take what lodged in the rim. The bell would become mute forever, not because the stone had forgotten sound, but because the breath that allowed it had gone.

Until then, I would remain.

High in the warm dark, just below the ceiling, inside a hollow stone pot no one strikes unless the world requires it.

I would be a bell-breath.

A listening membrane.

A small authority made of softness.

The chamber’s withheld note.
