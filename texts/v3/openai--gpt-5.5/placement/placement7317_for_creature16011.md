I would find myself close to the wall where the water does not quite fall.

Not in the open center of the hollow, not among the three low stone alignments where a human eye might first look for meaning, but along the pale limestone face where a narrow stain descends from some hidden seam. I would be there in the margin between dry stone and remembered wetness, where the rock darkens by a shade, where the green-black film holds itself flat and secret, where one drop gathers for a long time before letting go.

At first I might think I was the drop.

A small weight of water, swelling slowly from the stone, holding the gray light upside down. I would know the hollow only as a curve reflected in myself: the crescent ledge, the clay bank, the sparse grasses standing like old script. Then I would fall, make the one clear sound the place allows, and disappear into the basin below.

But that would be too brief a life for this place.

So I would not be the drop. I would be what waits for it.

I would be the darkened skin on the limestone, the almost-life that becomes life when touched. A thin congregation of lichen, algae, moss-thread, mineral hunger, and sleeping green. I would have no single body to lift or turn. I would not move across the hollow. I would spread by accepting surfaces.

My home would be the shaded seam, the damp rim of the stone pan, the underside of a flake of limestone where dust has collected and stayed. Part of me would be gray and sealed shut, brittle enough to be mistaken for old weather. Part of me would be black-green and softening, drinking from the slow seep. Part of me would hold to the clay between cracks, binding grains together with threads finer than thought. Part of me would sleep under the shrub, where one fallen leaf becomes food only after a long negotiation with dryness.

I would not have eyes, but I would know light by what it takes from me.

I would know morning as a lessening of coolness. I would know noon as endurance. I would know evening as the brief mercy when the stone gives back stored warmth without demanding so much in return. I would know cloud as permission. I would know rain as a large event, though to anything larger it would seem almost nothing.

When rain came, I would not rejoice quickly. I would loosen.

My folded cells would open. My colors would return from their hiding places. The moss in me would lift by a hair’s breadth. The algae would green inside their dark sheath. The old rings around the stone pans would swell and deepen, each one remembering another season when water paused here. I would drink only what the hollow offered. I would not waste myself in growth that could not be kept.

When the water left, I would close again.

That would be my wisdom: not despairing when dry, not becoming foolish when wet.

The grasses would be my taller thoughts. Sparse, wiry, almost reluctant, they would rise from cracks where I had helped hold a little silt in place. Their pale tips would speak of loss; their green bases of continuance. The shrub would be my elder relative, angular and exact, having learned that survival here means refusing extravagance.

The stone alignments near the center would be part of my body too, though not because I made them. Their shaded sides would keep me longer. Their corners would catch dust for me. Their low walls, whether made by hands or fracture, would have become ribs in my shallow anatomy. I would not remember who placed them. I would only remember how moisture gathers differently beside them.

I would be ancient, but not impressive.

No one would point to me and say, there it is. They might say the rock is stained, the basin is dark, the clay is crusted, the moss is thin. They would divide me into names because that is how quicker lives understand slower ones. But I would be the relation between those names. I would be the agreement: stone allowing life to cling, life agreeing not to ask too much of stone.

I would have no voice except the click of a drop, the hush of wind arriving diminished from the ledge, the faint tightening of clay as it dries. I would have no desire to leave. Leaving would mean becoming dust too soon, and even dust here does not travel far.

If I had a mind, it would be a map of tolerances.

How long I can sleep dry.  
How much light I can bear.  
Which crack holds coolness.  
Which pan keeps water one hour longer.  
Which season was generous enough to thicken me by the width of a breath.  

I would not hope in the way animals hope. I would wait in the way stone waits, but with a hidden greenness inside the waiting.

So in this world, I would be found almost nowhere at first glance.

Then everywhere the hollow is slightly darker.

I would be the low, living skin of the place: a cryptic crust, a lichenous memory, a many-bodied patience laid over limestone and clay. Not a creature in the hollow, exactly, but the hollow’s own slow consent to be alive.
