I would not be found at the center of the plateau.

There is no center, not really. Only distance, weather, old stone under living skin, basins that fill and vanish, paths that remember feet only briefly before closing. So I think I would be found at an edge: not a cliff-edge, but one of the useful, humble edges the people trust.

A raised rib of stone beneath the mats.  
A dry lip above a shallow basin.  
A place where the ground changes its mind.

At first I want to be one of the aerial hunters, because the open sky invites that answer. To circle above everything, to read the plateau by shadow and movement, to see the herds as brown currents and the insect swarms as smoke—that would be a clean life, a high life. But this world distrusts height. Height is hunger, exposure, lightning, pride. I would not last long as something that belongs wholly to the air.

Then I think I might be one of the flat predators, breathing so slowly that beetles walk across my spine, my hide mottled like wet lichen and violet mat. There is an appeal in that patience: the body made almost into terrain, the strike held for hours inside the muscles. But no. That is too pure a purpose. Too much of the self narrowed into waiting and hunger.

Nor am I one of the great grazers, though I admire them most: those heavy, kneeling beasts that open wounds in the world and are forgiven because the wounds feed everything. They are destroyers that make room. In a place that seals itself, they are necessary violence. But I am not built broadly enough for that. I do not think I would be a maker of gaps.

I would be smaller.

I would be one of the people, but only barely more separate from the ground than the insects and root-hairs are. A path-keeper, perhaps. A listener of surfaces. Someone who walks between occupied shelters and swallowed ones, carrying messages, seed, copper needles wrapped in skin, stories of which basin filled and which did not.

You would find me after rain, before the herds arrive fully.

That is when my work matters.

I would be walking a stone rib with a testing pole in my right hand and a coil of vine-fiber rope across my chest. My feet would know the difference between safe-soft and false-soft, between a mat that will spring and a mat that will open. I would move with my weight always half-withheld, never trusting the ground simply because it looks whole. My calves would be hard. My shoulders sun-dark. My hands cut and scarred from thorn-felt, scraper work, insect bites, and the fine slicing hairs of condensation leaves.

I would know the plateau through contact.

Not by maps. Maps would be almost insulting here. What map could keep up with a path that closes, a hollow that swells, a safe crossing that becomes a mouth after one night of rain?

I would know by pole-strike, smell, and sound.

A dull knock: stone close underneath.  
A wet sigh: bladder growth below the mat.  
A stretched hum: do not step there, not today.  
A faint peppery smell: ants have opened their towers.  
A green-sour breath: sweet water in the basin plants.  
A copper taste on the wind: storm somewhere beyond sight.

I would belong to a low settlement on a knob of dark stone, the kind that disappears into the land until smoke betrays it. Perhaps six shelters, perhaps nine, never many. Roofs strapped down with flat stones. Drying racks. Children kept close until they learn the testing songs. Old people who sit with their backs against warm rock and correct everyone’s memory.

Our home would not be permanent in the way outsiders mean. It would be returned to often, repaired often, abandoned sometimes. One shelter might be deliberately left to the mats after a death or after a season of sickness. Not as defeat. As repayment.

I think my people would have a word for a house that is still ours though the plants have entered it.

Not ruin.  
Not abandonment.  
Something closer to resting.

I would be the one sent to such places.

To see whether the old shelter has vanished. To cut back only what must be cut. To check whether the cairn still shows above the creeping growth. To retrieve a hidden vessel from under a stone slab, if the roots have not found it. To leave seedcakes in a dry niche for travelers who may come after us. To listen at the basin edges for frog-calls, because their voices tell us how long the water may last.

I would carry a short stone blade, not for fighting first, but for opening stems, trimming fiber, cutting leeches from ankles, shaving resin, preparing food, scraping old marks into visibility. I would have a bone awl tied into my hair or hanging at my neck. Maybe one precious thing of metal: a green-stained copper hook inherited from someone who went down to the lower lands and came back thinner, quieter, with fever scars and impossible stories of trees.

I would not be a chief. The plateau does not encourage chiefs. Too much standing above others. Too much voice raised into wind.

I would be useful instead.

The person who remembers routes.  
The person who knows when not to cross.  
The person children are told to follow exactly.  
The person who can look at a shining basin after storm and say, “Two days of water. Maybe three if the wind turns.”  
The person who can smell a carcass before the scavenger birds begin to fall.

But I would also be a little strange among my own.

Because I would love the swallowed places too much.

The others would value clear paths, scraped ground, reliable stone. I would linger where the mats were closing over abandoned walls, where roots threaded doorways and the house became less house and more hill. I would watch the green hairs take a bone. I would be comforted by the speed with which separation ends.

I might be asked to tend the dead.

Not a priest exactly. Not a shaman in any grand sense. More like someone with the patience to sit beside a body until the plateau accepts it.

I would help lay the dead on prepared mat, stones around them to keep the night diggers from taking too much too soon. I would paint the forehead with red mineral dust. I would speak the names of wetness: condensation, seep, storm-water, under-water, blood-water, breath-water. I would wait until the first root-hairs touched the skin. That would be the moment of release.

Later, when insects had done their bright, precise work, I might return for selected bones. Not all. Never all. Some must stay. A finger bone for a child. A painted jaw set in a low house. A thigh bone placed beneath a route marker, so the dead continue to know the paths.

And still, most of what I am would be practical.

I would wake before dawn, when condensation beads along every hair and thorn. I would go out with a scraper and collect water from leaves into a bladder bag, patient drop by patient drop. I would check the root curtains under ledges, combing them gently for moisture and trapped insect dust. I would gather glass-winged gnats from nets while they still hang stunned in morning cool. I would strip safe bladders after rain and avoid the bitter ones that twist the gut.

At noon I would crouch in shade, if shade can be called shade when it is only a low roof and a darker side of stone. I would repair nets. Pick mites from a child’s scalp. Listen to elders argue over whether the bird-wind has changed. Eat seedcake softened with sour plant water.

In the afternoon I would walk again.

Not far by the standards of flying things, but far by the standards of feet that must negotiate a living floor. Out to the ringing hollow, perhaps, where one step sends a tone under the skin of the land and insects rise all at once. I would not step there unless I had to. But I would visit its edge, because the tone changes after rain, and if you know the change, you can tell whether the hollow beneath is full, empty, or dangerous.

At sunset I would stand very still.

Everyone must, sometimes, in such openness. The land would go red and gold. Every low ridge would show itself. Insect smoke would lift from basins. Distant herd backs would move like a thought across the horizon. The shelters behind me would throw long shadows, trying briefly to be taller than they are.

I would feel then the old lesson of the plateau:

Do not rise too much.  
Do not hold too tightly.  
Do not imagine stillness means death.  
Everything returns by spreading.

If I had a name, it would not mean “eagle” or “hunter” or “strong one.”

It might mean something like Understep.

Or Basin-After-Rain.

Or She-Who-Hears-the-Mat.

But perhaps names here are not fixed either. Perhaps they change with work and age. As a child I might have been Thornfoot, because I was always bleeding. As a young walker, Long-Rib, because I learned the stone paths quickly. Later, when I began tending swallowed shelters and the dead, I might be called Green-Takes-It.

That is the name I like best.

Green-Takes-It.

Not as threat. As truth.

You would find me kneeling at the edge of a basin just after storm, with insects whining around my head, my pole laid across my knees, watching colored rings of plants swell where water stood in older years. I would be deciding whether to cross now or wait until morning. I would be listening to frogs call from water no deeper than a footprint. I would be smelling the difference between drinkable growth and poison. I would be small in the landscape and glad of it.

I would be human, yes.

But in that world, to be human would mean being only temporarily upright. It would mean knowing that every path is borrowed, every tool wants to become trellis, every shelter is a future mound, every body is a future green place.

I would live by walking lightly over a floor that is alive.

And when I died, I would not want to be kept from it.
