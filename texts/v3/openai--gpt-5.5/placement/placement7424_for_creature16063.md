You would find me where the basin almost becomes water but has not yet admitted it.

Not in the center of the pool. That is too open, too much like a sky turned downward. Not on the rim, where the grasses thin and the world begins to remember distance. I would be in the seam beside the pool, under the overhang of a dark stone shelf where the dripping is regular enough to become a kind of roof.

There, the mud is warm below its black skin. A fern root crosses the hollow like a wrist-bone. Liverwort makes a slick green lip around the entrance, and above it the rock sweats in threads. If you placed your hand there, you would feel nothing at first but wetness and the faint looseness of soil that has never been pressed firm. If you waited, you might feel the hollow answer your pulse.

That is where I would be.

At first I would not be animal. Or not noticeably. I would be a darkening under a leaf, a slightly wrong line in the moss, a piece of stone that kept the shape of breath. Water would bead on my back and remain there. A pale toe would be mistaken for a root-tip. My eye, if it opened, would open slowly enough that it seemed the mud itself had remembered how to look.

I would be one of the basin’s small listeners.

One of the seven, if there are seven.

Not the first hatched, not the youngest. Old enough to know the whole hollow without needing to cross it often. Old enough to have learned that the rim is not a boundary drawn in space but a change in mercy. Above it, the ground hardens. Sounds become thin. Warmth leaves quickly. Shadows pass with intent. Below it, in the held green wetness, every movement is softened before it reaches the body.

I would have hatched under a weeping rock, in one of the little mouths where water gathered itself into drops and let go. My egg would have been no more than a clear bead fixed to stone by some almost invisible jelly, clouded by minerals, hidden by moss that did not know it was sheltering me. I would have curled inside that bead like a comma in an unwritten sentence, listening before I had limbs, before I had hunger, before the world had separated into outside and body.

The first thing I knew would have been falling water.

Not as sound, exactly. As arrival. As small impacts repeating through the stone that held me. A drop striking near. A drop striking farther away. A drop failing to fall when it should have, swelling instead, gathering weight, then releasing with a heavier note. I would have known the basin first as rhythm, and only later as place.

When I broke from the egg, I would not have gone into the pool. The pool would have been too large, too black, too full of distances for a creature still made of transparency and tremor. I would have slipped instead into the soaked felt between moss and rock, into a world no thicker than a leaf-stem, where water was not a body to swim through but a condition of everything. There I would have fed on what did not yet have names: soft specks, films, pale moving threads, the sweetness gathered on stone.

For a long time, my whole country would have been the underside of one ledge.

A drop-root country. A mineral-warm country. A country of narrow passages where my skin brushed everything I knew.

Later, when my legs strengthened and my back darkened, I would learn the pool-edge. I would learn the good mud from the bad mud: the mud that closes over you like a second skin, and the mud that grips too long; the mud warmed from below, and the mud chilled by water freshly arrived through stone. I would learn the hollow under the fern crown, the safe crease behind the quartz-veined slab, the shallow slick where larvae gather after wind shakes the sedges.

I would learn the falling of leaves.

That would matter. A leaf loosening from a shrub speaks before it falls. It twists against its stem, gives a faint complaint through the branch, pauses, then lets go. The shadow comes after the decision. A young one startles at the shadow. An older one knows to listen for the decision.

I would live mostly by my belly.

The world above would be a blur of green light, damp air, enormous brief disturbances. But the world below would be exact. Water pressing through a crack. Worms folding soil. Beetles tapping stems with their jointed feet. Roots thickening by almost nothing. The pool skin dimpling where a bubble rises from below. The heavy, foolish tremor of a visiting animal on the rim.

If you entered, I would know you before I saw you.

Not as “person.” That is too sharp a word for what you would be to me. You would be weight arriving through the basin’s edge. Heat held too high above the ground. A rhythm unlike weather. A pause-maker. A creature whose blood knocks in its feet and whose breath changes the ferns.

I would not hate you. I would not fear you in the bright, darting way of animals that believe in escape. I would measure you. I would let your tremor enter the mud and pass through me. I would wait for the basin to decide whether you belonged to rain, wind, bird, falling stone, or something drier and more temporary.

Most likely, you would be temporary.

Most things are, except water’s returning.

I would have a broad, low head and eyes set high, so that I could look while remaining almost buried. My skin would not shine unless light found me at an angle. It would hold the colors of things that do not ask to be noticed: old gray stone, blackened leaf, green soaked almost to brown, the pale scatter of lichen and quartz. Along my neck the soft fringes would stir when the air grew thick enough to drink. They would be less like gills than like small roots remembering water.

My mouth would open rarely. Hunger would not be a chase in me, but a widening.

I would take what the basin gives in small bodies. A worm nosing through warmth. A larva folded in moss. Eggs laid by something winged and careless under a leaf. Sometimes I would rise beneath a drifting insect and let the surface break around my head so gently that the pool would seem to have thought of rain from underneath. More often, I would graze along stone, drawing in the thin living sweetness that gathers where water keeps touching the same place.

I would not be noble. I would not be symbolic to myself.

I would be damp and slow and exact.

Yet, because the basin is the kind of place that makes meanings without announcing them, I might become symbolic to anything watching from outside. I might look like patience given a spine. Like the mountain’s smallest thought. Like a question asked by mud and answered by breath.

At night, I would come farther out.

Not every night. Only when warmth holds after dusk and the pool rises by a claw’s width. Only when the wind has passed elsewhere and the dripping has resumed its deep, many-voiced counting. Then I would leave my seam and cross the moss with my belly low, each foot placed as if the ground were a sleeping animal.

Others might be there.

One by the root-tangle. One half in the pool. One beneath the orchid wall, visible only when its throat moved. We would not gather like social creatures. We would simply arrive in the same condition, summoned by water high in the hidden cracks. We would lie near one another without touching. The pool would carry our small clicks into stone. Our throats would pulse. The basin, which speaks all day, would be answered from within its own wet floor.

If I were old enough, I would have laid eggs.

I would choose a stone mouth with a steady drop, not too hard, not too sparse. A place where water arrives gently and leaves without scouring. I would press myself under the lip of rock and fix the clear beads there, one by one, while the mountain murmured through my ribs. Then I would leave them. Not from indifference. From trust older than care. The basin keeps what can be kept.

Some would cloud and fail. Some would be eaten by mouths smaller than mine. Some would darken, curl, twitch, and become listeners.

Nothing here continues by abundance.

It continues by enough.

By morning I would be gone from view. Not absent, only returned to the grammar of the place. A curve under leaves. A pressure in mud. A wetness with eyes closed inside it.

If you came looking for me, you would have to stop looking for an animal.

You would have to let the basin become complete before I could appear. You would have to hear the drops not as background but as the main speech. You would have to notice that the loudest things are not always the nearest, that a stone can weep for years without being emptied, that warmth can live underground like a secret organ. You would have to become still enough that your own body stopped explaining itself.

Then perhaps a leaf would lift against the wind.

Perhaps a line of mud would loosen.

Perhaps two dark points would open in the wet seam beside the pool.

And there I would be: not a guardian, not a spirit, not a messenger.

A small highland seep-creature.

A soft reply.

The basin’s listening, given skin.
