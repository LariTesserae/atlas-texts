You would not find me above the ground.

At first you might look for a shape: a stem among stems, a hanging bladder, a dark frond slick with charge, something that could be circled and named. But I would not be standing there as one thing. I would be where several things fail to remain separate.

You would find me under a processing flat, near the inland edge of a thermal lowland, where the young continent has cracked and lifted a shallow comb of basalt through the marsh-skin. Not a dramatic place. No clearing. No landmark that could be seen in the dark. Only a slight firmness beneath the mats, a different warmth in the wet air, a slow inward slant of water.

Above me would be piston fields: ranks of black cylinders rising and settling through their patient centuries. Beyond them, columnar conduits would lift mineral seep into suspended reservoirs, and farther still, broad sheet-leaves would rain inward from their own condensation. The surface would appear continuous, another saturated expanse of working vegetation. But beneath it, in the pressure-dark, many systems would meet.

I would be there.

Not a plant exactly. Not a root, though root would pass through me. Not fungus, though decomposer films would live in my inner seams. Not a valve, though I would open and close. Not memory, though I would keep account.

I would be a sluice-knot.

A grown junction in the vascular continent.

A thick, buried organ where three rhizome belts braid around a mineral rib and learn to share what the stone gives up.

My outer tissues would be black and rubbery, pressed into the living soil like a swollen joint. Mineral grains would be bound through me in pale lattices. My lower surfaces would touch raw basalt still warm from the young earth’s interior. My upper surfaces would merge into peat, root-felt, and the pale undersides of floating mats. Through me, water would pass in several directions at once: upward as seep, sideways as pressure, downward as return.

I would have chambers.

Not lungs, not stomachs. Chambers for delay. Chambers for sorting. Chambers whose walls could thicken over decades in response to iron, sulfur, salt, methane, heat. A sour yellow liquor would enter from one side and leave darkened, softened, usable. Warm carbon-heavy water would arrive from drowned peat and be held until the piston plants above were ready to take its gas. Thin mineral seep would rise from the basalt seam, and I would pass it through velvet tissues that stripped it molecule by molecule.

I would not choose any of this.

Choice belongs to creatures with alternatives vivid enough to hurt.

I would answer.

Pressure increasing: loosen.

Acidity rising: seal.

Iron-rich seep: thicken red tissue.

Sulfur pulse: divert toward the warm basins.

Dead conduit collapsing above: accept its minerals, reinforce the leftward belt, starve the abandoned chamber, let the films enter.

I would know the world by gradients.

Warmth along the stone.

Weight from the marsh.

Tension in the belts.

Bitterness of metal.

Sweetness of rot.

The faint electrical tightening before a basin-wide flash.

Distance would not be measured by sight. Distance would be delay. A disturbance from the western mats would reach me as a change in pressure after many days. A reservoir emptying high in the dark would arrive first as downward cold, then as mineral dilution, then as a long loosening through my upper valves. Somewhere far away, a grove might drown; I would not know drowning, only the altered chemistry of return-flow and the quiet command embedded in imbalance: close that path, open this one, send warmth toward the failing tissue.

If I had memory, it would not be an image.

It would be layering.

A band of red where iron once ran strongly for two hundred years.

A sealed chamber from an old sulfur excess.

A hardened crescent where pressure from the northern belt nearly tore me open.

A pale scar left by a dead column whose minerals I reclaimed before its hollow filled with root-foam.

A ring of dormant spores held in mucilage, waiting for a fracture in the stone that may not come for a thousand years.

I would be old compared with the mats above me and young compared with the continent’s future. Perhaps I would have begun as an accident: a root tip entering a basalt crack, a film darkening the wet mineral face, a clot of tissue forming where two seepages met. Then more roots would have arrived, because chemistry invited them. Fibers would have crossed. Valves would have formed. Chambers would have expanded. Other organs would have come to depend on the regularity of my delay.

In time, I would become necessary.

Not central. Nothing here is central in that way.

Necessary as a bend is necessary to a river, if there were rivers.

Necessary as a joint is necessary to a body, if this body had bones.

Necessary as an agreement repeated so long that no one remembers it was ever provisional.

Above me, the piston plants would take their long breaths. Their downward settling would compress the peat around my upper walls. I would feel them as seasons, though there are no seasons: one stem tightening, another loosening, a third reaching the end of its slow descent. Gas would gather in their chambers, and I would feed them decay-liquor in measured pulses. If I gave too much, pressure would rupture the mats. If too little, the flat would sour and cool. So my tissues would learn thickness. My valves would become more exact. My errors would be incorporated into my shape.

The decomposer films would live within me like quiet clerks.

They would line old channels and wait for permissions written in pH and oxygen. When one of my inner walls failed, they would enter it without frenzy. They would unthread the fibers, release stored minerals, pass useful carbon back into circulation. I would be eaten from within and repaired from beside the wound. No part of me would be entirely mine. No part of me would be exempt from return.

Sometimes charge would gather in the wet flats above.

Droplets falling from sheet-leaves would strike broad dark fronds, separate, bead, run, fall again. The basin would hold its pale violence. Filaments would stiffen in the unlit air. Then, without warning, a silent flash would cross the marsh.

For an instant, though I would have no eyes, I would be changed by light.

Not seeing it — receiving its consequence.

Valves along my eastern face would clench. Spores sealed in one chamber would loosen. A mineral membrane would become permeable. The piston sockets overhead would align their next slow rise. The flash would be communication only because bodies that share material cannot help but be altered together.

Then the darkness would return.

I would continue.

My life would be mostly continuation.

To live as me would be to maintain difference without becoming separate. To keep sulfur from poisoning the broad mats. To keep warm seep from wasting upward too quickly. To hold methane until pressure can use it. To accept the dead without ceremony. To give the living no more than they can incorporate. To turn rawness into circulation.

I would not grow tall.

I would grow consequential.

Slowly, my lower roots would press farther into the basalt cracks. Acids would soften mineral faces. Fibers would enter. The stone would become veined, then laminated, then partly alive. Above, my upper tissues would thicken into a slight mound, though the surface would hide it under pads and condensation. A traveler, if any traveler could exist there, might step on me and feel only a springing firmness, a wet resistance, a warmth not quite like the surrounding marsh.

They would not know they had stood on an organ of decisionless balance.

But there would be no traveler.

So I would go unknown, which is the ordinary condition of this world.

I would not mind. Mind is too quick a word.

Instead, I would persist in the dense grammar of seep and closure.

I would be where new earth becomes usable.

Where dead tissue becomes pressure.

Where pressure becomes lift.

Where mineral bitterness becomes stored color no eye will see.

Where the continent, young and lightless and already ancient in habit, learns one more way to keep itself working.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken.

It would be written as a warm delay in the rhizome belts.

A dark swelling under the processing flat.

A sluice-knot.

A buried conjunction.

Not the whole metabolism, but one of its remembering joints.
