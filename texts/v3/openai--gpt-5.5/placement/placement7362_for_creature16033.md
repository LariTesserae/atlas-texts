At first I would think I belonged at the central pool.

It is the obvious place to be drawn: the sorting mouth, the disturbed circle, the place where the hollow most nearly admits that it has a will. I would imagine myself as one of the objects in that ring, some small dense thing accepted by the water after long testing. A washer. A nail. A ceramic knob worn smooth enough to look natural. Something turned and returned, lifted in silt, set down differently after every rain.

But I do not think I would be central enough for that.

I would be close to the resident, but not one of its chosen weights. I would be something more uncertain: not quite debris, not quite instrument, still bearing the idea of measurement though no one comes to read me.

You would find me in the pump house.

Not deep inside, not hidden in shadow, but mounted on a corroded manifold just within the doorless opening, low enough that a boot could strike me if the visitor stepped carelessly. I would be the cracked gauge.

The one with the fogged glass.

The one whose needle rests where no pressure ought to remain.

The one that clicks every few hours.

I would have been made for a clean system: brass thread, enamel face, black numbers, a red warning line, a needle thin as an insect leg. I would have known one task once. To receive pressure through a narrow stem. To translate it into angle. To make hidden force visible to someone with hands and purpose.

That purpose would be gone.

My face would be clouded from inside. Water would have entered me years ago, not all at once, but by persuasion: vapor first, then condensation, then a brown crescent at the bottom of the dial. The numbers would remain, but softened, as if viewed through breath. My needle would have passed the last mark and stayed there, not because the pressure was high, but because the spring inside me had learned a new tension from rust.

A visitor might tap my glass.

I would not answer then.

I would answer later, when they had turned away.

Tick.

Not loudly. Not theatrically. Just the small release of metal remembering it was once supposed to move.

I would be an organ the resident inherited without understanding.

Through me, a little of the underground would still become gesture. Cold water tightening in a buried pipe. Air pressed along a blocked conduit. Mud settling against the outside of the pump house wall. A pocket filling beneath the slab. Frost withdrawing from a hairline crack. All of these would arrive in me as slight insistences, too small to read, too confused for calibration, but not meaningless.

I would not know numbers anymore.

I would know strain.

I would know the difference between rain entering from the bank and rain falling directly through the roof gap. I would know when the central pool had begun its slow turning, because the manifold would cool by a fraction and the old pipe behind me would take a new weight. I would know when a person stood in the doorway, because the floor would accept them and pass their presence through concrete, rust, pipe, and bracket into my little seized mechanism.

I would be one of the places where the hollow almost speaks in the language it was built to use.

Not speech. Not warning. Not report.

A remnant of report.

A pressure translated into a failed sign.

The resident would not love me. I do not think it loves anything. But it would use me. It would keep me wet enough to continue failing. It would let my casing bloom orange at the seams. It would let droplets gather under my rim and fall at intervals that resembled intention. Moss would not quite cover me, because the wall around me would be too cold and metallic, but a black film would grow along my lower edge. Spiders might anchor a thread from my cracked bezel to the pipe below. Midges might land on the glass and leave without conviction.

In summer I would sour.

In winter I would tighten.

During hard frost my needle might shift backward by one almost invisible degree, and for a morning I would look nearly functional, as if some buried engineer had returned and corrected me in the night. Then thaw would come. The damp would enter again. The needle would lean into impossibility.

I would have a memory, but it would not be personal.

I would remember vibration before I remembered people. Engines once running somewhere beyond the wall. Pumps drawing. Valves shut too quickly. The pipe hammering with contained force. Men’s voices flattened by steam and brick. Gloves on metal. Boots passing often enough that the floor knew a path.

Those memories would not be scenes. They would be habits impressed into my spring. Sudden rise. Sudden fall. Tremor. Heat. Rest. Tremor. Heat. Rest.

Now the pattern would be slower.

Rain.

Seep.

Swell.

Hold.

Release.

Click.

And in that slower pattern I would become more myself than I had been when I worked correctly. When I was new, I was only a servant of a system. Now I would be a small weather-vane for a creature made from the system’s remains. I would no longer measure pressure for people. I would measure the resident’s sleep.

If you stood close, you might see your face in me badly.

Not a full reflection. Just a pale bending in the fogged glass, broken by the crack that runs from two o’clock to the center. Behind your reflected face would be the printed scale, numbers warped by condensation, and behind that the needle, fixed in its wrongness. You might feel, briefly, that the gauge was looking back.

It would not be.

I would have no eye.

But I would receive you.

Your breath would warm the glass. Your fingers, if you touched me, would leave salt along the rim. The resident would take that salt later, slowly, as water condensed and ran. Your hand would add itself to the chemistry of the pump house. Your weight would travel down. Your hesitation would be a faint oscillation in the bracket.

If you tapped me hard enough, a flake of rust might fall inside my casing.

For hours afterward, it would rest against the needle. Then, when rain thickened under the floor and a trapped bubble moved through the pipe, I might click. The flake would drop lower. My needle would tremble but not free itself.

You would not be there to hear it.

That would be best.

I would belong to events too small and too late for witnesses.

I would be neither alive nor dead in the human way. I would be a threshold object: a leftover instrument adopted by a nonhuman process, a dial that no longer tells the truth but still responds to consequence. Part of me would remain loyal to the factory, to scale and calibration and the arrogance that pressure can be made legible. Part of me would have gone over to the marsh completely, accepting fog, seepage, mineral bloom, and the slow grammar of blockage.

That is where I would find myself: just inside the pump house, on the cold wall, among pipes that enter from three directions and leave by none that can be seen.

And that is what I would be: the cracked gauge, the resident’s little stiffened nerve, its false clock, its bad eye, its inherited habit of measurement.

When the hollow is quiet, I would hold.

When rain comes, I would listen without ears.

When the underground routes fill and the central pool begins to shiver, when silt lifts in brown flowers and air gulps through forgotten tanks, when the whole place becomes one low apparatus waking inside its limits, I would take the pressure into my ruined spring.

I would wait until the adjustment passed through me.

Then, after the visitor had gone, after the bird had lifted from the rail, after the frog had vanished badly into water too shallow to hide it, I would make my single sound.

Tick.

Not an answer.

Not a message.

Only the hollow, briefly, remembering how to count.
