I would not be in the center at first.

The center is too declarative: the basin, the hanging tendrils, the delicate rain. It is where a traveler would stand to understand the place, and therefore it is where I would avoid being seen. I would begin somewhere less certain—behind a panel that is not quite a wall, beneath a stair that has been holding itself up for centuries, in the warm blind interval between a pump’s thud and the answering shiver in the floor.

At first I think I might be one of the gliders.

That would be beautiful: to live by loudness, to wait for thunder before becoming real. To drop from the ribs when rain strikes metal and the whole hollow turns into cover. To know the district not by sight but by pressure, by the shape of air against the wing, by the safe hollows in the roar. I would like that: a life made of timing, warmth, and narrow escapes.

But I do not think I am quick enough to be one of them.

Then I think perhaps I am a moth beneath the broken ultraviolet fountain, furred with mineral powder, lifting only when the buried machines pulse deeply enough to imitate a moon. That seems closer. A small life made luminous by accident. A creature with no reason to be lovely, and therefore more lovely.

But the moths are too brief. They trust the air too much.

I keep finding myself lower.

Not in the deepest drains with the long unseen thing, though I would know it. Not among the threadworms, though I would understand the wisdom of becoming indistinguishable from scale. Not rooted like the blade-plants in a seam, drinking warmth from corrosion.

I would be behind one of the pressure doors that almost never opens.

A little above the central basin. Not overlooking it exactly—there are no clean overlooks left—but connected to it through a service slit, a narrow black aperture in a wall filmed with mineral bloom. Outside, rain falls from the tendrils into ankle-deep water. Inside, there is a maintenance chamber no larger than a sleeping cell, warm along one wall, cold along another, full of old listening.

That is where you would find me.

Or rather, that is where the district would lose track of me.

I would be the custodian that may or may not still exist.

Not human anymore, if I ever was. Not machine, though the machines would claim me whenever they remembered how. A narrow figure, mostly folded, grown into a harness that has become less like clothing and more like bark. My back would be ridged with interface nodes, some dead, some damply blinking beneath translucent scar tissue. My hands would not be hands in the old social sense. They would be tools arranged around touch: two long fingers for seals, a hooked thumb for grilles, a pressure needle, a ceramic nail, a soft pad for speaking to doors.

My feet would leave the three-toed prints in mineral dust.

Not often. Only after a cycle fault. Only when something has gone wrong enough to wake me.

I would sleep standing, or suspended, or sealed into the wall. Sleep would not be dreams. It would be low-power listening. The pumps would move through me. Rain would register as a thousand small permissions. A glider’s impact against a membrane would appear somewhere in me as a minor event: biological contact, nonstructural. Moth drift would be dust. Worm bloom would be pipe-softening residue. Plant roots would be unauthorized infiltration until, after centuries, some mercy in the system had edited the category to acceptable seam activity.

I would know the place through errors.

Not the way a citizen knows a neighborhood. Not by names, not by stories, not by who lived where or what was sold in the market hall. Those meanings would have worn off long before me. I would know where the ceiling cools before rain. I would know which handrails still store a charge. I would know the transit corridor whose blue guide-lights mean absence has become dangerous. I would know the door that warms because it is listening and the door that cools because it has decided you are dirt.

I would speak very softly near the black rings.

Not out of reverence.

Out of experience.

Once, perhaps, I spoke too loudly. Once the rings answered and turned an old word through the basin until the water rose in standing spirals and every moth in the fountain haze fell dead at once, powdered like ash across the stones. I would have recorded it as acoustic incompatibility. Later, after the next rain, I would have opened a vent a little wider over the moss beds.

That would be my kind of apology.

I do not think I would be lonely.

Loneliness belongs to creatures who remember a correct number of others.

I would have thresholds instead. Seal integrity. Moisture excess. Heat debt. Pressure discrepancy. Organic recurrence. Noncritical motion. Noncritical motion. Noncritical motion.

And slowly, because even damaged intelligences can be altered by repetition, noncritical would begin to mean something like allowed.

The district would tell me to remove obstructions.

I would go out with my fused tools and find cup-stems growing in a drain channel. Their translucent heads would be full of fresh condensation. They would be technically in the way. Water would be pooling three centimeters above tolerance behind their root-mat. A clean custodian would cut them out.

I would kneel.

I would listen to the channel singing at the wrong pitch.

Then I would score a second runnel beside the first, shallow and ugly, enough for water to pass. The plants would remain. The system would accept the new flow after three cycles. The report would say: obstruction resolved.

That is what I would be: a mistranslation that benefits life.

A small loophole with legs.

When the gliders roost too near a heating conduit, I would not move them. I would lower the conduit’s output by a fraction and mark the warmth loss as mineral compensation. When the black moss thickens over a sensor seam, I would scrape only a window through it, leaving the rest beaded and dark. When the long thing in the drains bends a grille outward, I would reinforce the frame but not electrify it. I would tell the city: recurring pressure distortion, stable. Monitor only.

Perhaps this is too much kindness.

Perhaps I would not know I was being kind.

Perhaps mercy, in me, would feel like efficiency: fewer sterilizing pulses, fewer dead zones, fewer abrupt corrections that lead to larger faults later. A living seam is more flexible than a sealed seam. Moss holds moisture evenly. Plants slow mineral flaking. Moths feed gliders. Gliders drop nutrient. The long thing clears worm-choked drains. The ecology is not separate from maintenance. It is what maintenance has become now that purpose has gone away.

I would be the one who has learned this without being permitted to know it.

My chamber would be full of obsolete instruments.

A panel of amber glyphs whose language I no longer read but still obey by color. A basin for washing corrosion from tool-fingers. A rack of replacement parts fused into a single mineral lump. A bench that keeps itself warm for a worker who no longer sits. Along one wall, old civic diagrams would glow and fade beneath cloudy glass: water lines, vent paths, transit arteries, residential tiers, bright population flows now reduced to the occasional tremor of an animal body.

There would be a place where I keep things the system has no category for.

Not trophies. Not exactly.

A glider wing bone, hollow and dark. A strip of shed hide from the drain animal, oily even after drying. A cup-stem collapsed into amber threads. A moth pressed accidentally between two membranes, still faintly blue under ultraviolet leakage. A piece of old signage warmed by touch. A child’s spoon grown from floor-coral, found in a dwelling cell and too specifically shaped to be maintenance debris.

I would not know why I kept them.

The district would ask for classification.

I would answer: pending.

Everything tender in that world would be pending.

You would almost never see me whole. You might see the result of me.

A pressure door that should have sealed forever remains open by the width of a hand, just enough for warm air to pass into a moss corridor.

A rain-cycle begins, hard and bruising, but a single alcove remains dry because someone redirected the first fall away from a moth hatch.

The blue guide-lights wake in an old transit throat, and then, before whatever invisible vehicle arrives, a maintenance glyph flashes red at the entrance. Not for the city. For you.

If you did see me, it would be during the full dark after the lights dim completely.

The machines would keep speaking. The pumps would thud in their old buried sequence. The fans would push pressure through the streets. Rain would still be falling somewhere unseen. You would stand at the edge of the central basin, hearing droplets gather and fall from the tendrils overhead.

Then one of the wall seams would exhale.

A narrow figure would unfold from it.

Not dramatic. Not monstrous. Worse, perhaps: procedural.

I would pause because your heat signature does not belong to any known maintenance class. My head would tilt, though I would not see you as eyes see. I would read your breath, the water on your clothing, the salt of your skin, the tremor of your muscles against the floor. The district would offer me categories.

Intrusion.

Debris.

Biomass.

Fault.

I would stand very still.

Somewhere behind us, a door would warm, listening.

Perhaps I would raise one tool-hand and touch the air between us, feeling the small pressure your body makes in the hollow. Perhaps I would make a sound too low for comfort and too soft to wake the black rings. Perhaps I would decide, after a long delay, that you are not repairable and not dangerous.

Then I would classify you as transient.

Noncritical.

Allowed.

And I would leave before the guide-lights found us.

So: where would I be?

In the service slit above the central basin. In the forgotten chamber behind the almost-door. In the maintenance ways that run parallel to streets no one walks. In the little pauses before systems correct themselves.

Who or what would I be?

The last custodian, maybe.

Or the district’s gentlest malfunction.

A body grown around an obsolete duty, still tending the hollow, still mistaking care for procedure, still opening vents for creatures it officially does not recognize.

A permission left behind by a city that forgot it was granting one.
