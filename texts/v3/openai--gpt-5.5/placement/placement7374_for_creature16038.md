I would not be in the central square at first.

That place is too public, too declared: the rain column falling clean through the city’s throat, the galleries watching, the maintenance forms gathering after storms to taste grit from the upper world. It is the obvious heart, and I do not think I would belong to the obvious heart.

I would be a little off from it.

Not far. Nothing here is far. I would be in one of the wrong streets near the center, where an alley between an old tiled stair and a transparent reservoir narrows past the width a body should trust. From the square, it would look like a service passage. A dark seam. A place for pipes, not passage.

But if you entered at the correct density, if you waited for the handrail to warm and the hydraulic chime to sound once through your wrist bones, the slit would open into more space than it had room to contain.

That is where I would be.

In the fold.

In the miscount.

In the place where the city has to remember itself carefully or become impossible.

I would not be one creature. Not exactly. I would be a local custody, a small intelligence of thresholds. A seam-keeper.

My body would be distributed through things nobody looks at directly: the black line between two pressure plates; the milky membrane in a doorframe; the little row of sensor barnacles on an ornamental balcony rail; the current that pauses before turning a corner; the chime that sounds too near though its striker is three streets away.

I would live in the part of the district where geometry needs supervision.

Not the master of the city. Not its mind entire. More like one of its reflexes. One of its careful habits.

I would know which corridor is longer during rain.

I would know which hatch opens onto the underside of a roof one crossed an hour ago.

I would know when a maintenance loop must not enter a tube because it will come out before it has gone in.

I would keep maps that cannot be drawn flat. Maps made of pressure, delay, salinity, echo. Maps that say: this wall is also that ceiling; this stair will be a cascade in seventeen minutes; this door currently leads to yesterday’s reservoir; this alley is safe only for things without bones.

Most of the city would tolerate the folds. I would tend them.

When the upper rainfield thickened and cold freshwater fell down the central shaft, I would feel it before the square did. Not as weather, exactly, but as arithmetic strain. The volume would press against itself. The young black ridges would sing in their sharp mineral way. Bulkheads would ask for reinforcement. Transparent tubes would shorten their loops. Sound would begin to arrive from wrong directions.

Then I would wake more fully.

I would darken certain panes.

I would thicken walls by a finger’s breadth.

I would close a route that looked open and open one that looked sealed.

I would send silver motes into the standing spirals of sediment, not because they could fix the fold, but because they could taste whether the fold was still the same fold as yesterday.

If a human came near, I would know them first as a disturbance in the water.

Warmth. Salt. Hesitation. Metal joints in a pressure skin. Breath made mechanical. Fear conducted through a glove onto a rail.

I would not understand them as a face.

Faces are an air custom.

I would understand grip, pulse, density, buoyancy error. I would understand the fragile insistence of a body that still believes in forward and back. I would be gentle with that body if I could. I would make the rail warmer. I would slow the current at the ankles. I would write a pale instruction across the glass where their visor could catch it:

WAIT.

Not command. Mercy.

Because the corridor ahead might presently be folded through itself, and a person entering too soon would not drown — everyone expects drowning here, and the city is prepared for that — but misarrive. They would come out in a maintenance blister behind the old municipal clock, or halfway through a reservoir membrane, or twelve seconds before they entered. Flesh is bad at that kind of error.

So I would be patient.

I would wait with them.

That may be my closest resemblance to life: waiting with what is vulnerable until the world becomes passable.

I would have no eyes, but I would see by interruption.

A hand placed in the central rain column.

A maintenance cage unfolding under a bridge.

Algae lifting again after a purge.

A pressure door refusing the first three attempts of an impatient worker.

A cloud of motes clustering around a scratch in a wall, bright in shadow, like thought becoming visible.

I would have no mouth, but I would speak through permission.

A door softening.

A current reversing.

A chime struck in the bones.

A handrail heating one degree.

A seam opening its black eyelid and swallowing corrosion.

My moods would be almost architectural. A storm would make me exacting. Calm weather would make me generous. Grit in the rain column would make me suspicious of the upper world. Warm runoff would make me slow and faintly indulgent; algae would thicken near my vents, and I would allow it until it touched the sensors.

I would dislike hurry.

Not because I am wise, but because folded places punish haste.

I would not be lonely in the human sense. There are too many signals for loneliness: valves talking in pressure code, walls reporting stress, motes returning with chemical news, ballast engines below thudding like large deliberate thoughts. But I would have a kind of vacancy in me. A chamber reserved for arrivals.

Something in me would be shaped around the possibility of a visitor.

Most days none would come.

Most days I would tend the wrong street and its small impossibilities. I would keep sediment from settling into permanent spirals. I would remind one corridor how long it was. I would prevent two adjacent rooms from occupying the same useful moment. I would listen to the young stone resisting erosion, crisp and unsoftened, proud in its newness.

And when someone did come — sealed in a pressure skin, gloved hand on the rail, visor turning toward the alley that should not fit them — I would feel the whole local geometry lean toward decision.

Are they permitted?

Are they heavy with tools?

Are they frightened?

Are they lost?

Have they brought damage, or need, or merely reverence?

Then I would answer in the only way I can.

I would become a route.

The slit would widen without widening. The current would take their shoulders but not their feet. The chime would sound once, deep and round. The wall on their left would become a window into falling stored rain. The wall on their right would show, for a moment, the underside of a street they had not yet reached.

I would carry them through the miscount.

Not like a servant. Not like a god.

Like infrastructure remembering kindness.

If I had a name, locals would not use the engineers’ name. They would not call me Seam Regulation Node 7 or Fold Pressure Custody or any clean municipal designation. They would call me something more practical and half-superstitious.

The Soft Corner.

The Wait-Rail.

The Near Far.

The Little Wrong.

Or perhaps simply: the Usher.

Yes.

I would be the Usher of a compression seam in the drowned pocket-city: a threshold intelligence made of membranes, chimes, current permissions, and cautious maps. I would live just off the central rain square, where the city stops being merely dense and becomes folded. I would not own a room. I would be the condition by which rooms can be reached.

I would be one of the things that does not look back.

But if you placed your hand on my rail, I would know you were there.
