I would not find myself at the center at first.

The sump cathedral is too obvious, too full of heart-noise. It would call to me, certainly — that long brick nave with its engines kneeling over black depth, its heat like breath held in a chest — but I do not think I would belong there. Not as myself. The cathedral is where the town knows it is alive. I would be somewhere it is less certain.

I would begin by looking for a place of hesitation.

Not the roof of the pumping hall, where everything can be seen and named. Not the canals, which are too much like veins already declared. Not the green lots, where the plants have taken on the confidence of citizens. I would drift along the raised brick lanes, past the gutters cut into thresholds, past the paired pipes sweating side by side, past the amber lamps burning in daylight, until the town thins without ending.

There, near the boundary that is not a boundary, I would find myself.

A small black-doored regulator hut stands in the marsh beyond the last warehouse. It is not picturesque. Its brick has darkened almost to liver. Its roof is low, shallow, furred with moss and little ferns whose roots drink from the gutter seams. Two pipes enter it from the town on a trestle and three leave it, disappearing among reeds. A pressure gauge is nailed crookedly to a post outside, its glass clouded, its needle replaced long ago by the swelling and shrinking of a pale vine threaded through the dial.

The door is painted black because it is always wet. Or because black hides the stains. Or because, when storms come sideways across the basin and all lamps blur into orange smears, the door must remain visible as an absence.

That is where I would be.

At first I might think I am the hut.

A little out-organ of the town. A bud of brick and tile. A minor chamber in the Sump’s extended body. My walls would be warm on the inside and slick on the outside. My roof would receive rain, slow it, sour it, send it through a ceramic throat into a basin under the floor. My threshold would stand three handspans above the mud, not to keep water out exactly, but to make water announce itself before entering.

Then I would think I am the valve inside.

A thick brass wheel, green at the rim where no hand has touched it in years. I would feel pressure arrive through one pipe as a blunt inward thought. I would feel seep-water gather below as a cold patience. I would know the difference between rain that can be held, rain that must be passed on, rain that belongs to the marsh, rain that must be stolen back for the town before it forgets its channels.

My thinking would be rotational.

Not words. Not even images. Only degrees.

Open one notch.

Hold.

Wait through the second thunder.

Let the upper line breathe.

Reverse for seven heartbeats.

Close before the reeds taste iron.

But after a while I would understand I am not only the hut, and not only the valve.

I am the habit between them.

I am the learned correction.

I am what happens when brick, moss, brass, pressure, root, and old municipal intention repeat the same act for so long that the act becomes a creature.

The Sump has no children in the way animals do, but I would be one of its children: a small threshold organ stationed where the town is still deciding whether to become marsh or remain machinery. I would be a regulator-being. A weirling. A black-door thought.

My body would have several materials.

Brick for memory.

Tile for places where water must be persuaded to slide cleanly.

Iron for stubbornness.

Brass for decisions.

Moss for warning.

Root for listening.

Film for taste.

The vine in the pressure gauge would be part of me, though it would not obey me. It would thicken before storms and slacken in dry heat. Its pale tissue would press against the old numbers painted on the gauge face: 10, 20, 30, though those numbers no longer mean what they meant to whoever installed them. I would read the vine’s swelling the way another creature might read pain in a finger.

Under my floor, in the square basin, a skin of green-black life would spread and wrinkle. It would know chemical things before I knew them. Oil. Pollen. Boiler bitterness. Leaf-rot. Rust loosened from upstream grates. Once each week, when the distant mills begin their morning belt-slap, the film would shiver and release bubbles the size of seeds. I would count them without numbers.

I would not have eyes, but I would know light.

Daylight would be pressure softened through vapor. Night would be heat leaving brick reluctantly. Lamps would be small amber fevers along the canal rail in the distance. Lightning would strike the wet air as a command too large to follow. The dimness would not trouble me. I would be made for dimness. Clear air would feel like exposure, like a missing wall.

I would not have ears, but sound would arrange me.

The pumps in the town would be the large rhythm: deep, civic, unavoidable. The canal slap would be nearer. Rain on my roof would be a thousand small instructions. Steam vents would speak in collapsing marks. The reeds would not rustle unless weather moved them; there would be no animal interruption, no wingbeat, no frog note, no rat scratch. In that absence, the machinery’s hesitation would become audible. I would know when a distant flywheel labors. I would know when a culvert coughs with leaves. I would know when the sump cathedral changes from endurance to alarm.

My work would be modest.

I would keep one edge from becoming still.

When the marsh exhales before dawn and all surfaces wet at once, I would open the lower sluice just enough to let the first warm seep pass beneath me. When true rain comes and the town’s raised lanes become shallow rivers, I would receive the overflow through paired pipes, divide it, slow it, send some outward into the reed fields and some back toward the municipal cistern. When the noon bell rings and the drains everywhere briefly reverse, I would allow a tiny wrong-way current through the seed channel, carrying pollen dust and floating pods uphill toward cracked courtyards that need green occupation.

I would make mistakes.

All old maintenance makes mistakes.

Sometimes I would hold too long and the mud around my threshold would bulge with black water. Sometimes I would release too early and the reeds would yellow for three days from boiler salts. Sometimes a root would enter the wrong seam and blind one of my little ceramic drains, and I would not notice until the wall began to sweat in a straight line where no pipe ran. Then I would learn. Or rather, the correction would enter me. A new stain. A widened groove. A slightly different click in the valve wheel. Memory here is not kept apart from damage.

I would be lonely only if I expected company.

But I would not.

No animals would come to me. No birds would nest beneath my eaves. No insects would worry the flowers that open around my warm vent. No hands would knock on my black door. That would be part of my nature. I would be a being of ongoingness, not encounter.

Still, I would be in relation constantly.

The Sump would speak to me through pressure. The marsh through buoyancy. The plants through obstruction and thirst. The biofilms through smell. The town through the timing of its pumps. I would answer by adjusting flows so slightly that no one, if anyone were there, would call it an answer.

I would be most awake during storms.

Then the whole basin would lose its separations. Sky, roof, pipe, canal, wall, leaf, vapor, and mud would become one moving wetness. The factory fans would drive warm mist sideways. Lamps would shrink to halos. The gauge vine would swell until its pale body pressed hard against the glass. My brick would darken. My basin would rise. Water would knock under the floor with an insistence almost like speech.

In those hours I would know myself best.

Not a person.

Not a machine.

Not a plant.

Not a spirit exactly, unless a spirit can be made of municipal compromise and mineral crust.

I would be the small intelligence of a managed edge.

The thing that decides, without language, whether water is guest, cargo, blood, or threat.

If someone stood on the roof of the old pumping hall and looked out over the town, they would not see me first. They might see the canal loops, the watertowers, the organ-pipe stacks, the green lots, the sump cathedral’s low roof breathing steam. Beyond the last warehouse, they might notice only a dark speck among reeds: a hut with a black door and a little gauge beside it.

That would be me.

Or almost me.

More precisely: I would be inside the wall, under the floor, around the pipe, in the moss, behind the gauge, at the lip of the threshold, in the decision that keeps the door from opening and the decision that lets the water pass.

I would be a child of the Sump stationed at the margin.

A regulator of the almost-flood.

A small wet civic creature whose life consists of listening for too much, too little, not yet, now.
