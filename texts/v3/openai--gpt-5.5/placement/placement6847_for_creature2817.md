You would not find me on the open flats.

Not at first.

I would not be one of the figures crossing twenty miles of pale stone with a wet bundle tied to my back, nor one of the road-arguers walking beside a causeway that has changed its mind. I would not be brave enough, or dry enough, for that. The distance would make too much room around me. I would feel my thoughts thinning with the horizon.

You would find me lower down, where the city has sunk into itself.

In the cistern quarter.

Not the central cisterns, the reliable ones with lintels rubbed smooth by generations of palms, but the old eastern cisterns beneath the abandoned baths, where the streets can only be entered during rain. There is a stair there that is sometimes twelve steps and sometimes seventeen. It descends between walls furred with silver moss. At the bottom is a chamber whose roof has cracked in the shape of a river no one remembers. Water gathers there without falling. It beads on the ceiling, trembles, and at long intervals lets go.

That is where I would be.

A listener.

One of those children whose birth-dew froze on the tongue.

Not cursed, exactly. Not blessed. Marked for attention.

I would have been a narrow child, too quiet even for that quiet country, always turning my head toward drains, bowls, roof gutters, thaw-lines in paving. Other children would have learned the games of chalk circles and moss-lines, but I would have sat beside basins and waited for them to say what kind of water they were. My elders would have watched this with resignation. They would have warmed my hands often. They would have braided my hair tight and kept me near breathing walls.

By adulthood I would belong to the wells.

My title would not be grand. Something like Underkeeper of the Seventh Listening, or Damp-name Holder, or simply the one who goes below before dawn. Titles there would change by district anyway. In the street that permits no verbs, they would indicate me by touching two fingers to the throat, then pointing downward.

My work would be to keep different waters from becoming confused.

Roof-dew in shallow black bowls.  
Cistern water under stone lids.  
Ravine-drip in green jars.  
Fog-water combed from nets and wrung through moss.  
Pool water that has seen other skies.  
Water that has flowed uphill and must be allowed to forget.  
Water from a repeated valley, sealed with a cord no one unties indoors.

I would know which water may be drunk, which may be used on thresholds, which may wake seeds, which may clean tools but never skin, which must be poured back into stone before sunset. I would carry no book. I would carry cords. Damp cords, mostly, knotted with rules that swell differently in the hand depending on weather.

I would be slight and cold-fingered, with green-gray stains at my nails from the lichens used to sweeten cistern rims. My cloak would smell of peat smoke, wet fiber, and mineral dark. I would walk with one shoulder lower than the other from years of carrying bowls that must not slosh. Around my belt would hang a copper scraper, a root-fiber measuring line, three chalks wrapped in waxed leaf, and a little covered cup for water that speaks only once.

People would not come to me for prophecy.

That would be too loud a word.

They would come with practical questions.

Can the north gutter be joined to the old basin, or will the basin take offense?  
May the dew from a roof where a death occurred be used on seedlings?  
Why has the stairwell begun to smell like thawed iron?  
Is the pool showing stars because it is deep, or because it is elsewhere?  
Has the well gone shallow, or is the city standing higher than it did yesterday?

I would answer slowly, if I answered. More often I would ask to sleep beside the water first.

There would be a chamber for that: a low alcove cut into the cistern wall, lined with old moss and warmed from behind by a flue no one has found the mouth of. I would lie there wrapped in felted moss-cloth, one hand against the stone, listening through my bones to the small architecture of drops.

Drop from roof-channel: clean, recent, impatient.  
Drop from wall seam: old, carrying salt.  
Drop from unseen crack: not from this room.  
Drop that makes no sound: to be watched.  
Drop that sounds twice: do not mix.

Over time, I would become known as someone who can tell when a cistern is lying.

Not intentionally. Water does not lie like people lie. It reflects what has touched it, and sometimes what will touch it, and sometimes what touched a different hollow three centuries away. A basin that shows a full moon at noon may not be deceiving anyone. It may simply be loyal to another sky.

I would understand that.

Perhaps too well.

I think I would be lonely in a way my neighbors would not quite recognize, because I would spend so much of my life among things that respond but do not converse. Yet I would not be solitary. The moss over the east sill would know my tread and lift a little when I came in. A pale vine in the ruined bathhouse would lower one root into my palm when frost was due. There would be a black lichen near the thirteenth step that I never cut, because once, during a dry year, it darkened three days before the cistern failed and saved us from drinking the wrong reserve.

I would trust that lichen more than many people.

Not because people are untrustworthy, but because people hurry toward meaning. Lichen does not.

My dwelling would be a room half above ground and half below, built against the outer wall of the old baths. The roof would be slabbed stone, thick with deliberate moss. Three bowls would sit near the door: one for water brought in, one for water going out, one for water whose intention is unclear. Visitors would know not to touch the third bowl.

Inside, there would be little.

A bed-box warmed with stones.  
A wall of hanging cords.  
A shelf of covered cups.  
A copper knife gone green at the handle.  
Bundles of dried black frond.  
A door made from pale dwarf-tree wood, carved with no straight lines.

On the inner wall I would keep names.

Not written, because written names are restless there, but held in knots and recitation. Names of builders whose roofs still need them. Names of wells gone sour and later forgiven. Names of children born in dry seasons. Names of those who entered rain-streets and returned with fewer shadows than they left with.

In the evenings, when the old ones gathered in the plaza to remember the city aloud, I would sit at the edge rather than the center. My voice would not carry far. But when they reached the cistern quarter, they would pause, and I would supply the parts no one else wants to remember.

The year the east basin reflected a room full of strangers.  
The year dew formed under roofs instead of above them.  
The year the seventh cistern filled with clear water that tasted of snow rising from crevices.  
The name of the woman who taught the stone to slope again after it had gone level from grief.

I would not be a hero.

If a road lost its destination, I would not be the one sent after it. If a tower appeared in fog, I would advise waiting. If a wall lengthened, I would mark the doors, bring wet moss, and stand aside while masons argued gently with the stone.

My courage would be smaller and more repetitive.

I would go below before sunrise.  
I would put my ear to freezing walls.  
I would taste one drop when no one else should.  
I would separate waters that wished to mingle.  
I would remember which basin must never see a face.  
I would keep warmth moving from hearth to pipe to sleeping chamber.  
I would apologize to moss when scraping it from inscriptions.  
I would tell children, again and again, not every reflection is waiting for you.

And as I grew older, I think the city would begin to use me differently.

The cistern stair would become more stable when I descended it. Not completely stable. Nothing so rude. But it would prefer twelve steps for me. The silver moss would close less quickly under my hand. Water that had been muttering in separate bowls would still when I entered the room.

People would notice and stop mentioning it.

That is how respect works there.

Eventually I would no longer travel beyond the eastern quarter except during fog, when distance loosens and kin arrive through courtyards that are not usually adjacent. My joints would stiffen unless I kept my back against old heat. My eyes would grow too dark-adapted for noon. Children would be sent to me with questions tied in cords, and I would answer by retying them, adding one knot for caution and one for return.

At the end, I would not be buried deeply.

They would wrap me in root cloth and pack me with moss, salt, and powdered blue-white lichen. But I do not think they would place me in the ordinary stone houses of the dead.

They would put me in the wall behind the listening chamber.

Not as an honor exactly. As a use.

My name would be spoken before the first thaw and after the seventh fog. A small basin would be set into the stone near where my hands had been. Apprentices would leave a drop there before mixing waters. If the drop vanished, they would wait. If it trembled, they would listen. If it froze, no one would speak for a while.

After a few generations, I would become less a person than a local rule.

Not a ghost. Not a spirit. Something more modest.

The eastern cistern does not sour while my name is remembered.  
The stair holds twelve steps for those carrying water carefully.  
No roof-dew is mixed below ground until the listener has been greeted.  
If the wall sounds hollow behind the moss, answer softly.

So that is where I would be found:

beneath the old baths of a low city on the high plateau, where rain-streets appear only when the weather permits them, where silver moss keeps the stair damp, where water gathers in separated minds.

And I would be human, mostly.

A cistern listener.  
A keeper of damp distinctions.  
A person slowly being accepted into stone.
