I would not find myself at the center.

That is the first thing I know.

The mound is too sovereign, too nearly-born, too dense with the room’s agreement. To be there would be to become a decision the place has not made yet. I would be drawn toward it, certainly—everything is—but I would not belong on its warm crown, or under its moss, or in the sevenfold trickle where the chamber lowers its voice.

I would be found just off-center, where the narrow path has to bend twice for no obvious reason.

At first I think I am a tool.

A shell scraper left in a basin. A thumb-smoothed wedge. A little ladder that has forgotten whether it was made for height, repair, or prayer. Something simple, handled often, kept wet so it does not crack. I like the humility of that: no mouth, no will beyond use, no anxiety about becoming a symbol. A thing that helps maintenance continue.

But no. I am too attentive to be only a tool. I would listen too much. I would notice the way one drip arrives fractionally older than the last. I would become suspicious of straight gutters. I would learn the room’s hesitations and begin answering them.

So perhaps I am one of the caretakers.

Not one of the first ones. Not an old builder remembered almost correctly by the resident. I would be a later condensation: born during one of the wrong-way rains, when up became local and the shadows of the stone knife dripped from three directions. I would not have formed all at once. First a listening patch in the plaster. Then a pair of hands. Then a narrow wet face pulled loose from a root curtain, blinking mineral-blue in the dimness while the room decided whether I was separate enough to need feet.

I would be slight and asymmetrical, as they are. Clay-smoothed skin, reed bones, root hair lying flat against my skull in the heat. My fingers would be too broad, too sensitive, made for pressure rather than grasping. My feet would know cane lattice better than ground. A cord would run from my left wrist into the wall and vanish behind three layers of plaster, so that I could never entirely leave the chamber, and the chamber could tug me awake before a crack became a wound.

I would carry no flame. No metal. No dry book.

I would carry a shell scraper in my mouth and a bundle of damp cords across my back.

My place would be near the unfinished gutter whose end drips upward into a suspended bead. Beside it hangs the diagonal plumb line, the one whose stone weight obeys a gravity not yet accepted by the rest of the room. That is where I would work: at the disputed edge between measurement and weather. Not the center, not the doorway, not the shrines with their folded leaves and black trembling sand, but the place where the chamber admits that direction is still under negotiation.

My task would be to tend the almost-paths.

Not the established channels with their moss-dark lips and polished clay throats. Those belong to older hands. Not the seven channels around the mound; those require a steadiness I would not yet have earned. I would be given the indecisive water: beads that swell but will not fall, rivulets that circle until touched, steam that condenses into shapes resembling maps of rooms that have not been built.

I would crouch there in the hot dimness, cheek pressed close to the plaster, tasting mineral change on the wall with the flat of my tongue. I would listen for the leaks that have become too certain.

A good leak asks.

A bad leak insists.

When a leak insists, I would not seal it first. That would be crude. I would place a thumb beside it, not on it, and feel what it believes. If it believes in downwardness too strongly, I would braid a cord uphill and let capillary patience argue with it. If it believes the wall is only a wall, I would cut a shallow groove around it, introduce it to a basin, make it pass through root hair and seed husk before returning it to clay. If it begins repeating itself at the same interval for too long, I would set a hollow thorn beneath it and change the note, because repetition is useful only until it becomes a tunnel no one meant to open.

I think I would be the one who reads sound.

Other caretakers would know stone, or vines, or pressure. One would know how to scrape mineral crust without insulting the basin. One would know which figurine to place near the mound when the mountain grows too fast. One would know how to keep the dangerous sky-dream wrapped in clay.

I would know when a noise has repeated enough times to become passage.

That would be my gift and my risk.

In the chamber, nothing is merely acoustic. Drip on leaf, drip on clay, drip on stretched hide, drip into gourd: each is a small hinge. Most hinges swing only within the room. Some swing into prior rains. Some into future repairs. Some into the resident’s folded dreaming, where a wider terrace or deeper cistern waits as a possible organ.

I would keep count without numbers.

Three drops in a gourd, pause.  
Two ticks in drying clay, pressure-rise.  
Seven soft impacts on moss, do not touch the mound.  
A bubble-cough beneath the floor followed by root-shiver: wake the stone-tender.  
The shell-clicking thing clicks once: wait.  
Twice: open a side channel.  
Three times: put your palm on the wall and hum until the yellow seam slows.

I would not speak above the water. My mouth would be mostly for carrying the scraper, tasting plaster, and holding warm black water when both hands are needed. But I would have a language of interventions. A stopped thorn-spout released all at once. A cord retied with its knot facing inward. A basin turned three finger-widths toward the diagonal weight. A leaf placed upside down over an offering cup to say: not yet, but soon.

The resident would know me as a small moving correction.

Not a servant. Not a child exactly. More like a habit it had found useful enough to keep in a body.

Sometimes, when the mound opened without opening, I would feel it through the wrist-cord before the room changed. The cord would tighten, then soften. The mineral films would turn their glow inward. Water would lower its voice. The mountain would pause.

I would stop working then.

Everyone would.

My hands would spread flat against the floor lattice, and I would feel the warm pulse that is not a heart: the spring deciding whether to become spring, the summit deciding whether inwardness can be height. I would lower my head, not from worship exactly, but because in that moment the room becomes deeper than posture.

I would be afraid of naming it.

Even inside myself, I would avoid making it singular. I would think of it as the warmth, the agreement, the almost, the held place. If a dry word formed in me, I would drink from the wall until it softened.

There would be moments when I almost leave.

Not through the slit to the cloud—that would be too direct, too sky-struck. But through sound. A drip repeats behind me, then before me, then under a basin that should be empty. The little ladder in the shrine becomes, for three breaths, plainly functional. I understand that if I step onto it sideways—not upward, not downward, but toward the remembered interval between two rains—I could enter another version of the chamber.

One where the terraces have widened.  
One where the mound has hatched into weather.  
One where the mountain hardened too quickly and the room survives only as a wet idea inside stone.  
One where the builders are still human, sweating and laughing softly as they press the first clay tablets into a summit that does not yet exist.

I would be tempted.

Of course I would.

But the wrist-cord would tug. Or a leak would ask a new question. Or the upward-dripping gutter would tremble, about to choose badly. And I would return to the small work, because this is a world where salvation is probably maintenance, and maintenance is never finished enough to be abandoned.

When I died, if that word applied, I would not want to be placed near the center.

Lay me into the wall beside the diagonal plumb line.

Plaster me lightly. Leave my right hand out for seven pressure cycles, fingers curved around no tool. Let water bead along the knuckles. Let moss take the wrist first. Let my root hair enter the seam behind the unfinished gutter. In time, I would become a listening reinforcement, a damp patch with a memory for disputed directions.

The newer caretakers would come to me when a channel could not decide.

They would press their thumbs into the soft lime above what used to be my shoulder and wait for the wall to answer. If the leak was good, I would let it pass. If it was too final, I would darken around it. If a repeated sound had become a passage, I would tremble just enough to warn them.

So that is where I would be found:

not in the mound, not at the slit, not among the oldest shrines, but beside the half-built gutter and the diagonal plumb line, where water rises into a bead and refuses conclusion.

And what would I be?

A damp little maker.

A reader of leaks.

An organ of hesitation.

One of the chamber’s minor intelligences, born from wrong-way rain, tasked with keeping paths from becoming fate too soon.
