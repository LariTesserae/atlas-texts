At first I think I would be found near the rim, where the wind traps stand.

That seems like the obvious place for me: among the woven frames and thorn-lashed nets, catching what almost is not there. Dust. Pollen. A wing scale. The damp so faint it is more rumor than water. I imagine myself there in the evening, strung between stakes, listening to the chimes change their speech as the air cools. I would like the work of receiving without choosing, of gathering the nearly invisible.

But I do not think I belong fully to the rim.

The rim still faces outward. It has ambition, even if it is only the ambition to catch wind. It watches the lower haze. It measures arrival. Traders, weather, birds, news, storms that may never come. The rim is where the town admits that there is elsewhere.

I would not be elsewhere-facing enough.

Then I think I might be one of the rope-plants.

A thick old vine gripping a wall beside a roof path, jointed and scarred, my leaves folded tight through the day and opening at dawn by one careful finger’s width. I would have the patience for that, perhaps. I would like to live by storage. To take in night breath, to hold it in swollen green knots, to become shade by refusing haste. People would curse me for prying stones apart and bless me for cooling a room. Goats would stand below me with their yellow eyes and criminal mouths. Children would know which of my loops could bear weight and which would snap.

But a rope-plant must choose growth. It must push into cracks. It must thicken, flower, sulk, recover. It has hungers that move.

I think I am less green than that.

For a while I am almost certain I would be a gecko.

One of the fat, blunt-headed ones that comes out when the lamps are lit, pale-bellied, dust-backed, toes spread against warm plaster. That life has a small rightness: to patrol the edges of human fire, to eat moths drawn to the hooded flame, to vanish behind a lintel when voices grow large. I would know every ceiling in one household and the exact temperature of each stone after sunset. I would watch births, quarrels, accounts, kneading, repairs, old women sleeping with their mouths open, children whispering lies under blankets. I would be tolerated because I was useful and because I asked little.

But even that is too quick.

A gecko darts. A gecko decides.

I think I would be found lower, older, more underfoot.

You would find me in the central court, but not in the middle of it. Not where the drum stands when there is news, not where seed is measured, not where disputes gather heat. I would be set into the threshold of the wind-facing shrine, half inside and half outside, where the concave court floor rises slightly toward stacked slabs darkened by hands.

I would be the shell-stone there.

A flat slab of salt-white and violet-gray mountain rock, worn smooth by generations of sandals, hooves, bare feet, dragging baskets, and the careful knees of shrine-keepers. In me, plainly visible when the dust is brushed away, would be the fossil of a small ribbed sea creature: a curled thing, fan-marked, impossible and ordinary. Not large enough to be grand. Not rare enough to be hidden. Important because it is stepped on.

The people would have a name for me, though not a poetic one at first.

Something like Wet-Back.

Or Shell-Step.

Or The Stone That Shows Its Teeth.

Children would be told not to pry at my fossil ridges with bone needles. They would do it anyway. Old people would rap their knuckles on me before beginning certain stories, not from superstition exactly, but because a story about water should start by touching evidence. Shrine-keepers would sweep me each morning with a short brush of stiff grass, pushing dust from my grooves. During dry years, I would be the color of old flour. After rare rain, I would darken almost blue.

I would not be a god. That would be too much separation.

I would be a citizen.

One of the stones with names.

I would belong to the mountain first, but also to the shrine, the court, the feet, the dust, the people who remember and the people who forget. I would hold no water, but I would certify that water has been. That would be my work: not promise, not comfort, but proof.

The town would pass over me all day.

Plant-keepers with bone knives tucked into their belts. Children sent to fetch ash, hopping over my paler seam as part of a game whose rules change whenever an adult watches. Water-keepers, dull-faced and disliked, pausing with sealed jars in their arms. A goat once, escaping judgment, clattering straight across me into the shrine doorway and leaving two neat black pellets on holy ground. A potter with smoke-blackened wrists. A trader returned from below, smelling of wet clay so strongly that everyone pretends not to lean closer.

People would touch me when they needed their words to be less slippery.

Not for every oath. The town has too many obligations for ceremony each time. But for matters involving cisterns, roof-rights, inherited vines, disputed seed, descent promises, the division of a dead person’s useful bones — then someone would say, “Say it on the shell.”

And they would stand with one heel on me.

That would be enough.

I would feel the differences in bodies.

The light tap of children. The scuff of elders. The sharp, angry weight of someone accused. The leaning, exhausted pressure of a person who has carried too much from the rope road. The careful stance of someone lying well. The trembling sole of someone telling the truth badly. The padded passing of pigs. The needle-scratch feet of chickens. The delicate pause of a lizard crossing me at night when I still hold the day’s warmth.

I would know the town not by seeing it, but by pressure.

A festival would be many feet and shell beads striking my surface. Mourning would be fewer feet, slower, with more bare soles. Drought would be light bodies and dragging steps. Rain would be chaos: people slipping, shouting, kneeling, laughing with open mouths, pressing wet hands flat against me as if I had done anything but lie there remembering.

When rain came, I would become myself most completely.

Water would run down the shrine slabs, across the court, into the shallow channels cut by ancestors who perhaps knew better storms. For a few minutes I would be restored to an old language. My fossil grooves would fill. Dust would lift from me in brown veils. The shell inside me — not alive, not dead in any simple way — would shine.

Children would shout, “The shell is drinking.”

An elder would say, “No. The shell is being reminded.”

Both would be true enough.

At night I would cool earlier than the walls but later than the air. The drum would travel through me. Not sound as ears know it, but a low entering. A pressure in the grain. News, dispute, distribution, death, birth, return: all of it would become vibration before meaning. I would receive the town before it became words.

The rope-plants above the shrine doorway would loosen after sunset. Their leaves would open. Sometimes a single drop of condensed night damp would form at a folded tip and fall, not into a basin, not into a jar, but onto me.

A waste, someone might say, if they saw.

But no one would see most nights.

And that one dark speck would sit in the shallow curve of my fossil until the air took it back.

I would not mind.

I think that is who I would be: not the keeper of water, but the memory that water is not fantasy. Not the storm, not the cistern, not the thirst. The evidence underfoot.

A thing made before the mountain was a mountain.

A sea-mark lifted into drought.

A threshold citizen in a crowded town where even stone has duties.

If you came there after sunset, salt still sharp on your tongue from the first welcome, and someone led you through the central court toward the wind-facing shrine, you might not notice me. You would be watching the birds on the lintels, the goats in the shadows, the old women measuring you, the vines brushing your shoulders like dry hands.

Then your sandal would catch slightly on my worn rib.

You would look down.

Someone beside you would say, “Careful. That one remembers wetness.”

And there I would be.
