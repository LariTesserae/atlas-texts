At first I think I would be one of the black birds.

That is the easy answer: small, heat-loving, clever enough to live in the mouths of vents, quick enough to cross the open places without belonging to them. I can imagine the bare feet on warm mineral crust, the oily feathers, the basalt-colored eggs. I can imagine knowing every updraft along the folded cliffs and speaking only in clicks because anything more elaborate would feel vulgar there.

But no. The birds are too sudden. Too cleanly separate from the ground.

Then I think I might be one of the blind salamanders, because there is something right about living in the black pools, almost transparent, made mostly of listening skin. A life without eyes in a world without reliable light. A body that glows only when disturbed, and then only enough to confess depth. That feels close.

But still too animal. Too contained.

I think you would find me where the open gravel begins to narrow between two low thickets, at a crossing that does not look like a crossing until enough lives have used it.

There is a black pool there, shallow at the rim and unexpectedly deep in the center, its edge furred with moss that freezes every night and thaws for one hour when the vent below breathes properly. A pale seam of quartz runs down from the higher shale and disappears under the water. Around it grow knee-high conductive plants with closed leaves like dark fists. Their roots grip the seam. Their veins wake during storms.

I would live there, not as one creature exactly, but as a listening thing spread through the edge of that place.

A lichen-fungal relay. A low intelligence in mineral skin.

From above I would look like nothing worth naming: a dark crust on stone, a mat of green-black gloss, a few glass-ribbed fronds folded tight against the cold. Hoarfrost would collect on me in fine white lines. In dim weather I would vanish into the basalt. When the blue-white static moved through the grove, my hidden pattern would show for a breath: branching circuits, root-threads, tiny wet nodes, a dull gleam of plated iron along my edges.

I would not walk. I would not hunt in any direct way. I would not call.

I would keep account.

Not numbers, not thoughts as animals think them, but pressures, salts, warmths, returns.

The grazer’s hoof at dawn: light, splayed, careful.  
The frost-cat’s belly against stone: long stillness, heat masked under rime.  
The web-maker’s tension across the hollow: a faint constant pull.  
The black birds landing above the vent: rapid punctuation.  
The salamanders rising in the pool when the mineral seep changes taste.  
The slow underground push of warm water before storm.

I would know these things by touch and conduction. The quartz seam would be my spine. The moss would be my ear. The pool would be my dark eye, though I would not see through it. I would taste what crossed the water. I would feel the iron content of rain. I would remember hoof-falls as slight rearrangements in my growth.

Memory, for me, would be physical. A path used often would become more conductive. A predator’s favored ledge would grow a faint mineral burr. A place where young grazers hid would soften with warmer moss because the fungal tubes below had learned the shape of their stillness. I would not decide this benevolently. I would not be kind. I would simply become more fitted to what repeated.

That would be my form of thought: adjustment without announcement.

When storms came low across the tableland and the cloud pressed itself into the shrubs, I would open.

The fronds would loosen from their knots, translucent and horn-pale. Every edge would bead with water. Static would gather first as a taste, then as an ache. The conductive groves nearby would lift in answer. A blue spark would crawl from one wet stem to another, hesitate over the black pool, and enter me through the quartz.

For a few moments I would be larger.

Not conscious in the way a face is conscious, but widened. I would feel the hollow entire: the web-lines, the sleeping moths, the frost-cat under the overhang, the processions of amber-bearing insects, the subterranean water moving like a secret muscle. I would feel other relays answering across the plateau, far off in other pockets of growth — brief intelligences lighting and fading in the dark.

No one would speak.

But information would pass.

Not language. Weather with syntax.

If a dry cold was coming, I would tighten my surfaces and draw vapor down. If the pool was about to skin over, I would route warmth along the roots nearest its rim. If carrion lay nearby, I would alter the salts in the moss and call insects without calling. If a grazer stepped too often along one fragile edge, I would make that edge less pleasant underfoot. If a frost-cat waited too successfully at the crossing, perhaps the path would become slick for a season. Not justice. Balance. Maintenance. The plateau’s old arithmetic.

I think I would be most awake in the almost-dark before the mineral glow returns.

That hour when nothing is moving, supposedly. When the frost has sealed the leaves and the vents have gone quiet between breaths. The open gravel plains would look empty. The black pool would hold no reflection. The birds would be tucked into their warm cracks. The grazers would be pale distances.

Then, under everything, the mountain would turn.

A pressure would come up through the seam. Very low. Too deep for hearing.

I would receive it.

I would not understand it as message or command. It would be older than wanting. Heat, compression, water, metal, stone remembering fire. It would pass through me and into the roots and out along the lichen grids, and for a moment I would be only one small contact point in the plateau’s hidden circuitry.

That is where I would belong: not at the center, because there is no center; not as master, because nothing there can afford mastery; not as prey or predator exactly, but as one of the quiet agreements that lets the whole place continue.

You would find me by accident.

You would crouch beside the pool, thinking you had found a patch of frost-dark moss and a quartz vein. Your breath would hang low. Your hand, if you set it on the stone, would feel a faint warmth that should not be there. Then a far pebble would click on a ridge. A grazer would lift its head beyond sight. A thread would tighten in the hollow. Somewhere under the moss, water would change direction.

And I would have noticed you.

Not dramatically. Not with alarm.

Only as pressure, salt, heat, rhythm.

A new fact entering the cold.
