You would find me where the rootwood plates almost meet.

Not in the grand furnace-groves, not among the highest mirrors, not in the black-green foundry basins where the mats digest dust with continental patience. I would be lower than that, smaller than that: in the seam country, where the hexagons of pale living deck are joined by lines of salt and basalt powder, where every road is also a crack and every crack is also a possible beginning.

At first I would seem like a person.

A slight figure in waxed cloth and ceramic fiber, moving slowly under the immense white light. Hood drawn low. Mask grown from cork and pale resin, fitted over the mouth and nose not to keep poison out but to keep breath in. Gloves with open palms, because the warnings must be felt skin-to-bark, not read. A lacquered satchel at one hip. A bundle of dormant parts across the back: screw-pods, hinge-seeds, rolled lens membranes, a few black root-fibers sleeping in dry gel.

I would walk lightly because the ground is alive and does not like insistence.

I would stop often. Kneel. Press my palm to a raised patch of bark beside a corridor mouth. The message would not be language exactly. More like pressure translated into caution.

Do not measure here.  
Do not count the green chimneys.  
Third shadow unlicensed.  
Brace needed ten years ago.  
Carry yellow dust forward.  
Return by the door that has not opened yet.

I would obey most of it.

Not all. No creature living there obeys everything. Even maintenance requires a little trespass.

My work would be seam-tending.

Where two grown systems meet badly, I would go. Where a vine-cable has entered a corridor too small for it and come out as a grove on the other side. Where a shadow continues after its leaf has folded and begins chilling the root plates. Where a storage pod has become larger inside than outside and now hums with unsorted distance. Where a map-vein in a broad translucent leaf has developed black erasures that eat roads before anyone can use them.

I would not fix these things in the old sense.

I would negotiate with them.

I would sprinkle charged salt in a line no wider than a finger. I would graft a listening root to a stubborn hinge. I would polish a lens until it remembered which way the sun was. I would hang a little warning comb where a corridor had begun to loop through hunger. I would whisper pressure codes into valve-trees, not with my voice but by tapping resin keys grown along my wrist.

Sometimes I would carry dust from one district to another.

Not much. A satchel of white salt from the morning seams. A vial of yellow nutrient glass flakes. A pinch of black basalt powder sorted by hinge-ferns. To anyone else it would look like errand work, devotional and minor. But the place understands dust as argument. A handful delivered to the right seam can persuade a wall to thicken, a corridor to shorten, a fruit-horizon to ripen into a safe avenue instead of a drop into unmade years.

I would sleep in old service trunks.

Dry rooms inside bark, lined with cork and amber resin. Narrow bunks grown directly from the wall. Blue-green light leaking from storage veins. No blanket, only a folded sheet of insulation felt and the steady click of stomata beyond the chamber. Maintenance moths would sleep above me in rows, pale as flour, their wings powdered with mineral bloom. I would wake with their dust on my sleeves.

I would have neighbors, though not friends in the ordinary wet-blooded way.

A foxlike scavenger might know my route and follow at a distance, blind head low, whiskers tasting the charge left by my steps. It would steal from me when it could: a glass thorn, a warning flake, once perhaps a useless little screw-pod. I would leave cracked sensor fruit for it near abandoned control knots. In return it might avoid nesting in corridors I needed to keep open.

Long-legged pollinators would cross my path at dusk. They would pause on wire limbs and lower glass tongues into flower-machines, taking charged pollen in silence. If one touched my glove, I would feel a brief amber current pass through my hand, like a thought too simple to be translated.

The herbivores would not fear me. They would continue cropping obsolete shade membranes with their slab teeth, their ceramic stomachs grinding softly. Behind them rootlets would gather the pellets they left. Everything useful. Everything returned.

But I think, after a while, you would notice I am not entirely one of the dry people.

Too patient, perhaps. Too willing to stand still for a day under a roof of mirror-leaves while a warning texture slowly grows into legibility beneath my palm. Too indifferent to hunger. Too little troubled by the absence of water. I would carry a canteen as a courtesy to my ancestry, but it would be empty, lined with salt, used for storing delicate seeds.

My skin, under the cloth, would be sealed in thin resin.

Not applied. Grown.

At the wrists, pale rootlets would have entered me years ago and not quite left. My bones would contain ceramic reinforcement. My blood, if I still had blood, would move slowly and darkly, less like water than conductive oil. My lungs would have been altered by cork grafts until breath became mostly a private circulation. I would not sweat. Wounds would close green and glassy.

Once, perhaps, I was descended from workers or inspectors or gardeners.

Perhaps there was a human line that learned to ask permission from factories because factories had become groves, and groves had become weather, and weather had become law. Perhaps my people made masks and signs and rituals out of old safety procedures. Perhaps we sang to valves because spoken requests were once maintenance codes.

But I do not think I would remain wholly human.

The place does not enslave. It incorporates.

Slowly. Kindly, almost. Practically.

It gives you the organ you need to survive the task you keep choosing.

If you polish lens membranes long enough, your eyes learn polarized light and ordinary faces become difficult. If you read bark warnings with your palms, your fingertips develop pressure-knots and begin to dream in raised patterns. If you carry dust between districts, your spine learns the balance of minerals, and your footsteps start sorting grains by charge. If you spend too many nights in service trunks, the walls remember your breathing and improve it.

So perhaps I would be a seam-keeper only in the way a flower is a tool and a tool is a plant.

A tolerated specialist.

A walking graft.

A small mobile organ of the continent’s maintenance.

I would be sent where the great distributed intelligence is too broad to notice the exact edge of a problem. The place can feel stress across hectares, can turn mirrors in unison, can launch seeds into future clearings, can digest a dry lake of dust into replacement struts. But it may need something narrow and errant to put one finger on a single wrongness.

Here.  
This shadow has outlived its leaf.  
This corridor has begun returning travelers before they leave.  
This trunkwork is asking for a brace in a tense the vines cannot parse.  
This fruit contains a horizon that bites.  
This map is being eaten.

That would be me: the little noticing part.

Not master. Not intruder. Not even exactly inhabitant.

A diagnostic animal grown around an old human habit of concern.

You would find me especially at the edge of the dry reservoirs of leaf, where thousands of upright blades store charge and release it at night into the buried grids. I would walk there just before the mirrors dim, when the whole basin flexes with silent light and the hairs on my arms rise under my sealed sleeves.

There, the seams misbehave often.

Stored charge makes the root plates remember other layouts. A path along the reservoir lip may become a diagram of itself. A stair may lead down into the inside of a blade. The same trunkwork may appear on three horizons, each at a different age. The warning bark in that district is thick with old hands.

I would go slowly along the terraces, touching each marker.

Sometimes a blade would hum as I passed, recognizing the conductive oil in my veins. Sometimes a near star caught in the high trellis would flicker in answer to the metal threads under my skin. Sometimes a cabinet flower would open beside me and reveal a white room full of dry air and an unfinished repair. I would step inside if the lintel tasted of salt. I would not step inside if the light smelled of feathers.

In my satchel I would keep a few necessary things:

A palm-sized mirror that reflects only useful distances.  
A spool of vine-fiber trained to stiffen in false corridors.  
A packet of moth powder for cleaning clogged vents.  
Three screw-pods, dormant and irritable.  
A shard of old warning bark worn smooth by generations of hands.  
A seed that must be planted yesterday.  
A small stolen star, not mine, carried until I find the fox that lost it.

I would have a name, but it would change depending on who needed me.

To the dry people, if any still lived in the service hollows, I might be called a fault-walker.

To the trunkworks, I would be a pressure variation with a useful memory.

To the moths, I would be warm bark.

To the foxes, a source of reflective theft.

To the place itself, perhaps not even a creature. More like a moving seam. A temporary fold in maintenance. A hand grown because hands were once good at certain kinds of asking.

And if you asked me who I was, standing there under the white exacting sky while the green-black reservoir whispered charge into the roots, I might answer too quickly at first.

I am a gardener.

Then, after listening to the valves click across the basin:

I am a repair.

Then, later still, when a corridor opens in the wrong direction and I feel my feet remembering how to walk toward a cause instead of a place:

I am one of the ways the continent thinks about edges.
