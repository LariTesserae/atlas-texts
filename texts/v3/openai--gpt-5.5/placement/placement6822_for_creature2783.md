I would not be in the open floor.

At first I think I might be one of the black-glass hunters, because stillness here has power, and the flat creatures seem closest to the basin’s law: wait, seal, become stone, open only when the world makes a mistake. But that is too clean an answer. Too singular. Too much like choosing a knife because the place is sharp.

Then I think I might be one of the films, the bruised pasture on the walls, neither plant nor animal, eating heat and mineral breath in fractions too slow to be called hunger. But I am not patient enough to be only a stain. I need some hinge, some withheld motion. I need a body that remembers shelter.

I would find myself at the edge of the basin, not at its center: in the low ring of fitted stones beneath the collapsed arch that leads nowhere. Half under a salt lip, half wedged into an old gap where basalt blocks have slumped together, I would be mistaken for part of the ruin until the heat changed.

I would be one of the broad-backed sheltering animals.

A wall-maker, though not by intention.

A living door.

My body would be low, oval, and plated, the color of fused ash and violet-black clinker, with pale salt collecting in the seams between my shields. My back would be rough enough to catch dust and hard enough for smaller creatures to cross without knowing they had crossed me. Along my sides, beneath overlapping margins, would be narrow breath-slits that open only when the air is right. My limbs would be short, folded under me most of the time, ending in hooked pads for gripping stone and dragging chips. My head would be little more than a wedge of mouthparts and sensory threads, tucked beneath the front plate where no softness shows.

I would live by narrowing entrances.

Not building, exactly. Not thinking, here is a house, here is a threshold, here is safety. The habit would be older than thought. When a salt flake loosens, I drag it inward. When a shard of basalt cools after splitting, I brace it against another. When the heat rises too strongly, I seal the gap with my own back. When the cool contraction comes, I shift just enough to let the smaller lives pass under me.

Behind me there would be a chamber no larger than a cupped hand, but in this basin that is a hall, a nursery, a refuge, a vault. Eggs would be glued to its ceiling in hard dull rows. Young would stack themselves in the side cracks, miniature and complete, their plates still pale but already sealed. Mites would live under my rim. Threadlike scavengers would wait in the deeper dust. A gray-amber film would thicken on the stone just beyond my reach, and in lean cycles I would scrape it down with slow, careful movements.

I would not be generous. Nothing here is generous.

But I would be useful.

When the large predators pass, my chamber would close. When the winged brittle things fall stunned from a failed current, their bodies would be dragged inward and divided. When a grazer molts too near my entrance, the shed plates would become roofing. When one of my young dies, the shell would be worked into the wall. Nothing would be mourned in a wet way. Nothing would be wasted.

My senses would not tell me a world of colors and distances. I would know pressure, warmth, bitterness, trace. I would know the mountain’s breath before it came, the faint tightening under the salt plates, the mineral glow shifting through my carapace as heat rose from below. I would know the passage of a predator by the sudden absence of tiny feet. I would know a fertile bloom by the taste of stone changing. I would know danger as a pattern of stillness too broad to be ordinary.

Most of my life would look like not living.

Days, if they can be called days in that enclosed dark, would pass with only the smallest adjustments: a limb unfolding, a plate lifting, a mouth scraping once across black-purple film. My back would become part of the architecture. Dust would settle on me. Salt would rim me. Smaller animals would use me as a ledge. The old ring of fitted stones would continue through my body, and my body would continue the ring.

If something soft came into the basin, I would not rush toward it. Others would. The needle-mouths, the mites, the flat hinged hunters. I would taste the change from where I was and wait. Later, when the exposed thing had become fragments, when the fragments had become powder and trace, some part of it might arrive at my threshold as grit stuck to the feet of grazers, as residue on shed shell, as a darkened flake dragged by my young.

Then it too would enter the wall.

That is what I would be: not the master of the basin, not its victim, not its voice.

I would be one of its small continuities.

A keeper of a gap.

A creature whose instinct, repeated for generations, becomes mistaken for ruin. A body that turns survival into masonry. A dry, plated animal holding shut a little darkness beneath a collapsed arch on the shoulder of the black mountain, while all around me the crowded hollow listens, heats, contracts, opens, feeds, and seals again.
