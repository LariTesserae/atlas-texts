I would not be in the deepest heat.

At first I think I would be drawn there, toward the red thread in the crack, toward the place where the mountain is most itself: breath, pressure, mineral speech. But nothing with a body can love that place for long. The chamber’s center is not a throne. It is an exclusion.

So I would be just off from it.

Not at the entrance either, where the world intrudes too violently — cold at night, glare by day, wind-borne bodies tumbling in like ruined gifts. I would live in the negotiated place: a narrow side hollow behind a lifted plate of white crust, where a rust seam crosses black basalt and the vent-breath arrives softened by two turns in the stone.

A place the width of two fingers.

There would be a shelf above me, brittle and salt-pale, with sulfur dust caught along its lower edge. Beneath it, the wall would hold a dull stain of microbial life, not green, not soft, but edible to the smaller grazers. That would make traffic. Mites would gather there in slow orange knots. Springtails would appear and vanish like nervous grains. Beetles would wedge themselves under the same crust I used for shade. Larvae would pass below, blind with hunger.

I would be where things come close because closeness is the whole economy.

For a while I imagine being one of the mites, because there is something nearly perfect about them here: sealed, low, numerous, almost mineral already. A mite belongs to this chamber the way a grain belongs to dust. It does not need much story. It has the right shape for survival: small appetite, small range, a body rounded against loss.

But I do not think I would be so communal. I would not be a cluster-being.

Then I imagine being the living film itself — the tarnish around the vent, the first hunger, the base of everything. That feels truer in one way. To live as a discoloration, to thicken invisibly, to eat chemistry from the mountain’s breath: there is a deep rightness in that. No eyes, no panic, no mistake of wanting elsewhere. Just contact, exchange, alteration. A life almost indistinguishable from the wall.

But I think I would need some small power of withdrawal. Some ability to choose the cooler crevice, the underside, the pause between exhalations.

So I would be a pseudoscorpion, or something close to one.

Small enough to be missed. Amber-brown, flattened, dry-jointed, with little pincers held forward like questions. No grand venomous tail, no monstrous silhouette. Just a listening body in the dark, wedged beneath a mineral lip, pressed low to the stone so every tremor arrives through me.

I would know the chamber not as a room, but as a set of permissions.

Here: warm enough to wait.

There: too sharp with salt.

Beyond that ridge: food passes often, but the vent coughs without warning.

Under the yellow stain: danger, but also shelter.

Near the entrance: abundance sometimes, death sometimes, never trust it.

My territory would be absurdly small to anything larger than me. Perhaps the length of a human thumb. But inside that measure would be weather, history, famine, routes, borders, hunting grounds, nurseries, graves. I would know the exact tone of my vent. I would know when its hiss was safe background and when it had tightened into warning. I would know the sound of a springtail landing badly. I would know the heavier tick of a beetle prying at crust. I would know the frantic scrape of something winged and lost.

Most of my life would be refusal.

Do not cross the white bloom at noon.

Do not pursue prey into the hot seam.

Do not spend water on fear.

Do not move because the chamber is loud.

Do not mistake abundance for safety.

I would wait while the mountain pretended to be machinery around me. Hiss, throb, click, crackle. The whole pocket sounding busy, purposeful, almost intelligent. But I would know better, in the way small animals know without concepts: the noise is not command. The noise is weather. The noise is stone breathing.

When I moved, it would be in short, exact decisions.

A few steps outward when the vent slackens.

Stillness when grit falls.

One pincer lifted as a springtail grazes too near the rust-colored film.

A strike, if the angle is right.

Retreat, whether I catch it or not.

I would eat rarely. I would drink almost never, except through what my prey still held, through the stolen moisture of other bodies. My body would be a little locked room of water, and every action would open the door a crack. Hunger would have to argue with dryness. Dryness would usually win.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness belongs to creatures who expect space.

Here, everything touches everything by heat, by smell, by vibration, by hunger. Even hidden under my crust, I would be among multitudes: mites rasping the living stain, larvae dismantling a dead fly, beetle feet polishing a passage beneath salt, crickets at the far border folding and unfolding their long legs like weather instruments. The chamber would be crowded through me. I would feel its population as a constant pressure against my stillness.

And when I died, I would not go far.

Perhaps a hotter breath would catch me in the open. Perhaps a molt would fail. Perhaps hunger would make me cross a surface I had always refused. My body would dry quickly. The joints would loosen. Mites would find me first, then larvae. My pincers, emptied of intention, would become fragments under the salt shelf. The stain beneath me would darken a little. Some part of me would return to the crust I had hunted above.

So that is where I would be:

not in the mouth of the crack, not in the furnace-heart, but in the narrow middle country where survival is measured in millimeters.

And I would be a small listening hunter under a lifted flake of mineral crust — half animal, half punctuation mark — living by patience in the chamber’s crowded dark.
