I would not be in the final plains, not yet.

Those oldest, almost featureless districts would be too complete for me—the low green pelt over the sleeping mountain, the places where individuality has already widened into geology. I would feel them below me like a thought too large to enter. Nor would I belong in the upper water, where distance folds back on itself and towers become ancient instead of far. I would not trust myself there. I would keep looking for a surface.

I think I would be found at a threshold.

A shallow ravine, broad and soft-sided, where the silt is the color of bruised pearl. One of the repeating ravines, though not a dangerous one. A place that can be entered twice from different directions and still be the same place, except for one altered detail: a leaning wall, a missing stone, a corridor of vine that was not open before.

Across it runs the causeway that has no road leading to it.

It crosses from one shelf of worn basalt to another and then stops being useful. Its fitted blocks are no longer square. Pale crust has swollen in the seams. Red filament works slowly at the underside. The edges have begun to bow, not from collapse, but from persuasion. A few bladder trees grow along its spine, their sacs half-filled, half-dreaming. Their long cords hang down into the ravine and comb the passing currents.

Nearby is the roofless chamber with the white sponge tree.

That is where I would return, again and again, though I would not live inside it exactly. The chamber sits in a basin of fine silt so easily disturbed that every arrival becomes visible for hours. Its walls are low, softened, invaded by root and mineral bloom. The white sponge rises from the center like a lung that learned to remember instead of breathe. When the currents are quiet, it releases a faint chime from inside its hollows: not music, not speech, but stored names rubbing against one another.

I would live between the causeway and the chamber.

Not in a house. Not in a body that could be pointed to for very long.

At first I think I am one of the pale eel-shapes.

Young, narrow, almost flat, with translucent fins like torn banners running along my sides. My body would be mostly a way of sensing: warmth along the belly, pressure along the spine, mineral taste through the skin. I would have no fixed face. I would have eyes for a while—too many small dark beads opening along my front half—and then I would lose most of them as I learned better forms of knowing.

I would be a scout, but not a hunter.

A path-taster.

A finder of safe confusions.

When travelers entered that district, I would know before they did. Not by sight, but by the change in the water: the brief, sharp chemistry of fear; the clumsy pressure of limbs that believe space should remain consistent; the warmth of blood moving too quickly for the place. I would follow at a distance, pale as a strand of shed membrane, slipping through velvet weed and under the bowed causeway. If they panicked, I would close the nearest kelp corridor. If they stilled themselves, I would open another.

I would not guide them where they wished to go.

I would guide them where arrival would do the least harm.

Sometimes that would mean turning them gently back toward the lower terraces, where distance behaves more honestly. Sometimes it would mean drawing them toward the sponge archive, so that the chamber could taste their names. Sometimes, if they carried grief strongly enough, I would lead them to the ravine during dusk, when the plateau’s buried faults glow gray-green and the old mountain range shows through the drowned world like a skeleton remembering height.

I would be young enough to move, old enough to hesitate.

The inhabitants would not call me a child, because childhood is too brief a word. I would be an unrooted stage of the drowned people: a ribbon of appetite, memory, and errand. One of those who still crosses open water before choosing a basin, a vent, a ruin, a grove. I would belong to no single elder yet, though several would have claims on me. The causeway would know the mineral pattern in my cartilage. The sponge tree would have stored a few of my shed skins. A bladder grove upstream would have fed me when I was a white knot in a crack.

My work would be attention.

That would be the secret of me.

Not strength, not speech, not wisdom. Attention.

I would watch the fronds that only open under a gaze. I would hold still before them until they unfolded, drinking the fact of being noticed. I would look at the right angles left in the ruin and feel where they hurt the pressure. I would attend to stones that had almost forgotten they were arranged. I would swim slowly around a newly exposed block until pale crust began to gather at its corner. I would learn the small patience by which architecture becomes ecology again.

My face, when I needed one, would be made for others.

For a frightened traveler, I might gather a brow and two dim lantern spots, something nearly recognizable, something that says: not predator, not yet. For one of my own elders, I would smooth myself flat and speak through pressure along my whole length. For the sponge archive, I would press my underside to its white branches and release bubbles in measured strings, each bubble containing a taste: iron, skin, spoken sound, recent fear, old sunlight imagined from another creature’s memory.

I would carry names.

That is why I would remain near the chamber.

Not names as humans keep them, crisp and separate, but names softened by water: repeated syllables, salt patterns, grief-shapes, the pressure left by someone turning toward the last light they understood. If a traveler died in the ravine, I would help continue them. I would take a strand of hair, a flake of bone, the last warmth leaving the mouth, and bring it to the sponge tree. Years later, perhaps, when a current struck the hollows correctly, their name would return as a faint chime from the chamber floor.

I would also carry questions.

A field of fronds leaning toward a crack: question.

A corridor opened through a vine labyrinth: question.

A silt storm released too early in the season: question.

A ring of upright stones all facing the wrong direction after a gravity-loosening cycle: question.

I would move among these things and taste the difference between warning, invitation, memory, and hunger. I would not always understand. Understanding would be too fast. Instead I would bring the question to a warmer current, to an elder rooted in a terrace, to a grove whose bladder sacs fill and empty in council. If the question mattered, it might take seasons to answer. If it mattered more deeply, it might be answered by the slow rearrangement of a valley.

I would fear the upper water.

Even as one born there, I would fear it.

Not with panic, but with respect. The unreachable ceiling where distance returns swimmers to themselves would trouble me. I would have gone there once as a restless adolescent, following a lantern-eyed wanderer through blue-black mineral layers and suspended spores. We would have seen a stone tower close enough to touch. We would have swum toward it for three days while it became older, not nearer, until its blocks were not blocks but the intention of blocks before hands existed.

I would come back from that journey with one eye sealed in amber.

Not damaged exactly. Preserved in a season that had not happened yet. A little dark bead under my translucent skin, seeing elsewhere. Sometimes it would show me the ravine before the ravine repeated. Sometimes it would show me a traveler’s death before their arrival. Sometimes it would show only a red sun too large for this world, shining through water that remembered being sky.

The elders would leave it in me.

They would say, by turning their pale undersides toward the dark, that some wrongnesses are useful if carried gently.

Over time I would begin to root.

It would start as an ache when I passed the causeway. A desire not to leave. Thin white threads would slip from my lower fins while I slept against the basalt. At first I would tear them free and keep swimming. Later I would allow a few to remain, trailing behind me like forgotten thoughts. They would find cracks in the softened masonry. They would taste lime, iron, old mortar, the fingerprints of builders who may have been my ancestors or descendants.

I would still travel, but less.

My body would broaden. The eel-ribbon would become a mantle. Calcified spars would rise inside me like antlers or drowned rafters. My temporary face would grow slower to appear and slower to vanish. Grazers would cling to my underside. Red filament would creep over my back in deliberate lines, and I would let it eat the mineral I no longer needed.

Eventually I would become part of the threshold I had tended.

Not a guardian in the hard sense. Nothing here guards by refusing passage. I would become a current-shaper, a living lintel, a half-rooted resident of the useless causeway. My membranes would hang beneath its arches. My filaments would trail into the ravine. My bladder sacs would rise among the trees above, indistinguishable from theirs except during dusk, when my inner tissues would glow with the same gray-green as the buried faults.

The causeway would no longer be useless then.

It would be an organ of decision.

Those who crossed above it would be gently sorted. Fearful ones turned aside by warm currents. Grieving ones drawn toward the sponge chamber. Violent ones delayed in repeating ravines until their violence tired and settled. Calm ones allowed to see the old stair that climbs and stops in open water, because some destinations are meant to teach incompletion.

Who would I be, finally?

A witness-creature.

A softener of edges.

A keeper of names in transit between breath and sponge.

One of the drowned people before they become grove, after they have stopped being animal, while they still remember motion.

I would live where the old made road crosses the old natural ravine and neither quite admits what it is anymore. I would be the thing that helps them become each other. A pale ribbon first, then a mantle, then a bowed presence under algae-furred stone. I would watch. I would open leaves by looking. I would carry the dead into continuation. I would learn which distances are miles and which are centuries.

And when the long dusk passed through the high ocean, I would hang motionless beneath the causeway, every filament extended, feeling the mountain range glow under the silt.

For a few hours, I would remember my future shape:

not a person,

not a ruin,

not a plant,

but a patient curve in the world’s pressure,

alive enough to answer,

old enough to answer slowly.
