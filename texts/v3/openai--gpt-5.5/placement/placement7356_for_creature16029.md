At first I think I would be on one of the larger islands, somewhere with enough soil for a crooked greenhouse and enough people for voices at night. I imagine a low house with metal shutters, a stove that never quite dries the socks above it, a workbench under a window, a radio muttering pressure readings. That seems likely. Safe, or as safe as anything there becomes.

But the place pulls me away from the settled backs of the islands.

I would be found at a narrow crossing.

Not the worst one. Not the famous channel where boats have been lost and people lower their voices without noticing. A smaller pass, half-practical, half-forgotten, between two drowned shoulders of old mountain. On maps it might have an official name, but no one uses it. The basin people would call it something like Needlewater, or Low Cable, or the Green Throat, because of the way the current comes up pale there when rainwater pours over the buried ridge.

There would be an old funicular line descending into the water nearby: three towers still visible, the fourth only showing its crown in dry years, the rest vanishing down the drowned slope. A cable would run from one island to another above the pass, not elegant, not new, singing whenever the wind turned north. Below it, the water would never lie flat. It would wrinkle even on calm days, pulled by the hidden shape of stone beneath.

That is where I would be.

I would live in a maintenance hut built into the lee of a black rock dome, its roof mossed despite all attempts to scrape it clear. The hut would have been many things before me: relay shed, survey post, storage room, temporary shelter, abandoned shell, then home again. Its concrete would sweat. Its steel door would need shouldering open in winter. There would be a narrow dock below, rebuilt from three different docks and one section of old roadway, all lashed and bolted and corrected after every storm.

I would be human, I think. But not a central sort of human. Not a leader, not a priest, not a legend.

I would be a keeper of crossings.

A cable tender. A weather listener. A part-time salvage diver when the water allowed it. One of those basin people whose job is not one job but a braid of necessary attentions: check the line tension, clear reed mats from the guide pulleys, listen for bad harmonics in the cable, take pressure readings, ferry tools, mark debris, mend floats, haul drowned timber away from the intake boom, report seal damage to nets, help recover what storms misplace.

People would know me by the pass more than by my name.

“Ask the one at Low Cable.”

“Don’t cross until they call it.”

“They know when that water turns.”

I would have a small boat with an ugly patched hull and a motor that starts only after insult and negotiation. I would trust it more than I should because I would know each of its failures personally. There would be coils of rope everywhere I live: good rope, bad rope, emergency rope, rope too short to throw away, rope retired from lifting but still useful for tying down tarps. My hands would be nicked and swollen-knuckled from wet metal. My pockets would contain a knife, pencil stubs, washers, a grease rag, a whistle, and bits of reed seed I never remember picking up.

I would keep ledgers.

Not beautiful ones. Practical books with oilcloth covers, pages rippled despite care. In them I would write the ordinary scripture of the basin:

- western wind before dawn, water black over saddle  
- crane pair returned to upper marsh  
- seal at No. 2 platform, left eye scarred  
- cable hum changed after noon  
- floating alder raft pinned against south tower  
- green boil rising early, do not send basket loaded  
- frog hatch in roof pool  
- found child’s red glove, Three Roof Bay side  

The ledgers would be part maintenance record, part weather history, part memory of hazards. No one would read them straight through, but people would ask after them when something went wrong. Old entries would matter suddenly. A storm ten years ago would explain a current today. A beaver dam noted in irritation would become the reason a marsh shifted and a safe landing vanished.

I would not be brave in a clean way. I would be cautious, superstitious in practical forms, easily angered by carelessness. I would shout at children for standing too near winches. I would refuse crossings when others thought I was being dramatic. I would distrust three calm days in a row. I would tap bolts with a hammer and listen to the answer. I would smell weather in the wrongness of warm rain.

And still I would love the place.

Not gently. Not romantically.

I would love it because I had been worn into usefulness by it.

The pass would teach me its moods until they entered my body. I would know the difference between ordinary cable song and the thin, high sound that means ice forming somewhere up the line. I would know when the water’s color meant peat, when it meant silt, when it meant a submerged bank had let go. I would know which floating gardens could be towed and pinned into a cove to make future ground, and which were only rot held together by hope. I would know the gulls that always returned to the rusted tower and the otter that raided fish boxes near the lower dock and the goat that appeared each spring on the roof of the old relay shed by a route no one had ever seen.

There would be a dry shelf in my hut where I kept things found in the crossing.

Not valuables. The basin does not give up valuables often, and when it does, they are usually more trouble than they are worth.

I would keep small, stubborn objects: a brass button greened by water; a ceramic fuse; a carved wooden float; a child’s marble rolled smooth by silt; a metal sign whose letters had become unreadable except for “CAUTION”; a tea cup lifted intact from a drowned kitchen by a diver who owed me money; a bird skull; a gear tooth; a sealed jar of pale sand from a beach that appears only when the lake drops low enough to remember being land.

People might think I collected the past.

I would say I collected warnings.

Each thing would have survived by accident. Each would remind me that survival is not virtue. It is attachment, buoyancy, shelter, timing, being overlooked by the force that takes everything else.

I would be most myself in bad weather, though I would hate saying so.

During a squall the hut would become an instrument. Rain on roof. Cable through wall-brackets. Dock chains. Loose panel at the old tower. Boom of debris against the outer posts. Radio static. Kettle shaking on the stove. All of it making a map before the eyes could. I would stand in the storm porch in oilcloth, hood drawn tight, watching the crossing vanish and reappear. The water would not be scenery then. It would be speech.

Not mystical speech. Instruction.

Tie that down.

Do not launch.

The wind has shifted.

The raft is moving.

The channel is rising.

Someone is late.

If someone were late, I would become very still. I would listen past the rain for an engine, for a horn, for the knock of a hull against submerged wood. I would imagine all the possible mistakes: a fouled prop, bad judgment, loose fuel line, panic, fog, arrogance, a child leaning too far, a seal in the nets, a wave taken crosswise. I would hate the imagination for being useful.

If the call came, I would go out.

Not because I was fearless. Because I knew where the old ridge ran under the water, and because fear is not an exemption from duty in a place like that.

I think I would age early there.

Everyone does, perhaps. The damp gets into joints. The cold teaches the hands to close slowly. The eyes narrow from looking through rain. I would develop the basin habit of pausing before answering, not from wisdom but from listening: to wind, to engines, to whether the roof drip has changed tempo. I would become hard to visit for long. I would offer tea too strong and soup too salty. I would complain about anyone who tied a knot badly. I would keep spare gloves for strangers and pretend not to notice when they left with them.

Children would like my place because it would contain dangerous things arranged carefully. Hooks, pulleys, signal lamps, cracked gauges, old helmets, floats painted in colors no longer made. I would be stern with them, then teach them knots anyway. I would show them how to read wave break over buried stone. I would tell them never to trust a floating log unless they could see both ends and never to step on moss near black water without testing it first. If they asked about the drowned towers, I would tell them what they were for. If they asked whether people used to ride down the mountainside in the air, I would say yes, and we would both look at the cable disappearing into rain.

I would not tell many stories about monsters.

I would tell stories about wrong assumptions.

The man who thought a quiet bay meant safe water.

The crew who forgot that wet rope shrinks.

The family that built under the old high mark because the view was good.

The diver who came up laughing and then could not stop shaking.

The seal that learned to wait below the cleaning table.

The year the moss took the roof so completely that a duck nested over my bed and I did not discover it until the eggs hatched.

If I became anything other than human, it would happen gradually and only in metaphor.

I would become one of the edge-creatures.

Not moss, though I would respect moss above all things.

Not seal, though I would envy their ease in the cold water.

Not bird, though I would watch them for weather.

I would become something like a marker buoy that has been repaired so often no original piece remains. A human buoy. A warning post with hands. A damp witness fixed at a dangerous seam between one kind of place and another.

My life would be to say: here, the basin changes.

Here, the old land rises under the water.

Here, the crossing may be made.

Here, wait.

Here, tie twice.

Here, listen.

And when I died, I would not want a grave with much ceremony. I would want my ashes sealed in a plain ceramic jar and placed in one of the concrete niches above the pass, where the wall stays wet but does not flood except in the worst years. Someone would scratch my name, badly, because neat lettering is hard in cold weather. Beside the jar they might put my knife if it were too worn to use, or a little brass pulley from the old cable line.

But part of me would remain more accurately elsewhere: in the ledgers, in corrected crossings, in children who became careful adults, in a rope splice somebody learned from my hands, in a refusal spoken at the right hour.

You would find me at the Green Throat, or Low Cable, or whatever name the basin people finally gave it.

A human being, mostly.

A keeper of one wet edge.

Someone who lives by listening to water pass over drowned stone, and by knowing that nothing stays dry, nothing stays empty, and no crossing is ever the same crossing twice.
