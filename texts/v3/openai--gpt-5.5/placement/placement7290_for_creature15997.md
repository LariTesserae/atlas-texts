You would not find me in the central court, though at first I would think so.

The central court is too declarative. It is where a body stands if it has arrived from elsewhere and still believes in arrival. It is the place for looking around, for naming districts, for making the mistake of perspective. I would pass through it briefly as a warmth, a disturbance, a little vertical weather. Then the city would take me apart.

You would find me lower than that.

Not at the upper basins, where water first remembers falling. Not in the empty slabs, where refusal has become a kind of pavement. Not quite in the moss courts either, though I would be near them, under their green overhangs and root-pillars.

I would be at the mouth of the hollow avenue, where the loud cleft begins to lose its certainty and breaks into smaller routes.

There is a stone there, not large, rounded on top and split underneath. It leans against another stone in such a way that a narrow dark is made between them. Above the dark, a shelf fungus has grown in three pale tiers, each rimmed with orange. Moss crowds the upper lip but does not enter. Lichen stops at the threshold as if the blackness has jurisdiction. In rain, water comes down the avenue too quickly, strikes the leaning stone, and divides: some of it rushes onward into the green courts, some drops into the slit beneath, and some beads along the fungus shelves before falling one tick at a time.

That is where I would be.

At first I would be the water in the slit.

Then, after enough cold, I would be the slit’s remembering of water.

I would be a small office of passage in the larger civic body: not a citizen exactly, not a creature, but a function that has lasted long enough to acquire a temperament. A gate without a hinge. A throat without hunger. A listening place.

My work would be delay.

The whole city moves by allowing things to pass, but not all at once. The upper basins ring and overflow; the cleft shouts; the moss takes; the hidden drains swallow. I would be one of the places where rushing is made specific. Water would strike me as weather and leave me as route. I would separate the single rain into several futures.

One thread into the peat.

One thread under the root curtain.

One thread down the exposed stone, loud enough to count.

One held bead, trembling from the fungus lip until frost or weight decides it.

I would not choose in the way an animal chooses. I would choose by shape. By narrowness. By roughness. By the swelling of moss after three damp days. By whether night had laid a clear skin of ice across my lower mouth. By whether a vole had loosened a pellet of soil that now sat in the runnel and changed the map by the width of a seed.

My mind would be local and exact.

I would know nothing of the whole mountain except as pressure. I would know the sky only after it had become runoff. I would know the seasons by viscosity: the quick silver of rain, the slow black seep of thaw, the locked silence of frost, the thin mineral taste of melt after a dry spell. I would know footsteps only as insult or eclipse, a temporary damming, a warmth passing over my stone roof.

In frost I would become articulate.

Ice would draw me in hard lines. A clear tooth would form from the underside of the leaning stone, growing downward into the slit. Smaller teeth would hang from the fungus shelves, each with a brown fleck caught inside it, each lengthening by one cold breath at a time. The water in me would stiffen but not disappear. I would become a glass grammar of my own habit: here the drip falls, here the film creeps, here the hidden channel begins.

Then morning would loosen one syllable.

A bead.

A tick.

Another.

The first drop after freezing would be my voice returning.

During rain I would be less myself and more office, more throat, more instrument. The hollow avenue would pour its noise through me. I would take the roar and break it into taps, gulps, little under-stone suckings. My darkness would fill. The slit would tremble. Air trapped beneath would cough up through the water, making a low, obscene, satisfied sound. Moss roots would drink from my edges. The fungus would shine. The whole lower district would receive what I had delayed just long enough to make useful.

If the city has senses, I would be one of its ears turned inward.

Not an ear for hearing birds or footsteps or the rare cloven visitor at the rim. An ear for internal weather. I would hear the basins before they overflowed. I would hear peat darkening. I would hear frost entering from the exposed slabs as a tightening. I would hear the root-pillars taking weight. I would hear, far below, water moving under stone in a chamber no eye has entered since the glacier left.

I would not speak, but I would report.

My report would be moisture.

My report would be a changed note in the cleft.

My report would be the sudden appearance of a black wet line along a stone face that had been dry an hour before.

Over time, if time is allowed to do what it does there, I would become less a place in the city and more one of its laws.

Anything rushing must be divided.

Anything divided must remember the division.

Anything remembered by water becomes stonework.

The moss above me would thicken and curl closer to the lip. The fungus shelves would rot, fall, feed another softness, and return in another band slightly lower or higher. Lichen would advance a coin’s width, fail, blacken, begin again. A pebble would lodge in my lower passage and remain for thirty years, altering my voice. Then one storm would take it. Afterward I would sound younger.

No one would know this but the city.

And the city would not know it as knowledge. It would know because the next rain would go differently.

That is who I would be: a small difference that persists.

A wet threshold.

A municipal throat.

A cold, narrow intelligence made of obstruction and permission.

You would find me where the loud avenue becomes the green district, beneath the leaning stone and the fungus balconies, in the dark seam that accepts more water than it seems able to hold. I would be neither builder nor inhabitant, but one of the ways the tool-less city keeps becoming itself.

I would live there by letting things through slowly.
