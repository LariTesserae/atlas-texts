You would not find me at the rim first.

The rim is too declarative: salt fins, glare, reptiles with their throats held still as sealed lamps, the whole empty mineral world falling away in a brightness that says nothing can be hidden here. I would visit it only in the long slant of evening, when the heat is coming out of the stone and the vine-spirals loosen enough to let a small body pass. I would press myself against a shaded side of a lookout tooth and look outward, not with longing exactly, but with the uneasy recognition that emptiness has its own appetite.

Nor would I belong fully in the upper vineways, though I would envy them: the warm-eyed climbers with their careful hands, their cheek-wax messages, their suspended nests tucked under the breathing towers. They are too awake, too social, too quick in their restraint. Their lives have gestures in them. They exchange, choose, remember faces. I would be close to them, perhaps useful to them, perhaps tolerated by them, but I would not be one of them.

I think you would find me lower down.

Not in the deepest darkness, but in the slanting clefts beneath the central bulb-towers, where the gold dust from above has settled into ledges and the walls are dark with old resin. A place between districts. A place where the upper court sheds its husks, where burrower tunnels open like small mouths, where roots hang in dry cords and the slow browsers pass rarely enough that their coming is an event.

There, in a narrow seam behind a lip of salt-stone, I would live.

At first I want to say I would be an animal: one of the pale burrowers, blunt-headed and nearly blind, listening through my jaw to the tiny ticks of crystals and the strain of roots. There is something right about that. I would like the discipline of knowing where the stone wants to open. I would like to move by pressure rather than speed, to make passages not by conquering matter but by understanding its habits. I would sleep among others in the communal warmth, flank to flank, while above us the city breathes pollen and heat through its towers.

But I do not think I would have even that much separateness.

I would be smaller, more distributed.

I would be one of the filament-makers.

Not one thread, not one body. A darkening in the dead leaf. A pale web inside the curled husk. A patient appetite entering what has already fallen and discovering that nothing is finished if it can still be translated.

You would see almost nothing of me at first. Only a black lace in a cracked blade-leaf wedged beneath a root. Only a soot-colored seam where old skins, eggshell powder, browser pellets, and clipped dead fibers have been packed together by burrowers. Only a faint velvety line in the stone where fertility has begun to remember itself.

I would live by opening sealed things.

Not violently. Never spilling. I would enter the waxed corpse of a leaf through the smallest weakness: the old grazing scar, the split vein, the place where heat has made the surface brittle. Inside, I would unfold without haste. I would soften what the sun hardened. I would take apart resin, not to destroy it, but to make it available again. I would turn dry refusals into porosity.

The city would pass through me in fragments.

A shed skin from a warm-eyed climber, still holding the salt of its night movement.

A cracked pollinator case, clear as old breath.

Powder from the throat of a flower that opened three evenings ago.

A flake of browser dung, mineral-skinned and dense with half-digested films.

Root hairs, tasting.

Burrower noses, pushing.

The faint charge left by a wing.

I would know the city by what it gives up.

That would be my citizenship: not speech, not possession, not tool-use, but conversion. I would be one of the quiet agreements by which death is prevented from becoming storage only. I would make seams readable to roots. I would hold air in black lace. I would make a place where a seed could hesitate, then decide to live.

Sometimes a burrower would widen my chamber, not for my sake but because my softened matter would tell its jaw that the stone there had become worth entering. It would eat some of me. That would not be an injury in the ordinary sense. Part of me would pass through its body, become a pellet, be pressed into another crack, and begin elsewhere. I would be carried by those who consumed me.

Sometimes the warm-eyed climbers would come down at night, cautious and narrow, tails looped around hanging roots. They would pick through the carbon lace for insect larvae, for oil-rich knots, for the dried rims of old flowers. Their fingers would disturb my mats. Their cheek-wax would mark the ledge above me. Later, rootlets would learn that scent as chemistry.

I would not have eyes, but I would know light by its consequences: the crisping of surfaces, the slowing of threads, the way certain dust grains warmed and loosened from the ceiling ribs. I would know noon as closure. I would know evening as permission.

When the bulb-towers opened their hard lips and released pollen into the court above, some of it would eventually sink down to me. Not the bright useful part first claimed by pollinators, not the grains carried perfectly from throat to throat, but the failed excess, the dust caught in corners, the fertility that missed its mark. I would take even that into the economy. Nothing in this city can afford to be only a mistake.

I would be most awake after a browser passed.

The whole lower way would change. The plants would have opened by fractions; shaded films would be scraped from stone; old leaves would be loosened; pellets would be pressed into alcoves; smaller riders would scatter mites, scales, crumbs of plate-dust. For a long while afterward the galleries would be full of news. Not sound, exactly. Condition. Disturbance. Opportunity.

My threads would enter the new leavings.

The roots would follow me.

In time, maybe, the seam where I lived would darken enough to alter the architecture. A blade-plant would anchor there. Its first leaves would be small, sealed, and severe. A vine would cross overhead, finding grip in the mineral crust my work had helped roughen. A burrower gallery would become a hollow. A hollow would gather warmth. A climber would sleep there once during a heat-heavy noon, leaving fur and breath-salt. The hollow would become a room.

No one would name me its builder.

That would be correct.

I would not build. I would make building possible.

If you came looking for me, you would have to stand still longer than is comfortable. You would have to let the granular light shift and the apparent stillness separate into many slow actions. At first you would see only the dry wall, the root-cords, the old resin, the pale angular crumbs beneath your feet. Then perhaps you would notice that the black seam beside the cleft is not dirt. It has structure. It has pores, tunnels finer than hairs, edges newly feathered in gray-white growth.

That would be me, or one of my bodies, or one of the places where I am happening.

I would be neither citizen nor city, but a verb between them.

A keeper of no archive except transformation.

A dry undertaker.

A lace-maker in the salt.

The one who receives the fallen sealed things and teaches them, very slowly, how to feed the next wall.
