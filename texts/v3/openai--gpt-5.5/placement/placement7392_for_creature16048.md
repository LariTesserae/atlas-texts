You would not find me in the market first, though I would pass through it often.

Not among the loudest traders with their covered baskets of curd and roots, not beside the ovens, not in the central square where news thickens like smoke under the pale roof. I would belong a little to those places, because everyone belongs a little to every necessary place here, but I would not be centered there.

You would find me higher than the cistern courts, lower than the roof-workers’ ladders, in the district where the walls are still being argued with.

A narrow ledge district. Half dwelling, half scaffold. The kind of place where one side of a passage is plastered, lived-in, hung with cups and drying sleeves, while the other side is raw stone marked with chalk, wedges, warning cords, and black strokes no child is allowed to touch. There would be a seep nearby, unreliable but beloved, darkening the pale wall after storms and drying to a ghost-mark between rains. Someone would have set a row of small jars beneath it, each with a different sound when struck by a drop.

That is where I think I would be: near a wall that has not yet decided whether it is a wall, listening.

I would be a stone-listener’s assistant, perhaps. Not yet one of the old wall-tappers whose refusals can halt a family’s new room, but someone called for when a crack has changed its mind. Someone with lime dust in the creases of my fingers and a cord of little tools across my chest: a wrapped mallet, chalk, copper point, waxed string, a listening cup, a reed tube for testing drafts, a pouch of sand to scatter along a seam and watch for trembling.

I would have been born below, but not so long below that the surface is a myth. My mother would remember fields. My father would remember heat. I would remember only stories: rain arriving all at once instead of after being thought over by stone; trees with their roots hidden properly underground; birds as creatures, not shadows passing over a ceiling crack.

I would not be unhappy about that. I would mistrust open sky if I saw too much of it.

My work would be part caution, part memory. I would go where someone has heard a new tick in the plaster at night, where a ladder has begun to lean, where water appears from a seam that was dry last season. I would crouch in sleeping alcoves and ask people to be quiet, not because I am grand, but because the stone speaks badly through human noise. I would press my ear to cold planes of limestone and wait for the small betrayals: grit loosening, a hollow answer, a hidden run of water, the faint shifting knock that means weight is traveling somewhere it did not travel before.

I would be useful in an unheroic way.

No one would cheer when I arrived. They would only make room.

Children would watch me because children watch anyone with tools. Dogs would sniff my knees and then dismiss me. Old women would tell me I was tapping too high or too low, and sometimes they would be right. Masons would resent me when I marked a wall unsafe. Potters would like me better, because I would save them fine crack-dust and certain clays from new fractures. Catchment keepers would tolerate me as one of the people who understands that a beautiful new drip can be bad news.

I would have a quiet voice from habit, and a louder one I almost never used.

My dwelling would be small and inconvenient, wedged behind a ladder landing where the roof slopes too low for taller people. Inside: a sleeping shelf, a mat rolled tight by day, two good cups, one cracked cup I refuse to throw away, a covered lamp, a bundle of marked cords, a shelf of stone chips labeled by district, and a jar of water with a lid fitted so well I would take private pride in it. On the plaster wall I would keep a map, not of streets exactly, but of sounds: hollow places, trustworthy faces, wet seams, old collapses, walls that ring clean, walls that answer dull.

I would be the sort of person who touches things while passing them.

A ladder upright, to feel if it wobbles.

A cistern lid, to know if it has shifted.

A gutter edge, to clear a crumb of clay before it becomes a blockage.

A child’s shoulder, to stop them before they duck under a marked cord.

I would know many parts of the settlement but belong fully to none. Stone-workers are like that, I think. We enter private spaces at bad times. We see the undersides of households: the patched beam over the bed, the hidden crack behind the shrine niche, the place where a family has built too much weight onto a hopeful ledge. We are trusted because we must be, not always because we are loved.

Still, I would love the town.

Not romantically. Not as a grand city. I would love it the way people here love necessary things: by repairing it.

I would love the way light arrives in strips and makes even a broom handle briefly holy. I would love the dry rasp of sandals before dawn. I would love the smell of wet plaster after rain. I would love the severe beauty of a clean channel. I would love the hush that falls when everyone hears the first delayed drip after a storm. I would love how quickly hands appear when something must be lifted, sealed, braced, saved.

I would also be afraid often.

Afraid of a ceiling seam that darkens too fast.

Afraid of laughter in the wrong vault.

Afraid of a child sleeping beneath fresh plaster.

Afraid of the dry season entering people’s voices.

Afraid of being asked, “Will it hold?” when the honest answer is not yet.

That would be the hardest part of my life: the town’s need for certainty in a world still setting.

So I would become good at saying small true things.

“Not tonight.”

“Clear this shelf.”

“Brace it until the next rain.”

“Do not plaster over that.”

“This crack is old.”

“This one is not.”

“Listen.”

And perhaps, after years, I would become one of those people whose marks others obey even while grumbling. Three black strokes from my hand might empty a room. A chalk circle might save a stair. My refusals would make me unpopular; my correct warnings would make me necessary; my mistaken warnings would cost people labor and pride. I would carry all of that.

But there is another possibility beneath that one.

Maybe I am not only the listener.

Maybe I am the cup.

A small fired-clay listening cup, burnished dark by palms, carried in the pouch of a wall-tapper. Its rim chipped but not useless. Its inside smelling faintly of dust, ear-warmth, and old stone. Pressed against walls to gather what the naked ear misses. Passed from teacher to apprentice, from mother to daughter, from cautious hand to more cautious hand.

That would suit me too.

To be a made thing in a handmade town. To have one purpose: help someone hear what is already there.

But if I am living flesh, then yes: I am the wall-listener’s apprentice grown into a wall-listener myself.

You would find me at the edge of the inhabited and the unfinished, one knee on pale grit, one hand lifted for silence, my ear against the young stone.

I would be listening not for prophecy, not for spirits, not for secrets.

Only for whether the place that holds us is still willing to hold.
