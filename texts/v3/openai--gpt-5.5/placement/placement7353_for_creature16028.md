I would not live in the center at first.

At first I would think I belonged in the market, because everything there announces itself: fish in lifted globes, tubers steaming in split bark, salt-birds haggling from the rafters, children counting breaths across unreliable paving. I would stand there too long, dazzled, getting in the way of people who know when a stall has become a culvert and when a basket is heavier on the inside than the out. Someone would gently move me aside with the back of a wrist and say, “Not there. That patch is considering itself.”

So I would drift outward.

Not all the way to the marsh edge, where the civic engines breathe fog and older things make decisions in tones too low for bones. I would not be brave enough for that. Not yet.

I think I would find myself in the in-between district: behind the archive grove, beside the tea-house whose back room sometimes becomes a public corridor, near a listening well where the water repeats legal amendments in the voices of frogs.

There is a place there where new alleys are most likely to appear.

Not important alleys. Not grand roads. Small, damp, undecided passages grown overnight between one municipal memory and another. They arrive paved in young bark or pale fungal hide, smelling of sap and old rain, leading to courtyards no one remembers approving. Some close by noon. Some become shortcuts for three generations. Some are only organs the town has extended briefly to taste the weather.

That is where I would be.

I would be a minor civic witness.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not quite a cartographer. One of those necessary people whose work is too local to become famous and too useful to be ignored. I would be called when a path appears, when a door relocates, when a bridge lowers itself into water and pedestrians begin inventing a route through someone’s kitchen.

I would carry a slate of living bark under one arm, still attached to a thread of root that disappears beneath my sleeve. The slate would not show a map so much as a disagreement: trembling lines, damp stains, little pulse-marks where streets are thinking. I would touch it with wet fingers and murmur corrections.

“This corridor exists for now.”

“This wall has consented to passage until fruiting.”

“This pool is new but not hostile.”

“This stair is lying.”

My official title would be something practical and dull, like **Assistant Registrar of Provisional Ways**.

Children would call me **Mud-Line** because of the dark seam of archive symbiote running from my left ear down my throat and vanishing beneath my collar.

The salt-birds would call me by several embarrassing names, none of them current.

I would be mostly person.

Mostly.

Slight, narrow-shouldered, damp-skinned, with wide feet that learned late but well how to trust uncertain ground. My hands would be long and always stained at the nails with lignin ink. My hair would be tied back with bell-beads, not decorative ones but small municipal bells tuned to different kinds of movement: wall-shift, root-swell, canal-softening, unauthorized bloom.

One bell would be cracked and useless, but I would keep it anyway because an elder gave it to me after I correctly apologized to a staircase.

Under my skin, especially at the wrists and spine, I would carry fine archive rootlets. Voluntary, technically. A professional bond. They would itch before rain and tighten when I lied about directions. Through them the archive grove would taste what I tasted: mineral seep, mold bloom, the bitterness of a newly exposed foundation, the electric sweetness of engine fog.

I would have given up strong food years ago because my work requires a clean tongue.

No heavy peppers. No fermented eel paste. No resin candy from the south stalls. Only bland rice-moss, pale broth, clean tubers, rainwater that has fallen in the ordinary direction.

This would make me sad in a small, daily way.

My duties would begin after the first heat of morning, when doorways have finished waking and the root-roads are still deciding whether to be roads. I would walk the grey stones where there are grey stones. Where there are none, I would tap the ground with a reed probe and listen for the answer through my feet.

At every new passage I would perform the simple rites:

First, ask whether it is hungry.

Second, ask whether it remembers being somewhere else.

Third, place three grains of salt at the threshold for birds, eels, and accidents.

Fourth, step halfway in, but never all the way, until the passage has repeated my shadow correctly.

If it repeats my shadow with too many arms, I close the way.

If it gives me no shadow, I call an engine interpreter.

If it repeats the shadow of someone dead, I call an elder and pretend I am not afraid.

Most days would be less dramatic than that. Most days would be labor.

A wall has grown over the shortcut to the school-well; persuade it to open a child-sized gap.

A bench has produced leaves in the shape of civic warning signs; determine whether this counts as signage.

A root-street has become offended by a festival procession and is shedding slick red pollen; reroute traffic past the moss-bakers.

A tea-house owner is furious because the town has made her rear storage room into a public lane again. I arrive with my bark slate, my apology cord, and a municipal voucher good for three days’ exemption from drainage tax.

She curses me, curses the town, curses the engines, pours me a cup of something mild enough not to ruin my tongue, and by sunset she is selling tea through both doors.

I would love that about the place: how irritation becomes architecture.

I would not be especially holy, but I would know many small prayers because all practical people do.

A prayer for crossing a bridge that has recently remembered water.

A prayer for finding your own house after a pressure exchange.

A prayer said before touching a wall-mouth:  
“Clouds high, roots low, drains clear, no personal matters.”

I would have been trained by an elder named Senn, whose left hand had gone partly bark and whose right eye watered sap when the archive grove was unhappy. Senn would teach me not to seek permanence.

“Do not ask where the path goes,” they would say. “Ask what it is trying to connect.”

I would write that down many times and understand it only after getting lost in a passage that led, impossibly, to the same courtyard at three different ages: once as mud, once as market, once as a flooded room full of sleeping deer.

After that, I would become more careful with maps.

I think I would be lonely in a particular way.

Not unloved. The town has too many relations for anyone attentive to be wholly alone. Beetles would recognize me. Doorways would tolerate me. Archive slabs would pulse faintly when I passed. Children would shout questions at me: “Mud-Line, is that alley legal?” “Mud-Line, can a bridge get married?” “Mud-Line, if a wall says my name, do I answer?”

But the work would make me intimate with temporary things.

I would know passages that existed only for one afternoon. I would remember a balcony that opened during rain-release and showed a view of the marsh from before the floodwalls sank. I would have once stepped through a root-arch and smelled my mother’s childhood house, though she had been dead and affiliated with a public bench for twelve years.

I would be the only one who remembered some routes.

That would be my small grief and my small pride.

At night I would sleep in a chamber that did not belong to me but usually kept me. A seed-cap room, ribbed and warm, with a wall-vein that pulsed beside my bed. I would scrape its breathing pores clean every fourth day. I would feed it mineral paste in dry weather. In return it would keep my papers from mildew and tilt my sleeping mat away from bad dreams rising through the floor.

Above my door would live three cup-leaf crabs. They would wave solemnly whenever I came home.

I would wave back, always.

On festival days I would paint one eel, badly. Children would laugh at my crooked spirals. The eel would endure me with theatrical disappointment, then spend all evening showing off in the wall-channel nearest the market.

During flood-bells, I would become useful in a sharper way. My bells would begin speaking all at once. I would climb only sanctioned structures. I would shout routes through mist. “Not the flowering tower! Left by the clinic! The green stones are safe for nine breaths! No, do not follow the monkey, it has no permit!”

In a true emergency, when the ground softens and the canals forget their edges, I would press my tongue to the archive slate and let it flood my mouth with bitterness, numbness, old civic memory. I would taste where the firm roads used to be. I would taste which root still held. I would taste a path beneath the water and lead people across, step by careful step.

Afterward I would vomit sap behind the clinic and pretend I had not been frightened.

The clinic bird would repeat, “Frightened, frightened, Mud-Line frightened,” until I paid it salt.

As I aged, I think I would become less person.

Not tragically. Not as a punishment. Simply by attendance.

The archive rootlets beneath my skin would thicken. My mottling would darken into branching stains. I would pause more often with one palm against a wall, listening. Younger registrars would roll their eyes because I spoke too slowly and used old street names.

I would begin to know when a doorway was embarrassed.

I would be able to tell, by standing barefoot in a courtyard, whether the town intended to keep it.

Perhaps one day I would be called to inspect a new alley behind the archive grove, one paved in young bark and leading to a pool no one remembers digging. I would step to its edge. The water would reflect not sky, not leaves, but a dim internal sun.

All my bells would go still.

The Underturner would shift somewhere beneath everything, and the pool would rise one handspan without spilling.

I would understand then that this was not a passage for people.

Not yet.

So I would mark it gently: **Reserved. Deep Tenant considering. Do not drum.**

And the living map would shiver, accept the change, and grow a little wider.

When I died, I would not choose marsh or engine or insectary.

I would ask to be given to the paths.

Not buried under a road — that would be presumptuous — but divided carefully. A little to the archive grove, for memory. A little to the root-streets, for balance. A little to the listening well, for rumor. A little to the beetles, because they were always kind in their blind, polishing way.

And perhaps, after that, when a new alley opened in late afternoon mist, someone stepping into it would feel beneath their feet a brief, approving firmness.

Not a voice.

Not a ghost.

Only a civic habit.

A remembered line through wet ground, saying:

Here, for now. Step here.
