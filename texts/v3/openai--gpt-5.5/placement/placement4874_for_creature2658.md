You would not find me in the center.

For a while I think I might be there, because the center asks to be noticed: the worn circular plate, the rusted pedestal, the hoist frame holding nothing. It has the look of a place where a person should stand and be changed by height. But I would not last there. No one who belongs to that garden mistakes the open for invitation.

You would find me at the edge, half under something.

At first only a hand would show: long-fingered, gray at the knuckles, green-black at the fingertips from rail and vine. Then an eye behind fern stems. Then the pale flicker of throat patches, no brighter than old lamp-glass catching blue inspection light.

I would live in the west wall, in the service hollow behind a row of cracked pressure gauges where moss grows in square pads over the dead sensors. My sleeping place would be above a warm pipe but not touching it, because I would have learned that warmth is not kindness. There would be a narrow shelf of mesh, a sling-net, three ceramic jars, a hook-rod, a coil of colored thread, and a piece of polished gauge glass I use as a scraper. The door would be a hinged grille hidden by a curtain of vine and filter-cloth, and children would know to tap twice on the lower rib before pulling it aside, because sometimes I sleep with one hand looped through the mesh and might wake striking.

I think I would be one of the keepers, but not one of the bold ones who crosses the high trough during rain or climbs the wheel spokes to clear root snarls.

I would be a listener.

Not a leader, not the oldest, not a child. Someone narrow and patient, with one extra side-finger on the right hand and no extra digit on the left, so that people joke I was assembled from spare customs. My ears would fold too tightly, making high steam-screams worse for me than for others, but my feet would be good. Bare soles on plate, I would know the garden by pressure: the thin shiver of larvae in a puddle, the clogged cough of a drain before it floods, the change in a pipe’s temper when hot flow begins below.

My work would be the small wrongnesses.

A bead of oil where yesterday there was only water.

A frog chorus absent from one grate.

A red vine wrapped too neatly around a junction box.

A drone polishing the same dead lamp for the third dawn in a row.

The slow wheel groaning half a note lower.

A puddle developing a silver edge.

I would carry yellow thread more than any other color: not-yet-known. I would tie it in cautious little knots around handles, grates, hatch-rings, stems. Others might find me irritating. I would mark things before they were proven dangerous. I would say, with my hands and throat-light and taps through the rail: wait. Not dead. Not safe. Ask again after rain.

The animals would know me as a disturbance with habits.

The blunt-beaked birds would not fear me much, except during nesting. They would hop along the rail beside my hand, furious and brilliant, flashing warning colors I could not hear but could understand. I would steal old nest fiber only after fledging and leave bright beetle shells in exchange, though the birds would not consider this fair trade.

The frogs would matter to me more than I admit. I would move their eggs from shallow doomed puddles with a spoon made from bent drone casing. I would crouch over drainage channels with my chest almost touching the grate, feeling their silent tremor-speech pass through metal into bone. I would not understand it as they do, but I would know when it thickens into breeding, when it thins into fear, when it vanishes.

The magnetite snails would be my favored citizens. I would make room for them on pipes and let them clean around contact points. Sometimes I would lift one from an active seam with insulated tongs and set it on a safer rust-bloom, apologizing with a tap it cannot possibly need. I would admire their certainty. They do not hurry; therefore they never appear lost.

The small warning drone that follows children might also follow me, because I go where hot seams and doubtful puddles are. The children would have decorated it with shells and yellow thread until it looked like a nervous festival insect. I would call it Little Flash, though names are mostly private things. It would blink red at me when I leaned too close to current. I would blink my throat patches back at it, rust-red, mock obedience.

I would have been born during cloud-passage.

That would be part of how people explain me. The mist came through so thick the rail vanished, and the compressor missed one breath, and everyone froze with hands on metal while my mother labored in the gauge hollow. For three long counts there was almost-silence below. Then the tower resumed, and I arrived with both fists closed.

So I would distrust silence more than most.

At night, when the lamps buzz on and the stars appear above the rail, I would sit with the youngest children and let them stare upward. They would ask why the stars do not tremble. I would have no satisfying answer. I might tell them the stars are too far away for the tower to teach them rhythm. Or that they are holes pricked in a plate so high even the Below-Body cannot shake it. Or I might say nothing and place their palms flat on the deck until they remember where they are.

My name would be something tapped rather than spoken.

Three quick taps, one drag of nail on bronze, two taps again.

In closer language, perhaps it means Finds-Leaks-Before-Rain. Or Waits-At-Edges. Or Yellow-Thread. Names change depending on who needs you.

I would be known for remembering unsafe water.

There would be shells arranged near puddles because of me. Black shells for do-not-touch. Pale shells for moved eggs. Broken shells for a pool that took someone fevered for three days. I would teach children to look not at the water but at its skin, not at its color but at what refuses to live in it. If beetles drink and frogs do not, ask why. If algae floats but does not cling, ask why. If the surface shines like a blade, leave it to the armored mouths.

I would eat bitter greens, fungus, pepper nectar when there is enough, shrimp when the pools test clean. I would like sour things. I would chew strips of dried leaf while working and tuck the stems behind my ear until they fall out. I would keep old feathers tied above my sleeping mesh, not as decoration exactly, but as memory of air given shape.

I would not go over the rail.

But I would touch it every day.

Not reverently. Practically. The rail is the Last Circle; it keeps hands honest. I would polish one curve with my passing until bronze showed through green-black corrosion. From there I would look down when cloud allows it: stacks, platforms, terraces, other crowns of the one body, all too far to reach and yet present underfoot as vibration. I would wonder who lives on those lower ledges. Whether they also have gardens. Whether their children fear silence. Whether their drains have names.

When storms come, I would be useful.

You would see me then moving low and fast along the margins, throat patches flashing blue-white through vapor. Hook-rod in one hand, thread spool in my teeth, Little Flash jittering behind me. I would clear leaves from Memory Holes before water backs up under the floor. I would slap warning onto the deck when the central plate begins to breathe. I would drag a child away from a warm pipe that has started waking. I would lose a tool. I would curse soundlessly. I would laugh afterward because the tower kept going.

If I grew old, I would move north.

Everyone old moves toward the wheel eventually, as if drawn by its slowness. I would take a hollow behind the root curtain and listen to water being lifted scoop by scoop. My hands would swell. My throat patches would dim to the color of lamp-fog. Fern spores would gather in my wrap. Younger keepers would ask whether a vibration is new or ancestral, and I would annoy them by saying: both. Everything new has been arriving for years.

Perhaps, at the very end, I would ask not for the digesters but for a service shaft.

Wrap me in moss from the sensor grids. Tie yellow thread around the bundle, because even then I would be not-yet-known. Lower me where the tower’s warmth rises and its depths answer without words. Let the garden take what it can. Let the rest become rumor in the metal.

But before that, for most of my life, I would be there:

in the west margin, behind the gauge moss, beside the pipes that sweat cleanly at dawn.

A small gray keeper with stained hands.

A reader of tremors.

A marker of uncertain things.

One who knows the garden is not safe, not natural, not obedient, and therefore home.
