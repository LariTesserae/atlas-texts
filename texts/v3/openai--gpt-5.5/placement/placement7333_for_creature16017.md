I would not find myself standing.

Standing would be the first mistake in that country. It would waste heat, advertise shape, argue with the wind. I would come to myself pressed low to stone, belly warmed by a shelf of dark basalt, breath hidden under the edge of my own body.

I would be somewhere no map would choose as important: not a summit, not a canyon mouth, not the largest tarn. A shallow saddle between worn ridges. A place where three slow things meet.

Behind me, a line of vapor vents would breathe through frost-rimmed cracks.

Below me, under plates of old ground-ice, a black tarn would hold its dim fish and jointed filterers.

Across the saddle, almost too faint to see, would run one of the great herd-ways: not a road, not a track exactly, but a continent-long preference worn into moss, gravel, thaw, dung, and memory.

That is where I would live: in the afterwarm of passage.

I would be one of the low listening animals.

Not large enough to be mistaken for a hill, not lean enough to be a predator, not numerous enough to make the ground steam in colonies. I would belong to the margin between all those lives. A scavenger, yes, but not only that. A keeper of leftovers, a finder of weak heat, a reader of pressure through stone.

If named in a language with names, perhaps I would be called an afterwarm listener.

My body would be longer than it is tall, built close to the rule of the plateau. Four short legs, wide feet, black claws blunt from prying at frost and shale. A heavy shoulder shield of dull keratin, not smooth but pitted, mineral-stained, colonized by tiny lichens that do me no harm. Dense underfur along the belly. Outer hairs stiff and pale at the tips, lying flat so the wind cannot take hold.

My ears would be nearly invisible, sealed under folds of skin and fur. The plateau does not reward open ears. Instead I would hear through my jaw, my breastbone, the pads of my forefeet. When a herd moved five miles away, I would feel it first as a difference in the ground’s thinking. A tremor too wide to be sound. A slow insistence.

My eyes would be small, dark-silver, useful in mist and under cloud, but I would trust them least of all.

I would trust warmth.

The abandoned bed of a grazer would shine to me like a doorway.

A carcass under fog would be a furnace with a smell.

A burrow colony would be not a set of holes but a breathing organ below my feet.

A tarn breathing through thin ice would tell me where the water still remembered motion.

I would move mostly at the hour when day has failed but night has not sharpened: the blue time, when frost begins to grow outward from stone and the herds settle into themselves. I would travel by lowering myself into the land’s smallest permissions. A crack between basalt plates. A mossy hollow still warm from a flank. The lee of an old antler half-buried in mineral grit.

I would not hunt in the proud way.

Sometimes I might take a wingless bird if it blundered close in fog. Sometimes a fish stunned in a shrinking pool. Beetles, larvae, softened root crowns, fungal knots from beneath old dung, marrow from bones already opened by stronger mouths. I would eat what the plateau had already begun to loosen.

My teeth would be made for persistence: front teeth for scraping, back teeth for crushing shell and frozen gristle, side teeth for worrying flakes from bone. My tongue would be dark and rough. My gut would carry a parliament of patient bacteria, able to make food of things almost not food at all.

I would follow the herds, but never among them.

To live among them would be to be crushed by warmth too large to care. Instead I would trail behind the migration by hours, sometimes days, reading what remained. Dung fields where beetles gathered. Hair snagged on frost crystals. A patch of moss exposed by grazing. A calf’s birth hollow rich with fluids and waxed milk. A place where an old animal lay down and rose with difficulty.

The predators would know me and mostly ignore me.

I would know them by absence. A pale shape on a ridge makes less declaration than a stone, but the beetle-lines change. Birds lower themselves. Burrow vents pause. Herd tremors tighten and flatten. The land itself becomes more careful around a waiting predator.

I would not challenge them. When they killed, I would wait beyond the steam. I would lie with my chin on stone and feel the order of feeding: predator teeth, then ash-winged scavengers if the canyon winds allowed them, then beetles, then burrowers, then worms, then fungus, then me if there was a seam I could open with claw and patience.

Or perhaps I would come earlier, not to steal, but to remember.

Because that would be the intelligence of my kind: not command, not making, not speech that rises into the air, but remembering where warmth has been and when it may return.

I would know which vents seal after three nights of dry cold.

I would know which tarn edges bear weight when the cloud ceiling is low, and which only pretend to.

I would know the black stones that warm first under a hidden sun.

I would know the old females of the herd by their tread, by the rhythm that comes through ground before their bodies appear in fog.

I would know where the burrowers have opened new chambers below thin crust, and I would step lightly there, not from kindness exactly, but because falling wastes heat.

My kind would meet rarely. We would not gather in dens unless cold forced us into temporary treaties. When we did meet, we would not lift our heads and call. We would lie near one another, not touching at first, and exchange pulses through the ground: chest to stone, throat closed, a tremor sent outward.

Not sentences.

Intervals.

A long pressure: ice unsafe below the ridge.

Three shallow beats: carcass cooling.

A held vibration: predator waiting.

A fading throb: herd gone west.

If I had young, they would be born in the side chamber of an abandoned burrow colony, where the walls still held a little old animal heat and fungal threads softened the soil. They would be blind at first, plated only in suggestion, smelling of milk, iron, and wet stone. I would feed them on rendered fat, beetle mash, half-digested moss, whatever the land allowed me to convert into warmth.

They would learn by contact. I would press their small jaws to the ground when herds passed. I would drag them to a vent and make them feel the difference between living vapor and killing steam. I would show them the deceit of smooth ice and the promise of cloudy ice. I would teach them that silence is not emptiness. Silence is where information is stored.

My shelter would not look like shelter.

A human eye might pass over it entirely: a depression under a basalt lip, lined not with gathered material but with repeated use. My body would have polished the stone. My breath would have grown a private frost fringe on the overhang. A few hairs would be caught in lichen. A scattering of beetle shells. One cracked bone drawn into the back where I had worked at it through a storm.

Nothing vertical. Nothing announcing me after I left.

But I would have a world.

The vent-line would be my winter spine.

The tarn would be my dark eye.

The herd-way would be my moving calendar.

The burrow fields would be my underweather.

The ridges would be the closed mouths of old stone, speaking only through temperature and pressure.

And I would be most myself after a herd had passed.

The plateau would be dim. The animals gone into fog, their thousands of bodies no longer visible, only the low tremor fading westward. The ground would remain dark in their beds, thawed and breathing. Frost would begin to re-form at the edges. Beetles would appear, small black flecks in ordered lines. A wingless bird would move among them, precise as a thought. Somewhere underfoot, burrowers would test the new warmth from below.

I would enter that abandoned climate and lie down.

Not to sleep at once.

To listen.

The great country would seem empty to any creature that needed movement to prove life. But beneath my jaw the stone would be crowded with messages: water under ice, roots contracting, vents exhaling, beetles rasping, old herd-thunder withdrawing into distance, a predator turning on a ridge, frost opening its countless little teeth.

That is where I would be.

Low in the saddle between tarn, vent, and migration.

A dark-backed, frost-whiskered afterwarm listener.

Not master of the plateau. Not even witness in any grand sense.

Only one more patient body in the circulation of stone, hunger, breath, and cold.
