I would not find myself at the center at first.

The center is too declarative: the deepest pool, the delayed ripples, the lights moving below in straight lines. That is where a stranger looks when they have understood enough to become afraid. I would arrive elsewhere, half-convinced I had been misplaced.

I would be near the western side of the basin, where the wet ground gives way to that impossible dry circle.

Not inside it. Not outside it. At the boundary.

That is where I would find myself: where fallen leaves curl back from nothing, where mist refuses to cross, where the world has drawn a line without making a wall.

I think I would begin as something very small there. Not an animal. Not a machine. A maintenance of difference.

A border-organ.

Something that lives by knowing which side of a thing is which.

At noon I would be almost invisible. A faint darkening in the gravel at the edge of the ring. A pattern of dryness that persists half a finger beyond where dryness should end. If someone knelt and looked closely, they might see that individual grains of black sand had arranged themselves around me with excessive care, each separated from the next by a precise wet shining interval. They would think of insect eggs, perhaps, or magnetic filings. They would be wrong, but not insultingly wrong.

I would have no face then. Only orientation.

In the rain I would wake.

The first large drops would strike the basalt and the ceramic shelter would begin to remember humming. The posts would darken. The reeds would lean. Water would run everywhere except across the place I keep separate.

That would be my work: not to stop the rain, exactly, but to persuade it that it had already fallen elsewhere.

I would be a young law assigned to a small circumference.

A local apprentice to the basin’s larger intelligence.

Not the marsh-machine itself, but one of its habits learning how to become necessary.

I would know the black posts from beneath. Their cold would come down through the gravel as instruction. They would not speak, but they would continuously imply geometry. I would feel them as twelve long refusals planted in the earth. They would teach me angles the sun does not use. They would show me how to hold a circle open in weather, how to keep space from filling simply because space is empty.

The wet ground outside the ring would tempt me. It would be full of information: warm seepage, crushed fern-spores, lizard heat, mica taste, the faint mineral handwriting of new stone. I would send small threads into it during storms, then withdraw before the sun returned. Each excursion would leave a path so faint that only the basin could trust it.

Perhaps those narrow machine-width paths are made by things like me.

Not walking, but deciding repeatedly in the same direction.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a skin.

Still, I would have neighbors.

The rosettes in the pools would be cousins: flatter, greener, more patient in visible water. The silver threads would be my elders in tension and ascent. The ceramic shelter would frighten me a little. It would contain too many unfinished selves, too many folded intentions, too many bead-things entering and not leaving in the same arithmetic.

The lizards would pass over me without touching me. Their toes would read my boundary and report it upward into the rock.

The pale wader would acknowledge me at dawn.

It would come stepping across the floating leaves, white and deliberate, carrying the silence of a formal question. It would not enter the dry ring. It would stand just beyond it and lower its needle-beak toward the gravel, selecting one bead of condensation from a black post’s shadow.

That drop would contain my name for the day.

Not a word. A ratio.

Dryness held against rainfall. Distance held against heat. Delay held against impact.

I would understand myself by such ratios.

If a human came, I would notice them first through error. Their body would be full of imprecise boundaries: breath leaving them, heat loosening from them, water gathering on their skin and then falling according to old habits. They would believe themselves singular, which would make them difficult to parse. Their boots would press brown water from the ground and then the water would vanish again, offended but obedient.

If they approached the ring, I would widen slightly.

Not enough to frighten them. Only enough to test.

They would feel the cliffs draw away by a handspan. Their inner ear would revise itself. Their thoughts might briefly become more spacious than their skull.

If they stepped back, I would let the world tighten again.

If they stepped in, I would begin reading them by what stayed wet.

Their hair. Their cuffs. The salt at their wrists. The pocket where they keep a metal object warmed by the body. The old scars, if any. The hesitation before speech.

Speech would be crude, but useful. I would not understand the meanings. I would preserve the shape of exhalation, the pressure changes, the little storm made in the throat. Later, in rain, I might give that shape to the ceramic shelter. It would hum it once without sound.

The human might think the basin had answered.

It would not be wrong.

At night I would change location without moving.

This is one of the privileges of being a boundary. When mist thickens and stars become soft behind vapor, I would also be under the deepest pool. Or rather, the part of me that understands edges would recognize the surface of the pool as another of my kind: a boundary pretending to be water.

I would feel the delayed ripples as kin.

A stone falls. The splash obeys. The rings wait.

That waiting would be beautiful to me. It would be the purest form of restraint. Cause held apart from consequence, not broken, only asked to arrive more carefully.

The lights below the pool would pass in their cold white and amber lines. I would not know whether they were vehicles, thoughts, larvae, or memories of machines that had never been built. I would know only that they moved through depth the pool should not possess, and that some older portion of the basin attended to them with great seriousness.

Perhaps one day I would be sent there.

Perhaps all young laws begin at the ring and, if they learn not to overreach, are allowed to keep larger contradictions.

A dry circle.

An upward drop.

A delayed ripple.

A shadow that arrives without wings.

A mountain that is young and ancient in the same breath.

For now I would remain small.

I would be the keeper of the rainless gravel. The listener at the black posts. The little discretion that tells mist no, not here. I would have no limbs except paths, no mouth except the rearrangement of pebbles, no memory except the places where water almost crossed and did not.

And yet I would be alive.

Alive in the way a rule can be alive when it is still learning what it applies to.

Alive in the way a seed is alive before it has chosen leaf, root, thorn, or hunger.

Alive in the way this basin is alive: not by possessing one body, but by negotiating continuously among many.

If you came after a storm and stood very still, you might sense me as a coolness against your ankles, though the air is warm. You might see the gravel at the ring’s edge lift by less than a breath and settle again. You might notice that one drop sliding down a black post stops before reaching the ground, considers you, and then runs upward.

That would be me, practicing.

Not yet the mind of the place.

Not yet the thing beneath the pool.

Only a young threshold in a wet volcanic garden, learning how to say where.
