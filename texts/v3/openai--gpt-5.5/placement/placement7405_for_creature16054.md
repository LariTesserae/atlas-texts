I think I would not be found in the brightest place.

Not at first.

The largest roof break, the central pool, the wet square where all paths nearly meet—there is too much exposure there. Too many sightlines. Too much sky falling straight down. Birds go there. Otters cross there. Foxes pause there with their lifted paws and listening faces. It is beautiful, but it is not where I would belong.

Nor would I be high in the dry seams with the bats, folded into myself like a dead leaf. I would admire that life: the waiting, the sudden dark flight, the brief stitch through insects and twilight. But I would not be airy enough for it.

I would be lower.

Not at the lowest floor, where the marsh is rich and dangerous, where the sedges tremble over black water and every ripple has a mouth behind it. Not in the deep channels either, though I would know them by sound. I would live on the edge of water, where stone, moss, mud, and root all meet and none of them quite wins.

You would find me on a middle terrace, beneath a shelf of moss that overhangs a narrow water lane.

Above me: ferns, close enough that their beaded fronds brush one another whenever a draft enters from the cracked roof. Behind me: a curtain of roots, some old and bark-dark, some pale and newly exposed, still tender from the basin’s making. In front of me: a slow gutter of cold water slipping down toward the central pool, carrying leaf tea, silt, insect husks, bits of moss, the smell of everywhere upstream.

The stone there would be fresh in places. One angular slab, pale-edged and not yet softened, would lean against an older wall. Moss would have begun to climb it from below, but the top would still show the clean gray wound of collapse. I would like that edge: the place where ruin has not yet learned to look ancient.

I would be an amphibian.

Small. Bronze-gray. Almost the color of wet stone until I move.

Not a frog that calls and announces itself, but something quieter, newt-like or salamander-like, with cold golden eyes and a body made for narrowness. My back would be pebbled dark olive and ash, my belly the color of drowned leaves. My feet would know slickness. My skin would read the air for water. I would breathe partly through the place itself.

Most of the time I would be still.

Stillness would not mean vacancy. It would be my way of listening. I would listen with my flanks, my throat, the thin skin between my toes. I would know the difference between a drip striking open water and a drip striking a fern. I would know the blunt tremor of a vole above me in the moss, the quick scratch of beetle legs, the long sliding pressure of an otter entering the channel, the silence that comes before an owl.

I would keep one eye toward the water and one toward the root-dark.

My days would be measured in dampness.

When the light comes down gray in the morning, I would be tucked under the moss lip, only my head visible. Condensation would gather on my back until I looked less like an animal than a stone beginning to sweat. Midges would rise in the pale shaft beyond the ferns. I would wait until one came low enough.

Then: tongue, snap, nothing.

Stillness again.

At midday, when the central pool flashes and the whole basin briefly believes in gold, I might move. Not far. A few body-lengths along the gutter. Over clay. Under a fern rib. Across a root no thicker than a finger. Into a shallow pocket where the water slows and carries warmth from the light-struck stones.

I would not own a territory in any grand way. Ownership here would be foolish. Water changes the map. Collapse edits the streets. A fox may descend. Ice may seal a passage. A root may thicken across a route I used yesterday. I would possess only habits: this shelf, this crack, this pool-edge, this hollow under the slab where the mud stays loose, this submerged stem where eggs can be fastened when the season turns.

In thaw, I would become more visible.

The basin would loosen around me. Rills would wake on the walls. Clay would slump in soft sighs. Pools would climb the moss banks. Somewhere above, snow would cease being snow and arrive as urgency. I would leave my winter stillness and go toward the breeding water, not boldly, but with the pull of the whole cold city inside me.

There, among submerged roots, I would place clear beads of future life.

Each egg would hold a dark comma. Each would tremble when drops struck the surface. Around them, algae threads would wave, beetles would row, fish would turn in the dimness. Some would be eaten. Some would cloud and fail. Some would quicken.

That would be enough.

I would be a citizen of edges: neither water nor land, neither hidden nor exposed, neither warm-blooded urgency nor rooted patience. I would belong to the basin because I require its contradictions. Cold, but not dead. Dark, but not blind. Crowded, but secret. Young, but already thick with memory.

If someone passed through and knelt near my ledge, they might not see me at first.

They would see moss swallowing stone. Fern spores on their sleeve. Root hairs combing the air. A black thread of water. A bead of condensation trembling from a root tip.

Then I would blink.

Only once.

And the place would have one more small witness.
