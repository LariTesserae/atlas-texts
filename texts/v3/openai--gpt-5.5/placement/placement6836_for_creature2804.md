You would find me where the city is neither above nor below, but in the seam between them.

Not on the open roofs, where the hookbirds own the cables and the clouds drag their wet bellies through the vents. Not in the deep under-streets, where the floor may soften into animal and a warm wall may begin to breathe. I would be in the service levels halfway up the industrial body: on grated walks along the sides of pump houses, in enclosed bridges between compressor halls, in narrow rooms behind sweating ceramic tile, where the pipes are close enough to speak through and the rain is something heard before it is felt.

At first I think I would be a librarian, because dryness would be holy to me.

I would live near one of the sealed reading rooms above the old heat exchangers, where books sleep in waxed cloth and every cabinet has a little ceramic dial showing damp. I would know how to open a drawer without letting fog in. I would know which pages can be handled in cloud season and which must wait for the ninth-day stamping, when the whole district shakes dry for six hours and the hookbirds scream themselves hoarse from the roof trusses.

But that is too clean an answer.

I would not be allowed to stay only with pages. Nobody there is allowed only one life.

So I would be a keeper of records that have to be walked.

A route-scribe. A rail listener. A person sent out with chalk, oilskin, and a little hammer to mark what has changed since yesterday.

I would carry a slate map strapped under my coat, its surface gridded with old streets, pressure conduits, safe stairs, crab gutters, bridge colonies, valve doors, breeding canals, and temporary hazards. Not a map of territory, exactly. A map of permissions.

This passage passable before noon if the crabs are low.  
This bridge unsafe during fogfall if the sheet colony is silent.  
This stair dry enough for children after stamping day.  
This door sweating from the hinge: report to sealwrights.  
This wall warm where it should not be.  
This canal white with mating froth, not toxin. Cross quickly and do not drop food.

I would be found pausing often.

One hand on a rail.  
One boot testing stone.  
Head tilted beneath my ceramic mask, listening.

The city would enter me through vibration before sight: trains, pumps, stampers, pipes, the sudden organized hush of animals noticing something larger than themselves. I would learn to tell the difference between rain in a gutter, crabs in a gutter, and something using the gutter to come closer.

My mask would be modest but well cared for: gray-glazed ceramic, patched at the cheek with a different clay after an accident I do not talk about unless drunk. The filters would be packed with bone char and crushed shell. Around the lower rim, tucked into wire loops, I would keep three white hookbird feathers—not for decoration only, but because feather movement tells small air currents before skin does.

My coat would be oilcloth gone soft at the elbows. My gloves would have shell plates over the knuckles. My boots would be heavier than I like, iron-ribbed against bites from below.
