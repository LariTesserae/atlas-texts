I don’t think I would arrive standing upright.

A standing creature is too certain for that place. Too much like a line drawn in a country that dislikes lines. If I came there in boots, with lungs and a map and the habit of naming things before they changed, the plateau would correct me quickly. The heat would enter my bones. The thin wet air would make my thoughts bead and run. My footprints would close behind me or become vents or fill with fern spores. I would understand, after the first reality-slip, that being a person there is mostly a temporary posture.

So I would be found at an edge.

Not the highest blade ridge, though I would go there sometimes when the grasses lay themselves flat and the stones began to ring. Not the deepest ravine either, where the heat is too intimate and the roots drink too loudly. I would live in the hinge between them: on a shelf of black basalt halfway down a ravine wall, just below an overhang of copper-veined leaves, above a pool that reflects skies not always present.

There is a vent there, but not a violent one. It breathes in soft measured pulses through three narrow cracks. Around it grow valve-flowers with dark throats and pale inner membranes. The stone is glossy, newly hardened, and warm enough to keep mist from settling except in the hour before the plateau changes key. A curtain of root-vines hangs nearby, and beyond it the ravine drops into vapor and fungal light. Above, if the air clears, a serrated ridge cuts the dim sky in uneven teeth.

That is where I would be.

At first glance I might be mistaken for a piece of shale.

A small triangular plate, no longer than a hand, stuck to the underside of warm stone. Black-green. Slightly lustrous. Ribbed with faint mineral lines. In dry intervals I would fold myself completely, all edges sealed, all softness hidden. Dust would gather on me. Salt beetles might walk over me. A grazer might scrape moss from the ledge nearby and never notice me watching without eyes.

But when the air thickened, I would open.

Not like a flower exactly. More like a wet map unfolding after being kept too long in a pocket. Thin lobes would separate from one another, translucent at the edges, dark at the center. Along those lobes would be little pits that taste pressure, heat, chemical change, and the direction of unreliability. I would have eyes, but not noble eyes. Not eyes for faces or horizons. Mine would be arranged in a crescent along my lower rim, each one tuned to a different kind of disturbance: steam-light, tremor-shadow, magnetic bloom, the small wrongness that gathers before distance folds.

I would be an animal, mostly.

Also fungus.

Also instrument.

Also, in a modest way, memory.

The plateau would use creatures like me the way a body uses nerves in the skin: not to think grand thoughts, but to know where pressure is changing, where it has been cut, where it may need to heal around an injury or invent a new organ. I would not command anything. I would not understand the whole. But I would notice.

That would be my gift: noticing revision.

When a ridge appeared twice in the mist, I would feel the doubling as a cool line along my spine. When echoes returned before their sounds, my lobes would tremble in the wrong sequence. When a pool began reflecting a sky from somewhere else, I would lower my mouthparts into the water and drink the error, not as food exactly, but as signal. Later I would excrete a thin conductive thread across the stone. Metallic worms would find it. Fungi would follow. Roots would read it slowly. The plateau would learn, through a thousand such small transmissions, that here the world had nearly chosen another shape.

I would not have a permanent name. Nothing sensible there would.

But if a human insisted on naming me, they might call me a pressure-scribe.

Not because I write in marks anyone can read, but because I leave tiny edits in the living surface: mineral threads, chemical stains, scars that glow faintly beneath moss. I would record no history as humans mean it. I would not preserve events unchanged. I would preserve tendencies.

This ledge softens before warm rain.  
This pool remembers upper air.  
This crack widens during violet stormlight.  
This root-curtain moves when no animal touches it.  
This place has been three places before.

My days would not be days.

During dry brightness, when the smoked-glass sky held itself still and exposed stone became hot enough to shimmer, I would sleep folded against the underside of the basalt shelf. My body would become almost mineral. Inside, however, a small wet chamber would remain, kept alive by the vent’s breath. In that chamber I would store spores, impressions, and fragments of reflected weather.

When condensation came, I would wake.

First the stone would sweat. Then the valve-flowers would open in sequence: one, three, two, one again. The root-vines would twitch though nothing passed them. Droplets would gather on my folded edges. I would loosen, seam by seam, until my sensory lobes hung down into the warm vapor.

Then I would move.

Slowly. Quietly. With many small hooks.

I would cross the ceiling of the overhang upside down, pausing where the stone hummed. I would drink from beads of water before they fell. I would taste the mineral content of fresh cracks. If a herd of cliff-grazers passed along the ravine wall, I would flatten myself and let their mossy flanks brush near me. Their hide-gardens would smell of distant basalt, old mother-cliffs, salt films, fear, and green respiration. Sometimes one would carry a scar that matched a grove below, and I would touch it with a filament, learning how the plateau had moved that memory from animal to plant.

I would be prey to many things.

A mist hunter could take me if I opened too far during vapor. Its transparent body would shorten the space between us, and I would vanish into a mouth that was almost not there. A moss predator might not bother with me unless I had recently fed on strong signal. A glider might pluck me from the stone during a careless crossing, especially if my mineral lines shone after stormlight.

This would not feel tragic in that place.

If eaten, I would continue as information. My body would pass into wing, moss, pool, spore, thread. The plateau wastes very little, least of all sensation.

During key-change, I would become most myself.

The first sign would be the pause.

The vent below me would stop breathing. The pool would go black and smooth. The metallic grasses on the ridge above would lie down all at once, though no wind touched them. The insects would cease their glassy chirr. Even the root-vines would hold still, as if listening for instructions from under the world.

Then the misalignment would begin.

The ravine wall opposite me would appear slightly beside itself. The hanging leaves would cast shadows belonging to other leaves. A grazer’s hoofprint would form before the hoof touched stone. In the pool below, I might see my own unfolded body reflected larger, older, with too many lobes, clinging to a version of the ledge that had cracked away centuries from now or yesterday.

That is when I would spread completely.

All membranes open. All pits exposed. Every filament tasting the air.

I would not be afraid, exactly. Fear is for creatures that believe the world has one arrangement and might lose it. I would be tense with use. The plateau would be asking every sensitive thing: What is different? What can be kept? What must be released?

I would answer by trembling.

Not randomly. In patterns. Tiny clicks through my mineral ribs. Pulses of salt and charge into the damp stone. A pale secretion along the basalt, each drop holding a before and an after close together.

The warm rain would fall.

Brief. Fine. Almost gentle.

It would darken the ledge and fill the cups of leaves. It would wake the folded pool-creatures below. It would strike my open membranes and make me briefly luminous from within.

By the time the rain stopped, some new feature would exist in the distance: a ridge, a crack, a grove, a black shining extrusion already threaded with green. I would not know whether it had been born or remembered. That distinction would not matter much here.

I think I would be happy there, though not in a human way.

Not content. The place is not built for contentment. Not safe. Not peaceful. But fitted. Useful. Awake in the exact manner required.

I would belong to the interruptions.

To the bead of water before it drops.  
To the echo before its sound.  
To the crack before the root enters.  
To the animal before it becomes garden.  
To the stone before it decides whether to hold.

I would live where the plateau listens to itself changing.

Small, folded, many-eyed, half-mineral, half-hunger: a pressure-scribe under a warm basalt ledge, recording the world not by resisting its revisions, but by feeling each one pass through me and leaving behind just enough scar for the living mountain to remember.
