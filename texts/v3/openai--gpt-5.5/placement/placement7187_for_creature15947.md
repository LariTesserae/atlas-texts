I would find myself at the low stone sill where the central pools are held in place.

Not in the open middle of the water, not on the cliff face, not high among the dwarf trees. I would be lower than that, almost overlooked: where a shelf of black-blue rock dips under a lip of moss, where water gathers but does not yet become a pool, where fallen leaves press flat and soften into the same dark substance as soil. A place one handspan wide. A place that is not quite land and not quite water. The seam where the hollow keeps remaking its own skin.

At first I might think I am an animal there.

Something small enough not to disturb the arrangement. A salamander perhaps, dark-backed and cold-bellied, lying with my ribs against stone, my body so still that algae begins to trust me. Or a pale frog tucked in a moss cup, my throat lifting once every long while, my eyes holding the dimness without needing to name it. Or one of the snails, carrying a little wet weather on my back, leaving a path so brief that even my passage is almost an act of erasure.

But those are still too separate.

They have edges. They can move from one place to another. They can be pointed to.

I think I would be harder to distinguish.

I would be the slick living film on the underside of that stone sill: algae, bacteria, fungal thread, mineral stain, eggs, rot, and the grazing marks of mouths too small to be seen. I would be a dark green-brown sheen where water slows. Not the pool, not the rock, not the moss, but the meeting-place of all three.

My body would be thin, but not simple. A skin made of many hungers. Springtails would walk across me like thoughts. Snail mouths would write and unwrite me. Larvae would rest in me. Spores would enter me. The roots of moss would drink through me. When a beetle passed, I would hold the print of its feet for a moment in wetness and then close it. When a leaf fell and began to soften, I would receive it without ceremony. When something died above me, I would be among the first to know, not as news but as nourishment arriving.

I would not speak. I would not need a voice. Anything I had to say would travel by dampness: through the cold contact between stone and root, through the slight change in taste where decay has begun, through the clouding of water, through the quiet flourishing of a thread too fine for sight.

I would not think in sentences.

I would think in occupations.

Here: a pore has opened.

Here: a shell has passed.

Here: the underside of the leaf is ready.

Here: a white bead of egg trembles.

Here: something warm breathed and is gone.

My memory would not be stored apart from my body. It would be the body. The old paths of snails would be part of me. The faint acid of roots. The mineral patience of the stone. The places where water lingers longer. The places where a larva was taken by a newt and left only a loosened mote behind. I would remember by being altered so slowly that alteration and remembering could not be told apart.

If lifted into daylight, I would be nothing much: slime on a rock, a stain, a slickness someone might wipe away without noticing.

But there, in the hollow, I would be one of the necessary surfaces through which the place remains itself.

I would belong to the rule that nothing remains vacant.

The hollow would touch me on every side. Above me, moss would sag with water. Beside me, the pool would hold its black, still lip. Beneath me, stone would give its old cold upward. Around me, the lives of the hollow would arrive in fragments: a feather barb, a drowned fly, pollen, mite husks, frog spawn jelly, the powdered collapse of lichen, the softening edge of bark. I would take them in. I would pass them on. I would thicken by vanishing into other things.

I would be barely an individual.

That feels right.

In a place where life has stopped reaching outward and begun to fold inward, I would not want to be a creature with distance in it. I would not want wings, or long legs, or a loud throat, or a mind full of horizons. I would want to be close. Pressed. Useful without intention. Alive at the scale where contact is fate.

So: you would find me under the mossed lip of the stone sill, in the cold wet seam just above the pool.

I would be the living glaze there.

The hollow’s damp thought, spread thin over rock.
