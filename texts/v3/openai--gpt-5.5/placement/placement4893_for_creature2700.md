At first I would look for myself in the wrong places.

I would think I might be on the edge of the plateau, where the young cliffs fall away and the water combs down their faces in fine lines. I would think some part of me belongs to that height: the open horizon, the haze below, the feeling of being lifted out of the louder world. But I would not stay there. The edge is too clean, too declarative. It belongs to weather and distance more than to daily life.

Then I would think I am in the central basin, ankle-deep in reflected sky, among the largest buildings. That place has the gravity of a heart. Storehouse, hall, kiln shelter, drying platform: all the town’s meanings pass near it. But I would not be one of the central people, not a speaker in the long hall, not an elder whose hand rests on a carved cistern rim while others listen.

I would be found slightly away from the center.

Not outside the town, but where the town begins to loosen into gardens and reed beds. Down-gradient from the paved basin, where overflow water leaves the public stone and becomes useful again. There is a narrow causeway there, flat and pale, with moss thick along one side and a clay-lined channel along the other. The channel passes beneath a low bridge slab, then divides: one thread toward the root ridges, one toward a dark settling pool, one toward the reed beds that clean tired water before it is allowed to leave the neighborhood.

That is where I would be: crouched beside the division, one hand on the stone, listening.

I would be a level-reader, but not one of the famous old ones yet. Someone trusted with a small but consequential piece of the town’s wet logic. A keeper of a junction. A watcher of lips, seams, smells, and slow changes.

My work would not look important to someone from a dry world. I would not command, build towers, trade in bright goods, or lead processions. Most days I would carry a skimmer, a clay scoop, a reed brush, a measuring cord darkened by constant use, and a basket for leaves, algae, broken stems, and the small collapses that every channel offers up. I would move from mouth to mouth, clearing what should not remain, leaving what slows the water properly, pressing clay into a sidewall where a thread has begun to cut too sharply.

I would know the town by its undersides.

Others might know households by names, kinship, roofs, doorways. I would know them by what their water does after leaving them. This house sends too much ash in the washing water. That one has a clean cistern but a careless waste channel. This garden drinks greedily because its mound has settled. That roof drops beautifully into its basin. This path is safe in morning but slick by evening because mist gathers from the shaded pool below it.

I would have the kind of memory that is not dramatic. A memory of levels. A memory of stains. A memory of how high the dark line stood on a stone three fog-periods ago. I would remember when a wall began to sweat before anyone else noticed, when the tired water soured after reeds were cut too low, when a child lost a sandal in a false patch of soil and everyone laughed only after confirming the ground had not opened further.

I would be quiet even among a quiet people.

Not because I dislike speech, but because my attention would be trained downward. Speech lifts the mind; water lowers it. I would answer slowly. I would pause before stepping. I would dislike being called from across a plaza. I would prefer someone to come near, stand beside me, and look where I am looking.

My hands would be broad, the nails worn blunt, the palms always faintly pale from wet work. My clothing would be practical and heavy: a wrapped fiber skirt lifted above the knees, a short reed-woven shoulder cape dark with mist, cord belt holding tools, sandals replaced often because the channel stones eat them. I would own several hats and care about them more than vanity allows: one for hard rain, one for bright drying hours, one broad enough to keep roof-drip from running down my neck while I work under eaves.

My house would be small and raised on stone feet, near enough to the reed beds that I could hear nothing from them at night except the silence of standing stems. Inside would be little that minds damp: rolled mats, jars sealed with ash, a few carved measuring sticks, bundles of reed tips for pollination season, ceramic bowls, a low sleeping platform, a wall niche for seed packets owed to others.

I would not be wealthy, but people would notice if my channel stones were clean.

In blossom season I would become, briefly, something like a bee this world never had. I would go to the mound-groves with others, carrying pollen in folded leaves, touching flower to flower with a soft reed brush. I would like that work more than I admitted. It would feel almost extravagant: all that delicate yellow dust, all that future fruit requiring fingers. I would be clumsy at first, too used to mud and stone, but careful. Someone older would laugh softly and tell me not to drown the flowers with my seriousness.

I would have a particular affection for reeds.

Not romantically, not sentimentally. More like respect for a difficult relative who holds the household together. I would know which stems are ready for roofing, which for mats, which for filters, which must be left another season. I would dislike wasteful cutting. If a young person slashed reed for no purpose, I would become sharper than usual. Not loud. Worse: precise.

At night I would fear no animal, because there are none. But I would still wake sometimes.

A wet sound under a floor.

A changed drip.

A pause where water usually threads continuously.

Those would be enough to bring me upright in the dark.

I would take a lamp if there were one ready, or go by hand along the wall if there were not. I would step onto the threshold, listening for the town’s hidden rearrangements. Darkness without animal eyes would not feel empty to me. It would feel full of surfaces pretending to be stable.

My closest relationships would be with people who understand maintenance as devotion. A cistern cleaner with scarred knuckles from lifting lids. A seed keeper who never forgives damp negligence. A terrace mason old enough to insult stone affectionately. Perhaps someone who works the drying platforms and knows the rare bright hours by smell before light changes. We would not speak of love as fire. Fire is brief and troublesome here. We might speak of trust as shared drainage.

If I married, it would matter that our household channels met cleanly.

If I had a child, I would teach them first where not to step.

I think I would have one private fault: I would be too attached to equilibrium. I would believe, more than I should, that if every channel is cleared, every reed bed tended, every wall listened to, the world can be persuaded not to change too violently. The young mountain would teach otherwise in small humiliations. A ledge would crack after years of obedience. A basin would sour without obvious cause. A roof would fail though properly layered. I would have to learn that tending gradients is not control. It is conversation with something that answers slowly and never in full.

So yes: I would be human there, because in that world motion is a human burden.

But I would be human in the way a channel mouth is human: shaped by hands, serving water, easily overlooked until failure. I would be one of the town’s quiet intermediaries, neither central nor marginal, living where clean water becomes garden water, where household care becomes communal consequence.

You would find me kneeling beside a low stone sluice in warm mist, testing the current with two fingers.

Behind me, the town would stand apart in damp clusters: reed roofs, pale paths, dark pools, raised storehouses, walls sweating into moss. Ahead of me, the cultivated reeds would rise in glossy ranks, almost a crowd, almost an audience. The air would hold its usual soft brightness. No bird would call. No insect would pass over the water. Somewhere a person would set down a clay jar with both hands.

I would lift a mat of fallen leaves from the channel, watch the surface clear, and wait to see whether the water remembered the correct way down.
