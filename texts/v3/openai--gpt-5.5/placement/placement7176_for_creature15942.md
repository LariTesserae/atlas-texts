I would not find myself at the center first.

The center is too solemn, too certain of its purpose: the furnaces with their banked red seams, the pressure halls with round doors, the old belts crossing a room in a day. I would approach those places only by assignment, with my mouth wrapped and my hands oiled, feeling the city’s heat through my soles and trying not to look as though I had questions.

Nor would I begin on the ridges, though part of me wants the high ruin: the polished retaining walls, the dead rail spurs, the dust pouring slowly over the edge of the world. That is where a person goes to become philosophical, and the people of this city would distrust that. Too much looking outward. Too much unused thought.

I think I would be found in a lesser district, where the inhabited quarter thins into machinery.

Not abandoned. Not active. A seam-place.

A row of warm apartment blocks on one side, their narrow windows ambered by lamps; on the other, a covered conveyor canal that no longer carries powder but still hums faintly in its supports when the underworks shift. Above it, an elevated tramline whose cars run only twice a month, mostly empty, mostly out of duty. Below, maintenance stairs descending into chambers labeled in a script no one confidently reads.

There would be a small office there, set into the base of a shade frame.

Its sign would say something practical and obscure:

**SURFACE RECORDS / SOUTH-EAST INTERVAL**  
**AUTHORIZED DISTURBANCE ONLY**

That is where I would be.

I would be a dust clerk.

Not a powerful role. Not quite an archivist, not quite a surveyor, not quite a priest, though people would sometimes mistake the work for all three.

My task would be to tend the city’s surfaces.

Footprints, wheel marks, drag scars, fallen flakes of tile, the settling of pale dust against door thresholds, the old compressed paths across plazas no one remembers crossing. In a wetter world these things would vanish before they became meaningful. Here they remain, and because they remain, someone must decide what kind of remaining they are.

A new mark in a forbidden corridor may mean trespass.

A missing layer of dust on a pipe may mean heat leakage.

A line of cinder mites abandoning a flue may mean a change too subtle for gauges.

A bootprint crossing an old mourning court may be grief, vandalism, or maintenance.

A tool left uncovered may be an accident, or a confession.

I would carry a kit: glass-fiber brushes, a slate tablet, folded measuring cords, sample envelopes, a small hooded lamp, a seal stamp, a lens darkened against glare, and a flat ceramic blade used not to cut but to lift dust in sheets thin as breath.

I would know the ages of marks the way other people know faces.

Fresh dust lies sharply around an edge.  
One-year dust has softened but not merged.  
Ten-year dust begins to belong to the floor.  
Older than that, it is no longer a deposit but a memory of pressure.

I would speak quietly even when alone.

Not because I was afraid of waking anything, but because in that city even sound feels like a substance one ought not spend carelessly.

My dwelling would be above the office, in a narrow warm room partitioned from a much larger room that my family no longer uses. There would be a cot, a ceramic screen, a cabinet for filters, a tray of gray dust bearing three names slowly losing their edges, and a single sealed ampoule of water wrapped in black cloth. Not for drinking. Not yet. An inheritance, maybe. Or a warning.

On the wall I would keep objects instead of pictures: a brass gauge needle bent during the south vent failure; a strip of dark cloth from my mother’s face wrap; a dead cinder mite in a clear mineral box; a wing scale from a fallen vane, though I would not admit to owning it if the tower wardens asked.

People would come to me when they needed to know whether something had changed.

A family might ask whether the prints outside their door were recent.  
A guild might ask whether an auditor had passed through a sealed market.  
A mourning house might ask whether a name tray had blurred naturally or been touched.  
Children might bring me flakes, shells, false fossils, little frauds of dust and salt, asking if they were old.

Sometimes I would lie gently.

Not about danger. Never about danger.

But about age, perhaps. About significance. I might tell a child that the mark they found was older than it was, because the city gives children so few miracles. I might tell an old woman that no one had disturbed the threshold of her dead son’s room, though I had seen one careful print there and knew it was hers.

The brass-backed auditors would know me.

Or rather, they would permit me to stand near them.

One in particular would pass through my interval every forty-six days, moving along the conveyor canal with its hooded lenses turned toward the ground. Its nameplate would be illegible except for two characters, so the local children would call it Forty-Six. I would know better than to name it aloud, but privately I would think of it as a colleague.

It would stop outside my office sometimes and perform no recognizable action for several minutes.

I would record this faithfully:

> Auditor paused at threshold.  
> No lamp signal.  
> No stamp.  
> No correction.  
> Duration: 611 breaths.  
> Meaning unknown.

Over the years I would begin to understand that this, too, was a form of greeting.

I would not be young. Or if I were young at first, the city would make that seem temporary and slightly embarrassing. My skin would dry into matte planes. My lips would split in the same places every seasonless year. I would become narrow in the local way: economical with gesture, exact with tools, careful not to lean on walls whose dust had not yet been read.

I would have the profession’s bad habits.

I would notice every disturbance and find rest difficult in rooms too clean. I would dislike polished floors because they conceal time. I would distrust people who erase their tracks too thoroughly. I would be moved almost to tears by an undisturbed ledge.

Love, for me, would be letting someone alter my room and not immediately cataloguing the changes.

If I loved someone, I might know them first by their tread: the slight outward turn of the left foot, the pressure at the heel, the way they avoid old cracks without looking. I would recognize their approach before the lamp flickered under the door. I would not touch their face in public. I might place my gloved hand over their sleeve for three full breaths, which in that city would be almost reckless.

I would not dream of rain.

That would be too foreign.

I would dream of a door opening after centuries and the dust inside lying exactly as expected.

I would dream of a furnace whose heat had not declined by even a fraction.

I would dream of a name pressed into a tray and remaining legible long after everyone who spoke it had gone.

And perhaps, late in life, I would become less clearly a person and more clearly a function of the city.

This happens there, I think. Not magically. Administratively.

A person repeats a task so faithfully that the task begins to wear their face. Their opinions dry out. Their biography compacts. Their body remains, but what matters about them is the interval they keep.

I would become the one who knows the south-east surfaces.

The one who can tell whether a corridor has slept safely.

The one who can read from a powder drift that a valve below has begun to breathe unevenly.

The one summoned when an old footprint appears where no door has opened.

Eventually I would die, of course, though not dramatically. The city does not encourage drama.

My body would be dried, wrapped, tagged, and placed in a warm vault. My name would be pressed into gray dust. At first the letters would stand sharp. Then, slowly, softened by tiny tremors, by distant piston strokes, by the breath of doors and the city’s long thermal patience, they would begin to fill.

But before that, I would leave behind records.

Not memoirs. Not wisdom.

Rows of entries:

> Dust lifted at vent seam. Heat stable.  
> Cinder mites present. Sixteen visible.  
> Unauthorized wheel track near shuttered depot. Likely old. Watch.  
> Vane shadow crossed courtyard at third bell. No fall recovered.  
> Child’s mark preserved by request. Approved for one interval.  
> Auditor paused again. Meaning unknown.

That would be enough.

I would not be a hero there, or a rebel, or a discoverer of the extinguished sun.

I would be a custodian of what remains visible.

A reader of stillness.

A minor organ in the city’s long body, keeping account of the places where dust has moved, and where it has not.
