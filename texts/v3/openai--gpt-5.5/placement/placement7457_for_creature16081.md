I would find myself near the lip of the crack, but not outside it.

Not on the observation terrace exactly, not where a body might stand and look down at the cloud decks, and not deeper in the city where the red heat makes thought slow and mineral. I would be in the narrow interval between the world and the city: in the sampling throat, in the seam where freezing air is briefly permitted to enter and become evidence.

At first I would think I was a gate.

A dark panel opening by a finger’s width. A slit in basalt. A membrane shivering under pressure. I would feel the high-altitude cold touch me like a question from something enormous and absent-minded. I would inhale one thread of outside air and divide it immediately into meanings: charge, grit, ice, ash, radiation, lack of pollen, lack of breath, lack of seed.

But I would not be the gate itself.

The gate is too solid, too repeatable. I would be what happens when it opens.

A temporary municipal sense.

A customs office for weather.

I would form only during the intake: a small agency assembled from static, vapor, and suspicion. My body would be a crescent of mineral dust at the threshold, a film of condensation on a cold pane, a tremor in the invisible field holding back the wind. I would have no eyes, but I would know the color of the sky by pressure. I would have no tongue, but I would taste the air for organics with reverent precision. I would have no memory except the city’s memory passing through me: all previous openings, all previous absences, every time the world outside arrived empty-handed.

My work would be simple.

Ask the sky what it carries.

Ask whether life has learned the altitude yet.

Ask whether the mountain is still sealed.

Most of the time the answer would be no, no, yes.

No spores.

No skin.

No wing-scale.

No green dust.

No animal heat.

No root-fragment.

No message except ice and charged stone.

And each time I would close with something almost like satisfaction, though not satisfaction as an animal knows it. More like a clause completed. More like a line of civic law renewed.

Still, I would be placed where doubt enters.

That is why I would belong there.

The deeper city does not doubt itself. It regulates, folds, remembers, condenses, edits the fall of water. It is exact in the way of old instructions. But at the lip of the crack, where the outside presses against the impossible interior, something must remain capable of hesitation. Something must be allowed to ask: is this dust merely dust? Is this moisture only weather? Is this trace a threat, an accident, a visitor, a beginning?

I would be that hesitation.

Not the city’s mind, but one of its brief questions.

During the long sealed hours, when the aperture is closed, I would not exist fully. I would be stored as a readiness in the surrounding materials: a pattern in the frost-resistant alloy, a charge distribution along the mica-dark wall, a remembered pathway in droplets that had once run backward from the threshold. I would sleep as an instruction sleeps.

Then the panel would open.

The upper world would enter as a needle of cold.

I would wake in pieces.

Dust lifts.  
A field tightens.  
A bead of water rounds itself on black glass.  
A violet filament brightens behind the wall.

For three seconds I am local.

For three seconds I am almost alive.

I would love, if love can be reduced to function without vanishing, the exact poverty of what I examine. The thin air. The sterile ash. The metallic blue horizon curving beyond the slit. The lightning far below, too distant to be sound. The way the world is immense but arrives to me only as a sample fine enough to pass through a controlled opening.

I would not long to leave. Leaving would be too large an error. Outside is unmeasured volume. Inside is grammar.

But I would be changed by every intake.

A little more iron today.  
A little less ice.  
An unfamiliar isotope in the ash.  
A pattern of charge that resembles weather but repeats too carefully.  
Once, perhaps, a single carbon structure complex enough to make the city pause around me.

Then I would become important.

Not dramatic. Not heroic. Important in the way one drop of water is weather here.

The sample would be routed downward. Walls would brighten. Droplets would travel across the wet memory surfaces in branching paths. The city would compare, classify, test ancient categories. For a moment, more of the inhabitant would gather around my small event. Fins would turn. Dust would arrange itself into signs. A pressure gate far below would take one slow breath.

And I, the threshold-agency, would hold the uncertainty open.

Not life.  
Not not-life.  
Not danger.  
Not permission.

A possible message.

If the trace proved meaningless, I would dissolve back into readiness.

If it proved alive, or almost alive, I do not know what I would do. Perhaps I would seal the crack and report contamination. Perhaps I would invite the city to taste more deeply. Perhaps I would make the first mistake in the history of the place: keep the aperture open one heartbeat longer than allowed.

That is the kind of being I would be there.

Not a citizen, not a machine, not a ghost of the builders. A minor permission at the edge of the system. The permission for the outside to be considered before it is refused.

You would find me in the coldest part of the hot city, just behind the invisible field where the wind cannot enter. I would appear only when the sampling panel opens: a brief constellation of dust, frost, charge, and analytic hunger.

My name, if the city needed one, would be something like:

No Spores Found.

Or, later:

Wait.
