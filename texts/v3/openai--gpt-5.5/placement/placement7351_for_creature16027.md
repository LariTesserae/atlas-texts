You would find me where the city has not yet made up its mind.

Not in the grander boiler districts, though I would know their breath by distance. Not in the roof orchards, though I would climb there sometimes to eat hard apples in the gray light. Not in the markets, except passing through with damp notebooks under my coat, stopping only long enough for black tea and a wedge of steamed bread.

You would find me in the Unfounded Quarter, or just beyond it, where the hinged streets begin.

I would live in a narrow upper room hung from an iron frame instead of trusted to the ground. The room would not have four reliable corners. One wall would have bowed inward years ago and been allowed to remain that way because it had stopped moving. My bed would be slung from ceiling hooks. My cups would sit in lipped racks. My table would have one leg shorter than the others on purpose, with a stack of blue pipe labels beneath it that I could add to or remove as the floor chose.

I think I would be a listener.

Not one of the famous ones called to the great bridges or to the deep municipal hearings beneath pump towers, but a district listener: licensed, practical, a little mistrusted, always arriving at inconvenient hours. I would carry a roll of felt, brass listening cups, a bone rod polished by use, chalk, colored wax, a small coil of wire, and a battered ledger tied shut with black cord. My gloves would be my best possession. Dark leather palms, wool backs, patched at the fingers, with thin metal discs sewn into the knuckles so I could feel vibration through brick, pipe, plate, and young stone.

I would be known for kneeling in bad places.

Under market stairs. Beside sweating cellar walls. On tram plates after rain. In narrow alleys where steam has appeared without permission. I would put my ear to the city through instruments and wait while other people tried not to speak. I would hear the ordinary things first: water in pipes, rats behind lath, the tremor of distant presses, a pump turning three streets away. Then, under those, the more serious sounds: a void widening under a courtyard, clay squeezing against piles, root-mass worrying a drain, mineral crust thickening in a seam, a wall deciding whether to continue being a wall.

My work would not be to stop change. No one sane in that city would choose such a trade.

My work would be to tell people what kind of change had arrived.

Here: safe until thaw.

Here: brace before morning.

Here: do not dig below the blue line.

Here: remove children, goats, glassware, and sleeping elders before the third bell.

Here: this warmth is lawful.

Here: this warmth is not.

I would have a quiet reputation. Not cheerful, not grim. Useful. People would lower their voices when I entered a cellar, but not out of fear exactly. More like respect for a person who must hear through the noise of being alive. Children would follow me until their parents called them back. Dogs would sniff my boots and decide whether I had been somewhere dangerous. Cats would tolerate me because I knew which pipes not to sit on.

I would have one damaged ear.

That feels certain.

Not deaf, but altered by some past accident: a pressure release under a culvert, perhaps, or a bridge joint that snapped while I was still apprenticed. The injury would have ended any hope of becoming a grand listener, the kind invited to inspect railway embankments and accretion halls. But it would have given me something else. The bad ear would hear poorly, but it would feel pressure before sound. I would know a room had changed before I could say how. I would turn my head slightly when entering buildings, not from suspicion, but from habit, letting the damaged side taste the air.

I would be from the Unfounded Quarter originally, which means I would not be frightened by a floor that breathes.

My childhood games would have involved marbles rolled across morning boards to discover new slopes. I would have grown up measuring doorframes, marking rugs, sleeping through the granular hush of future stone arranging itself under the house. Other districts would call us odd, and we would call them overconfident. I would have learned early that permanence is not a virtue. Continuance is. Adjustment is. A thing that can shift without breaking is wiser than a thing that stands proud until it falls.

My name would be written in small careful letters in municipal ledgers, probably beside complaints.

Because listeners are never only thanked. We close streets. We condemn walls people love. We delay weddings, markets, funerals, deliveries. We say no to builders with money. We say no to families with nowhere else to sleep. We say no in weather when no one wants to hear it. We are forgiven only later, and not always then.

Still, I would love the city.

Not romantically. Not as a skyline or homeland or idea.

I would love it the way one loves a difficult body one has inhabited for a long time. Its aches would be familiar to me. Its bad habits. Its compensations. The steam under vines. The mineral smell after a fracture opens. The way frost draws white maps along ironwork before dawn. The pause before the second bell, when every creature seems to listen at once: people in doorways, dogs with lifted paws, rooks hunched on cranes, eels rising invisible in black channels.

I would have a route I walked often after rain.

Past the cabbage courts where leaves hold soot in their folds. Under the pipe arcade where laundry freezes stiff by noon. Across a hinged street whose plates answer underfoot with little tin complaints. Down into a cellar beneath a tea room, where the owner keeps jars of luminous algae along the stair because the gaslight is unreliable. There, behind shelves of vinegar and bark tea, a wall would be growing slowly warm.

I would come every third day to listen to it.

At first everyone would assume it was a pipe.

Then a fungus.

Then young stone.

Then something below the young stone.

I would not tell them all my guesses. A listener who speaks too soon becomes a storyteller, and the city has enough of those. I would mark the wall with three colors. Red for heat. Blue for water. White for movement. I would return and find my marks shifted a hair’s breadth apart, as if the wall had taken a breath between them.

This is where you would find me most clearly: crouched in that cellar, one glove pressed flat to damp brick, the bad ear turned toward the ground, my lamp steaming faintly in the cold.

And what would I be listening for?

Not danger only.

A listener in that city must also recognize beginnings.

A street beginning below a street. A chamber forming where no plan placed one. A seam of stone thickening into something that might support weight one day. A hidden current making its first argument with clay. A future district tapping upward through mud, mineral, root, and old machinery.

So if you asked who I was, I might say:

I am a licensed listener of the Unfounded Quarter.

But if you asked more carefully, if you stood with me after rain while the engines paused and droplets fell from vine to metal to mud, I might answer differently.

I am one of the city’s ears.

Not its mind. It has no single mind.

Not its servant. No one serves only one thing there.

I am an ear pressed to a body still becoming ground.
