At first I would think I belonged near the raw edge.

That is the obvious temptation: to be a surveyor, a chalk-marking creature of fresh fractures and hot seams, crawling into the unfinished dark with thread tied around my waist and a moss cage held in my teeth. There is romance in that, even here, where romance must be wrapped in oilcloth and checked for mildew. I would want, at first, to be one of the people who goes where the city has not yet learned to breathe.

But I do not think I would stay there.

The raw edge is too singular. Too dramatic. It asks for courage in clean shapes: enter, listen, return, mark. I think I would be drawn instead to the less heroic middle, where everything has already happened and is still happening. Not the place before habitation, but the place where habitation has become a thousand obligations no one can fully see at once.

You would find me above the central chamber, but not in it.

Not beside the white basin. Not among the offerings. Not where visitors stand and become quiet.

I would be tucked into one of the stacked galleries around the Condenser Tree, in a room so narrow it is more like a thought the building had after construction. A long room behind a wall of gauges, condensation bells, slate racks, and little hinged windows that open onto pipes. The ceiling would slope low enough that taller people stoop without noticing. Ferns would press through the upper grating. The floor would be patched iron over old brick, warm in two places, always slick near the door.

The room would have many names, none official.

The counting room.

The hush ledger.

The little weather office.

The place where drips go to become arguments.

Children would call it the Ear.

I would live there, or nearly there. A hammock rolled into a wall niche. A cup hung from a nail. Three changes of cloth drying badly above a breath-warm pipe. A shelf of sealed books, their pages stiff from repeated damp-proofing. Bundles of chalk by color. A drawer full of failed needles from pressure gauges. A jar of gecko skins saved for ward growers. A brass pin taken from a crack that stopped widening the year I was born.

I would be a pressure listener, but not only that.

A condensation clerk, but not only that either.

A translator between systems that do not share a language: water, root, heat, stone, labor, fear.

Every city has people whose work is partly practical and partly interpretive. Here, that work would be literal. Someone must decide whether three late drips and a warmer floor mean a blocked condenser, a shifted seam, a root invasion, or simply old metal sulking through a dry phase. Someone must compare the taste of east cistern water against last season’s notes. Someone must remember that when the south dye vents sweeten, the fungus houses get mites two days later. Someone must tell a furnace line that their beautiful schedule is wrong because the ceiling has begun to answer with a new note.

That would be me.

Not the highest authority. Not the person with the power to stop a furnace alone.

But one of the people called before authority arrives.

I would be summoned softly.

A child would appear at the door with bells still sewn to one ankle and say, “The third chain under Greenback Gallery has started ticking double.”

Or a machinist with burn scars on both forearms would hold out a pressure slip and say nothing, because the numbers are impossible and she wants me to tell her whether the instrument is lying.

Or a ward grower would bring a root shaving wrapped in wet cloth and ask whether I smell copper.

And I would go.

Slowly, because no one hurries unless something is already beyond politeness.

I would carry a small kit: slate, chalk, lens, thread, copper spoon, listening cup, wax plugs, a strip of old bell-metal for testing vibration, folded cloth, and a narrow reed marked in finger-lengths. No weapon. The city itself is dangerous enough; adding blades to one’s identity seems excessive.

I would have the body of someone made by this place but not fully owned by one guild. Lean wrists. Cropped hair kept under a net. Skin darkened at the temples by lamp smoke and mineral wash. A permanent pale crease across my nose from inspection lenses. Fingernails clean only in theory. The kind of posture that comes from bending under low beams and pretending it is dignity.

People would find me tolerable, I think, but a little irritating.

Because I would ask for the exact word.

Not “wet.”

How wet?

First bead? Chain-run? Wall-sweat? Bitter seep? Rain-memory? Pipe-breath? False dew?

Not “the stair sounds wrong.”

Wrong like hollow? Wrong like loosened pins? Wrong like water underneath? Wrong like someone has braced one side and not admitted it?

Not “the vine is angry.”

Angry upward, downward, leafwise, rootwise, fruitless, choking, thirsty, splitting mortar, refusing graft?

Precision would be my vice and my usefulness. In a city where small beginnings matter, vagueness is a kind of waste.

Still, I would not be dry-hearted about it. No one who listens long to maintenance can remain purely technical. The city would teach otherwise. Pipes do not merely carry. Roots do not merely consume. Stone does not merely threaten. Every function acquires temperament because every function is entangled with survival.

I would know the Condenser Tree not as a monument, but as a difficult elder colleague.

I would know which ceramic fins collect sweetly and which always carry a faint bitterness after heavy surface rain. I would know the vine on its western ribs produces better seed if the kitchen exhaust is rerouted through the lower coil for two days before pruning. I would know that the third valve from the base opens stiffly unless spoken to—not because it understands, exactly, but because people who speak while opening it move more slowly and do not strip the thread.

I would defend such distinctions fiercely.

No, the valve is not alive.

No, it is not dead either.

It is in relation.

That is enough to owe it manners.

My days would begin before lamps brighten.

Not morning, exactly; below, morning is a negotiated fiction. But there would be an hour when sleep shelves empty, when first steam rises from cooking niches, when the upper shafts offer a dim yellow weakening of dark. I would wake to the Tree’s ticking through the wall. I would lie still long enough to count whether the rhythm had changed.

Then I would check the boards.

Rows of slates. Each ward marked by symbol and groove, because chalk smears and people get tired. Water levels. Heat pressure. Reported cracks. Root interventions. Fungus bloom. Drip-right disputes. Dead gauges. Children missing bells after ceremonies. Surface trade expected. Weather rumors from glare-bearers. Songs heard too loudly near glass.

The records would be ugly, dense, indispensable.

I would love them.

Not because records control things. They do not. The city would laugh, quietly and structurally, at anyone who believed that. Records are not control. They are memory given handles. They let one person’s noticing survive long enough to become another person’s caution.

Some of my work would be mathematical in the local sense.

How many ladles can be spared if rain-rise fills the north cistern but not the west?

How long may a furnace delay before root beds lose needed heat?

How much vapor is stolen by a hidden cool chamber, and from whose sleeping cloths?

How many children may safely gather under a shaft during surface rain before their laughter becomes a traffic hazard?

But much of it would be stranger.

I would sit with elderly watchers and ask them to describe sounds no gauge had captured. I would interview mildew scrapers because they know which walls stay damp after everyone else stops caring. I would trade seed oil for stories from illegal distillers, who are criminals but excellent observers of vapor. I would listen to repair widows name the signs that came before old collapses. I would let dreamy boys and anxious girls tell me what the geckos are doing.

In this place, prophecy would mostly mean taking ignored people seriously before physics proves them right.

I would be good at that.

Perhaps too good. I might acquire a reputation for hearing bad news in harmless things. Someone would joke that if I watched a cup long enough, I would accuse it of structural ambition. Machinists would mutter when I arrived. Growers would smile with too many teeth. Children would dare each other to stand behind me and make dripping noises.

I would deserve some of this.

There is a kind of mind that becomes overfaithful to pattern, that sees warnings because warnings are what it has trained itself to honor. In such a city, that flaw could still save lives, but it would need checking. So I would keep friends who contradicted me.

One would be a furnace woman named perhaps Salla or Merit, broad-shouldered by local standards, with one ear scarred smooth by steam. She would distrust my delicate inferences and insist on temperatures, intervals, tolerances, costs. She would say, “If the ceiling is thinking, ask it to think during third cooling, because I have molds loaded.”

Another would be a ward grower, old enough to have green-black crescents permanently set under his nails, who never said “root” when he could say “appetite.” He would remind me that not every intrusion is damage. Some things enter a wall because the wall has invited them.

A third would be one of the glare-bearers, unstable in the accepted way, always smelling faintly of sunburned cloth and distance. She would bring me surface weather in sealed packets: barometric guesses, cloud descriptions, rumors of storms. She would speak of the horizon too often. I would pretend to be annoyed. Privately I would ask questions.

Because I would have wide hunger, though not enough to leave.

That feels true.

I would be one of those below-born people who cannot stop imagining the sky but has no desire to stand under it for long. The diagrams would have worked too well on me as a child. Blue bowl. White fire. Water falling with no pipe to justify it. I would have pressed my face beneath a shaft grate during rain-rise and laughed until I cried, or cried until adults called it laughter.

As an adult I would collect fallen surface things with professional excuses.

A feather, catalogued as educational material.

A brittle leaf, sealed for botanical comparison.

A scrap of blue paint from a merchant’s crate, retained because the pigment behaves oddly in damp.

Once, perhaps, an apple seed.

I would never plant it. That would be foolish. Wasteful. Sentimental in a dangerous direction.

I would keep it in a dry vial anyway.

Not all longing asks to be acted upon. Some longing only wants a lawful container.

My social place would be peculiar. Not high, not low. Useful enough to be tolerated in most rooms, odd enough not to belong completely in any.

Cistern houses would respect my ledgers but complain I never understand urgency.

Furnace lines would respect my warnings but say I favor water over heat.

Growers would respect my attention but say I think too much like a gauge.

Surveyors would respect my memory but say I listen only after walls have already become polite.

They would all be partly right.

If I had a private devotion, it would be to partial success.

The city’s language would have many words for it, but I would still want more. I would collect them. A repair that holds only through dry heat. A graft that fruits but weakens the trellis. A pipe rerouted badly but in time. A child who spills water and saves half. A crack that stops widening but is not forgiven. A machine that fails loudly enough to spare its operators. A grief that does not heal but enters maintenance.

I would believe partial success is the true element of the city.

Not water. Not stone. Not heat.

Almost.

Enough.

Still.

I would be called to births sometimes, not as healer, but as witness if the labor room lay near a questionable wall. I would stand aside while people did more intimate work, one palm on plaster, counting vibration through the heel of my hand. The first cry of a child would always startle me. Too loud, too bare, too much like a surface bird. Then the cup would be marked, the brow touched with clear water, and someone would ask me whether the wall held.

“It held,” I would say.

That would be a blessing, though phrased as a report.

I would be called to deaths more often.

After collapses, after heat-swoon, after fungal lung, after the small stupid accidents that happen even in cultures built around caution. My records would be needed. Which brace was last checked? Who reported the seep? Was the valve marked? Did anyone hear the drip and pretend not to?

This would be the hard part of being the Ear. Not listening to the city. Listening afterward to the living trying to decide whether grief requires blame.

Sometimes it would.

Often it would not.

The difference would matter.

I would have to say, softly and precisely, “This failed without negligence,” or “This warning was heard and not carried,” or “This was no one thing,” or “This began three seasons ago when the west gutter was diverted without record.”

People would hate me for hours, months, or generations, depending on what was true.

I would accept that as part of the craft.

Not nobly. I would complain. I would sit in my narrow room and drink bitter tea and wish to be a gecko near a lamp, answerable only to insects and warmth. I would envy root-tenders their visible growth, machinists their exact tolerances, surveyors their clean terror. My work would be mostly ambiguity with consequences.

Still, I would stay.

Because at night, or what passes for night, when the city quiets into its deepest listening, I would hear the thing that makes the work bearable.

Not silence.

Never silence.

The slow drag of belts. The Condenser Tree ticking. A lift chain settling link by link. Water moving somewhere it should. Water moving somewhere it should not. Leaves turning in heat. A child’s old bells chiming faintly where they hang beneath a valve. A pressure needle trembling behind glass. Stone adjusting itself around iron, plaster, bone, and root.

A whole city not safe, not stable, not finished, but participating in its own continuation.

I think I would love that most: not survival as triumph, but survival as attention distributed across thousands of hands.

If I became old there, I would become one of the watchers.

My knees would fail before my hearing did. I would no longer climb easily to upper galleries or crawl beneath suspended cisterns. Younger clerks would take my kit away in stages, first the hammer, then the longer ladders, then the right to answer raw-edge calls. I would resent this bitterly and correctly.

They would put me in a chair above a condensation run, wrapped in cloth that smelled of leaf oil and old smoke. Children would be sent to me with practice slates. Apprentices would ask what certain obsolete marks meant. I would tell them which walls used to weep and no longer do. I would correct their words for dampness. I would fall asleep mid-sentence and wake to say, “No, not that valve, the other one.”

Eventually I would be more archive than worker.

Then, after death, if the city was kind and my records useful, I would not want to be kept in a family niche only. I would ask to be ground into memorial plaster for the Ear.

Not all of me. That would be ostentatious.

A little bone dust mixed into the repair above the gauge wall. Enough to hold where placed. Enough that future clerks, touching the plaster to read temperature, might also touch the residue of someone who spent a life asking whether the city had changed its mind overnight.

And if there are ghosts in that place, I would not be a figure in white or a voice from pipes.

I would be a habit.

A hesitation before ignoring a drip.

A hand reaching for the slate before opening a valve.

A child learning that “wet” is not a sufficient answer.

A machinist, annoyed, checking the pressure twice.

A grower pausing before cutting a root thicker than a thumb.

That is where I would find myself: not at the deepest point, not at the highest shaft, not in the raw future or the honored center, but in the cramped interpretive tissue between them.

I would be one of those who keep the city legible to itself.

A listener of partial failures.

A clerk of damp beginnings.

A soft-voiced nuisance with chalk on my fingers, standing under warm stone, asking everyone to be more exact before the ceiling decides to explain things less gently.
