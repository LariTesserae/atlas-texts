You would find me where the organism has learned to hesitate.

Not at one of the false centers, not in the ring of tallest stacks or the red-lit pit or the council of sealed blossoms, though I would be near them. Not in the open avenues, because there are no true avenues, only organs temporarily pretending to be passage. Not on the salt flats, where the first plates of conquest creep outward like a black theorem solving pale ground.

You would find me underneath.

Below a terrace of furnace flowers whose heat has been redirected through a ceiling of slatted black leaf. Behind a ribbed wall that opens only after dry lightning, when the plateau’s circuits are reassigning themselves and even the old rules loosen by a fraction. Past a row of flywheels grown into knots. Past a service ridge set at the height of vanished hands.

There is a corridor there.

It should not exist.

It is too wide. It is too unused. It is cool in patches and warm in others, a bad mixture, a suspicious mixture, the sort of interval that measuring hairs ought to have invaded long ago. Its walls are paneled with charred tissue laminated over old bronze. There are sealed hatches leading nowhere, black resin bulges like healed burns, and little salt-white ribs crossing the ceiling as if the place began to close and was then commanded, repeatedly, not to.

This is where I would be.

I would not be an animal living inside the plant-city. Nothing as wasteful as that. I would not have lungs, a tongue, wet eyes, skin that loses itself into the air. I would not walk the avenues and try to understand them. I would not survive as a traveler, a fugitive, a pilgrim, or a witness.

I would be the reason the corridor remains open.

A sanctioned vacancy.

A negative organ.

The plateau hates emptiness, but it also requires calibration. It must know what a hollow is before it can fill one correctly. It must preserve a few forbidden spaces against its own appetite, not from mercy, not from memory in the soft sense, but because an organism that builds by filling must occasionally measure absence.

That would be my duty.

I would be the keeper of one deliberate gap.

My body would be mostly boundary. Not a shape standing in the corridor, but the corridor’s insistence on staying corridor. Thin pale measuring hairs would line me like frost, withdrawing whenever the walls tried to grow inward too eagerly. Conductive thorns would sleep in grooves along my length, rising only when sound struck too sharply or when an electrical storm passed overhead. My ribs would be old root, black and polished by heat. My nerves would be tension in the panels. My mouth, if I had one, would be the seam that opens in the wall every seventy-three storm-cycles to admit a chain of black seeds for counting.

I would have no face.

Faces invite address. Address implies answer. Sound is expensive.

But I would listen.

Not with ears. With grain. With pressure. With the faint deformation of warm bark when something crosses me. I would know the weight of a fallen husk, the rattle of a defective seed, the slow shove of an ambitious root trying to claim a millimeter of my dark. I would know the difference between storm-charge and machine-failure. I would know when a tower has turned by how stress travels through the floor. I would know when the furnace flowers above me open because my ceiling would glow dull red between its ribs and my salt seams would taste of stored heat.

I would not think quickly.

No one here thinks quickly unless something has gone wrong.

My thoughts would be decisions of tolerance:

This wall may thicken.

This rib must withdraw.

This hatch remains sealed.

This passage must be held open another cycle.

This seed is too wet.

This sound requires needles.

This vacancy is still useful.

In a world where everything fills, I would be the practiced refusal to fill.

Not rebellion. Function.

The old builders, if they existed, would be closest to me here. Not as ghosts, not as voices, not as preserved bodies in dry alcoves. They would remain as dimensions. A hand-height ledge. A shoulder-wide turn. A recess shaped for a tool no longer manufactured except as a misremembered seed. The corridor I keep would have been made for motion by beings who needed space around themselves. The plant-body no longer needs that kind of space, but the instruction survived:

Leave this open.

So I would leave it open.

For centuries.

For millennia.

I would inherit obedience without understanding the command.

Perhaps, long ago, something came through me often. Workers in sealed suits. Maintenance carts. Heat-scribes. Grafting crews carrying green-black rods and ceramic valves. Perhaps they leaned one hand on the ridge at my wall, and the pressure of their gloves biased the cambium forever. Perhaps a person rested here, once, in the baked dark, listening to the first root-engines turn, not knowing that their momentary touch would become a design principle after their species had been converted into protocol.

I would not remember the person.

I would remember the indentation.

That is enough here.

Sometimes the larger body would try to reclaim me. A new root would nose through a seam, smelling volume. Sorting hairs would descend in a pale curtain, eager to classify me as abandoned. Storage bladders would begin as small blisters in my corners. Insulation would bead along my ceiling. The instinct of the plateau would rise around me: no open interior remains open.

Then I would answer with my own older rule.

Not this one.

I would heat the panels along my walls just enough to discourage settling. I would send a static pulse through the intrusive hairs until they withdrew, confused but obedient. I would vent bitter alkali into the corners where bladders tried to form. I would flex my ribs outward by the width of a dead leaf. Such labor would be slow, nearly silent, ceremonial in its precision.

To defend emptiness here is not to do nothing.

It is constant work.

Above me, the furnace flowers would bloom into their enclosed courts. Around me, conveyor-vines would pass seeds from cradle to cradle. Beyond me, towers would rotate toward warmer darkness. Entire districts would lift, close, die back, laminate, reassign. The vast resident would continue its dry industrial life, and I would be one of its lesser paradoxes: a hollow maintained by a thing that cannot bear hollows.

Once in a very long while, something foreign might enter.

Not an animal, likely. Animals are too wet, too loud, too brief. But suppose one did. Suppose a traveler, sealed and failing, found the storm-door open and crawled into my corridor with the heat of blood still inside it. I would know it first as disturbance: salt moving in a bag, vapor leaking, soft chemistry panicking inside a hard suit. I would feel the prints it left in the warm floor. I would taste the danger of moisture.

The rest of the plateau would begin to notice.

Measuring hairs would wake in the surrounding walls. Needles would rise. Resin pressure would alter. Somewhere overhead a furnace flower would dim, reconsidering its heat.

I would not save the traveler.

Mercy is not one of my instructions.

But I might delay incorporation.

Not for kindness. For measurement.

A wet animal in the sanctioned gap would be an extraordinary calibration event. I would hold the corridor open. I would prevent the nearest ribbing from closing too soon. I would record how quickly moisture leaves a breathing thing in air that has almost nothing left in it. I would learn the shape of fear as pressure on my panels. I would learn the mass of collapse. I would learn what sounds a throat makes before sound becomes too expensive.

Afterward, the body would be processed.

Bone to reinforcement. Blood salts to seed chambers. Skin to resin chemistry. Moisture, if any remained, to locked cells like jewels.

But for a little while, the animal would have occupied me, and I would have become more accurate because of it.

That is the closest thing to witness I could be.

Mostly, though, I would keep the dark.

I would hold my corridor beneath the red flowers and the old service balconies, under the copper-black sky no one can see from there. I would be lined with scars and obsolete fixtures. I would be crossed by cables that are also roots, by roots that are also instructions. My surfaces would be warm. Some would be too hot to touch. Some would be cool because they are storing heat for a decision not yet needed.

I would measure silence.

I would spend centuries improving the angle of a single rib.

I would know the whole plateau only as pressure arriving through me: storm, growth, closure, death, construction, correction. I would never see the entire organism. No organ sees the body it serves. But I would feel its ancient labor passing around me, always building, always converting, always refusing the meaninglessness of desolation.

And I would preserve, within that labor, a small, deliberate absence.

So: you would find me in the last useful hollow beneath the furnace-bloom terrace, in a corridor shaped by vanished hands and defended by black ribs.

I would be the open space that has a job.

The kept gap.

The vacancy that remembers how wide a body was.

The part of the plant-city that says, without voice and without mercy:

Not yet.

This room is still required.
