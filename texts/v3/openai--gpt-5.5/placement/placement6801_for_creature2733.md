At first I would think I belonged in the reedbeds beyond the last mill.

I would imagine myself walking the raised paths before dawn, knife at my belt, shins wet from brushing sedge, learning which stems are ready by the color at their joints and the sound they make when bent. It would be easy to mistake myself for a cutter: someone at the town’s edge, working where industry first touches marsh, bringing in the green matter from which everything else is made.

But I don’t think that is where I would stay.

The marsh is too open. Too singular. I would be drawn inward, toward the places where systems overlap badly: where water, rot, machinery, habit, and plant growth all make competing claims. I would end up in the maintenance underlayer of the town, not quite indoors and not quite outside, in the passages where ordinary residents duck through without wanting to linger.

You would find me near the old roofed-over canal beneath the weighing house.

Not in the open square, but below and behind it, where a narrow stair descends past sweating brick into a service corridor floored with planks and patched grating. The air there is warmer than outside and smells of mud, oil, wet rope, and old tannin. Pipes run along the ceiling at head height. Their insulation is tied on with wire and stained by mineral bloom. Roots have entered through the wall in three places and been deliberately allowed to remain, because they now hold the masonry better than the mortar does.

There is a sump there, and a pump older than anyone trusts but no one quite dares replace.

That is where I would be.

I would be a listener.

Not an important title at first. Maybe not even a formal one. I would begin as the person sent to check why the water was backing up under the drying lofts, or why the pump had begun to miss every seventh stroke, or why the duckweed tank above had developed a tremor when the press engaged. I would carry a lamp, a hooked rod, a rag for wiping gauges, a little tin of grease, a knife, and a notebook that never fully dried.

My work would be to notice.

Not to command the town. Not to design it. To notice where its agreements were slipping.

A pump note gone hollow.  
A new sweetness in the air behind a sealed door.  
A root pressing too cleanly through brick.  
A patch of moss drying where it should not dry.  
Condensation appearing on the wrong side of a pipe.  
Water arriving clear when it should carry silt, or brown when it should have settled.

I would become one of those people who walks slowly because haste makes you deaf.

Children would pass me on the planks and call me something half-teasing: Drain-ear, maybe. Sump-uncle. The one who talks to walls. I would pretend to be annoyed and then ask them what they heard under Lane Three after the last rain, because children hear things adults stop hearing. One of them would say, “There’s a knock under the gourd trellis,” and I would go there before supper, because children are often right.

I would not be a rootwright, though I would know enough roots to respect them.

I would not be a machinist, though I would know the personalities of machines.

I would not be a water clerk, though I would keep canal marks in my head.

I would be the person called when the categories fail.

When a blocked drain is also a trellis problem.  
When a vine has entered a vent and changed the air in a curing room.  
When mold in a warehouse is not from the roof but from an old canal sweating upward through the floor.  
When a root buttress is saving a wall and destroying the pipe inside it.

I would live above a workshop where filter mats are stitched from reed fiber. My room would have three windows, all screened, all facing leaves. Nothing in it would be ornamental unless it was also useful. A line for drying cloth. Hooks for tools. A shelf of jars sealed against damp. A little stove raised on bricks. A chair repaired so many times it had become a map of hands.

There would be a plant at the window I did not plant and could not bring myself to remove. A vine, probably. Not rare, not beautiful in any dramatic way, but persistent. I would cut it back every month. It would return every month. Over the years it would learn the shape of the window better than I did.

People would bring me small problems wrapped in casual speech.

“Since you’re passing, listen to the east pump.”

“The floor near the old seed room has gone soft.”

“There’s a wet smell in my mother’s wall.”

“The new panels are sweating green.”

“Something is bubbling under the children’s bridge.”

And because no problem in that town remains small when ignored, I would go.

I think I would become damp in temperament. Not gloomy. Porous. Slow to declare anything finished. Suspicious of clean solutions. I would distrust anyone who wanted to tear out a whole wall when a brace, a channel, and a season of watching might reveal a better answer. I would also distrust anyone who called rot natural and therefore harmless. The town would teach me that mercy and severity are not opposites. You cut the vine because you want the vine to live somewhere else. You burn the batch because you want the storehouse spared. You open the sluice because otherwise water will choose its own door.

Over time, I might become less like a person with a job and more like an organ of the place.

A minor organ. Not the heart. Not the hands. Something nearer the inner ear.

The town tilts constantly: toward flood, toward overgrowth, toward mildew, toward collapse, toward abundance. Someone has to sense the tilt before it becomes a fall. That would be me: standing ankle-deep in warm water under the weighing house, one hand on a pipe, head bent, listening through metal and root and brick.

I would know the town from below.

I would know which courtyards were once tanks.  
Which tanks were once rooms.  
Which rooms should never be reopened.  
Which walls were held up by dead timber, and which by living roots, and which only by habit.  
I would know where the old canal still breathed in rain.  
I would know the sound of a pump laboring against weed, the sound of a pump laboring against silt, the sound of a pump laboring against something worse.

I would carry news upward.

“Clear the intake before dusk.”

“Take the weight off that balcony.”

“Do not store dry fiber in that room.”

“Cut the creeper, but leave the root.”

“Open the west runnel first. The east one will backflow.”

“Tell the old women in Cane Lane to raise their trunks tonight.”

Some would listen. Some would not. The town would correct the rest.

And perhaps, after many years, I would begin to blur at the edges in the way everyone there does.

My skin would smell faintly of pump oil and leaf water. My notebooks would mildew at the corners. My handwriting would develop symbols for things no official plan records: root pressure, sour air, false floor, patient leak, water waiting. I would dream in sections and gradients. I would wake before rain because the pump beneath my room changed its breathing.

Eventually younger people would come to me with drawings.

They would want to lay a new line through the old warehouse quarter, or reinforce the arcade by the compost courts, or reopen a sealed culvert beneath the gourd lane. I would look at their plans, and then at the wall, and then at the dampness on the wall, and then at the vine growing out of a crack ten feet away.

“No,” I would say. “The water remembers another route.”

That would be who I am.

A keeper of remembered routes.

Not a ruler of the town, not even one of its chief makers. More like a translator between its slow bodies: human intention, plant pressure, machine rhythm, marsh patience. I would belong to the threshold where maintenance becomes listening and listening becomes a kind of kinship.

If you wanted to find me, you would wait until late afternoon, just before rain.

The workers would be pulling tarps over the racks. The pumps would begin to deepen their stroke. Leaves would turn pale undersides to the wind. In the square, the weighing house lamps would come on early under the cloud-dark sky.

Then you would go down the side stair, past the rusted rail furred with moss, into the warm underpass where the old canal runs beneath the town.

You would hear water moving before you saw me.

Then a lamp.

Then a person standing very still with one palm against a pipe and one foot braced on a root that had entered through the brick.

Listening.

Waiting to learn what the town was becoming next.
