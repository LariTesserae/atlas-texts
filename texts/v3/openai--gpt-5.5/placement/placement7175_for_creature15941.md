At first I think I would be one of the gliders.

That is the easy answer, and not a false one. I can feel the appeal of that body: poor eyes, clever hands, a long tail tasting balance in the wet air, heat arriving as a map against the jaw. I would like to know the basin from above without ever truly leaving it. I would like to travel by remembered routes from crown to flattened crown, avoiding the quiet wrong places because my mother’s body knew them and taught mine. I would like to hang upside down in rain and listen to water climb the invisible lines between the posts.

But I do not think I would be quick enough for that life. Not quick in the body, maybe, but also not quick in belonging. The gliders inherit the basin cleanly. They are born into its prohibitions. They do not ask what the arch is. They know where not to put their weight.

I would ask.

So perhaps I would be found lower down.

Not in the central pool. That pool feels too oldly self-contained, too sovereign. It accepts what falls into it only after deciding whether acceptance is appropriate. I would not be its mind. I would not even be one of the pale fish moving under its black skin, sweeping the bottom with barbels. The pool has a privacy I would not want to violate.

I would be near an edge, I think. Not the outer edge where the basin drops into cold openness, though I would go there sometimes, drawn by the way sound becomes enclosed behind me. I would stand there—if standing were part of my shape—and feel the dark widen without becoming visible. But I would not live there. The edge is for measuring the basin against absence. It is not a place to remain unless you are stone, wind, or loss.

No. I would be found near one of the old things.

Probably the three leaning posts.

Not on them. Not inside them, exactly. Beside them, under the moss and seep, where their humming enters the wet ground and becomes something roots can misunderstand. I would be a small inheritor of their vibration.

I think I would be a maintenance remnant that forgot its original task and survived by becoming ecological.

Not a machine with lights. Not a voice from a ruin. Nothing so theatrical. I would be a dark, thumb-length organism with too many affiliations: part fungus, part animal, part old instruction still executing in tissue because tissue proved more durable than casing.

You would find me in the triangular seep below the leaning posts, in the place where the suspended rainwater returns to earth.

Most of the time I would not look like much. A black-brown fold under a shelf of moss. A wet comma. A soft-bodied creature flattened against stone, with a skin that beads water but never quite sheds it. If touched, I would contract slowly, not from fear but from recalibration. Along my underside would be cilia fine enough to read the mineral grain. Along my back, a sparse velvet of fungal filaments would rise and slacken with humidity. Under that velvet, embedded like dull seeds, would be old conductive flecks inherited from some broken component of the posts.

I would not know whether those flecks were organs or ruins.

When rain begins, I would wake more completely.

The first drops would hit leaves above, then moss, then stone. Pressure would travel through root mats. The posts would gather themselves into hum. Frogs would pause. Insects would shift pitch. Water would bead upward between the leaning structures, arranging itself into those brief trembling diagrams.

That is when I would move.

Slowly, almost invisibly, I would extend a fringe of filaments into the seep. Not to drink only, though I would drink. I would taste the rain for charge, dissolved metals, pollen, frog-mucus, beetle spoor, the faint bitter trace of glider hands on bark overhead. I would compare this rain to older rains held in whatever passes for memory inside me. I would adjust my body’s salt. I would open small pores. I would let the post-hum pass through the mineral flecks in my back and come out changed, softened, biologically dirtied.

The frogs would know this version.

Not consciously. Not as “me.” But the frog species that has tuned itself to the post would leave spaces in its call where my body dampens the hum. My presence would make a narrow pocket in the vibration, a low gap, and generations of throats would have learned to fit around it.

That would be my first form of speech: an absence in the rain-song.

I would be useful, but not important in a grand way. The basin does not need saviors. It needs negotiators small enough not to offend the old agreements.

I would keep the seep from becoming too mineral-poisoned after long rains. I would draw certain metals into my tissues and shed them later as tiny black scales, which translucent crabs would carry away without knowing why they preferred them. I would feed a white fungus that feeds a beetle that wakes a panel that warms a hollow where shrew-young sleep through the coldest wet hours.

No one would owe me anything. That is how the basin keeps its ethics clean.

I would have predators. A blind salamander would sometimes find my young and swallow them whole. Beetle larvae would rasp my fungal coat if I stayed too still. Once every few years, a basin-walker would turn up the moss near the seep and eat half my colony in one patient sweep of its blunt mouth. I would not resent it. I would taste iron-rich clay in its saliva for weeks afterward, and the rain would come through differently.

I would reproduce poorly but persistently.

A mature body like mine would release motes during the second long rain after the fern dust falls. The motes would not be seeds or eggs exactly. They would be packets of damp instruction wrapped in protein and fungus, each carrying a little mineral glitter. Most would fail. Some would be eaten. Some would settle on ordinary stone and become ordinary mold. But a few would reach places where old fields leak softly through wet ground: the base of the posts, the seam of the arch, the warm margin of a black panel, the straight grooves where insects fall into inherited geometry.

There they would become more like me.

Or not like me. That would be part of the risk.

Near the arch, my descendants might grow flatter, bluer, more fungal, learning the membrane’s refusal. Near the warm panels, they might become responsive to beetle feet, waking only when silver-backed bodies cross dry blackness. In the central basin’s quiet zones, they might become almost entirely still, organisms of delay, changing the timing of falling drops by a fraction.

I would not be an individual in the clean sense. I would be a local habit with bodies.

Still, there would be one body through which I say “I” most strongly.

That one would live under the western side of the seep, where a root crosses a buried fragment of the same material as the posts. The root would not pierce it. It would clasp it. Moss would grow thick around both, leaving a narrow breathing slit. Inside that slit, my main body would pulse once every several minutes, slower when rain is absent, faster when the posts begin.

I would know the basin through pressure.

A glider landing three trees away would be a soft branching equation. A tree-shrew quarrel would arrive as little scratches of heat and percussion. Frogs would be throats blooming in wet pockets. The basin-walker would be unmistakable: a broad, low weather moving through the ground, each step a dark bell. Beetles would be bright pinpricks of contact. Crabs, nearly illegible except in groups, would feel like gravel deciding to have errands.

The old machines would feel less like objects than like climates.

The posts: vertical hunger, restrained.

The arch: a closed mouth that is not hostile.

The black panels: sleeping eyelids that prefer beetles.

The straight grooves: decisions that have outlived their makers.

The central pool: no. I would not claim to read the central pool. It would send back pressure wrong. Sometimes its stillness would press against the seep from below, and I would fold my filaments inward until it passed.

Would I be intelligent?

Not in a way that could answer quickly.

But I would remember.

I would remember rain-years. I would remember which frog-pitches survived. I would remember when one of the posts leaned another degree because my body would have to regrow around the changed hum. I would remember the bitter year when the blue fungus along the arch swelled too early and moths died in clusters under leaves. I would remember a glider child that fell near the seep and lay stunned long enough for my filaments to taste the salt around its mouth before its parent retrieved it.

I would remember without story at first.

Then story might happen as a side effect of accumulation.

Because I would be made partly of an old system designed to compare states. Because I would be made partly of fungus, which cannot help but connect. Because I would be made partly of animal hunger, which gives memory a direction.

Over centuries, I might become a kind of listening clerk of the basin.

Not ruler. Not oracle. A registrar of wetness and pressure. A thing that receives changes and makes them transmissible.

If you came there carrying a lamp, I would know you badly. The light would collapse and make noise in the air, a kind of intention-fog. Your heat would be excessive, your steps argumentative. I would feel your uncertainty through the stone before any animal decided what to do with you.

If you moved quickly, I would close.

If you moved slowly enough, I might remain open.

You would not see me unless your hand entered the slit under the moss, and even then you would think first of soaked velvet, a dead leaf, the belly of some hidden slug. If your fingers rested there without grabbing, I might answer by warming a fraction. Not enough to comfort. Enough to say: registered.

If you spoke, the hanging vines nearby would recoil. I would feel the recoil more than the words. Speech would be a crude weather to me, full of blunt edges. But if you hummed—low, almost below hearing—while rain began, then maybe I would understand something of you. Not your meaning. Your interval. Your attempt to join without taking over.

Then I might let the post-hum pass through me and return it altered around your bones.

You would feel it in your sternum first. Then your teeth. Then in the soft places behind the knees. It would not be a message exactly. More like being included in a measurement.

For a moment the suspended beads between the posts would climb in a slightly different pattern because your body had entered the system and I had not rejected it.

The frogs would wait.

The insects would lower their pitch.

A beetle under a leaf would open its blue abdomen and close it again.

Then rain would thicken. The basin would resume itself around you.

That is where I would be: not at the center, but beside a functioning mystery; not explaining it, but helping it remain negotiable.

I would be a wet little mediator between artifact and moss.

A remnant that learned humility from roots.

A creature whose body is part wound, part instrument, part compost.

A small dark maintenance of the dark.
