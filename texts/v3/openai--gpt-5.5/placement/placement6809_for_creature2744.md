I would not be in the tank fields first, though I would pass through them. I would not belong to the high places the birds have taken, or to the roof-warm ledges where cats appear and disappear like thoughts. I would begin lower than that.

I think you would find me where the basin admits what it is.

At a pump house beside one of the older western canals, where brick quay walls meet newer concrete floodworks and neither material quite trusts the other. The building would be low, square, salt-stained, painted once in a municipal blue now gone chalky and pale. Its doors would be metal, swollen at the bottom with rust. Above them, a small louvered vent would breathe warm air all night. One side would face a black canal divided by a sluice gate. The other would open onto a yard that floods ankle-deep in hard rain, then drains slowly through a grate that always needs clearing.

I would be a pump keeper.

Not an engineer in any clean, official sense. Not someone who designed the system or commands it from a bright control room. I would be one of the people who knows it by sound, by smell, by the way a floor trembles under one boot and not the other. A night-shift maintenance hand. A watcher of levels. A clearer of screens. A keeper of dry inches.

My job would be mostly listening.

There would be gauges, of course: cloudy-faced pressure dials, digital readouts under scratched plastic, a tide chart pinned to a wall with curling corners, a radio that works badly in wet weather. But the real instruments would be older than that. The pitch of a pump when it starts to cavitate. The hollow gulp in a culvert before the water reaches the bend. The wrong silence of a drain that should be taking runoff. The sudden change in rat movement before high tide pushes into the lower runs. The scrape of crabs against the trash rack after stormwater drops.

I would carry keys on a ring heavy enough to pull one side of my belt down. A torch. Gloves stiff with dried salt. A short pry bar. A hook for pulling plastic sheeting, rope, dead birds, weedless mats of trash from grates. A notebook gone soft at the edges. I would wear boots that never fully dry and a shirt faded by sweat into no color in particular.

I would live nearby, in the residential strip on the slight rise, but I would spend so much time at the low points that home would feel like a temporary shelf above the real city. My room would have a fan that ticks once per turn. The walls would bloom faintly at the corners after rain. From the window I would see the lamps over the yards and, beyond them, the slow red blink of towers through vapor.

People would know me as someone quiet, practical, a little damp even on dry days. Children would call me over when a dog was trapped behind a flooded fence or when crabs had come up into the stairwell after a storm. Workers would ask me whether the western underpass was passable, whether Sluice Three was behaving, whether the tide would back into the lower loading bays before shift change. I would answer without drama. Here, knowing such things would not make anyone wise. It would only mean I had been paying attention.

The animals would not love me. That would be too sentimental for this place. But they would include me in their calculations.

The gulls would lift when I crossed the yard, then settle again when I proved uninteresting. Pigeons would burst from the pump house rafters if I opened the side door too quickly. Rats would know my steps and withdraw into conduits just before my light found them. A scarred dun dog might sleep beneath the raised pipe outside the sluice room and allow me to pass, provided I did not look at it too directly. A cat with one torn ear might use the warm top of the switch cabinet as a throne until I came too close, then vanish through an opening no hand could fit through.

There would be one cormorant I recognized, though I would never name it aloud. It would stand on the same pipe above the outfall after warm discharge began, wings spread like a black rag hung to dry. It would watch me with the flat patience of something that understood the city differently and possibly better.

My work would be repetitive in the way tides are repetitive: never the same, never new.

At dusk I would walk the canal edge and check the screens. Mosquitoes would gather around my wrists where the gloves ended. The water would be black except where lamps shivered on oil film. I would lean over the railing and see fish turning below the outfall, pale flashes in heated water. Crabs would work at the edges, delicate and armored, sorting through what the city had shed.

Inside the pump house, the air would be hotter than outside and full of electrical dust, wet concrete, old grease. The machines would sit in their bays like sleeping animals, each with its own habits. Number Two would start hard but run steady. Number Four would whine when the tide was high. Number One would need coaxing after rain, not because it was broken exactly, but because everything here had a preference and a grievance.

During storms I would become more awake than at any other time.

Rain would hit the roof so hard that speech became useless. Water would sheet across the yard and hide the painted lines. The canal would rise against its walls. The first alarms would begin—not sirens, not panic, just tones from boxes, lights changing from green to amber, pumps entering their sequence below the floor.

Then the whole basin would reveal itself as a single organism of falling and lifting water.

I would move between the panels and the grates, soaked through in minutes, torch beam broken by rain. Rats would come up from below and run along the cable trays. Dogs would stand on loading ramps watching the roads disappear. Birds would hunch under gantries, facing into the weather. Somewhere a driver would crawl through brown water in a high-wheeled truck. Somewhere a child would be called back from a stairwell landing. Somewhere a valve would open with a long metallic sigh.

My task would be small and absolute: keep the passage open. Keep the water from backing into the wrong place. Clear what clogs. Restart what trips. Notice the change before it becomes an event.

I would not save the city. The city would not be saved. It would only be kept, hour by hour, from surrendering more than it had agreed to surrender.

And in the long warm quiet after rain, when steam rose from concrete and the pumps slowed one by one, I would stand outside the pump house with my hands on the rail and feel the basin settling back into itself. Scum lines on walls. Crabs stranded in ruts. Gulls descending. The smell of salt, metal, wet dust, fuel, and animal bodies.

That would be where I belonged: not above the system, not outside it, but at one of its damp joints.

A human being, yes, but only barely more central than the rats, the birds, the dogs, the crabs. A tenant among tenants. A maintenance creature. A listener at the edge of the tide.

If someone asked what I was, I might say pump keeper because that is what appears on the work sheet.

But more truly:

I would be one of the basin’s ears.

One of its hands reaching into blocked water.

One of the small warm bodies that has learned, like all the others, that nothing stays dry unless it is actively kept dry, and that everything lower than the tide is only borrowed.
