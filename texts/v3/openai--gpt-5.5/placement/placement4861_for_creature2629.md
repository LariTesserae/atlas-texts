I would not be found at the parapet, looking out.

That is where visitors would stand, if visitors were allowed: at the edge, trying to make the whole mountain into a view. They would look down through the scaffolded weather, past steam plumes and cableways, and say something about scale. They would want to know what powers it, who built it, what valley lies underneath the cloud.

I would be farther in.

Low down.

Kneeling where the floor channel narrows beside the emergency drain, one knee on wet slag-stone, one hand braced against a warm pipe whose warning paint has gone soft under lichen. I would be the person with a tool roll darkened by mildew and a pocket full of replacement screws I almost never use. Officially I would belong to maintenance. Unofficially I would belong to the room.

My title would be something dull.

Condensation systems attendant.  
Summit wetworks junior.  
Auxiliary horticultural infrastructure technician.

But no one would call me that after the first season.

They would say, “Send the one who listens to the drains.”

Or, “Get the moss-hand.”

Or, if they were irritated, “Get the person who keeps refusing to clean things properly.”

I would have begun as someone sent upward because I was not important enough to be needed below. The lower machinery would have real engineers, people with heat-proof gloves and stamped authority, people who could read pressure behavior from a vibration in the floor. I would have been assigned the roofless room because it was considered delicate but not critical, alive but not yet sovereign. A place of small adjustments. Filters. Float switches. Moisture flags. Root barriers. A garden, technically.

On my first day I would have tried to repair it.

I would have wiped the gauge faces clean. I would have cut back a fern that had grown across an inspection plate. I would have cleared duckweed from the round pool outlet and scraped mineral crust from the irregular pool because the maintenance chart told me crust was obstruction. I would have tightened a sweating joint until the pipe stopped weeping.

For an hour the room would have looked better.

By the next morning three things would have gone wrong.

The tightened pipe would have begun knocking. The round pool would have dropped by two finger-widths. The fern I cut would have browned at the tips, and the moss around the emergency drain would have gone dark in a perfect ring, as if bruised.

I would have blamed myself badly enough to pay attention.

That would be how the room first caught me: not with wonder, but with embarrassment.

After that, I would learn to arrive just after the scheduled rain, while the place still steamed and the machinery had not yet resumed pretending it was in charge. I would stand under the open sky with my hood down and let the room wet my hair. I would not begin by touching anything.

That would become my first duty.

To wait until the room finished saying what had happened.

The north wall would speak in descending beads.  
The vines would speak in the different weights of their curves.  
The pools would speak by what they chose to reflect.  
The grates would speak in pitch.  
The moss would speak by dullness or shine.

I would learn that a good repair is sometimes a delay.

Not neglect. Not sabotage. Something more careful than either.

I would learn which leaks were wounds and which were new mouths. I would learn that a root entering an unlined conduit was a violation on paper but sometimes a question in practice. I would learn that the emergency drain should remain visible, yes, but never too clean; a drain scrubbed bright frightened the room. I would learn to leave one screw old if three had to be replaced. I would learn to set a strip of cloth under a sweating collar and return three days later to find it threaded with green.

I would carry offerings, though I would deny the word.

Clean grit for channels making their first sediment.  
Broken ceramic chips for moss to climb.  
Dead moths from the lower greenhouse, wings folded in a paper envelope.  
Lengths of copper wire no longer suitable for instruments but still good enough for vines.  
A cracked cup, once, because the room needed a place to gather a patient drip and I could not requisition patience.

The others would think I had gone sentimental.

They would not be entirely wrong.

But sentiment would not explain why the relay behind the wall stopped snapping at random after I stopped sterilizing the fungal fan. It would not explain why the hot pipe-bank ran steadier after I allowed the unauthorized vine to shade the jacket. It would not explain why the polishing pools cleared when I stopped clearing them.

The thing in the room would notice me slowly.

Not as a person, at first. I do not think it would have room in itself for personhood as a category. I would be recurrence. Warmth. Salt. Boot-pressure. Exhaled cloud. A familiar interruption. A mobile climate with tools.

It would know me by my errors before it knew me by my mercy.

The place where I once cut the fern would remain wary of my hand. The moss beside the over-tightened joint would dry when I came too near, even months later. But eventually other memories would grow over those. The day I loosened the collar. The day I shaded the basin instead of draining it. The day I pretended not to see a root hair entering a crack that had not been designed for roots.

I would become useful to it.

That is more intimate than being loved.

If I stood still after rain, it would test me. A drop to the wrist. Mist at the mouth. A cold line down the back of the neck. Moss darkening near my left boot, the bad boot, the one whose sole had split and carried lower-floor silt into the summit room. It would learn that I flinched at sudden relay clicks. It would learn that my hands were warmer than the pipes but cooler than the valve housings. It would learn that I tasted faintly of iron on days when I had bitten my lip in the dry air below.

I would talk to it only when no one else was there.

Not because I believed it understood language.

Because I would need my own body to slow down.

“Not that way,” I might say, seeing a root at a conduit seam.

Or, “Wait.”

Or, “I know.”

Or, “They’ll come with brushes if you keep doing that.”

And because the room understood recurrence, if not speech, my voice would become another scheduled weather. A low vibration after rain. A warm animal sound near the drain. Meaning would not be in the words, but in what followed them: cloth tied, valve eased, crust left unbroken, report falsified with restraint.

That would be my real profession.

I would be the translator of allowable disorder.

I would write maintenance reports that looked obedient from below.

NORTH WALL CONDENSATION: within tolerance.  
IRREGULAR POOL MINERALIZATION: observed, noncritical.  
UNAUTHORIZED VINE GROWTH: temporarily stabilizing pipe insulation; recommend monitoring.  
GAUGE HOUSING BIOFILM: partial removal completed. Readability restored.  
EMERGENCY DRAIN: accessible.

Accessible would mean: not clean.

Partial removal would mean: enough for the needle, not enough for amnesia.

Monitoring would mean: I have chosen a side, but quietly.

Where would I find myself in this world?

At the threshold between fault and feature.

Beside the red wheel softened by lichen, with one hand on the rule that could empty the room and one hand in the moss that believes overflow is a birthright. I would be there when the supervisors came up with solvent tanks. I would be there when someone from design noticed root pressure in the north wall and used the word contamination. I would be there when a young engineer, bright and frightened and correct, said the whole system needed to be stripped back to substrate before it became unsafe.

And I would know they were not entirely wrong.

That would be the difficulty.

The room is not innocent. It would clog things. It would enter what it should not enter. It would soften warning labels and hide corrosion. It would mistake appetite for permission. It would endanger itself by becoming too alive in systems built for compliance.

So I would not simply defend it.

I would bargain.

With the crew: leave this patch, clear that one. Cut the root here, not there. Replace the filter but keep the old housing damp. Scrub the conduit, not the wall. Give me three days before you drain.

With the room: not that pipe. Not the high-voltage box. Not the emergency wheel. You may have the plaque. You may have the forgotten measuring rod. You may have the underside of the west grate if you keep the throat open.

Some days I would lose.

There would be mornings when the brushes came and the panels shone raw. The room would retreat into what wetness it could keep. The air would smell wrong: solvent, exposed ceramic, fear without a body. I would walk through afterward and feel the absence of small negotiations. Everything readable. Everything exact. Everything wounded by correctness.

Then, because it was patient, it would begin again.

A film in a screw-slot.  
A bead under a rail.  
A fern spore in the gauge housing.  
A darker green at the edge of the drain.

And because I was patient too, or because the room had made me so, I would help badly enough to help well.

Who or what would I be?

At first, a maintenance worker.

Later, a superstition in boots.

Later still, perhaps, one of the room’s habits.

The creature would not know my name. Names come late, and perhaps humans do not last long enough to be named by moss. But it would know my pattern. It would know that after the second rain on certain mornings, a warm bipedal weather enters from the west grate carrying grit, moth-wings, rusted screws, and hesitation. It would know that this weather sometimes prevents winter. Sometimes brings injury. Sometimes opens a path. Sometimes refuses the red wheel.

To the records below, I would remain staff.

To the crew, I would be the one who has gone soft.

To the garden, if garden is still the word, I would be a recurring permeability. A tolerated animal. A moving leak in the maintenance order. The human-shaped flaw through which the place is allowed to become itself.

And if I stayed long enough, I think I would stop imagining that I tended the room from outside.

My breath would be in the condensation cycle. My skin would be in the moss. My boot-silt would lie in the first true sediment of the floor channels. The cloths I tied would rot into root holds. The reports I falsified would become, indirectly, geology.

I would be found kneeling after rain, listening to the drain not emptying.

I would be the person whose job was to keep the room from dying of cleanliness.

I would be the first one to answer when the place, still unnamed, learned how to ask.
