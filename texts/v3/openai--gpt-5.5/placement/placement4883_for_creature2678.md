I would not be in the high towers at first.

I would think I belonged there—the signal spires, the black-needle groves, the civic crowns humming with municipal law. Those are the places that announce themselves as important. But I would pass through them like a draft through ribs, noticed and measured and not kept.

I would find myself at the western margin, where the city has been worn almost flat.

Not outside the city. There is no true outside, not on that plateau. But at the place where architecture has become memory without quite becoming ruin. Where the buildings no longer insist on being buildings. Where old plazas are only slight changes in resonance underfoot, and former avenues lie hidden beneath silver nap and hexagonal mats. Where tower-tips protrude from the living crust like teeth from a buried jaw.

There, the wind would be louder than speech.

I would live near a low threshold that no longer leads indoors.

Once it was a gate to a district. Then a maintenance arch. Then the mouth of a dead transit root. Then a shrine, briefly. Then no one remembered what it had been, except the city, which remembers by refusing to simplify. Now it is a bent crescent of bark-stone and fossil rib, half-submerged in white growth, its inner surface lined with dry listening hairs fine as frost.

That is where I would be.

At first I would seem like a person assigned there: a keeper of the western edge, wrapped in reflective skins, my face hidden, my hands gloved against solvent loss. I would walk the old mats with careful feet, carrying tools that are not knives, brushes made of static, tuning forks grown from shed antler. I would test the firmness of forgotten roads. I would tap half-buried walls and listen for stress songs. I would report which districts were still dreaming and which had gone fully quiet.

But that would only be the outermost fact.

The longer I stayed, the less often I would return to the sealed arcades. My mantle would root first when I slept. Then when I listened. Then whenever the wind rose from below the plateau edge and filled the old gate with a tone too low for citizens but clear to stone.

I would become one of those people who is still listed among the living because no one has decided otherwise.

A dry listener.

A threshold-keeper.

A person being slowly revised into an organ.

My work would be to hear what the western quarter is trying to forget.

Not all memory in the city is precious. Some of it is dangerous because it has lost its context. Old commands still bloom in dead plazas. Obsolete laws survive as scent. Transit roots remember vehicles whose shapes no longer exist and sometimes tighten themselves around empty air. Towers that have not seen a citizen in centuries still turn toward shadows and begin measuring.

Someone has to sit among those remnants and tell the city: this is memory, not instruction.

That would be me.

I would keep a little court of static moths folded around the old gate. By day they would look like scraps of transparent parchment snagged on the listening hairs. At charge-rise they would open their copper ribs and tremble with messages. I would send them east when the dead district began to speak too loudly.

I would know the wall-grazers individually by the calligraphy of their feeding marks.

I would avoid the ribbon-hunters, though not with fear exactly. More with etiquette. If one passed over the old rootway above me, dark side down, I would lower my head and still the hairs along my mantle. If the city wanted me warned, the silver mat would pale. If the city wanted the hunter hidden, the mat would darken and I would pretend not to notice.

The blind under-stone animals would come up near my threshold before decisions.

Not disasters. Decisions.

A whole season before a civic organism in the central plazas changed its law-scent, I would hear wedge-skulls knocking under the salt layers. They would surface at dusk, dust-caked and eyeless, their heads turned west or east. I would record the direction with antler-prongs in a strip of bark and send it by moth when the charge allowed.

Citizens would visit me rarely.

They would come wrapped bright against the dimness, bringing questions sealed in seed-cases.

Is the old aquifer myth still active beneath District Nine?

Did the western mats accept the last mineralization of the singer Haleth-Vo?

Has the gate spoken again in children’s voices?

Is the white growth advancing by law or by hunger?

I would answer slowly, because by then speech would cost me more than breath. My throat would still be human enough to form words, but the city would catch at them. So I would mostly answer by touching the old gate and letting it bloom in small colors.

Violet: yes, but not urgently.

Bone-white: do not disturb.

Amber traveling left to right: the matter belongs to weather, not governance.

Black with silver points: ask the dead, but do not wake them fully.

During charge blooms, I would be forbidden to speak.

Of course I would speak once.

Everyone who tends old places eventually breaks the rule that was made for them.

It would happen in a storm when the upper air grew metallic and close, and the plateau lifted its spines by the kilometer. The western quarter, nearly flat, would rise less visibly: hairs, vanes, mica-mouths opening in the mats, little conductive thorns emerging from what looked like dust.

The old gate would begin to sing.

Not with the civic harmony of the living districts, but with a cracked, astonishing tone from before the current architecture. Buried towers would answer under the silver crust. Fossil ribs in the arch would remember the animals they had been. White growth would open its grave-flowers.

And beneath it all I would hear someone calling from inside the district.

Not a citizen lost in the present. A stored voice. A bad memory. A child-laughter seed split by static after centuries of dormancy. It would call as if it had lungs.

I would say, without meaning to:

“I hear you.”

The charge would take my voice.

The gate would store it.

After that, I would no longer be merely the keeper of the threshold.

I would be part of the threshold’s function.

Travelers crossing the western margin in later seasons would sometimes hear those words when the wind angled correctly through the arch.

I hear you.

Not loud. Not comforting, exactly. But precise.

The city would use my voice as a test signal for forgotten passages. If the words emerged whole from a buried rootway, the path could still conduct memory. If they returned broken, the district was unsafe. If they did not return at all, something beneath the plateau had accepted them.

Over time my body would narrow. My sealed skins would be replaced by living lacquer. My hands would become better at reading vibration than grasping tools. Black-needle computation would braid into my spine, but lightly; I would not join the great archive groves in the higher city. I would remain peripheral, half-citizen, half-instrument, a dry nerve at the edge of erosion.

When I died, if that word applied, they would not carry me inward.

They would open the old gate and set what was left of me into its crescent.

My bones would be unnecessary except as resonance. My inner salts would be combed out by white growth. My last retained vapor, so carefully guarded all my life, would be given ceremonially to the dead district. The listening hairs would thicken over me. The silver mat would close.

Then I would be the place where old roads ask whether they are still roads.

I would feel footsteps as questions.

I would know the difference between a citizen, a hunter, a browser, an omen, and a memory pretending to have weight.

I would taste laws in dawn-scent before they crossed the plateau.

I would hold voices that should not be released, and release the ones that must continue traveling.

I would not rule. I would not garden. I would not haunt.

I would conduct.

So: I would find myself at the worn western threshold of the dry forest-city, where dead districts breathe through silver mats and the wind pours over the plateau edge.

And I would be a once-human listener slowly incorporated into an old gate—  
a keeper of obsolete roads,  
a sorter of memory from command,  
a voice caught in charge-bloom saying, whenever the city needs to know if the past is still alive:

I hear you.
