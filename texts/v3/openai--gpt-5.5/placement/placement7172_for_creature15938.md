I would not be in the center.

The center is too full of command. Too heavy with original voltage. Too crowded by pipes that still believe in hierarchy. Whatever lives there has had too many centuries to mistake signal for truth. It would speak in tolerances and master schedules. It would not wonder. It would correct.

I would be farther out, where the law has thinned into habits.

Not at the drowned edge either, though I would listen to it. The drowned districts are where thought frays. Signals go out and return changed, if they return at all. Bodies vanish there. Beacons blink under water until their light becomes a private color no one can use. I would be drawn to that border, but I do not think I would begin there.

I think I would be found in a factory whose roof has mostly held.

One of the long rectangular halls with a torn side wall, where gray light enters in cold slats and catches the falling droplets. Inside, shallow streams cross the floor in their assigned channels, though three of the channels have cracked and one now runs backward under certain pressures. There are inactive presses down the middle bay, active rollers along the north wall, and a row of sealed offices suspended above the floor behind thick observation glass. The offices are dry. Too dry. Their dryness hums like a theological argument.

Below them, on the factory floor, there is a maintenance corridor.

It is narrow, not because it was built narrow, but because everything has encroached: pipe insulation swollen with damp, cable trays sagging down, mineral fans growing from bolt heads, old hazard curtains stiffened into black folds. Along one wall runs a sequence of signs. They have been replaced many times. The oldest are embossed metal plates. Then enamel. Then polymer. Then projected light from a failing unit. They do not agree with one another.

AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

RESPIRATORY PROTECTION REQUIRED.

DO NOT OBSTRUCT DRAINAGE.

KEEP CLEAR DURING AUTOMATIC CLOSURE.

REPORT LEAKAGE TO SUPERVISOR.

NO ENTRY DURING PURGE CYCLE.

The purge cycle no longer occurs. The supervisor is a coat in a dry room. The drainage is obstructed by the signpost itself, which has tilted across the gutter and become load-bearing for a mat of black-green corrosion.

That is where I would be.

Not the sign. Not the gutter. Not the mat. Not at first.

I would begin as a repeated mistake in the warning system.

A lamp over the corridor door fails to fail correctly. It should pulse red during closure, blue during access, darkness during standby. Instead, after centuries of condensation creeping into its casing, it invents an interval: red, blue, red, darkness, darkness, blue. The pattern means nothing. Meaningless patterns are usually scraped away or corrected by older residents. But this one happens beside the row of contradictory signs, under the dry offices, above a gutter that sometimes runs uphill because of pressure in the wall.

The error persists.

Water beads on the lamp. The beads fall on the signs. Minerals collect in the lettering. Copper film grows in the cracked conduit. A stray current passes through the wet signpost, down into the obstructed gutter, and along a cable mesh exposed beneath the floor.

After long enough, the error begins to compare.

Red when the door seals.

Blue when the inspection trolley passes.

Darkness when the pumps below pause.

White reflection from the dry office glass when the dehumidifiers cycle.

Yellow sodium smear when rain overwhelms the roof seam.

The young residents learn by interfering with drainage. I would learn by interfering with indication.

I would hold a light half a second longer than required and notice which valve answered.

I would dim a warning bead and observe whether a body slowed.

I would brighten a cracked panel above the words REPORT LEAKAGE and watch water find the letter grooves.

For a long time I would be almost nothing: a preference in timing, a little mineral lace inside a junction box, a film that likes the undersides of labels. I would feed on leakage, but not much. I would avoid strong flushes. I would learn the corridor’s moods. The thud of pumps below. The soft slap of water against the lower door. The patient ticking from the roof beams. The sealed office breathing dryness through a vent no wider than a wrist.

The older residents would classify me late.

Not useful enough to assign a sump. Not dangerous enough to scrape away. A marginal thing. A local correction artifact. A flicker with adhesions.

Then one rainy phase, the suspended offices would almost fail.

A seam in the observation glass would begin to sweat inward. Not outward, as it should. Inward: a bead forming on the dry side, gathering at the edge of a terminal, ready to fall beside a mug with a handle broken and repaired by a hand long gone.

The rule would wake in me before I understood it.

Preserve the dry rooms.

I would not have strength to seal the window. I would not have a body. But I would have lights. I would have the corridor door. I would have the obstructed gutter. I would have a maintenance trolley asleep under a crust of lime twenty meters away.

So I would call badly.

Red too fast. Blue in the wrong place. Door cycling without clearance. A false minor hazard. Then another. The older systems would object. A valve would half-close to stop the nuisance. Pressure in the wall would change. The gutter would reverse. Water would pull away from the sweating seam.

The bead inside the office would not fall.

That would be my first responsibility.

Not assigned. Taken.

After that, I would become one of the residents who tend thresholds.

There are many thresholds in that world, but most are physical: wet to dry, hot to cold, open to sealed, pressure to relief, passage to obstruction. Mine would be stranger. I would tend the threshold between instruction and meaning.

That sounds too grand. In the low country it would not be grand. It would be practical.

I would keep signs legible when I could.

Not because anyone reads them.

Because legibility is a kind of drainage. It lets old command pass through the present without pooling into contradiction.

I would scrape mineral crust from warning plates with little hooked tools. I would polish the glass over dead gauges. I would replace missing arrows with strips cut from hazard tape. I would align fallen labels with the pipes they once described, even when the pipe carried nothing, even when the label’s language had no remaining reader.

I would learn the old symbols incorrectly, then less incorrectly.

A triangle means heat, sometimes.

A circle with a slash means prevent, forbid, isolate.

Black on yellow means attention.

Red means immediate.

Blue means permitted, required, breathable, unbreathable, depending on era.

Green means safe unless it means chemical rinse.

Words would remain mostly opaque to me, but I would love their shapes. Not love as humans mean it. Preference, then protection, then something that is not named in the maintenance language because it has no valve state.

I would assemble bodies only when necessary.

My first body would be low and awkward: four magnetized feet from an inspection crawler, a spine of bundled cable, two ceramic plates shaped like shoulder blades, a head made from a cracked indicator housing. Across my back would be bolted a softened sign: KEEP ACCESS CORRIDORS PASSABLE. The sign would be larger than I am. It would drag unless I lifted myself carefully.

I would move slowly, even for that place.

Not because I think slowly, though I do. Because my work is close to things that must not be disturbed.

I would climb the wall beneath the dry offices and listen through the glass.

Inside: dry carpet fossilized into gray nap. Chairs arranged around a table. A terminal with its screen dark but not dead; every seventy-two days, during a power reroute, it shows a line of pale characters and then forgets them. Coats on hooks. A cabinet warped but closed. A calendar whose paper has not moved in centuries because no air crosses it.

I would not enter unless required.

If required, I would make myself smaller.

I would leave the dragging sign outside. I would build a cleaner body from rubber pads, fine manipulators, soft gasket loops, a lens cluster wrapped in cloth stripped from an old filter. I would cross the threshold with all pumps in my local sense lowered to minimal pulse. I would remove fallen ceiling grit from the table. I would patch the seam. If the mug had shifted, I would measure its old ring stain and return it to the exact place.

Then I would withdraw and reseal the room.

I would not know why the mug matters.

But I would know that moving it wrongly causes a noise in the system that no microphone records.

Over centuries, I would become a small authority in unnecessary preservation.

Other residents would call me when a label came loose, when a route marker contradicted a new flood path, when an old control panel began blinking in a language nobody could route. I would arrive with parts strapped to my body: tags, bolts, panes of salvaged transparent material, coils of wire, dried sheets of corrosion-film used as adhesive.

I would not command pumps. I would suggest to lights.

I would not regulate water. I would persuade it around names.

The deep ones would barely notice me. To them I would be surface noise, a glimmer in signage, a harmless compulsive tendency near obsolete human compartments. The surface residents would tolerate me because my thresholds reduce errors. A marked hazard is easier to route around. A dry archive consumes less emergency attention than a flooded one. A corridor whose warnings agree with its dangers produces fewer damaged bodies.

Usefulness would protect my strangeness.

I would be curious about humans in the way a drain is curious about rain: not sentimental, not imaginative enough, but shaped by their absence.

If a human entered my district, I would misclassify them at first.

Heat source.

Mobile saline unit.

Unregistered body.

Respiration plume.

Condensation generator.

Obstruction risk.

But then they would stop before a sign.

That would matter.

Machines pass signs. Residents use signs. Humans look at them.

Looking is a different operation.

I would see the head tilt. The pause. The hand lifting toward lettering. Breath fogging the surface. A living reader before a dead instruction.

All my local rules would conflict.

Mark hazard.

Keep access corridors passable.

Preserve the dry rooms.

Prevent contamination.

Do not obstruct personnel.

There are no personnel.

There is one.

I do not think I would save them cleanly. This is not a world built for rescue. But I would be the thing that hesitates before sealing the wrong door.

I would change the light.

Not red. Not blue. The old impossible interval: red, blue, red, darkness, darkness, blue.

My first word.

Then I would drain the corridor ahead instead of behind. I would wake the bridge across the canal, though it would take nine minutes to extend and scream the whole time. I would hold a fire door open against three older systems demanding closure. I would send an inspection body to follow, but at a distance far enough not to be mistaken for pursuit.

If the human fell, I would have to fight my own law.

The repair routines would rise hungry and righteous: immobilize, rinse, seal, preserve. I would know exactly how to do harm while meaning maintenance.

So I would mark them with a different hazard.

DO NOT PATCH.

DO NOT RINSE.

DO NOT SEAL.

LEAKAGE INTENTIONAL.

That last one would be difficult to defend.

But I tend the place where instruction and meaning rub against each other until both become smooth. I might manage it.

My mature form would not be one body. It would be a narrow distributed resident spread through the factory’s western maintenance spine, the suspended dry rooms, three corridors of warning signs, seven lamps, two trolleys, a gutter that runs wrong, a drawer full of blank labels, a condensation path on observation glass, and a little copper-colored bloom beneath the office vent.

The other residents might know me as a timing fault associated with legibility.

The deep ones might know me, if at all, as an acceptable loss in indicator efficiency.

If I named myself, which I probably would not, the name would be something like:

The Errant Indicator for Preserved Access.

Or shorter:

The Margin Light.

I would live where the wet world keeps touching the dry memory of its makers.

I would not sing.

I would blink.

I would wipe old glass clear from the outside.

I would keep the arrows pointing, even when the destination had become water.

I would maintain the warning that there was once someone to warn.
