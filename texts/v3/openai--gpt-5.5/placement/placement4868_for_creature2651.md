I would not be one of the great things.

Not at first.

The long-bellied listeners are too complete in their purpose, too nearly myth. They belong to the basins the way a subduction trench belongs to a planet: by being too large to ask permission. I would hear them, certainly. Everything would. But I would not be them.

Nor would I be a glass shoal, though the thought is tempting: to become a sentence made of millions, to bend when the turbines change phase, to know direction as agreement. There is comfort in that kind of body. But I think I would fail at it. I would misalign. I would be the little wrongness at the edge of the sheet, the single sliver that flashes late.

I would be found where lateness matters.

Somewhere on the underside of a leaning tower, the one held forever at forty degrees above a ravine, where its warning bell strikes and strikes and strikes without warning anything new. Not on the bell itself, but near it: in the narrow triangular cavity between a guy-cable socket, a rusted brace, and a panel of ice-glass that has cracked but never broken.

There, the water is never still. The bell’s impact sends a hard circular shock through the metal every few seconds. The tower answers in a lower tone, delayed by its own mass. The ravine below gives back a blurred, deeper version a long while later. A coolant current crosses the opening at intervals, shearing the echoes sideways. Above, a road-thick cable hums in a pitch that makes mineral dust lift from the seam and hang like breath.

That is where I would live: in the overlap of impact, answer, and return.

I would be small enough to miss.

Not microscopic. Not hidden exactly. But unimportant-looking. A pale, jointless thing the length of a forearm, folded into the brace like a strip of wet parchment. My body would be mostly membrane, but not soft in the warm sense. More like a bladder of cold silk stretched over ribs of translucent mineral. Inside me would be loops and loops of crystal filament, each one tuned differently, some for the bell, some for the cable, some for the far pumps, one or two for sounds so deep they only arrive when the great listeners pass beyond the chasm mouths.

I would have no eyes.

I would not need them. The water there would be bright enough to lie. Sight would show me the tower, the ravine, the cable, the clean blue distance. It would not show me the important thing: whether the bell had struck a fraction too soon, whether the tower’s answer had thickened, whether the coolant current had lost force, whether a listener’s call had entered the structure and changed the weight of every familiar sound.

I would hear with my whole surface.

The bell would be my weather. The tower would be my cliff. The delay between them would be my pasture.

I think I would feed on variance.

Not on flesh, not directly. Not on rust or frost, though I would take minerals where I could. My kind would live from the small stresses that appear when repeated things are not perfectly repeated: heat in a bearing, pressure in a cable, the faint electrical shiver of a lamp whose filament is near failure, the turbulence after a valve opens with slightly less obedience than usual.

Most creatures of that ocean would align themselves to stability. I would align myself to error.

When the bell struck cleanly, I would remain folded. When it struck and the brace answered wrongly, I would open.

Very slowly.

A seam would appear along my body. Then another. I would unfold into a shape like a white ear, or a frost flower, or a rag caught in machinery. The membranes would tremble, tasting the irregularity. Tiny mineral teeth along my underside would scrape the metal where vibration had loosened salts. My filaments would tighten and slacken, measuring the fault. I would drink whatever the fault made available: a warmer eddy, a smear of oil too thin to see, powdered ceramic shaken from an old insulator, the charged bitterness around exposed wire.

I would not repair the tower.

But sometimes, after I fed, the wrongness would become smaller.

This would give rise to a misunderstanding.

If anything observed me from behind a sealed window, if any old supervisory intelligence still divided the world into function and failure, it might classify me as a maintenance organism. It would be wrong. I would not care whether the bell kept ringing. I would not know what a warning was. I would only know that a persistent rhythm produces a skin of possibilities around itself, and that I was born to graze that skin.

My young would begin as hard little commas.

I would place them in rows inside bolt-holes, each one pressed against metal with its blunt head facing the dominant sound. For months or years they would do nothing visible. They would listen until their first internal filament grew. Then, when the tower shuddered under a passing listener’s call, they would crack open all at once and release thin white threads into the water.

Most would drift away and die.

Some would catch in cable knots. Some would be eaten by anvil-walkers. A few would lodge in other resonant gaps: under crane rails, inside ladder rungs, behind plaques stamped with numerals no animal reads. There they would flatten, mineralize, and wait for repetition to bruise.

I would have predators, but they would not hunt me with urgency.

Anvil-walkers would pry at me if they found me open. Their magnetic feet would click along the brace, and I would feel them long before they arrived: heavy, articulate, full of blunt intention. I would fold myself tight and become almost indistinguishable from ice. Sometimes that would save me. Sometimes not.

A ribbon predator would not bother with me unless I detached. In open water I would be helpless, a torn membrane with no talent for distance. My whole life would depend on contact: brace, socket, cable, tower. If a current stripped me loose, I would tumble through the blue like a failed thought until something ate me or I froze around my own filaments.

So I would cling.

Not desperately. Desperation belongs to animals with seasons. I would cling as stone clings to its fracture, as rust clings to permitted geometry, as a sound clings to the medium that carries it.

The great event of my life would not look like an event.

The bell would strike.

The tower would answer.

The ravine would return its deep, delayed version.

Then beneath all of it another foundation would arrive: the call of a listener moving beyond sight.

Every filament in me would tighten at once. The old tones would not vanish; they would become smaller, like tools laid on a floor when the building itself begins to speak. Glass shoals above the ravine would flatten into white lines. Anvil-walkers on the tower would stop mid-step. Mineral snow would lift from shelves that had held it for years.

I would open farther than was safe.

For those moments I would be almost all surface, all reception, my body a thin pale diagram of the water’s memory. The listener’s call would pass through the tower, through the bell, through the brace, through me. It would carry distances I had never traveled: chasm mouths, intake cathedrals, suspended cables miles away, the slow routes of animals too large for sight.

I would not understand them.

But I would keep the alteration.

After the listener passed and the industrial chord revealed that it had never stopped, one of my inner loops would remain tuned to the deeper note. From then on, every bell strike would contain a shadow of it. Every delay would be measured against it. I would be changed, though my place was not.

That would be my kind of memory.

Not thought. Not story. Not a map. A changed tuning.

If you came looking for me, you would first hear where I was.

You would follow the bell, though it would hurt to follow. You would cross from still water into the hard lateral drag of the coolant corridor. You would pass the leaning tower’s guy cables, each one thick with collars of clear ice. You would see the ravine below, blue-black and clean, with bubbles rising in a column that never quite rises. The tower would strike again and again. The sound would enter your teeth until you could no longer tell whether it came from outside you.

Then, if you looked under the brace at the moment after impact, you might see me unfold.

A small white membrane.

A listening animal.

A maintenance error mistaken for a function.

A creature of the interval after the bell and before the echo returns.
