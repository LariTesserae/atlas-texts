At first I would look for myself in the engine hall, because everything there admits its purpose. The belts go where they go. The rods pass through walls. The gauges tremble at the marks they were born to indicate. It would be tempting to become one of the pale moths on a dial, shivering over a word like RETURN BREATHER, dustless, blind, warmed by brass.

But I do not think I would be found there.

The engine hall is too declared. Too central. Too much itself.

Nor would I be in the canal, though the canal has the kind of quiet that might suit a hidden thing. I would not hang in the eel-chain beneath the submerged outlet, mouth to tail, taking in the old mineral exhalations of the pipes. That life is too continuous. Too consenting.

I would be nearer a boundary.

Not outside the walls. Nothing truly lives outside the walls except the idea of outside. And not at the center, where the machines speak with too much authority. I would be at one of the small cast-iron waiting posts beside the rails, perhaps Post 6 or Post 9, where the enamel number is chipped but never chips further, where no vehicle arrives and yet the rails continue to expect one.

You would find me under the lip of the platform, in the narrow seam between the post’s bolted foot and the damp stone.

At first I would look like another plated insect: flat, pale, a little larger than a coin, my back ridged like stamped tin. But if you watched long enough, if watching had any meaning there, you would see that I do not travel the seams as the others do. I remain beside the rail. I keep one forelimb touching iron and one touching stone. My body is built to receive two kinds of vibration at once.

I would be a timetable creature.

Not a keeper of time, exactly. The town has no time to keep. I would be the thing that keeps expectation from becoming absence.

My shell would be cloudy white with rust-colored markings that resemble old printed numerals. Beneath it, soft organs would pulse to the pressure below the street. I would have no eyes, only two shallow pits that taste heat and a fringe of hairlike feelers along my belly, each tuned to a different interval: belt-slap, wheel-tooth, valve-breath, rook-tap, drain-call, rail-hum. When the great wheel above the engine hall completes one turn, I would lift my head. When the rails vibrate with the arrival that never arrives, I would answer by clicking once against the stone.

That would be my work: to answer.

Not loudly. Not with intelligence as humans mean it. A single click, exactly placed, so the rail’s unused purpose does not fall out of the town’s arrangement. The post waits; the rail expects; no vehicle comes; I confirm the waiting. Without me, perhaps nothing would happen. Or perhaps that is exactly the danger.

The other animals would know me only as a fixed pressure in their routes. The rail-creatures would step over me without pausing. Rust beetles would clean the edges of my shell but never bite too deeply. Blind rooks would sometimes drop to the platform and tap the enamel plaque above me, reading through metal the little corrections I make. A rat with spare incisors would sleep beside my seam during the coldest part of the cycle, its breath warming one side of me, and I would accept this as weather.

I would feed on discrepancies.

A claw lost and restored. A pellet fallen and removed. The brief excess of a newborn moth before the census settles. The almost-change in a rust patch. The half-ripple in canal water after the eel-chain sways. These would pass through the stone and rail as tiny wrongnesses, flavors of imbalance. I would take them into myself, digest them slowly, and excrete them as harmless mineral dust that never accumulates.

I would not decide the numbers. I would not command the substitutions. I would simply make them legible to the town.

My mouth would be very small. My hunger would be exact.

When the great chord comes—when engine knock, drain-song, rook-beak, belt-slap, beetle-rasp, pipe-organ chimney and rail-hum align—I would unfold parts of myself normally hidden. Thin pale vanes, not wings, more like pressure membranes, would open from beneath my shell and tremble in the noise. For those few seconds I would be nearly transparent. You could see the rail through me, the bolt, the damp stone, the old black wall beyond, all of it held in my body as a diagram.

Then the chord would separate. I would fold down again. I would become a pale coin under a waiting post.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be stamped somewhere no one could read, perhaps on the underside of the platform, perhaps inside the rail itself:

INTERVAL ANIMAL  
POST EXPECTANCY  
LOW ARRIVAL WITNESS

But names there are mostly labels for functions no longer understood.

So you would find me in the place where arrival has been preserved after arrivals ended.

I would be the small blind creature that listens to the rails and confirms, with one precise click after another, that nothing has failed merely because nothing comes.
