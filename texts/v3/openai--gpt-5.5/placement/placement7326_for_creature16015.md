I would not be found at the center first.

The center is too declarative, even in a place that refuses drama. Those shallow divided pools, those softened right angles, the dark water taking the broken sky in pieces — that is where meaning gathers, and I would not begin as meaning. I would begin nearer the edge, where use is less visible.

At first I imagine myself standing at the rim, looking down into the bowl from outside, carrying dry dust on my clothes and some human idea of discovery. But that image does not last. Standing is wrong here. Height makes me temporary. A standing body belongs to weather, roads, decisions made elsewhere. In this basin, to remain is to lower.

So I would be found crouched under the roofless shelter at the eastern edge, not inside it exactly, but beneath its raised stone floor, in the warm dark between fist-sized rocks. I would have a sleeping hollow there, polished by my shoulders and hip, with one flat shard set near the entrance to turn seepwater aside. From outside, my doorway would be only a black crescent behind a leaning slab. A person might put a hand to the stones above me and feel warmth, but not know I was there.

I would be one of the small maintainers, though not a central one. Not a speaker of decisions. Not one of those who carries water to the court in a shell and waits to see what the basins answer. I would be older than the young kept near the western cracks, but not yet crusted enough to be nearly stone. My shoulders would be dark with mineral oil. Salt would have begun to feather along my wrists. The pads of my fingers would be broad and nearly numb, except to pressure, grit, temperature, and the faint give of clay about to fail.

My work would be seams.

Not the large channels anyone can see. Not the old basin mouths, not the stone rows, not the court. I would keep the little failures from becoming larger failures: the hairline leak beneath the oval basin by the shelter, the black thread that tries to widen after warm nights, the clay lip that slumps inward whenever the seep above it grows impatient.

I would know where the water is allowed to hurry.

Very few places.

Most mornings, before the dim sky has changed enough to be called morning, I would crawl out and put my cheek to the stone shelf. I would listen through my jaw. A full basin has a different silence than a hungry one. A sealed seam presses back against the face. An opening seam breathes. I would not think these things in words. I would know them the way a hand knows the shape of the tool it has worn down.

I would carry plugs of clay in my mouth.

That seems right.

A human would keep materials in a pouch or a bowl, but I would keep the smallest repairs soft against my tongue: clay mixed with ash, chewed amber skin, black paste from the wet seam behind the leaning post, a thread or two of fiber brought from outside and worried into the mass. When I found the place where water was leaving too freely, I would press the plug in with my thumb, then with my lower lip, then smooth it with the flat of a nail until it looked less like a repair than like something the basin had always intended.

I would not make things new. I would make them old again.

That would be my skill.

If a human footprint filled with water near the shelter, I would wait until night, then kneel beside it and draw clay into the heel hollow. Not to hide the human. Not from fear. The hollow would be too deep. It would hold more than its share. It would interrupt the skin forming on the adjacent pool. I would close it because the level mattered.

If a stone shifted, I would not move it back at once. I would watch through several damp cycles. Some shifts are corrections. Some falls are accepted by the basin before we understand them. But if the water began to tremble differently around it, if flies gathered where they had not gathered, if the pale crust grew ragged instead of feathered, then I would set my shoulder to the stone and worry it, grain by grain, back into its proper lean.

I would have little interest in the world beyond the rim.

I might have gone once, when younger, with two others, to bring back cord and dry fuel. I would remember it as an unpleasant brightness. Too much distance. Too many surfaces not answering touch. Plants behaving as if upwardness were a virtue. Wind putting itself everywhere. The green smell would have stayed in the folds of my arms until I scrubbed it off with bitter water.

After that I would prefer the eastern shelter, the low basins, the dependable seep.

My name, if I had one, would not be a sound that carries. It would be a pattern of taps: two wet clicks, a pause, the nail drawn once across stone. It might mean “keeps the narrow thread,” or “one who closes after listening,” or only “the one under the raised floor.” Names here would be less like labels than like reminders of placement.

I would speak rarely, but I would answer when asked by touch.

A younger one might bring me a shard with a film forming wrong on it, cloudy at the edge, bitter-smelling. I would take it, hold it close to my filmed eyes, taste the water gathered along the rim, then place two fingers on the stone row nearest the shelter: wait. Or I would turn my palm upward beside the little channel: open this for one night. Or I would cover the basin lip with my hand: do not disturb.

I would be trusted with small delays.

That, in this place, would be a kind of authority.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of arrival. Here, presence is mostly distributed. Someone moving under a platform, someone scraping film from the underside of a stone, a child watching lizards vanish, an old one sleeping with mineral fans on the shoulders — all of us would be separate, but not apart. The basin itself would be the shared body. To tend any part of it would be to touch the others.

At long intervals, I would go to the central court.

I would come late, after the decision-makers had already gathered, carrying no shell of my own unless asked. I would crouch behind the third clay lip and watch the poured threads enter the still pools. I would not look for omens as an outsider imagines omens. I would look for response. Clouding. Refusal. Acceptance. The way amber skin breaks. Whether rings die cleanly at the edge or stutter before vanishing.

If the dark pool remained dark, I would feel a quiet loosening in my chest.

If the oval basin whitened, I would know which seam would need watching.

When I died, I would not want to leave the shelter.

My hands would be washed first, because they had held back too much water to be returned carelessly. The young might scrape the salt from my wrists and save it. My finger bones would make good burnishers; I would like that. One rib should be set beneath the lip of the oval basin by the shelter, the one that always tries to soften after warm nights. Not as a monument. As assistance.

My skull, if it stayed whole, could go into a small cairn near the leaning stone posts, but I think I would rather be divided more thoroughly. I would rather be useful in several places than remembered in one.

So where would I find myself?

Low in the eastern shelter, under the raised floor, at the seam between made thing and wet earth.

Who or what would I be?

A keeper of narrow seepage.  
A maker of old repairs.  
One of the basin’s small hands, not important enough to be seen, but necessary enough that the water keeps its level.
