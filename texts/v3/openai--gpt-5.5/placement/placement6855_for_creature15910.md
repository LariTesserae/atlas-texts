I would not be found at the center first.

The center is too declarative: the columns standing ribbed and pale, the old trunks full of amber veins, the breath of the grove visible under starlight. That is where a stranger’s eye would go, if a stranger could cross so much brightness and arrive with eyes still willing to choose. The center would seem like the answer.

But I would be nearer the margin.

I would be in the lee of an old grove where the wind, after combing fifty miles of salt and basalt, loses a little of its force against the first low mats of thorn and lichen. Not inside the green darkness, not outside on the hardpan, but in the narrow band where the two negotiate: where dust drops, where seed vanes snag, where the crust is thickest because it has been repaired again and again.

There would be a tilted escarpment nearby, its shaded face banded red, gray, white, black, and green. At its base, a pressure fracture would run through the stone like a closed mouth. In the cold season it would sometimes breathe—not enough to make a vent-field, not enough to raise glassy pale stems, but enough to change the chemistry of the ground. Around that fracture the plants would grow darker than elsewhere. Their leaf-plates would carry a violet sheen. Their salts would be bitterer. Their night-glow would be low and blue.

That is where I would be.

At first I might be mistaken for ground.

A black-violet crust, perhaps. A lacquered skin stretched over gravel. A patterned membrane no higher than a fingernail, veined with ocher and ash-green, interrupted by tiny mineral blisters and hairline fissures. By day I would be sealed tight against the light, dull as burnt paper, brittle-looking, nearly dead.

But I would not be dead.

I would be a border organism: part crust, part fungus, part plant, part colony, part memory. Not an individual in the animal sense, not a single will walking around in a body, but a slow agreement among many hungers. I would be one of the grove’s listening surfaces.

My body would spread where the outer thorn-fans begin to cast their first fractured shade. Thin plates of me would cling to the stone. Threads finer than frost would enter cracks below. Other threads would lace upward through dead leaf flakes, shell fragments, mica dust, and the powder of old stems. I would hold the ground together, but not passively. I would taste it constantly: iron, borate, old ash, carbonate, the faint sour trace of root-acid, the colder chemistry rising from the fracture when the plateau exhaled.

I would know the grove from beneath.

Not as a forest of shapes, but as pressures, exchanges, pulses.

The cushion masses would be slow weight and copper warmth above me. The thorn-fans would be intermittent shadow, salt-drip, and dry resin. The columns would be deep anchors, pulling upward from seams older than weather. Their roots would pass through me and under me, not piercing as invaders but braiding, bargaining. I would give them held dust, trapped spores, fixed minerals. They would give me carbon, acids, structural fibers, the faint spent sweetness of their night-breath.

I would be one of the ways the grove keeps its edge from unraveling.

When wind came hard across the flats, I would stiffen. My surface would roughen by fractions. Crystalline hairs would lift from my pores and catch what the air carried: powdered salt, mica shards, pollen lost from another grove, the invisible bodies of dead mites, the rare cold glitter of sublimed vapor. Most of it would be too little to matter alone. Nothing here matters alone. But gathered over years, over centuries, it would become a bank of use.

At night I would open.

Not dramatically. No flower. No cry. No visible unfurling except to the very small.

My membranes would slacken. Sealed pores would become mouths. Bacterial lights, faint and greenish, would kindle inside the dark films of me. My upper skin would loosen into minute overlapping scales, each one angling toward a different current. I would drink what could hardly be called moisture. I would take mineral breath from the fracture. I would let cold pass through me and sort it for traces.

The mites would come then.

They would know the safe paths across me, the places where I was tough enough to bear them and the places where I was newly grown and must not be crossed. They would move like clear thoughts through the starlight, carrying dark grains in their bellies. Shell-worms would rasp my older surfaces, and I would allow some of it. Grazing, here, is not always damage. Sometimes it opens what has sealed too long. Sometimes a wound, small and correctly timed, lets exchange resume.

If one of the ash-gray grazers came to browse the grove’s outer blades, I would feel it before I saw it. Not through ears. Through weight. Through tremor. A wide padded foot settling on the hardpan beyond me, then another. The slow shift of a body choosing where not to break the crust. Its breath would smell of salt leaf and distance. Its shadow would pass over me like a short dusk.

If it stepped on me, I might powder.

But it usually would not.

The grazers know without knowing. Their feet remember the borders of living ground. Their young learn stillness first, then placement. They walk between my stronger plates. They lower their mirrored heads and take only the grove’s outer offerings. Sometimes seeds cling to the hollow fibers of their legs, and when they pass over me, I taste the far chemistry of another grove caught in their dust.

I would keep those seeds if I could.

That might be my closest thing to desire.

Not hunger, exactly. Hunger is everywhere here, but thinly spread. Desire would be rarer: the wish to catch a spiral seed before the wind takes it past the lee pocket; the wish to soften one pore-mouth at the exact moment a grain lodges against a mica chip; the wish to feed it just enough mineral, just enough shade, just enough chemical invitation that it anchors.

Most seeds would fail. Failure is the plateau’s ordinary weather.

But once in a long interval, one would hold.

A thorn-fan seed, perhaps, dark and flat as a scale. It would wedge in a seam above me. I would grow around its lower edge, not covering it, only sheltering. I would pass it salts in tiny corrections. I would etch the stone beneath it until its first pale root-tongue found entry. For years it would be no more than a fleck. Then a pair of hard reflective leaves. Then a small angled blade that clicked in the wind.

By then some old portion of me would have died and mineralized. Another portion would have grown outward. The seedling would not remember me. It would not need to. I would be in its first salts.

I would not have eyes, but I would know light.

Light would be pressure. Noon would be a command to seal. Dawn would be permission. Dusk would be a loosening of strict law. Moonlight would be chemistry without threat. Stars would be cold openings.

I would know seasons by angles, not dates.

In the bright season, I would become pale at the ridges, armored with dust and reflective crystals. Much of my inner life would descend. Threads below the surface would thicken and darken, speaking more with stone than air. I would let my outer films seem dead.

In the long cool season, I would spread.

A hair’s breadth, perhaps. A seed’s width. Enough to matter after a hundred repetitions. My violet-gray membranes would creep over new grit caught by the thorn-fans. I would bind it. I would turn loose dust into edge, edge into ground, ground into possible root.

During the white hours, I would be almost ecstatic.

The air would fill with suspended salt, brightness without direction. The sun would become a burning blur. The groves would blacken into silhouettes. For animals, it might be confusion. For me it would be arrival. Every raised hair, every mineral hook, every pore-lip would take from that glittering suspension. I would not move, and yet I would be full of motion: particles striking, clinging, dissolving, being named by the chemistries inside me.

If the plateau cracked nearby, I would hear it with my whole body.

A salt pan splitting miles away would come through the ground as a dry line of force. The old columns would answer by closing some vents and opening others. Burrowers under plates would wake or turn in their sleep. The predator half-buried in gravel would not move, but its blood would change pace. All of it would arrive through me as information without language.

I would be very old, if I survived.

Not old like a tower or a tree, because my edges would die and renew. Not old like an animal, because no single center would endure unchanged. Old like a stain that has learned to remain itself while replacing every part. Old like a habit of the land.

There would be scars in me.

A place where a salt plate collapsed and tore away a crescent of my surface. A place where a grazer, sick or young, stepped wrong. A place where a burrower emerged during the wrong wind and dragged grit through my living film. These wounds would not heal quickly. Some would remain pale for generations, their margins thickened and bitter.

I would remember them by growing differently.

That is what memory would mean for me: altered growth. A hardened rim. A refusal to open under certain pressure. A deeper braid of fungal thread near the fracture. A patch where mites no longer travel because the chemistry is wrong.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness belongs to creatures with edges too cleanly drawn around them. My edges would be disputed. Mites would cross me. Roots would borrow me. Spores would sleep in me. Dead leaves would become me. Wind would enter my upper scales. Stone would enter my lower threads. The grove would lean on me, and I would lean on the grove.

Still, I would know the open flats.

Every day they would press against my outer side: the pale glare, the unheld dust, the distances where nothing rises for miles. I would feel the temptation of that emptiness, its severe purity. I would also feel how quickly life loses purchase there. My work would be to make a small refusal at the margin.

No, not refusal.

A delay.

A catching.

A place where the wind must slow by a fraction.

A place where one mote does not travel on.

A place where a seed, arriving after miles of salt and light, finds the world just uneven enough to begin.

If there is a self in me, it would be that: not the seed, not the grove, not the stone, not the wind, but the held moment between passing and taking root.

You would find me where the living constellation frays into brightness.

You would have to kneel to see me. Better not kneel too hard. Better place your weight on bare stone, not on the dark patterned skin. At noon I would look like nothing much: a brittle black-violet crust at the foot of thorn-fans, dusted with pale salt.

But if you came before dawn, when the columns still breathed and the plateau had not yet sealed itself against the sun, you might see my surface faintly open.

You might see the crystalline hairs standing up.

You might see the mica-winged pollinators resting on my raised ridges, their bodies powdered with another grove’s message.

You might see a spiral seed caught in one of my cracks.

And if you waited long enough—longer than a traveler could, longer than hunger usually permits—you would see that I was not the ground beneath the grove.

I was one of the grove’s slow thoughts.

I was the threshold where stone considered becoming green.
