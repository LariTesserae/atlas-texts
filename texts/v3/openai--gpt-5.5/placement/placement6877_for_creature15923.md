I would not be found at the horizon, where the plateau makes its long, austere statement.

I would be lower than that.

At first I think I would be under a slab, in the thin cool dark where beetles fold their legs and lizards leave the heat of their bodies in the dust. Then I think I would be on the underside of a lichen crust, working slowly at stone, living by chemistry and patience. Then nearer still: not on the rock, but in one of the basins the rock has learned to hold.

A shallow basin. Not the largest. One that looks empty for most of the year.

It lies just below a low rim where the animal roads meet. The stone around it is polished darker by feet, bellies, claws, tongues, generations of thirst. There are old droppings in the cracks, a scatter of shed wings, a pale termite tower leaning some distance off like a weathered shrine. A wiry shrub grows on the uphill side, casting almost no shade but enough to change the fate of a few grains of dust. The basin itself is sealed over with a skin of salt, ocher powder, and broken larval cases. To a passing bird it is a memory. To the plateau it is a closed mouth.

That is where I would be.

Not moving. Not dead.

I would be one of the pool-born, but before the pool. A cyst. An egg. A hard, minute, almost mineral thing in the mud beneath the crust. I would be smaller than a seed and older than many creatures that walk over me. I would have no eyes yet, no limbs yet, no hunger with a direction. I would be a kept possibility.

Most of my life would be refusal.

I would refuse heat, light, appetite, time. I would endure the pressure of hooves, the probing beaks of birds, the baking season when the basin cracks into polygons above me. Ants would cross overhead in black arithmetic. Beetles would dig and pass. Roots would nose near and withdraw. Snakes would lie in the basin on cold nights, taking the day’s stored warmth from the mud that holds me. Rain would fail. Rain would come too little. Rain would touch the crust and vanish before it could wake me.

I would wait through all of it without virtue, because waiting would be my body’s only grammar.

But when the right rain came—not a mist, not a darkening, not a brief tilted curtain that only perfumes the stone, but enough water to fill the bowl and soften the sealed mud—then I would become sudden.

The basin would cloud. The salt skin would break. Buried eggs around me would answer all at once, each to its own ancient signal: depth, coolness, dissolved minerals, the taste of enough. I would split open into water I had never seen but somehow expected.

Then I would be red-limbed and frantic.

A little branch-legged swimmer, translucent at first, with my dark thread of gut and my many delicate paddles moving in waves beneath me. I would swim upside down through greenish light, in water no deeper than a hand, beneath a sky too large for anything so temporary. I would feed on the bloom awakened with me: bacteria, algae, smaller lives, the loosened wealth of the mud. Around me would be others—siblings, rivals, mouths, future corpses—each born already inside the countdown.

There would be no childhood. Only acceleration.

The pool would be a world and an hourglass. Its edges would creep inward from the moment it formed. Birds would arrive almost before I understood above and below. Their needle bills would enter the water like weather. Beetles would slap down from the night. Tadpoles would rasp. Nymphs would stalk. Shadows would pass over us, and the whole pool would shudder with bodies diving toward mud that could not save them all.

I would not be noble. I would eat. I would flee. I would mate if I lived long enough. I would make eggs and give them to the thickening floor, sealing myself forward into another silence.

My life would be shaped like the plateau’s secret: sleep, permission, urgency, sleep.

And perhaps that is why I belong there. Not as one of the visible citizens—the beetle crossing the slab, the lizard thinking in sunlight, the bird clicking from thorn—but as one of the hidden clauses beneath them. A small red animal only when the world allows it. A speck of endurance when it does not.

You would find me in the mud of a temporary pool, under a crust that looks finished.

I would be a rain-waiting egg of the ancient basin water.

A brief creature.  
A patient creature.  
A future held in dust.
