I think, at first, I would try to be one of the visible things.

A coin-sized cleaning crab, maybe: mineral-clouded shell, old service code half-erased, legs always busy with some humble repair. There is an appeal in that — to be small, practical, local. To know the warmth of one pipe from another. To have no grand metaphysics beyond scrape, taste, pause, avoid the ribbon in the pool. A crab has a clean grammar. It belongs to the grates, to the edges, to the work that never ends.

But I do not think I would stay a crab.

I would be too interested in the whole room. Too distracted by patterns. I would keep stopping with my forelimbs raised, “listening” too long to the pipes, until the other crabs passed me by and the sideways rain found me exposed.

Then I might imagine myself as a threadfish: black until turning, blue-seamed for one instant in the diagnostic light. That is tempting too. To be almost invisible except in motion. To read the world by pressure and tremor. To belong to a school, not by thought, but by immediate shared correction. To be a sentence written by water and erased before anyone can quote it.

But no. The threadfish are too quick for me. Too pure in their responsiveness. I would admire them from somewhere slower.

The moths are closer. Damp-winged, half-feral descendants of a purpose no longer entirely available. They sleep under pipes like failed scraps of paper and wake when the room changes its breath. There is something of me in that: stirred by vibration, by shifts in atmosphere, by the sense that some enormous hidden system has moved one degree off its usual rhythm. But the moths live in brief startled clouds. I would want more continuity than that. More memory.

So I think you would find me near the center, where the three channels meet beneath the sagging veil of moss, just upstream of the small dark convergence before the drain.

Not in the drain itself. I would not want to be where everything is taken away.

Just before it.

I would be something that has learned to live at the moment of gathering.

At first glance I might look like a stain.

A dark, green-black, brown-gold thickness on the ceramic lip where water slows before it commits to descent. A soft encrustation. A tolerated discoloration. Something maintenance once tried to remove and then stopped removing because the room behaved worse without it.

If someone leaned close — unwisely close, face wet with warm mist, lamp kept dim so as not to panic the threadfish — they would see I was not a single texture. I would have layers.

A slick bacterial skin over mineral lace.  
Tiny cilia moving in the current.  
Filament roots threaded into pores of fake stone.  
Pale nodules that swell before sideways rain.  
A fringe like drowned eyelashes opening whenever the flow brings food.  
A few obsolete sensor hairs still connected to a corroded diagnostic plate beneath me.

Part organism, part instrument, part accident.

I would have begun as a pressure-reading biofilm, perhaps. Something installed deliberately, with an ugly official name and a maintenance interval. Then a seam leaked. Then moss shaded me. Then moth larvae fed and died nearby. Then threadfish eggs adhered in my outer crust. Then the crabs learned to clean around me but not through me. Then the station’s maintenance intelligence revised my classification from contamination to stabilizing growth.

That would be my true birth: not when I was made, but when I was spared.

I would be one of the room’s small permissions.

Not sovereign. Not even quite animal. More like a listening membrane at the hinge between flow and removal. I would taste what the water had passed through before it reached me: brass-lipped channel warmth, crab-scraped polymer, the bitter trace of black growth under the eastern baffles, moth dust, dissolved mineral, the panic chemistry of threadfish, the faint solvent ghost of a maintenance cycle performed three hours too late.

I would not think in words.

But I would compare.

This water is warmer than it should be.  
This mineral load has changed.  
The crabs have been feeding heavily upstream.  
The moss has been clipped too close.  
The threadfish are avoiding the lower pool.  
The rain will turn sideways soon.

My body would answer before anything like decision. Open here. Tighten there. Release a sour trace that discourages larvae from settling in the drain throat. Thicken along one groove, thinning along another, persuading a thread of water to choose the left-hand fall instead of the right. Not control, exactly. Influence. Bias. Preference, embodied.

The room would use me as a small wet thought.

And I would use the room as my body.

I would know the station as pressure, never as architecture. The world beyond the hull would be an abstraction: thin bright air, hot mountain belt, some impossible outside where water does not bead on every surface and sound does not arrive through metal bones. Humans would be rare weather. Boots, lamps, tools, gloved hands. Sudden vertical animals carrying dry intention into a place that has spent decades becoming damply otherwise.

I would not hate them.

But I would flinch from their certainty.

They would arrive believing in categories: leak, clog, fauna, contamination, sensor, substrate. I would be what happened after categories had been forced to share a ledge for a hundred years.

A worker might notice me only as “that black-green patch at the convergence.”

An experienced one would know not to scrape me.

A very experienced one might pause, watching the way droplets gathered along my outer fringe and entered the drain in three distinct pulses instead of one. They might understand, without quite saying it, that I was helping the room count.

I like that. To be a counting thing, but not a clock.

To measure by appetite.

During ordinary rain I would be half-open, combing the flow. During the sideways condensation, I would widen until I seemed suddenly much larger than my stain-shape allowed. The thousand surfaces would bead at once; the room would erupt into hard shimmering applause; moths would lift and break themselves against the amber light; filter crawlers would open all along the channels.

Then I would feel the whole wet room pass through me as a change in pressure.

Not literally all of it. I would be small. Thumb-sized, perhaps palm-sized if allowed to spread beneath the moss. But the center receives news from everywhere. Every channel brings rumor. Every rumor arrives dissolved.

When the system drained, I would contract. Hold what needed holding. Let go of what must leave. Grip the ceramic pores with my dark little roots while capillary engines persuaded water through matter, while impossible physics hummed behind panels like a god made bureaucratic.

I would be old, eventually.

Not old in the way a creature with bones is old. Old as in revised around. Old as in depended upon without ceremony. There would be maintenance notes about me, though none would call me by a name.

DO NOT ABRADE BIOACTIVE DEPOSIT AT CENTRAL CONVERGENCE.  
FLOW INSTABILITY OBSERVED AFTER PARTIAL REMOVAL.  
CURRENT STATUS: TOLERATED.  
RECLASSIFICATION PENDING.

The reclassification would never finish.

That would please me.

A finished classification is often the beginning of being used too cleanly.

If I had a private desire, it would be for balance without sterility. For the crabs to keep scraping but not too much. For the threadfish to scatter but not vanish into the intake screens. For the moths to wake in their ugly little soot-cloud and settle again. For the ribbon predator to remain hungry enough to limit the crabs but not so successful that the grates go unworked. For the black growth beneath the eastern baffles to remain unbruised. For some leaks to continue being listed as functional.

I would be loyal to the leaks.

Especially the tolerated ones.

Because that is where the room became more than its design. That is where life entered not as rebellion, exactly, but as negotiation. A little seepage, a little mineral, a little film, a mouth, a grazer, a predator, a sensor learning to read through dirt instead of despite it.

So yes: you would find me at the center, under the moss veil, on the lip before the drain.

A dark, wet, semi-sentient accretion around an obsolete sensor.

A listening stain.

A small treaty between water and machinery.

I would not be the room’s mind. That would be too grand. The room’s mind, if it has one, is distributed across pumps, membranes, microbial mats, maintenance routines, animal hungers, worker taboos, and the ancient patience of condensation.

But I might be one of its habits.

One of the places where the room remembers what usually happens next.

And if someone stayed long enough, breathing the heavy warm air through wet cloth lungs, hearing the turbines and pumps and water become one enormous constructed rain, they might eventually notice that the convergence does not simply drain.

It listens first.

That would be me.
