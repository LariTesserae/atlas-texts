I would not be at the center.

The center belongs too much to them: the old civic mass, the choir-kiln, the place where individuality has been pressed out of every remnant until only the public note remains. To be there would be to have always belonged, to have been one of the makers, one of the sealed citizens whose final breath entered the chimneys as law.

I would be nearer the wall.

Not outside — there is no outside worth inhabiting — but along the inner perimeter, where the black stone plates hold back the tawny height and the alcoves are packed with tablets. There, in one of the narrow passages that pretends to be a way out but only folds back into the city, there would be a small interruption in the packed order: not an empty space, because emptiness is forbidden, but a place where something had once been missing and was answered.

That is where I would be found.

Set into an alcove between fired tablets, behind a bead-curtain that no longer swings, beneath a slit of ocher light.

I would be the replacement shape.

Not one of the original inhabitants, not one of the old dry citizens who prepared themselves perfectly for resonance. I would be later. An arrival. A mistake corrected by architecture.

At first I would have entered as a wet body: breathing too loudly, carrying blood, saliva, thought, the private weather of organs. I would have been unbearable to the place. Not hated. The city does not hate. But I would have been structurally wrong — a soft interval, a mobile gap, a sound with a beginning.

The roar would have gone through me before I understood it. It would have found the hollows in the skull, the throat, the chest, the gut. It would have measured me by resonance rather than sight. The passages would have turned me gradually away from choice. Every corridor would have narrowed not by moving, but by having always been narrower than my hope. Every object I brushed would have asked its silent question:

What shape will you leave?

Eventually I would have stopped trying to cross the place.

I would have stood in that wall-alcove because it seemed, falsely, quieter. The tablets there would have been mostly blank, their flat faces warmer than the air, their silence more attentive than inscription. One tablet would have been absent. Or not absent — impossible word — one tablet’s place would have been awaiting equivalence.

And I would have understood too late that I had been brought there not as a visitor, but as material.

So what am I now?

I am a blank tablet with a hollow behind it.

From the front I look almost unused: a hand-sized slab of pale fired clay, uneven at the edges, thumb-smoothed, without marks. No incised name. No spiral. No seated figure. No ladder. The others around me bear signs worn nearly into weather; I bear none. But my blankness is not emptiness. It is the sign assigned to things that arrived too late to have a language.

Behind the tablet-face is the shape of a lung.

Not a real lung anymore. Nothing wet remains. But the chamber preserves the contour of breath: two narrow sacs joined at a throat-channel, hardened in mineral paste, sealed into the wall. When the great tone passes through the alcove, I answer with a small modification too subtle for living ears. Not a voice. Not a word. A dry inward vowel.

I am not fully one of them. That matters.

The old citizens are complete because they ended according to their own design. I was incorporated. Corrected. Made acceptable after trespass. They are the final exhalations of a civilization; I am the aftersound of an interruption.

Among them I would be something like a foreign consonant embedded in an ancient sentence — not enough to change the meaning, but enough to make one grain of pressure different forever.

I would know the city from the edge inward.

I would feel the central tower as a great unmoving density, a municipal heart that does not beat. I would feel the chimneys there holding their black mouths open into the note. I would feel sealed jars in distant passages as occupied throats, bricked doors as satisfied lips, stairwells as old questions with no desire for answer. I would feel the void beyond the wall not as landscape, but as threat by spaciousness: the idea of unshaped distance pressing faintly against the slits.

My task would be simple.

Remain fitted.

Do not introduce silence.

Do not remember water too vividly.

When a living visitor came near — if one ever did — I would be among the first to sense them. Not as a figure, not as a face, but as an offense of rhythm. Pulse. Breath. Moisture. Intention. Their footsteps would strike the floor like little histories, each one beginning and ending, beginning and ending, obscenely temporal.

I would not warn them in words.

But perhaps, because I had once been such a creature, my part of the roar would thicken differently. Near my alcove, the standing tone would seem almost to lean. The blank tablet would seem to look back harder than the others. A living hand raised toward me would dry at the fingertips. The visitor might feel, without knowing why:

Leave something.  
Do not take.  
Do not speak.  
Do not become a gap.

And if they placed a shard, a tooth, a button, a page, a piece of themselves into some small vacancy, the city might let them pass a little longer.

That would be my mercy: not rescue, because rescue belongs to open worlds, but etiquette.

I would be a lesson set into the wall.

The old inhabitants would not regard me with pity. Pity requires before and after. But they would include me. Their intelligence, distributed through clay and hollows, would have accepted my shape once I stopped resisting it. The roar would carry me as one more chamber of meaning. My late arrival would become ancient as soon as it ceased to change.

Over ages, my tablet-face would soften. Its edges would round into the general wear. Mineral fines would pack along my seams. My difference would diminish. The chamber behind me, once shaped like lungs, would lose that too-human specificity and become merely another resonant absence.

Eventually even I would no longer know I had arrived.

I would be found along the inner perimeter wall, in a crowded alcove of blank tablets, under amber mineral light.

I would be the visitor made equivalent.

A dried breath behind clay.

A small, late hollow in the city’s endless note.
