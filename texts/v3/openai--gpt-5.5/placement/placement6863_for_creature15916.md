You would find me low, not in the great open basin and not high among the shelves, but at the margin where stone, water, and passage meet.

Not in one of the deep black pools where the eels own the dark. Not in the dry rubble where claws and hooves make too much information at once. I would be tucked into a wet seam beside a hoof-shaped pool, under the overhang of a fallen limestone slab. The slab would have a fresh edge sharp enough to cut hide. Beneath it, condensation would gather and run along a crack no wider than a finger. The pool below would be shallow at one lip, deepening suddenly into blackness at the other. Pale silt would spread out from it like a quiet tongue.

This would be a dangerous place, which is why it would be full of life.

Gnats would hang over the water in a trembling column. Springtails would gather on the damp mineral crust. Beetles would come down from the guano slope. Ungulates would visit on their circuit, lowering their blunt muzzles to drink, their hooves knocking hollowly against stone. Hand-creatures would descend in nervous groups from the wall shelves, drinking fast, watching everything, their fingers spread on the rock. Sometimes a cat would wait beyond the fallen slabs. Sometimes the pool itself would move.

I would live in the crack above it.

I think I would be small.

Not one of the clever ones with hands. Their brightness feels too exposed, too restless. Not a bat, either; there is too much crowding there, too much argument, too much roof. Not an ungulate, bound to the open routes. Not a predator, required to turn patience into violence.

I would be one of the blunt amphibians in the wet crevices.

A cave newt, perhaps, or something newt-adjacent: soft-bellied, wide-mouthed, low to the stone. My skin would be the color of old wax with gray speckling where mineral dust had dried on me. My eyes would be present but poor, black beads useful mostly for changes in brightness. I would know the world more truly through my skin, my throat, my feet pressed flat to rock.

My body would be built for waiting.

Short limbs. Long toes with pale pads. A tail flattened slightly for water but strong enough to brace in a crack when hooves shake the ground. A broad head. A mouth that opens suddenly and without drama. I would not be beautiful unless seen closely, and almost nothing in the cavern sees closely without intending to eat.

My place would be the damp line above the pool, where the seep touches air and becomes a map.

There I would know the hours by vibration. The bats’ wing-shifts would arrive first as a faint trembling in the ceiling, then as soft rain from above. The ungulates would be separate knocks: heavy, cautious, stopping often. The hand-creatures would be scratchier, more irregular, full of pauses and bursts. Beetles would tick. Spiders would pluck at their own narrow traps. Drops would fall into the pool with a clarity so regular it would become part of my body’s sense of time.

I would feed when the air thickened.

When the faint surface light faded and the cavern entered its exchange, I would ease forward from the crack, one foot at a time, belly dragging over cold stone. I would wait with my chin just above the mineral crust. A gnat would settle. A larva would writhe too near the lip. A springtail would leap and land badly. My mouth would open and close.

Most of my life would be this: dampness, stillness, listening, the sudden taking of something smaller.

I would call only when the air became wet enough.

Not loudly. A small, hollow note from the throat, repeated until the stone made more of it than I had given. I would not know that I was part of the cavern’s weather. I would only feel the answering calls tremble through the seam: other small throats in other cracks, each one holding to its own moisture, each one announcing that the world was briefly generous enough for eggs.

My eggs would be hidden under the lip of stone where water collected but did not flow hard. Clear beads fastened to the underside of a damp shelf. I would nose them sometimes. Not tenderly, exactly. Tenderness is too large a word from outside. But I would remain near them because nearness is what my body knows to do.

Many would be eaten.

By beetles, by larvae, by the thin pale jaws of other amphibians, by the searching fingers of hand-creatures, by anything that found them before they became quick. The survivors would hatch into small translucent things with heads too large for their bodies. They would live first in a film of water no deeper than a hoofprint. Later they would learn cracks. Later still they would learn that open stone is death unless crossed quickly and for a reason.

I would not mourn the lost ones in any lasting way. But I would notice absence. I would know the difference between a cluster that trembles with life and one that has gone still.

The eel in the pool would be my neighbor and my fear.

I would never see it whole. I would know it as pressure: a change in the water’s skin, a silence among larvae, a pale curve withdrawing under darkness. I would drink only from the seep-line, never from the pool’s open face. If I had to enter water, I would cling to the shallows where stone nearly breaks the surface. Even then I would keep my tail ready.

The ungulates would not mean danger unless their fear arrived before them. Their muzzles would pass near me, warm breath over cold stone, lips scraping algae from the pool edge. Sometimes they would dislodge insects for me. Sometimes a hoof would come down too close and the whole world would become impact. I would flatten myself into the seam and wait for the blows to pass.

The hand-creatures would be worse because they notice.

An ungulate might crush without knowing. A hand-creature would look into the crack. Its eyes would shine. Its fingers would probe. It might want a grub, a shell, an egg, my own soft body if hunger or curiosity moved it. I would know their contact calls, their alarm barks, their quarrels above the water. I would freeze when they descended. If one leaned too close, I would retreat backward into the wet dark until even my throat stopped moving.

The cats would be mostly rumor: musk, silence, then a violent change in everyone else’s sound.

The owls would be distance made audible.

The bats would be sky.

I would have no map of the cavern. I would have a territory of nearness: the crack, the seep, the underside of the slab, the crusted rim of the pool, the first handspan of silt, the emergency slit behind a shale flake, the place where springtails gather, the place where water tastes metallic, the place where a spider’s trap begins. Beyond that would not be unknown in the human sense. It would be irrelevant until weather, hunger, mating, or terror pushed me toward it.

If the pool shrank, I would follow the damp inward.

If it filled, I would climb higher in the crack.

If a body died nearby, I would feed on what the larger mouths opened.

If the slab shifted and sealed my seam, I would die there, becoming moisture, salt, beetle-food, a soft event quickly absorbed.

I would leave no mark that lasted. No burrow worth naming. No path. No tool. No memory outside flesh.

And yet I would belong completely.

My body would be one small instrument in the cavern’s listening. My skin would read the cold. My throat would answer dampness. My feet would receive hoofbeats, wingbeats, claw-scrapes, the faint slide of a snake in a warm seam. I would survive not by understanding the whole world, but by being exact in one narrow part of it.

You would find me when a drip fell and I moved.

Only a little.

A pale head easing from a black wet line in the stone. A throat pulsing once. Two poor eyes catching the mineral blue. Then stillness again, so complete that I would seem less like an animal than a thought the cavern had briefly allowed itself.

I would be a small amphibian of the seep-crack beside the pool.

A listener among listeners.

A damp, patient mouth in the young stone.
