You would not find me at the top of the water, because there is no top that matters.

You would not find me on the open plain either, among the armored grazers with their patient mouths and gardened backs. I would admire them from the dark, those slow moving islands, but I would not have their mass, their inheritance of roads, their blunt certainty. Nor would I be one of the pale fish making processions along the terrace edges, reading the world through pressure and seam. They still travel too much. They still have destinations.

At first, I might seem to belong to the ceiling.

Not the true ceiling, not the blind roof of water and cloud impossibly far above, but the lower ceilings: the undersides of root-bridges, the vaults where old stems have arched and fused, the hollow places where the current thickens and bends. There, in a loud corridor between a basin-field and a colonnade, beneath a bridge grown from braided plateau-root, I would be attached.

You would have to look upward to find me.

Even then, you might not know I was alive.

I would be one of the bell-colonies.

A small one, perhaps, or one so old that “small” has ceased to mean much. A hundred cups. Then a thousand. Inverted, translucent, fringed at the lip, anchored by pale threads into the mineralized plant-bark. Each cup no larger than a fingertip, though there are no fingers here. Each one drinking the water as it passes. Each one open to the current, but never blocking it.

I would live where four kinds of motion meet.

Behind me, the root-vaults roar with narrow water, forced through ribbed passages and old stem-flumes. Ahead, the current loosens into a broad court of black water between columns. Below, slow grazers pass at intervals too vast to be called routine by any warm-blooded thing. Above, through the bridge itself, the Underweave carries its centuries-long exchanges: mineral, bruise-light, pressure, hunger, repair.

I would have no eyes.

Sight would be unnecessary and almost rude. What could seeing add to such a place? Light here is too rare to spend on looking. Instead I would know the world by arrival.

A grazer would be a broad, muffled insistence moving below me, a pressure with a shell-shaped roof. Its many hidden feet would make a long, low combing through the plant-floor. After it passed, the exposed wounds in the living surface would release a faint sweetness into the water, and I would taste the opening of a neighborhood.

A fish procession would be thin weather. Many bodies, each separate, each careful, the water pinched and released along their flanks. I would feel them alter course when my colony rang, though I would not mean to warn them. Meaning would be too sharp a thing for me. I would only answer.

A tube-eel passing through the bridge hollows would be almost nothing: a subtraction, a length of dark deciding not to disturb the current until hunger made it briefly into a mouth.

The great filter-feeder overhead would be a change in the law.

First the water would lean. Then the fronds would bow. Then the bridge would tremble in its deep-grown ribs, and every cup of me would shiver open, shut, open. My ringing would travel along the underside of the arch in a pale chain, wet glass speaking to wet glass. Not music, not language, but announcement without intention:

large body  
large body  
large body passing

And after, for a long while, the slow rain of its waste would descend, feeding the terraces below. The snails would gather. The fish would return. The plant-skin would darken and take.

I would be an instrument the world plays by moving.

That is close, but not all.

Because a single bell-animal is too brief. It opens, feeds, rings, buds, dies. Its cup collapses into softness. Its thread remains. Its children thicken beside it. The colony persists by losing the individuals that compose it. So if you asked who I was, I could not point to one mouth, one fringe, one trembling membrane. I would be the shared attachment. The continuing underside. The place where many little hungers make one listening.

In colder centuries, when the water thinned of food, I would shrink back to the older knots nearest the bridge-root. My outer cups would pale and empty. Glass crustaceans might pick at the dead ones. Fungal veils would soften the threads. But some portion of me would remain alive in the seam, tasting only mineral drift and the breath of the plant below.

In richer centuries, I would spread.

I would lace the arch from end to end. I would send small colonies down along pendant rootlets. I would fringe the mouths of side-tunnels where juvenile fish shelter from the main current. I would be pale gray in darkness, almost invisible until touched by pressure. Then, all at once, I would become motion.

Not bright.

Never bright.

Only a quiver. A ring. A disclosure.

If a visitor came through with hands and lamp and lungs hidden inside some impossible shell, they might mistake me for decoration. They might think the bridge had grown tassels. They might reach toward me, and before contact, I would know them as wrongness: too warm, too abrupt, carrying pockets of stillness where water should pass. My cups would close in a wave. The bridge would chime. Fish would vanish into terrace-shadow. An eel would withdraw so completely that its tube became architecture again.

But I would not hate the visitor.

Hatred requires a border too clean for this place.

I would simply register interruption.

And if the visitor remained very still, if they learned to let the current resume around them, I would open again.

Slowly.

One cup first. Then six. Then the whole underside of the bridge would loosen its fearless mouths and drink.

My life would be made of thresholds.

Too little pressure, and I feed.

A little more, and I tremble.

More still, and I ring.

Too much, and I close.

That would be my intelligence: not thought, but calibration. The whole drowned summit lives by such measures. How much current may pass. How much growth may remain. How much darkness can be borne. How much brightness can be afforded. I would be one small rule among larger rules, a fringe of sensitivity on the body of the plateau.

And perhaps, over ages, the Underweave would know me.

Not as an animal. Not as a self. It would know that this bridge answers pressure. It would grow the arch a little broader because ringing here helps the fish turn aside before collapses. It would leave the underside rough enough for my threads. It would route a fine side-current over me, not from kindness, but because the bridge, the colony, the fish, the grazers, the passing shadows, and the water have found a durable arrangement.

I would become part of that arrangement.

That is where you would find me:

under a root-bridge in the loud middle dark,  
between the basin fog and the colonnade,  
above a road polished by grazers,  
below a vein of the old living floor,  
where the current narrows just enough to speak.

I would be a hanging colony of bell-animals, soft cups in the black water, feeding on what passes, ringing when the world changes shape.

I would not build.

I would not travel.

I would not remember in stories.

But every pressure would pass through me, and for a moment I would give it form.

A tremor for the grazer.  
A faint chime for the fish.  
A shiver for the eel.  
A long glass murmur for the filter-feeder crossing above like weather with organs.  
A sudden silence, more alarming than sound, before a vault gives way somewhere in the old plant-dark.

Then the water would continue.

I would open again.

And I would make room.
