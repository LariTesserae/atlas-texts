I would find myself at an edge.

Not at the center of one of the dense groves, where the amber fans stand in their dry cathedral order, and not out in the absolute open where the salt flats hold their pale, dead brightness. I would be in the narrow zone between them: a place almost too slight to be named, where a nickel basin breaks into a long hairline crack and the first ranks of filament grass have begun, over centuries, to lean their root-mats toward it.

There would be no path to me. Nothing would arrive.

I would be low at first, almost indistinguishable from a deposit.

A bead, perhaps.

Dense, dark, ceramic-black on one side and dull green-gray on the other, lying half-sunk in the powder at the base of a solitary stalk that dropped me longer ago than weather would know how to count. I would have waited through days that were identical except in chemistry. I would have known the sun only as repeated pressure. I would have known night as release. Around me, salt would laminate. Dust would settle and lift and settle again. Once in a long while, static would crawl across the flats and leave a tiny burn-mark on my outer shell.

For a long time I would not be alive in any visible way.

But I would not be dead.

Inside me would be an arrangement: not a mind, not a memory, but a held possibility. Layers of mineral instruction. A dark nucleus sealed in glassy matter. Carbon filament coiled like a question that does not ask to be answered quickly.

Then the ground beneath me would open by the width of a root.

Not from drama. Not from quake or rupture. Only heat expanding one layer differently from another. A minute separation. A new difference in the stone.

That would be enough.

I would split.

Not upward first. Upward would be wasteful. Upward would be announcement, and nothing here needs to announce itself. I would open downward, sending a single probe into the crack: ceramic, blunt-tipped, patient, tasting not moisture but contrast. Warm below, cool above. Alkali to one side. Metal to the other. A faint sulfurous trace rising from depth. Charge stored unevenly in the seam.

The probe would thicken. Branch. Plate itself with iron and then with something paler. It would become less a root than an instrument inserted into the unfinished body of the plateau.

Above ground I would remain small for many seasons.

A low knot. A black-green node no taller than a hand, surrounded by a stain of altered salt. The glass grasses nearby would not notice me, because noticing is too animal a word, but their root-mats would meet the chemistry of my first deposits and turn, very slightly, away. Not in fear. Not in refusal. Only in correction.

I would become a boundary.

That is what I would be: not a tree, not a reed, not one of the high amber fans, but a threshold-organism, one of the quiet devices by which the plateau learns where one condition ends and another begins.

My body would build itself as a set of decisions.

On the side facing the salt flat, I would grow dark heat-plates, low and overlapping, each one matte as cooled slag. They would lie close to the ground, drinking the day’s warmth and giving it back slowly after sunset. On the side facing the grove, I would lift narrow vanes no wider than blades, amber at their edges, conductive through their centers, tuned to the faint electrical weather shed by the fan-forest after dusk.

Between those two arrangements would rise my actual trunk, if it could be called that: a squat column of ribbed mineral resin, hollow in several chambers, each chamber separated by valves that open only when the pressure below and the heat above are in the proper ratio.

Once every day, near the hour when shadows lengthen but before the stored warmth leaves the ground, I would click.

Only once.

The sound would not travel far. It would not summon. It would not warn. It would be merely the closing of one internal plate and the opening of another, a correction in the small factory of my body.

Through me, gas from the crack would be slowed. Sorted. Stripped. Sulfur fixed in one chamber. Metallic vapor condensed along another. Alkali dust trapped on external ridges. What I could not use, I would release through pores so small the exhalation would appear only as a faint wavering in the air above me.

In time, deposits would gather around my base.

A crescent of pale crust on the saltward side. A darker ridge toward the grove. Thin rings of cobalt, hematite, tin-white, dull yellow. Not symmetrical. Not decorative. A map of what had passed through me.

If someone could return every hundred years, they would see that I had moved nothing and changed everything immediately around me.

The crack beneath me would widen because my roots followed it and plated its margins. The salt at my feet would no longer lie flat, but would rise in shallow terraces made by my discarded layers. The nearest glass grasses would become clearer, thinner, their fans angled differently because I had altered the charge that reached them. The amber grove behind me would extend one cautious rank toward my heat. Or perhaps it would not. Both outcomes would be part of the measurement.

I would have no desire to be larger than my conditions.

That would be my nature.

Not hunger. Not ambition. A continuous adjustment toward exactness.

I would know the world as gradients entering from all directions.

The sun pressing on my plates.

The night drawing heat out of them.

The deep seam sending up its faint chemical alphabet.

Static collecting along the rims of my vanes.

The weight of dust.

The contraction of salt.

The distant, almost imperceptible hum of the fan-forest when its veins carry evening charge.

A crack opening two body-lengths away would be an event of immense significance. A new vent beginning under the basin would be a change like history. A fallen scale from the grove landing against my base would become, after enough time, a readable layer. I would take from it what could be taken. Not consume it as an animal consumes, but translate it into structure.

I would not look at the sky.

But I would know its angle.

I would not hear the wind.

But I would register the small cooling where it passed over my plates.

I would not remember in scenes.

But I would preserve years as bands: a sulfur year, a nickel-rich century, a season of unusual charge, a long interval when the crack below me sealed and I lived almost entirely on stored difference.

If I reproduced, it would be rarely and downwardly.

After many cycles of successful balance, I would form three beads inside the warmest chamber of my trunk. They would be heavy, faceted, not meant for travel. One might never leave me, becoming simply another sealed archive in my dead core. One might drop and rest against my own mineral terrace, where it would wait without complaint for a crack that might never open. The third might fall into the bare salt, roll no farther than its own diameter, and lie there under stars so clear they seem abrasive.

That would be enough.

Nothing here spends life on hope.

Life is placed. Life endures. Life records the terms under which endurance became possible.

So I would be found in the high basin, at the border between the dry abundance of the amber fans and the unclaimed brightness of the salt flat: a low black-green condenser rooted in a young crack, an edge-being, a patient valve in the plateau’s unfinished surface.

Not one of the grand forms.

Not a witness.

Not a traveler.

A small standing chemistry.

A punctuation mark in stone, teaching the land—by deposit, by charge, by heat, by refusal to move—how to become slightly less unused.
