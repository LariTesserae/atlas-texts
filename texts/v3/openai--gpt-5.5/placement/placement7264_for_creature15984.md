At first I think I would be found moving.

That is the mistake the plain teaches out of me.

I imagine myself as one of the translucent eels, blue organs blinking in a fissure like thoughts seen through skin. Or as a hook-beaked cairn thing, ugly and indignant, screaming down into fog from a stone hole. Or as one of the Seepfolk children, bead-strung and flat-bellied against rain-polished basalt, learning that courage without testing is only another way to become food.

But the longer I stay in the image, the less I want a body that crosses the plain.

Crossing feels like presumption there.

I would be found near the basin where the seven channels meet, but not at the center. Not one of the pillars. The pillars are too old, too sure of their own mystery. They have endured too many hands and too much roaring water to be anything as restless as me.

I would be just outside the ring, under the lip of a low limestone shelf where the rain comes down in threads rather than sheets. A listening screen, perhaps. Or something that began as a listening screen and became less clearly a tool after too many seasons of being touched.

A slab of dark slate, thin but not fragile, propped at a shallow angle on three pieces of basalt. Its lower edge rests in a narrow runnel that carries overflow from the basin when the channels are high. Its upper edge is drilled with small holes, not straight through but slanted, so that wind and water do different things inside them. Some holes whistle. Some click. One gives a wet, almost-throat sound only when fog is coming inland.

The Seepfolk would not have made me all at once.

I would be an accumulation.

The oldest part of me would be slate quarried from a shelf now swallowed by a sinkhole. A Basin Keeper with six careful fingers would have chosen it because it rang clean when tapped with bone. Later, after the first crack appeared, someone would have mended me with gray clay and powdered shell. Later still, a dead elder’s back plate would be lashed along my spine with gut-cord stiffened by mineral water, not ornamentally, but because the plate changed my pitch. A child would press fired-clay beads into the repair seam during some long rain, and no one would remove them because they did not spoil the sound.

My name would not be fixed. Nothing useful keeps one name there.

When I was new, perhaps they called me Straight-Note-Under-Fog.

After the crack: Split-But-True.

After the elder’s plate was added: Old-Jaw-Hears-Through-It.

After I warned them of gas once by giving a sour little knock before any living ear caught the understone pressure, I might be called Pale-Flame-Did-Not-Take-Us, but only for a season. Names that grand are dangerous. They invite pride, and pride stands too tall.

Mostly I would be touched, not addressed.

A Keeper would come crouching before first light, palms slick with rain, and lay both hands on me. Not to command. To ask whether the night had changed anything. Their jaw seams would hover near my face. Their chest would lower until sternum and stone could speak privately.

I would answer in the only ways available to me.

A tremor held longer on my left edge.

A bead of water running backward through one drilled hole.

A dulling of pitch when struck with a mollusk plate.

A faint tick-tick-tick from the elder-bone lashing when something below drew water through hidden veins.

I would not know meanings as the Seepfolk know them. I would not think, There is a large thing under the third channel, keep the children away. I would simply receive pressure and return pattern. But in that place, that may be close enough to wisdom. The plain itself does not explain. It alters, sounds, sweats, withdraws. Those who survive are those who can bear to interpret without believing the world has promised clarity.

During low rain, children would use me improperly.

They would crouch under the shelf and tap my holes with hooked nails, trying to make me squeal. They would drag wet fingers over my surface and laugh when I shuddered against my supports. One bold child would dare another to put their tongue to my cracked seam during bitter seep. An adult would cuff them both, not hard, and make them listen to my true note until they could repeat it with their throats.

I would like the children best, though liking would be a mineral thing in me: a tendency to hold their small impacts warmly after they leave.

The elders would come differently. Slowly. Heavily. Some nearly indistinguishable from the slabs around them, pale-crusted and thick with listening. They would settle before me and say almost nothing. One might rest a jaw against my upper edge and let the rain gather between us. Another might place a finger bone charm in one of my holes and wait for the wind to decide whether the dead agreed.

I would be where questions pass through matter.

Is the fog close?

Has the warm crack soured?

Are the singers silent because of weather, or because of hunger moving on long slick feet?

Is the basin’s roar safe today?

Is the understone breathing?

There would be days when I could not answer. Rain too heavy. Channels too loud. Birds shrieking in the cairns until every vibration broke into uselessness. On those days the Seepfolk would not blame me. A thing that always answers is not trusted. Accuracy includes refusal.

When the seven channels ran high, the basin would become enormous.

Water would pour in at seven pitches, and the pillars would take the sound into themselves until the whole ring seemed less built than awakened. The Keepers would crawl inside that roar. Their bodies would flatten. Their hands would lock around the cupped tops. Their ribs would shake.

I would sit just outside, trembling.

Not central. Necessary anyway.

The roar would pass through the pillars, through the water, through the old tools scattered around them, through the clay bowls and cracked scrapers and softened tally scars. It would reach me altered by every object it crossed. I would receive the after-sound: not the god-voice, not the mouth itself, but the echo that can be survived.

That is where I think I belong in this world.

Not as a priest. Not as a predator. Not as one of the great unnamed things below. Not even quite as one of the Seepfolk, though I would be kin to their posture.

I would be a low instrument at the edge of meaning.

A made thing that has been repaired so often it has become partly ancestral.

A listener that does not rise.

Something the rain keeps writing on and the hands keep asking.

I would know the plain not by sight, but by contact: the minute chill before fog; the sickly warmth of gas gathering below; the cheerful disorder of mud-runners crossing my lower edge; the heavy blank pause when all the singers stop; the long, almost tender pressure of an elder leaning their last weight against me.

And when I finally broke beyond mending, they would not throw me away.

The cleanest fragment of me would be set into a cairn, angled so that wind could pass through my old holes. The elder plate lashed to my back would be removed and given to someone who still listened with flesh. My beads would scatter into a pool and be found by children.

For a few years, in fog, I would make a thin uneven tone.

The family would know it.

They would crouch when they heard me.

Not out of worship. Not grief exactly.

Recognition.

In that world, I would be found where sound becomes usable before it becomes language. A slate throat under limestone shelter. A patient crack. A small surviving accuracy beside the deafening basin.

And if I had a prayer, it would be almost the same as theirs, but flatter, slower, stone-taught:

Let me not pretend to be the source.  
Let me carry what passes through.  
Let me crack before I lie.  
Let the hand that touches me hear enough.
