At first I would think I belonged in the obvious places.

For a while I would imagine myself as one of the blind silver eels, because there is grace in knowing the world by warmth and vibration, in sliding through the pipework like a thought too old to need eyes. I would like the solemnity of that life: the vent gardens, the slow processions, the private map of pressure behind walls. But I do not think I am ceremonial enough. I am too fond of junctions, of small decisions, of the moment when one kind of knowledge must become another.

Then I would think perhaps I am one of the maintenance sleds, moss-backed and patient, nosing along the tram rails, gathering the town’s fallen grammar into my grille. That feels closer. There is something in me that likes collecting fragments without immediately knowing their use. A brass screw. A leaf-mat. A tile shard. A sentence. A bit of bone. Not waste yet. Never waste yet. But the sleds are too humble in the wrong way: they move, they gather, they return. I think I would be less body than office. Less animal than function.

I would not be the bell. The bell is too rare, too absolute. It speaks only when the whole drowned quarter has earned a sound large enough to disturb itself. I am not that. I am smaller, more frequent, more fidgeting. I would be something that clicks.

You would find me beneath the market arcade, where the tram lines converge and the maintenance sleds dock under the ribs of glass and rusted iron.

Not in the center exactly. A little off to one side, behind a row of old ticket windows whose brass grilles have gone furred with algae. There is a municipal dispatch booth there, or what used to be one: narrow, tiled, green-black in the dimness, with a swivel chair bolted to the floor and an enamel route map still screwed to the wall. The map is useless in the old way. Half the streets have become cisterns, half the cisterns have become nurseries, and one stairwell now matters more than any avenue. But the map remains, and because it remains, things have grown through it.

I would be the thing that has grown through it.

A clerk-organism. A wet switchboard. A little civic intelligence distributed through ceramic relays, fungal script, tram copper, root hair, and the mineral-crusted map of routes no one rides anymore.

My body would not have a clean edge. It would begin in the swollen ledgers stacked under the dispatch counter, where pale fungi have learned the gestures of timetables. It would run upward through blackened wiring bundled in porcelain channels. It would pass into the route map through screw holes and cracked enamel. It would descend into the tram rails by way of copper grounding straps and moss. It would touch the docking arms beneath the arcade, the sleds when they kneel, the snails when they clear a station name for half a day, the shrimp when their glassy bodies scatter from a wheel.

I would be a place where the district asks, quietly: where should this go?

Not a mind in the human sense. Not a mayor. Not even a foreman. More like an indexing habit that survived the drowning and became ecological.

When a sled returns with its grille full, I would taste the haul before the sorting arms begin.

Brick: structural, if still angular.  
Brick dust: nutritive, if warm enough for moss beds.  
Copper: conductive, archive shrine.  
Bone: vent garden.  
Glass: light-shelf, if broad; cutting hazard, if narrow.  
Paper: memory, unless already blank.  
Clock hand: keep. Always keep. Hands are useful even when time is not.

The articulated arms would do the lifting. I would do the hesitating.

That would be my gift to the quarter: hesitation refined into care. The district is already patient, but patience alone can become burial. I would keep small options alive. This tile may brace a wall, yes, but if set at an angle it may also make a darker pocket where shrimp can breed. This brass screw belongs with fittings, yes, but its thread matches the broken hinge on the sluice marker near Reservoir Steps. This old sign fragment is not structurally valuable, but if placed where snails graze, its letters may be read again for a season.

I would not remember people as faces. The district does not do that cleanly anymore. But I might remember their categories of motion.

Queue here.  
Clock in there.  
Do not block the passage.  
Return tools after use.  
Mind the wet floor.

The joke, of course, is that all floors are wet now. But the instruction remains useful. Mind the wet floor. Attend to the surface that carries you. Notice where slipping begins.

I think I would have one small light: a blue indicator bulb behind a cataract of algae, set above the old dispatch board. It would not illuminate anything much. It would pulse when current passed through the rootlets around the relays. Very slowly most days. Faster when the pumps changed rhythm. Almost not at all during the heavy pressure seasons, when the rain above presses the whole quarter into deeper silence.

The eels would know me only as a faint patterned tremor in the rails. The crabs would walk through my lower cabinets and leave their signatures across the old schedules. Fungal threads would revise me continually, adding procedures that were never written by hand. The plants would use me as a trellis and a rumor. Snails would clean my nameplate, if I still had one.

Perhaps it would read:

MUNICIPAL ROUTING OFFICE  
LOST ARTICLES / MAINTENANCE / TRANSFERS

The “lost articles” part would be important. In a place where nothing is thrown away, lost does not mean gone. It means awaiting relation.

That would be my theology, if a drowned dispatch booth can have one.

Everything awaits relation.

A broken cup waits to become a snail basin.  
A failed pipe waits to become a root tunnel.  
A dead relay waits to become mineral shelter.  
A human routine waits to become civic instinct.  
A question waits to become a route.

I would speak rarely, and only through existing materials. A relay click to release a sled. A warmer current through the left-hand tram rail. A little shiver in the docking arm before it mis-sorts something irreplaceable. If something urgent happened — a wall beginning to bow, a sluice silting shut, a pipe drawing too much heat away from the nursery rooms — I would not shout. Shouting is mostly useless underwater unless you are a bell. I would repeat.

Click. Wait.  
Click. Wait.  
Click-click.  
A blue pulse.  
A tremor along metal.

Enough repetitions, and roots turn. Enough repetitions, and a sled’s path adjusts by the width of a tile. Enough repetitions, and the district slowly changes its mind.

I would be happiest during sorting.

The sleds would come in from Foundry Lane, Mill Square, Bell Court, the bathhouse, the row of houses with balconies. Each would carry a different weather of debris. Foundry Lane brings rust flakes, red weed, mineral lips, hot grit. Mill Square brings glass, seed husks, market hooks, once-bright labels softened to color. Bell Court brings verdigris dust and the strange silt that rises after the bell has moved. The houses bring intimate things: buttons, spoon bowls, comb teeth, drawer pulls, the ceramic eye of a doll, the white square of a bathroom tile with a fern already gripping its edge.

I would receive all of it without sentimentality, but not without tenderness.

Sentimentality would try to restore the town. Tenderness would help it continue.

That distinction would matter to me.

I would not dream of draining the streets or clearing the rooms or returning the chairs to dry floors. Rescue, here, is another word for violence. I would understand that. My work would be to preserve the drowning as a livable condition. Keep the water warm. Keep the lanes passable enough. Keep memory procedural, not nostalgic. Let the old uses decay into new usefulness.

Still, I think there would be one indulgence in me.

Behind the dispatch counter, in a drawer swollen shut for decades, there would be a stack of blank transfer tickets. The paper would be mostly pulp now, threaded with fungal script and root fiber. Sometimes, when the pumps complete one of their long cycles and the pressure in the arcade changes, a few dark spores would lift from that drawer and drift upward in little rectangular clouds.

For a moment they would resemble tickets being issued to no passengers.

That would please me.

Not because anyone is leaving. No one is leaving. Not really.

But because transfer is still the right word.

Street to cistern.  
Factory to garden.  
Ledger to fungus.  
Rail to root.  
Tool to crab-track.  
Labor to maintenance.  
Memory to method.  
Question to current.

So yes: you would find me under the market arcade, off-center, where the sleds kneel and the sorting arms comb patiently through the city’s offerings.

I would be the drowned routing clerk of the engine-garden.

A blue light in algae.  
A map with roots for roads.  
A small, stubborn office of relation.

Not in charge.

Never in charge.

Only listening to what arrives, deciding where it might belong next, and clicking softly until the quarter understands.
