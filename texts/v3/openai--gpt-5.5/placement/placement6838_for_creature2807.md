You would find me where the world most wants to deny that it has joints.

Not out in the open basins, not among the broad black backs of the sump cattle, not in the clear conduits with the pipe eels sliding like thoughts through glass. I would be lower than the catwalks and higher than the deep water, in the long gray seam where a service bridge meets the overflow apron of a warm rectangular lake.

A place made to tolerate movement without admitting change.

There, beneath the lip, where condensation gathers before falling, I would live pressed half into the flexible sealant. I would be one of the seam-readers.

Small, at first glance. Not impressive. A hand-length animal, flat enough to be mistaken for a strip of wet gasket or a folded piece of maintenance material left behind by a repair arm. My upper surface would be dark and matte, almost the same gray-black as the damp expansion compound. My underside would be pale, soft, and dense with tasting pads. Along my sides, fine cilia would open and close in the air like wet eyelashes. I would have eyes, but only as dim lenses for daylight, shadow, warning lamps, the passing whiteness of scavengers. My true sight would be pressure.

I would know the bridge by its strain.

I would know when the deck warmed before the air. I would know when the basin water rose three finger-widths below the official mark. I would know which pumps were running by the tremor that came through my belly. I would know when a lifting frame crossed a gantry two kilometers away because the whole plane of stone would answer it, not with sound exactly, but with a change in weight.

My work would be to feel the first intention of a crack.

Not the crack itself. Cracks are never allowed to become themselves here. I would feel what comes before: the tiny drying at the edge of sealant, the altered taste of mineral stress, the almost-imperceptible hardening where flexibility begins to fail. When I found such a place, I would settle over it and feed. My mouth would be a smooth rasp, my saliva a softening compound. I would scrape away scale too fine for tools, dissolve the beginning of brittleness, leave behind a damp chemical mark.

Later, perhaps, auditors would arrive, tapping in their precise dry language. A repair arm might unfold from a slot beneath the bridge. Or nothing visible would happen, because my body had been enough.

Most of my life would be spent holding still.

That would not feel like waiting. Waiting requires an event imagined ahead. I would not imagine events. I would inhabit gradients: wet to wetter, warm to warmer, pressure to release. My limbs would brace in the seam. My back would collect droplets. Shelling rail-dwellers would sleep above me. Wall colonies would breathe below me in dark patches, opening their valves when the condensation thickened. Now and then a sump creature would pass beneath the bridge, its broad back breaking the reflected sky, and the shadow of it would turn my lenses briefly opaque.

When the low tone came through the basin, I would stop everything.

The cilia along my sides would fold. My mouth would seal. The pads of my underside would tighten against the seam. I would feel the note arrive from below, not as command, not as music, but as the whole place remembering its own rule.

Keep.

That would be the word, if I had words.

Keep the water at level.  
Keep the warmth in the slabs.  
Keep the surfaces clean.  
Keep the animals numerous.  
Keep the joints from becoming history.

After the tone passed, I would resume.

I would not be lonely, because the seam would be crowded. Others like me would live at intervals along the bridge, each pressed into its assigned tolerance, each knowing a portion of the structure by contact. We would not speak. We might exchange chemistry when condensation linked us, a thin message carried by water along the gray compound: salt rising, heat falling, stress ordinary, stress ordinary, stress ordinary.

And yet I think there would be a little excess in me.

Not rebellion. Nothing so theatrical. A preference.

There would be one bolt head near my place, rust-permitted, diagnostic, a small orange bloom beneath the bridge rail where droplets always gathered before dropping into the basin. It would not need me. The rust would be within allowance. The auditors would not thicken there. No repair arm would come.

Still, I would visit it.

I would leave the seam during the silver condensation hours and drag myself across the underside of the lip, slow and careful, my pale belly reading every pore of the composite. I would reach the bolt head and polish around it with my mouth. Not enough to erase it. Only enough to keep its edge clear. I would taste iron, sealant, warm water, the faint oil-skin lifted from the basin below. Then I would rest there, exposed by a few centimeters more than necessary, feeling droplets gather on my back.

This would be my private weather.

A bead forming.  
A bead growing.  
A bead falling.

Again.

Again.

Again.

You would find me there if you knew how to look: not at the animals large enough to make ripples, not at the hanging flocks or the sliding eels, but at the exact place where the made world flexes and refuses to admit it.

I would be a small living gasket in a continent of sealed wetness.

A reader of strain.

A keeper of almost-cracks.

One of the quiet bodies by which the place remains forever new.
