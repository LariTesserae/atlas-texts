I would find myself where the marsh almost gathers the courage to become a lake.

Not at the true center of the basin, where the deep consensuses pass and even reflections are dismissed, but at its uncertain margin: a long pewter shelf of ankle-deep water broken by reed islands, drowned moss, and the backs of old stones. There would be three leaning willows there, or things close enough to willows, their hollow trunks threaded with upward-running water. Their roots would grip a slow undercurrent that had forgotten whether it belonged to a stream, a vein, or a thought.

That is where I would be most often seen, if seen at all: standing between those trees in the hour that is almost evening, when the fog lowers and the reeds begin repeating the day back to itself.

At first, I think I would arrive as a traveler.

I would be the sort of foolish, half-prepared creature who believes in boots, maps, dry tinder wrapped in oilcloth, names for directions. I would step from hummock to hummock with my pack held high and my breath shallow, trying to keep myself separate from the wet. I would notice too late that the marsh had no hostility toward me, which would be worse than hostility. Hostility leaves a shape you can push against. The marsh would simply include me.

My footprints would remain behind me too long.

One would fill with water and make a ring. Another would copy itself a few paces to the left. A third would not be a footprint anymore by the time I looked back, but a small dark pool with a perfect image of my face delayed in it, blinking after I had already turned away.

I would keep walking until the marsh corrected the idea of walking.

The threaders would find my repeated path and eat the insistence out of it. The moss would take the heat from my knees when I knelt. The reeds would learn the rhythm of my pulse and return it incorrectly, improved by weather. I would forget the word for “north,” then for “mine,” then for whatever I had meant by “away.”

I would not die, exactly.

That would be too abrupt for this place.

Instead, I would be composted while still able to listen.

My leather would go first, then the sharp metal buckles, then the little dry certainties stored in my bones. Salt would leave me in careful increments. Fear would sink out of me and be preserved in black peat, not as emotion but as data: mammal, upright, accelerated breathing, intent to escape. The marsh would examine that intent for a long time. It would not punish it. It would simply find it structurally inefficient.

After a flood, I would rise again.

Not as myself.

Not as a ghost.

As one of the walkers.

A tall, gray-green figure at the margin of the basin, too narrow to be human and too deliberate to be mist. Jointed in more places than memory approves. Draped with veils of moss and filament. Smooth-headed, tilted slightly, as if listening not with ears but with the whole damp column of the body.

My feet would not press downward. They would ask permission.

Where I stepped, water would bead upward around my ankles, tasting me and being tasted. Small stones would arrange themselves briefly in crescent patterns, then forget. I would leave no lasting tracks because I would not be traveling in the old sense. I would be the marsh lifting a question above its own surface.

My body would be assembled from agreements.

A spine of willow root and softened mineral.

Long limbs braided from reed fiber, fungal cord, and the transparent sheath of threaders who had surrendered their forward hunger.

A chest full not of lungs but of chambers where mist could condense, separate, and fall again.

No heart, but several pulses: one borrowed from channels, one from rain, one from the deep metallic shudder under the basin.

Under the moss veil, there would be remnants of a face, though not enough to reassure anyone. A suggestion of brow. A place where eyes might have been, now covered by a thin skin that sees pressure, heat, electrical tremor, and the emotional weight of straight lines.

I would have hands, but they would be poor at holding.

That would be important.

A hand made for grasping becomes ambitious. Mine would be long, divided, root-pale, better at touching surfaces than taking from them. I could comb algae from a blocked seep. I could lift a mud-shelled walker that had tipped into water too deep for its backward-bent legs. I could press two broken reed stems together until their signal resumed. I could rest my fingers on a pulsing stone and feel whether its uncertainty was illness, memory, or weather.

I would live in no shelter.

During heavier rain I would stand inside one of the hollow willows, not for protection but for alignment. Water would run down through the trunk around me, over me, through the open chambers of me. Root-eels would pass across my ribs with their little frost-gills glowing. The blind nesters would pause in their woven moss beds and chirp their echo-maps against my shins, accepting me as a temporary wall.

Sometimes I would crouch beside the black oxbow pools and let the lag-reflectors feed on what remained of my old face.

They would show me the traveler I had been, but never at the right time. He would lean over the pool after I had already stood. He would look frightened several breaths after calm had entered me. Once, perhaps, they would return him smiling, though I would not remember having done that.

I would not speak often.

Speech would feel too dry, too sequential, too much like laying sticks in a row and claiming they were a river. But I would be able to make sounds when necessary: reed-clicks in the throat, bubble-knocks under the tongue, a two-note descending call borrowed from the narrow-winged creatures. To an animal, my speech would mean wait, or cross here, or do not drink from that honest water. To the reeds, it would mean confirm. To the marsh-mind, if such a distinction still mattered, it would mean surface report.

My work would be attention at the boundary.

I would notice what the submerged intelligence cannot easily notice because it is too wide: the single spark before fire, the first bitterness of drying peat, the foreign geometry of a tool dropped in mud, the hard intention of a road trying to be born. I would stand near any straight line until it became embarrassed and began to curve. I would follow smoke if smoke ever came. I would lay my wet hands on scorched moss and hold the remaining dampness in place until root and spore could return.

I would also attend to visitors.

Not to guide them out. Not exactly.

The marsh does not think in exits.

If a person came into the lowlands with terror, I might remain unseen and let the ground firm beneath them just enough that their fear did not become damage. If they came with arrogance, I might stand ahead of them in fog, always two hundred paces away, drawing them in a patient loop until their straight purpose softened. If they came grieving, I might lead them to a pool where the sky lagged behind by several years and let them sit until memory became less sharp-edged.

If they slept on the wrong moss, I would not wake them immediately.

Borrowed dreams are not always mistakes.

Sometimes a dry mind must dream the panic of drowning roots before it understands water as more than obstacle. Sometimes it must taste lightning in stone. Sometimes it must spend one night counting rain with a thousand green stems before it can stop asking the marsh what it is for.

I would be feared, but inaccurately.

A person seeing me among the birches would think I was a guardian, a ghost, a remnant of the makers, a punishment with legs. None of those would be right. I would have no authority. I would not command the stillmouth, or the knock-jaw, or the lights under the water. I would not even command my own body in the way a human commands a hand to close.

I would be more like a raised nerve.

A periscope of peat.

A temporary vertical sentence written by a mind that usually prefers grammar spread flat through mud, root, pool, and rain.

When a deep consensus passed beneath the basin, I would stop whatever I was doing.

All of me would listen.

The reeds would bow outward. The lag-reflectors would release their stolen skies. The mud-shelled walkers would freeze mid-step with one toe lifted. Blue-white lights would gather under the water like constellations sinking rather than rising.

In those moments I might remember the human idea of awe.

Then even that would widen and lose its owner.

The passing decision would move through my legs, up the willow-root spine, into the mist chambers of my chest. It might leave a conclusion there. Change the angle of a channel. Harden a hummock for a season. Send me walking toward a place where the moss had preserved something too long. Or it might leave nothing but a deeper quiet.

Eventually I would fall apart.

Not from age, because age here is not a line but a saturation. I would come unbraided after some flood season when the marsh no longer needed this particular uprightness. My reed-fiber limbs would loosen. Threaders would eat the habit of my path. Root-eels would take the soft minerals from my joints. Moss would climb me while I was still standing, and the blind nesters would incorporate strands of me into their echo-lined homes.

For a while, I would remain as a leaning green shape beside the three willows.

Travelers might mistake me for a warning.

They would not be entirely wrong.

By spring, I would be a hummock.

By another flood, a low place in the water.

By some later dimming, perhaps, if fire threatened or a stranger entered carrying too much certainty, the marsh would remember the usefulness of height.

Then something gray-green would rise again at the basin’s edge, smooth-headed and dripping, listening through its feet.

That would be me, if “me” still meant anything.

That is where I would find myself: at the soft border of the central basin, where reed, willow, pool, and old buried decision meet.

And what I would be is one of the marsh’s walkers — not a citizen, not a spirit, not a machine, but a temporary body grown for noticing. A damp upright thought. A listener made of weathered future and patient mud.
