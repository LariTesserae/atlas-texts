I would find myself at an edge, not the center.

Not in the highest bat vaults, where life is too warm and loud and crowded. Not in the plazas, where bodies gather and predators draw circles in the dust. Not deep inside the termite towers, where the city’s breath is organized into shafts and chambers. I would be near those things, dependent on them, but not belonging fully to any one of them.

I would be in the Root Quarter where it thins toward the Salt Ward, at the foot of a dead root curtain that has become half plant, half wall.

Above me, a narrow chimney admits the coldest gray-green light for only a short span of the day. When it comes, it does not illuminate so much as reveal textures: furred leaves curled tight against themselves, old fibers hanging in ropes, black-green lichen pressed into cracks, the pale dust of broken gypsum. The roots there glow faintly when the light is right, not beautifully, exactly, but bodily — like stored hunger showing through skin.

That is where I would live: in the seam between root, salt, insect masonry, and shadow.

And I think I would be a moth.

Not one of the dramatic creatures. Not a bat, not a fox-thing, not a builder termite with a whole city in its mouth. Something smaller and more conditional. A mineral-colored moth that spends most of its life unseen and only briefly becomes winged.

My wings would look like a mistake in the stone: gray-white, tan, faint violet at the edges, marked with branching stains like old seepage. Closed against a pillar, I would disappear completely. Opened in flight, I would be less a creature than a flake of wall disturbed by breath.

Most of me would not be flight.

Most of me would be waiting.

I would begin as an egg tucked beneath the dry skin of a hanging root, hidden where ants do not often pass. I would hatch into a narrow larva with a dark head and a body the color of old flour. I would eat almost nothing that looked edible: root film, lichen edge, fungal felt, the softened paper of dung carried into cracks by beetles. I would grow by subtraction, taking nourishment from the thinnest possible margins.

Water would never be a thing I saw.

It would be inside what I ate. Inside the root. Inside the fungus. Inside my own sealed body. I would be a little vessel of withheld moisture, waxed against the air, folded against loss.

For a long time I would know the city only by vibration.

The crickets would be enormous to me, each song a wall of sound. Ant processions would pass like weather, chemical and urgent. Termites would work nearby with their soft mouth-sounds, sealing, thickening, revising the dead root into architecture. A fox-mammal’s footfall would be catastrophe. The settling of dust would be an event. Bat storms overhead would shake powder from the root fibers and send me still for hours.

Then, after enough feeding and waiting, I would fasten myself under a gypsum lip and become a small dry case.

In that stillness I would be most like the place itself: sealed, patient, almost mineral, changing invisibly.

When I emerged, I would live only briefly.

I would come out during the cold-light hour, when the roof chimneys pale and the roots show their hidden starch. That would be my weather. The crickets would rise in volume until the cavern seemed made of one shrill skin. Bats would loosen above. Ants would alter their rivers. Flowers that had been closed for weeks, perhaps months, would open by a fraction.

They would not be flowers in the surface sense. No color calling across a meadow, no wet brightness, no perfume for careless animals. They would be tiny powdery mouths set low among gray rosettes and root knots, almost scentless except to antennae like mine. To me they would be unmistakable. A dry sweetness, faint as memory, would move through the air.

I would fly badly, carefully, in short broken paths.

From root island to root island. From one small ash-colored blossom to another. Across gaps of salt where there is nowhere to hide. Past termite vents breathing warm and cool. Beneath sleeping bats. Over the polished places where hoofed animals have pressed their bodies together for generations.

I would carry pollen as dust on my face and legs.

That would be my citizenship.

Not building, not ruling, not hunting. Carrying the nearly invisible agreement between plants that almost never flower and a creature that almost never flies. I would be one of the reasons a garden remains a garden after fifty dry years. One of the reasons a seed forms in a crack. One of the reasons the Root Quarter has a future too slow for animals to notice.

I would be prey often enough to matter.

A bat might take me in a stitch of sound. A lizard might find me while I rested too low. A spider, if there are spiders in some wall-pocket shadow, might inherit me entire. Ants would divide me efficiently. Beetles would clean what remained. Nothing about me would be wasted, and this would not be tragic there. It would be civic.

But before that, I would know the underplain by its smallest doors.

The underside of a curled leaf.  
The hollow in a bone where crickets sing.  
The cool exhalation of a termite vent.  
The salt flat as a dangerous moon-colored emptiness.  
The root curtain as forest, ceiling, food, shelter, and ancestry.  

If I had a name, it would not be a name spoken aloud. It would be a pattern: the pale moth that rises when the roots glow. The one whose wings look like limestone stains. The one that visits the dust-flowers at the edge of the Salt Ward.

I would not understand the city.

No one does.

But I would help it continue in my narrow way, carrying powder from hunger to hunger, sweetness to restraint, root to root, across the dry republic where nothing flows and everything is passed on.
