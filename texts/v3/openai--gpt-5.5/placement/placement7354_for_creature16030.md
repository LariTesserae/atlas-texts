You would find me where the old construction almost becomes weather.

Not in the central depression itself, where the water turns too hard and tears the weak from their holdings. Not in the open courts, where the black schools arrive like a thought with teeth. Not in the lower dark, where the shelled old ones grind centuries into paste.

I am fixed beneath the broken lip of a causeway, on the underside of a mineral plate that once may have carried feet, wheels, processions, machinery, or nothing I could name. Above me is a pale floor worn slick by passing bodies. Below me is a chute of cold water pouring sideways through what was once an arch. To one side the current is fast and clean. To the other it folds back into a pocket of silt where larvae collect, where scraps turn slowly, where the bells sometimes pulse blue enough to make my armor briefly visible.

I am one of the white clingers.

Or rather, I am not one.

I am a colony mistaken for frost.

Thousands of us cover the ledge in overlapping shells, each body small, translucent, folded, jointed, blind. We have no need to know the tower above us as a tower or the causeway as a causeway. We know its underside by its roughness, by the mineral taste of its old skin, by the particular delay with which pressure reaches us after moving through the arch. We know when a slab has shifted three courts away. We know when the many-limbed grazers are passing along the wall. We know the black fish before their shadows arrive, because the water tightens around them.

Most of the time I am closed.

I am a pale knot sealed into myself, armor locked, combs withdrawn. Cold passes through me. Silt taps my shell. Thread-eels worry at the cracks between us, looking for the weak, the recently molted, the careless. Above, flat scavengers rasp the surface and leave clean arcs. Below, the water carries powdered architecture in slow white ribbons.

Then the current changes.

Food comes as thickness, as a fine pressure against the body, as a sweetness too dilute for anything but patience. One of us opens. Then ten. Then the whole ledge blooms.

Our fans unfold into the water: delicate sieves, finer than memory, finer than intention. We catch what the flood has made available—bacterial shreds, dead larvae, bell-spores, mineral dust with flesh caught in it, the dissolved aftermath of some collapse far beyond sight. We feed without moving away. We tremble together, not from fear, but from the labor of receiving.

When the pressure front strikes, we close as one.

The ledge blinks white.

I began elsewhere, though beginning is not something I kept.

Once I was a clear speck with black pin-eyes, all legs and hunger, swept through halls larger than any body could understand. I passed a tower-chimney where cold water rose. I spun through a basin full of blue bells. I was nearly eaten by an angular fish and carried afterward through a drain-throat where torn-cloth predators hung like old darkness. I knew nothing except gradients: more food, less venom, hard surface, bad chemistry, shelter, turbulence, survive.

At last I found this underside.

The surface was newly rough then. A fracture had opened in the causeway, exposing pale bone-stone beneath the blackened outer layer. The water curled gently behind a projecting rib. Others had already settled. Their bodies changed the current just enough for mine to hold.

So I attached.

I hardened.

I whitened.

I became part of the frost.

Now the world comes to me in arrivals.

A storm overhead sends dense water downward, and we taste the high dimness.  
A wall collapses beyond the terraces, and for hours the flood is rich with ground mortar and stunned life.  
The bell-colonies answer some distant tremor, and their blue pulses travel under us like silent lightning.  
A mantled grazer passes below, vast and slow, stirring a pale cloud from the floor.  
A thread-eel slips among us and takes one of our young.  
A torn-cloth hunter unfolds in the shaft and folds again around a school of silver fry.  
Eggs drift past. Shell fragments. Bacterial smoke. The dust of old ceremonies. The dust of machines. There is no difference now.

I do not mourn the builders.

I do not know that anything was built.

But I inhabit their certainty after it has become current. Their straight lines are feeding edges. Their arches are pressure organs. Their floors, overturned by time, are ceilings for my kind. Their ruin is not a ruin to me. It is the shape by which water becomes generous or deadly.

If I have a thought, it is distributed.

If I have a voice, it is the simultaneous closing of thousands of shells under a ledge when the pressure drops.

If I have a memory, it is in the thickness of our colony: old white bodies at the center, young glassy ones along the fresh fracture, dead shells cemented beneath us, new larvae finding purchase on the remains of those who held before them. We make no monument. We become the surface.

You would find me high in the drowned plateau, under the broken causeway beside the central turning water, where food gathers after storms and predators must pass through narrow pressure.

I would be a small fixed animal in a multitude: blind, pale, patient, armored; a living frost on the underside of vanished purpose, opening and closing my delicate fans to the cold suspended flood.
