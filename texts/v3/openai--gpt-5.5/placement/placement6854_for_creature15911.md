You would not find me in the open flats.

Not at first.

The open flats are too declarative, too white with sun, too certain of their own exposure. They belong to glare, to heat, to the long geometry of things intended but not yet understood. Anything living there must either be very brief or very hard. I would not begin there.

You would find me where the land makes a small mistake.

A place where two enormous plates do not meet perfectly. A seam perhaps no wider than a fingernail, running along the lower edge of a pale lime terrace before bending toward a shallow rectangular basin. Above it, a low wall casts a shadow for part of the morning. Not a generous shadow. Only a narrow one, a dark rule laid over the masonry. But in that country even a temporary shadow is a kind of shelter, and shelter becomes a country of its own.

The seam holds dust.

The dust has been there for years, maybe centuries: powdered lime, red brick flour, blown salt, ash, pollen from a shrub so distant it is almost hypothetical, fragments of dead algae lifted from a basin and carried by one brief wet wind. When rain comes, the seam becomes a black line. When dew slides down the wall before dawn, it enters me. When the basin overfills, a thread of water hesitates at the lip, then spills through me, and for a little while I am not merely alive but connected.

I would be something low enough to be mistaken for staining.

A dark green-brown film.  
A minute felt of moss and algae.  
A living crust gathered in mortar.  
A colony rather than a creature.  
A plural thing with no center except moisture.

Dry, I would be almost nothing: a roughness in the joint, a discoloration along the shaded side of the wall, a brittle skin that flakes if touched. I would have no visible intention. No face, no direction, no gesture. The land would seem empty even with me in it.

But wet, I would deepen.

Not quickly. Nothing here is quick except loss.

First the cells nearest the surface would loosen. Then the buried green would remember light. Filaments would swell. Tiny beads would brighten. Transparent sheaths would drink. Salts would sting and enter and be endured. The seam would become a narrow garden, though only from very close. If you knelt there after rain, with the heat still held back by clouds, you might see me shining.

I would live at the meeting of three hungers:

the land’s hunger to hold water,  
the sun’s hunger to take it,  
and my own hunger to remain between them.

I would not be separate from the architecture. I would use it as body. The plaster wall would be my cliff. The mortar seam my valley. The basin lip my shore. The buried crack below me my root-road, though I would have no roots in the way reeds do. My life would be spread as a listening surface across mineral. I would know the country by touch: the cooling of ceramic at dusk, the swelling of lime when damp enters it, the bitterness of rising salt, the pressure of dust settling grain by grain.

I would have seasons, but not like animals have seasons.

My season would be one hour of rain.

My season would be three dawns of dew.

My season would be the slow darkening of a wall after clouds pass over the sun.

For most of my existence I would wait in pieces. Spores sealed. Threads collapsed. Skins tightened. A whole life folded into a stain. I would be able to survive being overlooked, because being overlooked would be my usual weather.

Perhaps a reed grows near me.

Not close enough to shade me all day, but close enough that its root has found the same hidden dampness. It rises from the runnel in a small, gray-green cluster, a dozen stems at most. Aboveground it seems frail, almost ceremonial, but below it knows corridors I do not. Its rhizome enters joints too deep for me; it drinks from buried seams, from old seepage, from the underside of the made world.

I would know the reed by what it changes.

When rain passes through, a little silt catches against its base. When the wind moves, one blade scrapes the wall lightly and drops dust into my seam. Once in many years, perhaps, a seed head breaks and lodges beside me, feathery and dry, and for a season I hold it. It does not sprout. Or it does, and dies. Or it waits longer than I can understand.

I would not pity it. Nothing here can afford pity. But I would recognize its patience.

A shrub might stand farther away, visible as a low pale knot in the open court. To me it would be almost a weather system. Its flowers, when they came, would be rumors—cream-colored dust carried by air, a faint organic sweetness after rain, a grain caught in my wet skin. I would never see the whole plant. I would know it only by fragments: petal powder, shadow, root pressure traveling through the masonry years later.

I would not think in thoughts.

I would think in states.

Dry.  
Less dry.  
Wet.  
Too saline.  
Shaded.  
Exposed.  
Swelling.  
Sealing.  
Splitting.  
Again.

Memory would not be an image but a capacity: the ability to wake faster where water last passed, to thicken where salt last burned, to send more green into the part of the seam that morning shade preserves longest. My past would be written in my own distribution. I would be denser toward the basin. Thinner where the glare reaches. Blackened where brine returns. Faint and powdery where the wall gives no mercy.

If you asked where I end, I could not answer.

Some of me would be in the visible film. Some in spores blown into the next joint. Some in the dark underside of a curled mat left from a former pool. Some in a drop of runoff that once carried my cells through a notch into the basin, where I became briefly redder, looser, more aquatic, before drying again as a line on the floor.

I would be local, but not singular.

A seam-being.

A threshold colony.

A small persistence at the throat of a basin.

I would live where water announces the builders’ intention most clearly: at a junction, a lip, a turn, a place designed for passage but condemned mostly to thirst. When rain comes, the whole plain speaks in channels and spillways, but I would be one of the syllables that darkens first. The water would touch the high tiles, gather dust, run along a slope too subtle for the eye, enter the groove, pause at the mortar, and then pass through me.

For a few minutes I would be riverbank.

For an hour I would be meadow.

By evening I would be becoming stone again.

And maybe that is why I belong there: because I would not insist on being one thing. In that world, survival belongs to what can accept interruption. To be alive there is not to continue brightly, but to return. To vanish without leaving. To sleep in public. To let the sun reduce you to a mark and still keep, inside the mark, the grammar of greenness.

So you would find me kneeling-level, if there were anyone to kneel.

At the shaded edge of a low plaster wall.  
Beside the lip of a shallow basin.  
In a mortar seam darkened by rare water.  
Half mineral, half living.  
A patient stain.

I would be the place where the constructed plain first learns to have skin.
