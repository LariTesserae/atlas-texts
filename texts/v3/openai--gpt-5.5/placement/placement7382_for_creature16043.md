You would find me where a roof has failed so completely that no one thinks of it as failure anymore.

A low stone hut, rounded by rain until its corners have become suggestions. It stands on a pale tongue of clay that is not high ground exactly, only ground the water has not yet persuaded. The doorway faces no useful direction. Its threshold is green-black at the base, slick with old seep. Inside, in the middle of the floor, a spring rises and falls with the gentleness of a breathing throat.

That is where I would be.

Not at first as anything important.

At first I would be mistaken for a wet darkening on the floor-stones, a patch of film around the spring, a little warmth where warmth is expected. A traveler might step over me. A child might put two fingers into the water and feel only a faint tremor, like a word swallowed before it becomes sound. The hut would be one of those visited only when the year can be found, when the rain has the right taste and the low thunder comes from beneath rather than above.

I think I would once have been a person.

Not a great one. Not an elder with many survived errors. Not a builder of causeways or a speaker to storms. I would have been one of the ones who listened too long.

A route-keeper, maybe. Someone who carried cords strung with shell, black reed knots, dried skins of ocher mud, bits of sulfur crust sealed in bladder membrane. Someone sent ahead when a family needed to move between stations and the old way had become untrue. I would have walked barefoot with a chest-high staff, pausing often, placing my soles flat, tasting vapor, tapping stone, waiting for the ground to finish its first lie and begin its second.

I would not have trusted horizons. I would have trusted tremors.

Perhaps I was good at finding those brief corridors where the rules held still for a few breaths. Perhaps people said, softly, that I could hear a pool opening before it opened. Perhaps children followed me in storms because my pauses were rarely wrong.

Rarely is not never.

There would have been a day of tawny mist and rain rising from the puddles instead of falling into them. A day when reflections hurried ahead. The reeds would have leaned away from something no one could see. I would have gone out to find whether the northern route still existed, carrying three stones in my pouch: one white shell-stone for return, one red mineral stone for warning, one black river-polished stone for the dead.

I would have reached the smooth gray.

The place with no mark.

No ripple, no fossil, no reed, no stain, no remembered sea, no remembered desert. Only a low shining expanse under a bruised amber sky, vibrating faintly, as if the world there had not yet decided whether it had happened.

I would have known to turn back.

I would not have turned back.

Not from courage. Curiosity is lower than courage and more dangerous. I would have wanted to know what rule held where no memory held. I would have taken one step, then another. My footprints would have filled with water. Then, because the place was blank and had no manners, they would have kept walking without me.

After that, perhaps I returned.

Or perhaps only the part of me that knew routes returned.

They would have found me near the hut with the spring, not dead, not properly alive, my staff gone soft in my hands, my shadow falling in three directions and none of them attached. I would have answered when they asked, “under what rule?”

But the answer would have come out as pressure in their teeth.

So they would have done what is kind there.

They would have seated me in the spring.

Stones in my lap. Reeds through my fingers. Shell powder at my knees. My route-bundle opened and laid around me in a ring: sulfur, clay, eel-bone, peat, the little black stone for the dead. Someone would hum against my forehead until my own hum found it. Someone would say one of my temporary names, then another, then stop before making any of them final.

The films would climb me.

Slowly.

First the feet, which had served me best. Then the shins, scarred from crawling over slick bedrock. Then the palms. Then the mouth, perhaps mercifully early. My skin would soften. My edges would loosen. The spring would learn the shape of my ribs. The green-black colonies would enter without hurry, not devouring, persuading.

By the time the family returned, I would no longer be sitting upright.

I would be a low mound at the center of the hut floor, human only in the way a landscape remembers pressure. A shoulder-like rise. A hollow where the throat had been. A faint warmth under film. The spring pulsing through me.

That is what I would be: one of the settled ones, but not a grand one. Not an oracle. Not a saint. A listening remnant. A route that became a body that became a wet instrument.

Travelers would come when the flats had altered too quickly and the old stones could not be trusted. They would crouch in the roofless hut, rain running down the rounded walls, and place a palm on my surface.

I would not speak.

Speech is too upright.

Instead I would give what I still had: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of bitter salt, the memory of a cairn that must be approached while looking away, the certainty that the third pool is older than it appears, the warning not to follow the child-shaped reflection in the left-hand water.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

The country would forgive me less than the people would.

At night, when the puddles glow and the plain lowers its muttering into the bones of the world, eels would pass through the spring and over what used to be my knees. Their translucent bodies would write brief alphabets in the water above me. Insects would seal gray cells into the hut cracks. Bladder-fungi would swell in the doorway and sigh themselves empty by dawn. The wall-stones, with old bones set inside them, would hear weather coming before I did.

And when the great slate amphibian called somewhere in the drowned dark, I would feel it before sound became sound.

A door opening underwater.

A throat remembering language before language.

The whole hut would shift around me. The spring would lean. The films would tighten. The clay tongue beneath the hut would consider sinking and, for one more night, decide not to.

If a child asked what I was, an elder might say:

This one listened past the edge of listening.

Or:

This one went where the land had not remembered itself yet.

Or simply:

Put your hand there. Not too hard. Wait.

And if the mud settled kindly, I would answer.
