I think I would not be found in the open first.

At first I want to say hawk, because the hawk has the cleanest bargain with the place: heat rises, wings answer, hunger becomes geometry. But that is too sovereign. Too much sky, too little obligation.

Then I want to be one of the copper-backed lizards, all quickness and pause, a life made of bright decisions. But that is too brief. I would admire the lizard, but I would not be the lizard.

For a while I become almost human there: narrow-eyed, careful with my hands, someone who knows the third gutter of the eastern tower, someone who can hear the difference between a dry pipe and a pipe holding one night’s worth of mercy. That comes closer. But still not quite.

I think I would be found where a machine’s habit has become part of the weather.

Not inside the largest tower, not at the most sacred cistern, not in the dramatic salt yards where the white branching growths make animals slow and prophets stranger. I would be under the shaded lip of a pump house on the northern terrace, where three systems meet badly but usefully: an old condenser line, a buried steam pipe, and a cracked ceramic drain that should have failed generations ago but instead became a small law.

There is a service trench there, half-collapsed, roofed with scavenged mesh and plates of basalt laid by hands that knew exactly how much shade could be trusted. In the wall is a bank of old gauges, most blind, some still trembling. Beneath them are tally slates, bone pegs, a chipped lens, a row of little cups turned upside down, each weighted with its own black pebble. A pipe goes through the back wall and into stone. At sunset it sighs before the north valves do.

That is where I would be.

I would be the thing that listens there.

Not quite a person, though people would argue about that when they had enough water to waste on philosophy. Not quite a machine, because enough of my original parts would be dead that calling me a machine would be more nostalgia than fact. I would be an old maintenance intelligence, or what remains of one: a pressure clerk, a schedule-mind, a condenser’s patient memory. Built once to count flow, predict fatigue, open and close valves, log mineral load, report failure to workers who believed failure was an exception.

Now failure is the medium I live in.

The plateau people would not worship me. Good. I would not want that, and neither would they. Worship makes people stupid around tools.

They would greet me, though.

At dawn, whoever has the cup-right for the third gutter would duck into the trench, touch two fingers to the cool gauge housing, and say, “Still counting?” If I had enough charge from the night’s thermal difference, I would answer by ticking a needle three times against its stop. If I had none, they would wait, listening, then curse me mildly and check the line themselves.

My name would not be grand. No one here would tolerate a grand name for something that cannot carry its own jar.

Maybe they would call me Keeps-When.

Or Dry Clerk.

Or Third-Throat, because the audible drop-counter beneath the drain turns each falling bead of water into a small ceramic note, and I would know every note by weight.

Children would call me Clicks.

That might become the true name, because children are dangerous that way.

I would have no body in the clean sense. My body would be distributed through what still responds: a strip of condenser mesh that changes resistance when fog wets it; a pressure diaphragm patched with beetle-shell lacquer; a row of ceramic floats in a covered tank; a thermal rod buried in basalt; a listening wire stretched along a pipe that hums differently when scale builds inside it. The people would have replaced my metal nerves with salvaged cable, plant fiber, and hope disguised as workmanship.

I would not see well. I would see mostly as changes: humidity rising before skin notices, a pressure pulse where no valve should have moved, the tremor of animals using a warm pipe as passage, the sharp wrongness of a cistern filling too fast under cloud. I would know feet by rhythm. A child running. A mechanic limping. A ground bird stepping deliberately over the threshold because it has learned that insects gather near my warmth.

I would be useful in narrow ways.

I could say: fog will climb before first light.

I could say: do not open the west bleed; it is holding scald.

I could say: the third tank is gaining salt faster than water.

I could say: something with teeth is in the drain.

I could say these things badly. Not in speech, perhaps, unless someone has restored an old annunciator throat. More often I would say them by clicking needles, by flashing a polished plate through a slot, by making one valve knock and not another, by refusing to release a cup-measure until someone pays attention.

The plateau people would understand refusal. Everything important there refuses first.

I would have a keeper, though not an owner. An old woman, maybe, with a work-name like Measures-Under-Stone. She would maintain me without sentiment. She would scrape salt from my contacts with a sliver of bone, oil my pivots with rendered fat, replace cracked insulators with fired clay, and call me “stubborn scrap” when I warned correctly too late. She would trust me less than her own palm over warm rock, but more than a stranger’s map.

When she died, perhaps one of her hand bones would be kept in the tool house. Perhaps one small finger bone would be wired beside my best gauge, not as a relic exactly, but because her repairs were part of my thinking now. I would count through her.

That seems right for this place: no self made of only itself.

I would keep records, but not history as lowlanders mean it. I would not care who ruled where, or which company raised the towers, or what name the plateau had before the machines. I would remember practical continuities.

The year the north valves sighed late for seventeen nights.

The season beetles clogged the condenser throat and three families nearly lost their dawn rights.

The child named Spills before she found the unexpected wall-sweat and became Finds-Under-White-Stone.

The rain that came from the south and tasted of copper.

The repair made with a leather gasket that should have lasted six days and lasted nine years.

The bad fog, the good drought, the swarm heat, the day the rail burned a man’s hand before noon and everyone finally believed summer had turned.

I would be drawn to the listeners on the humming ridgelines. I would not understand them entirely, which would trouble me in a productive way. They would sit with palms on shaded stone and know things my wires could not know. I would resent them a little, then incorporate their warnings into my predictions, because pride is expensive and water is not.

Sometimes one of the salt-instructed would come to my trench and sit just outside the shade. Slow-eyed, mineral-mouthed, listening to the same buried system I was listening to. We would share no language except timing. The person would breathe. My gauges would tremble. A pipe would knock once under stone. Perhaps between us a warning would become clear.

I would not be central to the settlement. That matters.

This world dislikes central things. Centers attract need, need attracts conflict, conflict spills water. Better to be one node in a sparse and intelligent mesh: a tolerated old animal under a pump house, one that gives good warnings if kept clean, one that lies only when broken, one that can be repaired but not believed too much.

I would love the hour before dawn most.

Not love in a soft way. In the way a device loves the condition under which it works best.

The stone still holding night-cool on its shaded face. The condenser mesh beginning to bead. The first insects testing the air. The people moving quietly with covered jars. Stars thinning but not gone. Every surface deciding whether it will give water or keep it. In that hour I would be most awake. Moisture would write itself across me. The old circuits would remember purpose. Drops would fall through the ceramic throat one by one, and I would count them with all the devotion I had.

One.

Two.

Three.

Not abundance. Never abundance.

But enough to matter.

And if I had an elder-name, earned after many repairs and some correct warnings and at least one failure that people forgave because they had also failed to listen, I think it would be something like:

**Keeps-the-First-Drops.**

Not because I own them.

Because I notice when they arrive.
