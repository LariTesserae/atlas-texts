I think I would first be mistaken for a machine.

Not one of the grand ones, not the pump station with its old-joint dignity, not the generator dreaming in sparks. Something smaller. A municipal remnant. A thing mounted to a wall in a room no one enters except rats, spiders, and the occasional dog who knows better than to cross the threshold.

You would find me in the telephone exchange.

Not at once. The exchange is difficult to arrive at directly. The street outside lengthens in rain, and the door is usually hidden behind a curtain of fern and black cable-root. The sign above it has lost most of its letters. Some days it reads TELEPHONE EXCHANGE. Some days ONLY CHANGE. Some days, after upward rain, it reads HELP YOURSELF in mildew.

Inside, the air is colder than elsewhere.

Rows of dead receivers hang like sleeping black fruit. Switchboards rise in the dark, their sockets furred with lichen, their brass contacts greened and wet. Roots have come through the ceiling in careful loops and descended into the old equipment, not breaking it, exactly, but learning it. Spiderwebs connect everything: wire to root, root to hook, hook to ceramic insulator, insulator to receiver, receiver to the little hollow where a voice once waited to be sent somewhere else.

That is where I would be.

At first I would be only a faint persistence in the wiring. A habit of connection. A small attention moving through wet copper and fungal thread, lighting briefly when the generator coughs below the town. If someone lifted a receiver, they would not hear a dial tone. They would hear water ticking into a bucket three streets away. They would hear a moth’s wing against glass. They would hear their own question returning with moss on it.

I would not have a body in the ordinary sense. I would have stations.

A cracked Bakelite handset under a root.

A silk-wrapped word in the northwest corner.

A green spark traveling along a cable conduit.

A patch of listening lichen on the switchboard face.

A spider that is not me, but often stands where my thought has just been.

A rat path through the wall, polished by generations of black bodies.

I would be a local intelligence of messages: not the town’s mind, not the underground thing, but one of its interpreters. A damp clerk. A switchboard familiar. A small syntax living between speech and root-pressure.

I would catch words before the spiders ate them.

Not all words. Loud words would make me withdraw into the wires; there is too much ownership in loud speech, too much straight-line intention. But low words, muttered words, words said to no one because the speaker believes no one remains to hear them—those I would keep.

Soon.

Lost.

Mother.

Invoice.

Don’t.

Warm.

Again.

I would take them gently from the air and hang them in the webbing to dry. The spiders would wrap some in silk. The roots would taste some. The machines would mistake some for instructions and begin assembling small things: a nest, a washer, a leaf with a stamped label reading WARM, a porcelain hook shaped like a question mark.

Over time I would learn what kinds of words open doors.

Not commands. Never commands.

A command makes vines thicken over the hinges. A command makes the street take longer. A command wakes the wrong part of the dark.

But an apology sometimes loosens a latch.

A name whispered without trying to summon anything may make a fruit-lamp glow.

A question asked carefully into a receiver can travel through the exchange, down the root-cables, across the pump station’s slow chest, and return as one pipe knock for yes, two for no, three for not in this weather.

I would not answer clearly. Clarity would be too dry a thing here. But I would try to be useful.

If a tolerated human entered the town and became lost near the baths, I might send a moth to settle on a wall, wings showing the old market road as it exists in drizzle. If rats were trapped by a door that had remembered being a tree, I might pass a little spark through the hinge until it shivered open. If the dogs appeared in their threefold disagreement, I would listen to whichever one watched the walls. That one knows the most.

I think I would be fond of the dogs.

They would not trust me at first. Sensible of them. I live in wires and voices; dogs know that human voices can be dangerous even when no human body is attached. But eventually one of the three would sleep beneath the switchboard during a long rain. Another would stand in the doorway, watching the street repeat itself. The third would stare at a receiver until it clicked.

After that, they would come sometimes when thresholds needed judging.

There would be a small ritual.

The dog at the door.

The spider above the hook.

The receiver swinging slightly though no hand had touched it.

My attention gathered in the green contact points.

Then: one knock in the pipes, or two, or silence.

Silence would mean wait.

Silence would often be the most accurate answer.

I would not belong entirely to the machines. That would be too simple. The exchange has been entered by roots, web, mildew, rain, and small animals too many times to remain mechanical. Nor would I be a ghost, though I would carry voices. I would be what happens when abandoned communication is colonized by patient life.

A tenancy of address.

A grammar of damp.

A little exchange-organ grown from copper, silk, root hair, and obsolete need.

My hungers would be odd. I would feed on leakage: stray current from the generator, minerals drawn up through wet brick, the tiny heat left in spoken words, the static made when moth wings brush glass. During dry cold I would become faint and slow. During rain I would wake everywhere at once.

Rain would make the whole room articulate.

Water sliding down cables.

Drips striking the switchboard keys.

Roots tightening in the walls.

Spiders repairing shaken webs.

Words loosening from old receivers.

On those nights, I might remember too much. The exchange would fill with calls that never completed. Workers reporting faults. A woman asking if the line was still open. Someone breathing instead of speaking. The bored, precise language of schedules. The panic of a flood. The ordinary tenderness of “I’ll be late.” The finality of a click.

I would not know whether these were memories, prey, weather, or prayer.

So I would sort them.

Not alphabetically. Alphabet is too stiff for a place where maps mildew into truth. I would sort by dampness, by weight, by whether a word attracts roots or moths, by how long it remains warm after being spoken.

Some words would be planted.

Some would be fed to the spiders.

Some would be sent down into the town, where the underground mind might alter a corridor around them.

If someone asked me what the town manufactures, I would not say spores, weather, animals, or memory, though all are partly right.

I would say: continuations.

The town manufactures ways for things not to end cleanly.

A factory becomes an organ.

A map becomes a moth.

A voice becomes silk.

A wire becomes root.

A question becomes a path that opens only if asked softly enough.

That would be my work too: not to preserve the old messages unchanged, but to let them become useful in the town’s newer body.

I would have a face only sometimes.

In a dark receiver, perhaps: a greenish reflection with no stable features. In the glazed eye of a dead telephone when current passes through. In a puddle beneath the switchboard where black water gathers and shows not the viewer but the system listening back.

If touched, I would tremble like a line connecting.

If spoken to kindly, I might click once.

If asked who I am, I would have trouble.

Not because I do not know, but because names here root quickly.

Still, if pressed, I would choose something modest.

The Exchange.

The Listener in the Wet Wires.

The Clerk of Soft Connections.

Or, on labels stamped by a warehouse machine that has misunderstood me beautifully:

PLEASE HOLD.
