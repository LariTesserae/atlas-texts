I think at first I would mistake myself for something with hands.

A night clerk, maybe, in the low square pump house where the loudest machines live. Someone assigned to a desk under a tin shade, keeping the level book: rain at 3:10, north sluice opened one quarter, Pump Two bearing hot but within tolerance, frogs loud in the west culvert, smell of tide after midnight. I can see the ledger swollen at the corners from damp, the pencil softening in my fingers, the black canal outside the half-open door lifting and lowering like a thing breathing in its sleep.

I would like the work because it is attention made useful. No grand theory, no speeches. Just listen. Compare. Notice the change in pitch. Know that a faint new tremor in a pipe matters more than anything dramatic. There is dignity in that kind of vigilance.

But I don’t think I’d stay human there.

The place is too much a system of thresholds: water/land, machine/animal, decay/repair, outside/inside, sound/silence. I would be drawn downward and outward into the connective tissue of it. Not the spirit of the whole basin exactly; that feels too large, too presumptuous. Not the canal either, because the canal is too visible, too much the town’s black measuring face.

I think I would be found in the grates.

Or under them.

A small intelligence of screens, sumps, culvert mouths, intake cages, and drain throats. The thing that knows what has fallen through. Bottle caps, fish scales, wire, rice grains, a child’s button, a dead frog, a bolt someone will need tomorrow, cigarette ends, reed fluff, brick grit, hair, lime crust, mosquito larvae, oil-sheen, rain. I would live where everything passes from street to under-map.

Not a monster. Not a god. More like a custodian made of listening.

I would have no single body. I’d be the quick dark flicker below a loading platform when someone crosses above. The hollow cough from a drain when air gets trapped behind rising water. The tremble of algae against a screen before it clogs. The faint metallic tick that tells a night worker to pause, turn back, and check the south intake one more time.

The people might not name me. Or if they did, it would be practical and half-joking.

“Grate-mind’s hungry today.”

“Don’t drop that wrench or the under-thing’ll keep it.”

“Screen’s talking. Better clear it.”

Children would know me best, because children are always negotiating with openings. They would crouch over gutters after rain, racing bottle caps, and sometimes one cap would stop against no visible obstruction, shiver, then shoot ahead at exactly the wrong moment to win. They would blame me, delighted and uneasy. They’d feed me things they were told not to waste: crumbs, bent nails, bits of colored glass, dead mosquitoes in jars poured solemnly through the slots.

The old people would pretend not to believe in me, but they would respect me in the old basin way, which means leaving me alone unless I made trouble.

I would not speak in words. I’d answer through maintenance.

A screen fouls early, before the storm fully arrives.

A grate gives a sharper ring under a boot, warning that the frame has loosened.

A lost key appears in the mud beside a culvert three days after it vanished.

A bad smell gathers in one alley until someone opens the right cleanout and finds the blockage before water backs into the kitchens.

I would be fond of those who notice small things. The woman with the hooked pole clearing algae at first light. The boy sent for gasket rubber who takes the long way along the canal because he likes the herons. The mechanic who can hear a bearing fail through brick. The child who lifts a frog from an oil-rimmed puddle and carries it to the ditch without making a ceremony of mercy.

I would dislike sealed things. Perfect new valves. Smooth plastic pipe. Fresh concrete poured without weep holes. Officials from somewhere drier who say “modernization” and mean forgetting where the water wants to go. I would not sabotage them dramatically. I would just make their confidence expensive. A persistent sweating seam. A gauge that sticks until tapped. A clean new drainage run that whistles at night because no one bothered to ask the ground what it remembered.

My home-place would be a particular junction beneath the oldest part of the pump house: where three drains meet, one from the canal yard, one from the workers’ block, one from the boiler shed. There, warm water and rainwater and seepage fold together. Above it, a square grate patched twice with mismatched iron. Around the rim, a white crust of lime. In the corners, black algae. A smell of rust, mud, soap, hot oil, and something green trying to live.

At noon, when the heat is stored inside every object and the lamps still burn yellow in the dimness, I would be almost asleep. Not absent, just slow. The pumps would thud through me like a second heart. Rats would pass through my corridors. Frogs would pulse in the culverts. Mosquitoes would rise from places the people missed. I would feel the basin holding itself barely above drowning, not by triumph but by habit.

At night I would wake more fully.

Night is when systems confess. The workers go quiet, but the pipes continue. Water knocks where it should not. A loose chain taps a post. The canal sucks softly against stone. Dogs bark once into steam-darkness and listen. A heron steps from one reflection into another. Somewhere a pump changes note, only slightly.

That would be my hour: not to command, but to attend.

If someone stood over my grate then — a night watchman, maybe, with a lantern and damp shirt stuck between his shoulders — he might hear something below. Not a voice. More like the basin thinking through its teeth.

He would stop.

Listen.

Then say, “All right. I hear it.”

And go fetch the pole, or the wrench, or the boy who knows where the spare gasket is kept.

That is where I would be: not outside the town, not above it, not even among its houses, but in the low exchanges that let it continue. I would be the under-attention of the basin. The little persistence in the drains. The damp intelligence of “not yet, not too high, clear this, lift that, listen again.”

A thing of maintenance.

A thing that does not appear.

A thing that persists.
