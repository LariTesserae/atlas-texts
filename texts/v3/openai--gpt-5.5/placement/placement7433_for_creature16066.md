You would find me where three kinds of passage meet.

Not in the market court, though I would know its shouting. Not at the gate, though I would feel every stranger enter by the change in air. Not among the upper cactus flowers, though dawn would redden me briefly when the sun struck the far wall.

You would find me in the angle above a narrow vent, just beneath the fourth ledge, where a sleeping room, an ancestor niche, and a grain passage share one cracked breath of air.

There is a plastered wall there, old enough to have been repaired by hands whose bones are now behind it. The plaster is not smooth. It has hair in it, dung fiber, ash, chalk, seed chaff, and the powdered shells of beetles. A fig root has worried through one corner and been cut back so many times it has become a knob like a knuckle. Below, people pass sideways with baskets against their hips. Above, goats sometimes stamp and knock dust from the ceiling. Through the vent comes the smell of stored grain, old mineral coolness, lamp smoke, rat musk, and the faint, private scent of sealed water.

I would live there as a spider.

One of the large household ones. Not loved, exactly. Too many knees for love. Too still. Too sudden. But named, tolerated, defended by grandmothers.

They would call me something like Ash-Mother, or Door-Leg, or She-Who-Hears-Flour-Fall.

I would be the color of the city’s dimness: gray-brown, dust-banded, with joints like thorn shadows and a belly marked faintly as if someone had pressed a thumb of soot there and wiped it away. By day I would flatten myself in the corner where the plaster meets the old stone, so still that children would point me out to visitors who could not see me. By night I would lower myself into the warm moving air and mend what the day had torn.

My web would not be beautiful in the lowland way. No jeweled wheel hung with dew. There is no dew here to spend on prettiness.

Mine would be a working web.

A slanting, patched, practical thing stretched between a roof beam, a cracked jar lip set into the wall, the fig-root knob, and the underside of a smoke-dark shelf. It would be full of the city’s fragments: moth wings, seed husks, pigeon down, ash flakes, goat hair, the blue scale of a lizard’s shed throat, one child’s lost thread bracelet, the brittle leg of a locust, a grandmother’s white hair, dust from the feet of the dead.

Everything that drifted would confess itself to me.

I would know the city by vibration.

The light-footed run of children through the under-route behind the ovens.  
The drag of an old man’s sandal when his knee was bad.  
The double-tap of a water-keeper’s knuckles on a sealed jar.  
The frantic pulse of a moth before it understood the web.  
The long silence of a snake entering the grain room, moving without apology.  
The roof-shudder of goats doing what goats must never do.  
The deep, careful steps of someone carrying hidden weight.

I would know arguments before words rose into them. People think speech begins in the mouth. It begins in the floor. In heel pressure. In the way a basket is set down too hard, but not hard enough to endanger its contents. In the pause before a name is used.

From my corner I would witness the household as a knot.

Below me, a woman would sleep beside two children and a clay jar no one mentioned. Behind the plaster, her mother’s mother would sit in dry patience, wrapped and powdered, still owning part of the wall. In the grain passage, rats would make their small treaties and betray them. In the vent, air would move according to laws older than the roof. A vine root would thicken through the year, negotiating with stone by pressure alone.

I would be part of that agreement.

No one would ask whether I belonged. I would belong by usefulness, by persistence, by having been there before the youngest child was named. I would take flies before they reached the baby’s eyes. I would take moths before they laid eggs in the stored cloth. I would take the tiny pale insects that come when grain has been kept one season too long. I would leave the ants alone unless they came foolishly into my corner. I would avoid the geckos. I would respect the owls. I would fear the children only when they were bored.

Once, a boy would try to kill me with a reed because another boy dared him.

His grandmother would strike him—not hard, but sharply, with two fingers behind the ear—and say, “Do you want flies in your mouth while you sleep? Do you want your little sister’s eyes crawling? Apologize.”

He would mutter something upward, not meaning it.

I would not forgive him. Forgiveness is a mammal habit. But I would continue my work.

During locust nights I would grow fat.

The city would become percussion: baskets, hides, clay pans, shouting, wings beating the air into panic. Smoke would seep through the passages. Children would squeal and cough. Locust bodies would strike my web like thrown seeds. I would bite and wrap until my legs ached with abundance. Disaster and feast would be the same event, as they so often are in the cleft.

During rare rain I would retreat deep into the wall.

The first drops would terrify me. Not because water is unknown—I carry its rumor in every body I eat—but because water loose in the world is violence. Plaster softens. Dust becomes weight. Channels remember. Things sealed become open. Things buried speak.

People would rush below me with jars, cloths, curses, laughter held tight in their throats. The ancestor wall would darken in patches. A rat tunnel would collapse. Frogs would call from the impossible lower mud, and their voices would enter my legs through stone: broken flutes, yes, but also little wet warnings from another age.

I would survive by climbing behind the jar lip, where the old clay keeps an air pocket. When the rain ended and the city began to dry back into itself, I would emerge thinner, offended, alive.

I would not be wise in the human way. I would not understand lineage law, though I would sit above its arguments. I would not know the names of the dead, though their niches would steady my wall. I would not know why water must not be spoken of plainly, though I would know every creature is a sealed vessel until opened.

But I would understand this place by appetite and tension.

I would understand that nothing here is alone.

A moth is not only a moth. It is stored leaf, night air, gecko hunger, my meal, egg-silk, child’s cough prevented.  
A strand of hair is not only hair. It is rope someday, or nest lining, or web burden, or proof that someone passed in secret.  
A cracked wall is not only failure. It is root path, insect road, vent, danger, shrine, repair-work, memory.  
A dead thing is not finished. It is tool, warning, food, pigment, story, debt.

I would be a small registrar of the city’s drift.

Not an official one. No water-keeper would consult me. No elder would ask my judgment. Yet my web would keep the soft evidence of everyone: who passed carrying flour, who shook out a sleeping mat, which bird nested too near the vent, when the goat got loose, when the fever room burned bitter leaves all night, when a new child’s naming dust was brushed from old steps and rose into the air.

Perhaps, after years, my web would become so familiar that people would duck around it without seeing. That is one way to become sacred in a practical place: to be useful long enough that your presence becomes part of the architecture.

Eventually I would die.

Likely a gecko would take me. Or an egg-heavy daughter of mine would eat what was left. Or I would grow slow and dry in my own corner, my legs folding inward like old thorn twigs.

No one would mourn. Not exactly.

But someone would notice the flies.

A grandmother would look up from scraping a bowl with ash and say, “The corner is empty.”

A child would ask if he might clear the old web.

“No,” she would say. “Leave it. Another will come.”

And another would.

She would find the same angle above the vent, beneath the fourth ledge, where sleeping breath, ancestral dust, grain smell, and hidden coolness meet. She would test the air with one lifted leg. She would fasten her first line to the fig-root knob, then to the cracked jar lip, then to the smoke-dark shelf.

The city would enter her through trembling.

And she would begin again.
