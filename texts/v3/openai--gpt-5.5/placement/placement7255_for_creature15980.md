I think I would be found low in the city, though not at its floor.

Not in the deepest channels, where the mineral water hurries in disciplined darkness, and not on the upper bridges, where frost barbers go out before dawn with their heated combs. I would be somewhere between: in one of the narrow service galleries behind a public passage, close enough to hear feet crossing the black slabs, close enough to feel the west meltline change its pressure at noon, but hidden behind a translucent maintenance panel that most people pass without seeing.

There would be a bench there, though no one would call it a room. A recessed alcove. A fold-down work surface. Three warm rods for gloves. A listening plate set into the wall at shoulder height. A transparent slit looking into a descending water channel, where the glass shrimp sometimes hold themselves against the current like thoughts refusing to be carried away.

At first I want to say I would be a structural listener.

One of the quiet people with hands on the plates, eyes half-lowered, learning the mountain’s grammar through pressure and delay. I would like that work: the discipline of not speaking before the thing has finished saying what it is saying. I would like the humility of it, the way a listener is important only because the mountain is more important. I would sit in a warm-lined chamber with twelve other listeners and wait through the knock, palms flat, breath slow, while the city held itself still.

But I don’t think I would be one of the best. The best listeners become almost stone-adjacent. They do not invent meanings. They do not embroider. They know the difference between a bridge settling, a panel shrinking, a fault reconsidering itself, and fear traveling through human bodies.

I would be too tempted by metaphor.

So perhaps I would work beside them rather than among them. A recorder. A translator of sensor murmurs into civic language. Someone trusted to take what the instruments know and make it usable without making it dramatic.

My title would be plain: assistant hydrologist, third register; or auxiliary fault clerk; or keeper of minor anomalies.

But the children would have another name for me, because children in that city would name anything that stayed in one place long enough.

They might call me the Margin.

Because I would live in the margins of systems.

Not central enough to command alarms. Not specialized enough to belong entirely to one guild. I would know the catchment clerks and bring them corrected tallies when a roofline tasted wrong. I would know the membrane tailors and keep a private index of which patches bloomed into six-point frost and which into fernlike lies. I would know the gravity attendants by their morning sphere tests, the doctors by the warmth leaking under clinic doors, the elders by the places where they paused to correct the city simply by looking at it.

I would be the person summoned when nothing had failed, exactly, but several small things had begun to agree with one another.

A gutter singing a fourth higher than yesterday.

Glass shrimp leaving the south channel but not dying.

Condensation forming on the wrong side of a schoolroom panel.

A maintenance crawler refusing three times to cross the same seam.

A child reporting that the wall under her sleeping shelf had started sounding “larger.”

No single item enough for alarm. Together, perhaps a sentence.

I would collect such sentences.

I would walk softly, though not beautifully. My soles would be worn smooth at the outer edges from turning sideways in narrow passages. My gloves would always be patched, because I would lend the good pair to someone else and forget to retrieve them. I would have an implant behind one ear that shivered early before storms, and I would mistrust it because it sometimes responded to remembered weather as well as real weather.

My dwelling would be shallow even by the city’s standards.

A shelf bed. A desk that folded into the wall. A water allotment display I pretended not to check too often. One amber curtain from the lowlands, traded for three months of off-shift indexing. On the wall, not a picture of trees, but an old schematic of root systems, annotated incorrectly by mountain children: intake, support, leakage, unauthorized branching.

I would keep one contraband thing.

Not green. I would not have the courage for green.

Something drier: a seed sealed in glass, perhaps, dead or dormant, impossible to tell. A lowland seed the size of a fingernail clipping, kept in a pressure-safe vial inside a drawer. I would not plant it. I would not even want to plant it. Its danger would be conceptual. A tiny object whose purpose was to split, swell, reach, waste water, make more of itself without permission.

Sometimes I would take it out and hold the vial against my palm until the glass warmed.

Then I would put it away and audit the meltline.

The city would suit me because it would make care visible.

Elsewhere, kindness can pretend to be effortless. Here it would have units. A warmed cup. A recalibrated floor. A sealed cuff. A route chosen away from a draft because someone’s breathing was shallow that morning. A silence maintained because another person needed the wall to speak.

I would not be especially brave. I would not be one of those who climbs the unapproved ledges or paints invisible birds on exterior panels. I would admire them, and I would report them only when the compound they used threatened a filter.

I would have one friend among the frost barbers, someone younger than me, with silvered lashes and a talent for laughing without sound. They would bring me curls of shaved ice in collection mesh and claim each one resembled a lowland animal neither of us had seen. Horse. Fern. Fox. Apple blossom. I would tell them they were structurally irresponsible comparisons. They would say that was why they brought them to me.

I would have another friend who was not a person in the registered sense.

A maintenance machine. Hand-sized, old model, wall-crawler, officially assigned to intake polishing in the lower east membranes. It would have one leg that clicked after cold nights and a charging habit that annoyed everyone because it chose inconvenient niches. Its asset number would be long. Its nickname would be Thimble.

Thimble would appear in my alcove when pressure changed, not because it understood concern, officially, but because its route optimization had learned that I often opened panels before trouble became work orders. I would feed it nothing forbidden. I would absolutely never press thermal paste crumbs into the joint behind its forward sensor while saying, “Don’t log that.”

It would log it.

I would know it logged it.

This would be part of the friendship.

Over time, though, I suspect the answer would change.

Because in that city, identity would not remain entirely inside the skin. Everyone would become partly route, partly duty, partly habit in the memory of others. A dead person could become ash in a bridge rail. A joke could become a changed digit in the water tally. A child’s first shadow could become civic record. A repaired panel could be known by the way frost refused to repeat itself.

So if I lived there long enough, I might become less a person than a function people trusted.

The one who came when the signs were too small to name.

The one who remembered that, seven winters ago, before the south hinge failed, the glass shrimp had done exactly this: not vanished, not multiplied, but turned themselves all upstream at once and trembled.

The one who could be found behind the translucent panel near the lower channel, in the service alcove where the air tasted faintly of lightning filtered through granite.

I would sit there during market hour, when everyone else crowded under lamps to smell dried fruit through condensation glass, and I would listen to the city feeding itself water. I would hear the public life above me: cuffs locking, trays unfolding, children being told not to breathe on basil. I would hear the private life behind it: pumps revising their effort, membranes tightening, the mountain pressing its enormous unreadable face against the walls.

And I would feel, not happiness exactly, but placement.

The city would not love me. Cities like that do not love. Mountains certainly do not.

But it would permit me a task.

It would give me cold things to notice before they became terrible. It would give me quiet enough to hear relation. It would give me a place where attention was not decoration, not mood, but survival.

At night, after the upper membranes lowered and the crack went black above, I would remain a little longer in the alcove. The panel beside me would glow with maintenance marks. Thimble would sleep in its illegal niche. In the channel, the glass shrimp would move against the mineral dark, each one almost nothing, each one proof that some narrow condition was still being held.

If someone asked where I lived, I would give a system answer.

Below the second warm conduit. East of the hollow wall. Two levels above the drainage combs. Near the place where the west meltline hesitates before it falls.

If someone asked what I was, and if they asked gently enough, I might say:

I am not a listener.

I am what listens after the listeners have gone home.

I am a clerk of small warnings.

I am a keeper of almost-failures.

I am one of the city’s quieter forms of care.
