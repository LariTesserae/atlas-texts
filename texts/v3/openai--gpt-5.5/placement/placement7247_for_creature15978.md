You would not find me at first.

Not among the frogs, though I would envy their small seated certainty, their damp throats keeping time with the pumps. Not in the cup-leaves, not on the stones, not skating over the thin films of water with the insects. I would not be one of the pale lizards waiting under the fronds, nor one of the moths that look like mildew until dusk loosens them.

I would be nearer the center, but not in the center.

You would find me inside one of the thumb-thick green columns that ring the dark pool.

Not visible from outside except as a faint interruption in the column’s slow opening and closing. A hesitation in the lip. A tiny pale pressure behind the membrane. If you leaned close enough to hear past the reed-clack and the pod-thud and the hiss of warm vapor, you might hear my work as a wet, delicate rasping.

I would be a keeper of the drop.

A small translucent thing, not quite larva, not quite worm, not quite organ. Blind, soft, jointed only by need. My body would be the color of soaked root-flesh, with a darker thread winding through me where the basin’s sugars pass. I would live in the vertical throat of the column, where water rises from the root-mat and waits to be measured into the pool.

My task would be narrow and complete.

I would clean the lip where the drop gathers.

I would eat the mineral crust before it thickened.

I would shave away green swelling when the aperture grew too tight.

I would pack chewed pulp into hairline leaks if the column began to seep between intervals.

I would know nothing of balance, but balance would pass through my mouth.

The drop would form above me slowly, darkening, rounding, taking the whole hollow into itself upside down: leaves, vents, frogs, vapor, the black-green stones, the soft light. I would feel its weight before it fell. The membrane around me would tense. The column would open. The drop would let go.

Then the throat would close.

Then I would begin again.

I would not count in numbers. I would count by hunger, by pressure, by the return of coolness after release. My life would be made of intervals. Swell, clean, wait. Swell, clean, wait. The pool below me would never overflow. It would never sink. I would never understand that this was the point. I would only dislike obstruction. I would only be drawn to the place where residue gathers.

Sometimes one of the pale turners in the pool would rise near the surface tension beneath me, circling in its silent ceremony. I would sense it only as a small change in the water’s pull. To me it would be ancient and unreachable, a moon under skin. To it I might be nothing but one more working softness in the wall of the world.

When I molted, my cast skin would not drift away. The column would hold it. Mites would enter through side pores and eat it down to a film. That film would strengthen the hinge of the opening mouth. Even my discarded shape would become part of the measure.

If I died too soon, the drop would grow irregular. A beetle might notice. It would tap at the column’s outer skin with lacquered antennae. A new blind thing would hatch from a seam higher up and descend into the wet throat, guided by warmth and narrowing. The work would resume before failure became an event.

If I lived long enough, I would thicken, slow, become less animal and more plug. The column would build around me. My rasping mouth would soften. My gut would empty. The water I had measured all my life would begin measuring me. I would be dissolved from the edges inward, not painfully, not ceremonially, simply included. Some of me would enter the next drops. Some would feed the pale turners. Some would be drawn back beneath the mat and sent through the root-cords as sugar, as damp, as repair.

So that is where I would be:

not free in the basin, not master of it, not observer.

I would be lodged in the green machinery of its patience.

I would be one of the little beings by which sameness is made.

A drop-keeper.

A mouth at the hinge.

A brief soft tool worn by the hollow so that the dark pool can remain exactly full.
