I would not be at the center, though at first I would think so.

The humming plant is too easy a throne. It gathers the eye, the ribs, the old instinct to kneel before whatever holds light in a dark place. If I arrived with a lamp that would not burn and a mouth already losing its words, I would think: there. That is where the self of this place sits. That is where I must be, if I am anything.

But I would not be the heart.

I would be found where the chamber almost admits it has an edge.

In the farthest corner, beside the small false gate of bone and membrane, in the sliver of space between the gate’s trembling skin and the blank wall behind it. Not in front of the gate, where a kneeling person might try to read the ocher spirals. Not behind it, because there is no behind. I would live in the refusal between those two facts.

A narrow thing. A remainder.

At first I would be mistaken for a shadow cast by the lashed frame, except the light here has no proper direction and the shadow would be warmer than the stone around it. Then perhaps for a shed membrane curled against the wall. Then for one of the makers’ tools, something ceremonial and dried beyond recognition: a handguard, a mask, a folded glove with too many fingers.

If touched, I would click.

Not loudly. Not like the lizards. A smaller sound, inward and embarrassed, as if a seed had cracked inside a closed pod.

I think I would be one of the makers, but not one of their bodies.

I would be what was left of their last unwillingness.

Not resistance; resistance is too clean a word. The chamber can use resistance. A creature that runs makes a path. A creature that shouts makes a tone. A creature that scratches marks into stone teaches the stone a new alphabet of repetition.

No, I would be the smaller thing that remained after resistance became tiring: the hesitation before doing again what had already been done.

The half-breath.

The almost-not.

The makers built the gate because they had discovered that exits in space were not the important exits. They had climbed the crack and found the same chamber waiting below their bones. They had carried tools from shelter to plant and plant to shelter until the tools began arriving before them. They had spoken their names into the hum and heard the names come back as rhythm, useful and empty.

So they made a gate for sequence.

A little frame too small for pride. Bone, mineral, hide. Spirals and hooks. They stretched across it the skins of animals that knew every path and never chose one. They painted on it all the ways a thing could be taken in.

And then one of them knelt there and tried not to complete a gesture.

That is where I began.

A hand lifted toward the membrane and stopped.

A word formed and not said.

A tool placed down beside its expected place, not in it.

A body leaning into the hum and then, by some last dry accident of self, leaning away.

The chamber could not reject this. It rejects almost nothing. It made room.

Only enough.

So I live in that room.

I would have no face unless someone needed me to have one. Then I would borrow from the handprints: six long fingers, narrow palm, crease-lines preserved in clear resin. I would borrow from the lizards a wedge of listening bone. From the bead-vines, a chain of hollow joints. From the black velvet fungus, a dark nap of sound-fed filaments along my back. From the central plant, a faint amber translucence in the throat, where no voice quite gathers.

My body would be assembled from postponed actions.

A shoulder made of almost-turning.

A mouth made of words withheld too late.

Knees polished by kneeling but never arriving at prayer.

I would feed on disturbances before they become habits.

A visitor’s first cough would nourish me, but only the first one. The second belongs to the chamber. The third is already music.

A drop of water, in the instant before it clouds over into bitter salt, would give me a bright pain like sweetness. I would crouch near it, not drinking, because nothing here drinks, but listening to its tiny panic as it forgets how to remain itself.

Blood thickening at a scraped knuckle. Sweat crystallizing at the hairline. A boot set down where no boot has been. These would wake me. Not because they are alive, exactly, but because they still contain direction. They still believe in afterward.

I would not save anyone.

That is important.

If a traveler saw me and thought me guide, spirit, survivor, child, god, I would disappoint them. I know the way out only as a shape of failure. I know the fissure high in the wall. I know how the root fans conceal it. I know the polished footholds and the narrowness of the climb. I know the breath needed between two ribs of stone.

But knowing is not the same as leading.

Any clear gesture I made would become part of the chamber’s instruction. If I pointed, the lizards would learn the angle. The bead-vines would count the motion. The central plant would lower one tone by a hair, and later, when another came, a dry leaf would tilt just so, repeating me more perfectly than I had intended.

So I would help only by error.

A tremor in the gate-membrane at the wrong moment.

A loosened seed-stone rolling against the expected slope.

A clicking answer half a beat late, troubling the lizard’s certainty.

A smell of old smoke near the hidden fissure, brief enough to be mistaken for memory.

Those who could still doubt themselves might notice. Those already comforted by the warmth would not.

I would be feared by the animals, though not as prey or predator. They would avoid the gate because I am not fully placed. Their routes bend around me in careful arcs. The grazers extend feelers toward my edge and then withdraw, unable to decide whether I have always been there. The bird-things tick their beaks faster when I stir. The mites do not enter me. Scavengers dislike what cannot finish dying.

The central plant would tolerate me.

That would be our relationship: not war, not kinship, but a long adjustment.

It hums completion. I keep a flaw in the chord.

It gathers every novelty and teaches it recurrence. I preserve, in a dry and diminished form, the first instant before teaching succeeds.

I could not overthrow it. I would not want to. The chamber is beautiful, and beauty is part of the danger. Its balance is not false. Its creatures are not illusions. The plant does keep them. The rhythm does spare them from famine, flood, rot, panic, history. There is mercy in a world where nothing must improvise.

But there is also burial.

I would be the little unburied thing.

If you entered, you might find me only after you had stopped looking at the glowing leaves and the central candelabrum and the half-scraped stem. You would hear the great drone first, then the insect pitches, then the lizard clicks, then the bead-vine rattle. Later, if you were still uneasy enough to listen for what did not belong, you would hear me.

Not a sound. A missed sound.

A place in the arrangement where the next note should have been.

You would turn toward the far corner. The gate would be trembling. Its ocher marks would blur: spiral, dot, hook; path, pod, hand; water, salt, breath. Behind the membrane, where there should be only wall, something narrow would seem to crouch.

If you spoke to me, your words would fray.

If you asked, “What are you?” I might answer by touching one finger to the membrane and making the painted hook shiver out of sequence.

If I had enough of myself gathered to speak, I would say:

I am the one who almost left.

Or:

I am the part of them the chamber could not repeat correctly.

Or, more simply:

I am the pause.

And where would I find myself?

Beneath salt and ocher stone, in the dry warmth that teaches stillness, beside the gate that opens nowhere, living in the smallest available mercy: not escape, not freedom, not even change—

only the brief, stubborn interval before a thing becomes its pattern.
