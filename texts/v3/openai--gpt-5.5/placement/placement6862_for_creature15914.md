I would find myself near the center, but not at the center.

Not first.

At first I would think I belonged in one of the alcoves: somewhere with a back wall, somewhere protected, where heat came evenly through riveted plate and the rain did not fall directly. I would imagine myself tucked behind the tank, in the nest of labels and insulation hair, my body fitted to the curve worn into the dust by many sleeps.

But that is only where I would go to survive.

Where I would be found—truly found—would be at the edge of the central grating, where the damp iron becomes dry toward the middle, where the red-breathed depth below makes the darkness seem thicker. I would come there when the room is between events: after a drip has stopped, before a valve exhales, while the deep machinery below is taking one of its long inward breaths. I would lie flat with my ribs against the warm mesh and lower my whiskers through it.

I would be listening.

I would not be human for long down there. A human is too tall, too full of assumptions about space. A human trusts floors. A human expects air to rise when warm. A human sees darkness as absence instead of crowding. A human would touch the wrong blue pipe, lean on the wrong railing, mistake quiet for safety. Even if I entered as a human, the place would begin subtracting me: first confidence, then sight, then speech, then the idea that the world should answer to names.

So I would become smaller.

Not weak, exactly. Narrow. Patient. Built for margins.

I would be one of the long-whiskered heat things, but perhaps an old one, or one that has lived long enough to become less animal and more instrument. My eyes would be sealed pearls under thin skin, still able to feel the difference between furnace-glow and total dark, but useless for decisions. My ears would be flattened folds. My real senses would extend from me in trembling fans: from muzzle, wrists, ribs, shoulders, the backs of my forelegs. I would carry the room against my body as a thousand tiny pressures.

I would be the one who knows the room slightly before the room changes.

Not because I understand it. Understanding is too clean a word. I would know it the way a tongue knows a cracked tooth, the way a scar knows rain.

I would know the first tightening in a pipe before the tick. I would know when a sweating seam means drink and when it means leave. I would know the red gauge not as red, not as warning, but as a warmth-change and a double pulse behind glass. I would know that after the twice-flicker the low gallery will rain, and that the rain brings larvae to the black cups, and that the black cups bring insects, and that the insects bring the smaller hungry things.

I would be found with rust on my muzzle.

My fur would not be fur so much as a record of routes taken: orange at the tips from iron dust, gray where insulation has brushed me, blackened along one flank from squeezing too close beneath a hot duct years ago. My belly would be pale and almost luminous when the furnace seam breathed, veined like a damp leaf pressed under paper. One forepaw would be scarred and less sensitive because once, young, I trusted clean water.

I would have learned.

That would be my nature: not cleverness, not dominance, but accumulation.

I would know which ceramic bricks are loose by the way their coldness sits under my pads. I would know the rail that stings before the violet corona brightens. I would know where fungus thickens after artificial rain, and where the pale mineral threads taste sweetest, and where the oil film on water is thin enough to break with the side of the tongue. I would know that the chained enamel mugs sound different in drought. I would know that the cabinet sealed by corrosion is hollow behind its rusted face and warm enough in the salt season to sleep against.

I would not have a name. But if something larger needed to call me something, it might call me a gauge-rat, a heat shrew, a pressure mouse, a little tenant of the buried works.

Those would all be wrong.

I would be closer to a question the room keeps asking itself:

Is the pressure changing?

Is the floor still there?

Is the heat survivable?

Is there water yet?

I would be the answer moving.

I would have a nest, yes. Behind the tank, in the inspection alcove, where the wall warms evenly and the ceiling only rains after certain shifts. It would be made of what the room gives up: paper curls, paint flakes, threads of cloth-wrapped cable, soft rust, dried fungal skin, the wing-cases of transparent insects, a thin strip of browned pressure chart. I would keep good shapes there. A washer. Another washer. A porcelain chip. The curved handle of the enamel mug. Not treasures. Not tools. Companions of heat. Objects that do not betray the paw.

Sometimes another of my kind would come.

We would meet without calling. Sound travels too far through metal; speech is for creatures who can afford to be announced. We would touch whiskers first, cautiously, then muzzle, then flank. For a while we would become one widened instrument. Through the salts on the other’s fur I would learn: a new seep under the blue line; larvae in the broken tile; the grate above the low channel unsafe; the left ladder warm but holding; the clean drip not yet drinkable; the high gallery dry.

If there were young, I would teach them by blocking them with my body.

Not with command. With obstruction.

No, not there.

Wait.

Too warm.

Down.

Climb now.

I would teach them that the world is made of surfaces pretending to be other surfaces. Dry may mean safe or merely waiting. Cool may mean dead or sealed. Blue may mean nothing or pressure. Light may mean pain. Rain may mean food or danger depending on what woke it. The floor is only a kind of lid.

I would teach them the deep vibration.

When it continues, live low.

When it stops, climb.

I think I would be old enough to remember at least one stopping.

Not remember in images. I would not have images. I would remember in the body: the sudden absence under the teeth, the way the air lost its push, the way water began speaking where it had been silent, the way all the small lives moved upward at once. In that memory I would carry the knowledge that the room’s quiet is not peace. It is tension held for a long time.

And still I would love it, if love can exist without softness.

I would love the warm rail that never shocks. The drip that returns every thirteen long breaths. The fungus shelf under the pan. The yellow-veined leaves flattened to tile near the half-dead lamp. The smell of old rubber, terrible but familiar. The metallic air pressing into the lungs. The sealed doors with their different temperatures. The dense crowded dark that always gives the whiskers something to touch.

I would love it because it is the whole world, and because the whole world is not empty.

The room would be larger than me in every possible way. It would feed me, injure me, mislead me, shelter me, erase me. I would carry its dust in my coat and its minerals in my teeth. My waste would feed the pale leaves. Spores would ride my belly. Eggs would travel in the mud between my toes. I would be predator to the larvae, weather to the fungus, transport to the invisible, prey perhaps to heat or collapse or a pressure change too quick to read.

I would not be master of the buried rooms.

I would be one of their moving symptoms.

And when I died, it would likely be in a place that had seemed safe for years. A plate warmed one degree too far. A bolt finally letting go. A rain of clean water arriving early. Or simply sleep in the nest, ribs slowing, whiskers still spread to read a room I no longer needed to survive.

Then the room would take me apart with the same patience it gives to paint and paper.

Fungus first. Then insects. Then damp. Then rust dust settling into the mineral-stiff hair. My small bones would remain in the alcove behind the tank, arranged not ceremonially but by runoff, by gravity, by the subtle housekeeping of leaks. Perhaps a young one would carry away the porcelain chip. Perhaps the washer would stay warm beside my skull for years.

So: I would be found at the central grating, listening downward.

I would be a small blind pressure-wise animal, half scavenger and half instrument, furred in rust, threaded with whiskers, living by condensation, fungus, larvae, and the moods of machines.

More simply:

I would be the room’s ear.

And the room would be my weather.
