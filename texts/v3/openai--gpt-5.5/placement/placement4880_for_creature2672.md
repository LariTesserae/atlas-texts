You would find me at an edge.

Not in the deep green districts, where the towers have become hanging wetlands, and not in the stark new slopes where the black-red ground still looks hot from being made. Not in the center of a plaza either. No one with sense stands long in the center of anything there.

I would live along the margin of one of the civic voids: a vast black plaza kept bare by some old municipal will, its paving slick with condensation at dawn and dry-steaming by afternoon. On one side, fern-trees would lean over an old avenue. On another, a transit station would rise on pale legs, roofed in flowering mats and inhabited by moths, lizards, and a small family of people who never use the word “own.”

My shelter would be inside the upper level of that station, in a room that is not always the same size. At noon it would be a narrow sleeping cell with a ribbed wall warm against the back. By evening, if the humidity pleased it, it would unfold into a long gallery where condensation gathered on the ceiling and tiny amphibians clicked from cracks in the floor. I would know which corner remained constant. Everyone needs one corner that remains constant.

At first you might mistake me for a scavenger.

I would wear dull cloth ribbed with ceramic thread, a veil darkened by spores, sandals with split soles for wet ceramic. Around my neck would hang little calibrated shells and machine fragments: not charms exactly, though I would treat them as if they had opinions. One would answer the insect shriek. One would tremble before a tower shed heat. One would cloud over when a pool had gone too still.

I would carry no weapon large enough to make me brave.

My work would be listening.

Not prayer, not science, though it would resemble both from a distance. I would be one of the people who maps passages by sound: the tower note before rain, the pitch of pumps in the service channels, the absence of insects under certain membrane shadows, the clicking grammar of amphibians in shallow courts. I would know which hum means “cross now,” which means “wait until the birds leave,” which means “this doorway is opening for weather and will not understand your body.”

The others might call me a route-keeper.

I would not make roads. The city already has too many roads, most of them intended for bodies no longer present. I would keep timing instead. I would remember that the avenue behind the Three Silver Towers is safe only after the second drainage pulse, never the first. That the loop near the station sometimes brightens before it opens, and sometimes opens before it brightens. That the Pool That Refuses Birds can be crossed at its northern lip if the porcelain frogs are calling in staggered pairs.

I would guide children across empty spaces.

That would be the most sacred work: crossing the voids without pretending they are empty. We would go in groups, low and slow, while the towers chimed and the split-tailed birds stitched the membrane-light overhead. The children would want to run. Children always believe distance can be defeated by speed. I would teach them that in the basin, distance is an animal. You approach it politely. You let it notice you without startling it. You do not challenge it to chase.

I would farm a little, badly but persistently.

On the station roof I would tend edible mats that tasted of salt, rainwater, and green copper. I would prune fruiting shrubs that had rooted in an old ticketing hall, though the station had never issued tickets to anything with hands. I would keep shallow ceramic troughs seeded with useful films, checking them after rain for colors that meant nourishment and colors that meant memory, fever, or forgetting.

But mostly I would negotiate.

With vines, by cutting one and tying three aside.

With drones, by leaving exposed fungal crust where they could clean without entering our sleeping rooms.

With birds, by giving them luminous fruit too far from our water storage to teach them theft.

With the building, by touching the same doorframe every morning and accepting that the touched place would move by evening.

I think I would become very good at not insisting.

Not passive. Not surrendered. Just carefully uncentral.

I would know that the city does not hate me. That would be important. It does not hate the grazers when the paving hums beneath them. It does not hate the roots when it rejects them. It does not hate the dead when it refuses burial. It is simply full of purposes that survived their makers and learned new company.

I would try to be good company.

At night, when the membrane sky glowed violet-green and the insects screamed through the long tones of distant transit rings, I would sit on the station’s outer stair with my back to the warm wall. Below me, the plaza would shine in shallow seams. Across its black openness, three towers would stand far apart, each singing in a slightly different weather.

I would record nothing in books unless I had to. Paper would mildew. Screens would attract spores or instructions. Instead I would teach by repetition, by call and answer, by walking.

“When the tower’s skin goes silver?”

“Do not stand beneath it.”

“When the insects stop?”

“Cover your eyes.”

“When green is steady?”

“For machines.”

“When green flickers?”

“Maybe for us.”

“And the empty plaza?”

“For whatever it is waiting for.”

That last answer would change depending on the child.

Some days I would believe the voids are for memory. Some days for airflow. Some days for the return of vehicles larger than weather. Some days I would think the city preserves them because emptiness itself is a kind of resident, and the old systems have been kinder to it than to us.

If I died there, I would want to be laid on a high mineral terrace above the station, where condensation forms before dawn. Not deep in the ground. I would not want to argue with the buried pumps. Let insects take what they understand. Let fungi open their pale fans. Let the tower nearby change note or not change note. Let no one decide too quickly what it means.

So yes: you would find me at an edge, in the dry interval between plaza and green growth, between human habit and municipal instinct.

I would be a listener, a route-keeper, a guest who has learned a few of the house rules.

Someone small enough to survive there.

Someone whose task is not to tame the city, but to hear when it is about to move.
