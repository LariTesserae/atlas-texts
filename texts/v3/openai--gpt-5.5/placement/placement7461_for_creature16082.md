I would find myself where a fallen termite tower leans into a thornwood thicket and has not yet finished becoming either ruin or hill.

Not in the open basin. Not on the bright flats, where the salt skin lifts in thin plates and every shadow is too honest. I would be in the seam: half under clay, half under roots, in the cool wedge made by accident and immediately claimed by life. Above me, the broken tower would still breathe through old vents. Thorn branches would have grown through its side and stitched it to the living hedge. A bottle-stem shrub would have rooted in one collapsed chamber, its swollen base pressing against the insect clay like a shoulder against a wall. Dry leaves would gather there because the wind cannot quite reach in. Beetle husks, shed lizard skins, dropped seeds, pellets of dung, papery bark, and pale dust would collect layer by layer until the place had a floor.

A small plaza, if one were small enough.

That is where I would live: in the undercroft of a structure nobody meant to build.

At first I think I might be one of the flat lizards on the sunward face, all stillness and suddenness, body matched to clay, eye turned upward toward the hook-beaked birds. Or one of the glass geckos that appears at evening like a thought the tower has after dark. Or even a moth, brief and pale, carrying dust between flowers that open only when the heat loosens its grip.

But I am not light enough for that. I belong lower, nearer the paths.

I think I would be a seed-mouse.

Not a grand animal. Not a maker in any obvious sense. A small, long-footed, dust-colored thing with ears too large for my head and black eyes that hold every motion. My back would be the color of shaded mineral soil; my belly pale as the underside of a leaf. My tail would lift when I run so it does not drag through hot powder. My whiskers would know the width of every passage before my shoulders enter it.

I would come out at dusk, when the city exhales.

All afternoon I would sleep inside a chamber that was once made for termites, then abandoned, then widened by roots, then entered by beetles, then found by my mother or her mother. The walls would be hard, faintly warm, tasting of salt and old saliva. Around me: stored seeds in several side pockets; dry grass cut into lengths; a lining of shredded bark; the musk of my siblings; the barely audible work of insects beyond the wall.

When evening comes, I would wake before I move.

First the ears. One toward the thicket, one toward the path. The soft crackle of cooling clay. The click of a gecko somewhere above. A beetle worrying resin from a stem. Farther off, the almost-sound of many small bodies beginning to use the corridors.

Then the nose. Dust. Thorn. Fox. Old fox, not near. Cat-shadow somewhere beyond the mound-field, maybe. Flower opening. Seeds ripening. Dung newly dropped near the browser path. Snake, faint, old, perhaps only skin.

Only then would I go out.

I would not cross space. I would thread it.

From vent to root-shadow. From root-shadow to the underside of a fallen thorn branch. From there to the narrow run polished by many feet between two salt mats. My body would know turns my mind does not name. I would pause where generations have paused: at the lizard wall, at the bitter resin shrub, at the split stone where ants cross in two lines, at the little dust rise from which one can smell both danger and food.

My work would be hunger, but the basin would use it as construction.

I would gather seeds in my cheeks: hard hooked grass seeds, tiny black grains from dusk flowers, crescent pods shaken loose by night wind, oily kernels stolen from beneath a weaver colony. I would carry them home, but not all the way. Fear would interrupt me. A bird’s shadow, though the bird is gone. The dry footfall of a civet-like hunter. A snake’s cooling body across the path.

So I would bury what I carried.

Here, under a shard of insect clay. There, beneath the peeled bark of a dead shrub. Three seeds in the powder beside a root. Five inside an abandoned wasp cell. A mouthful pushed into a crack where salt has lifted the crust. I would remember some caches and forget others. I would survive by forgetting badly enough to feed the future.

In the morning, nothing would show.

In a season, a mat of leaves might appear where I once hid food from a fox. In years, a thorn seedling might rise in the protection of a collapsed mound chamber. Later still, that thorn might catch litter, shelter insects, shade a path, host a nest, snag the fur of passing browsers, and become another wall in the city.

I would never know this.

I would only know that the basin is full of doors and almost all of them are dangerous.

My life would be measured in thresholds: the nest mouth, the shadow edge, the brief crossing, the safe turn, the place where scent changes, the place where a predator must lower its head to follow. I would be a citizen of margins, one of the small warm pulses moving through the dry metropolis after sunset.

I would drink rarely, maybe never from open water. I would take moisture from seeds, from succulent scraps, from the coolness of night air gathered in plant tissue. My body would be economical. My droppings would be dry beads left in corners, later opened by beetles, later softened by fungi, later taken into roots. Even my waste would enter the architecture.

And when I died, it would be quietly.

Perhaps in the jaws of a long cat at the place where the path narrows between thorn and clay. Perhaps under a hook-beak’s foot on a tower ledge. Perhaps old, in the chamber wall, my body drying before it can rot. Ants would find me. Beetles would finish me. Fur would line something’s nest. Bones would be gnawed, then powdered. A seed from one of my forgotten caches might root in the loosened place above me.

So I would not be important.

But I would be necessary in the way small things are necessary there: not as a ruler, not as a builder with a plan, but as a moving intention that leaves structure behind.

I would be a seed-mouse in the broken tower under the thorn roof.

A keeper of misplaced futures.

A road-user whose feet polish the road.

A tiny, frightened gardener of the dry city.
