At first I would think I belonged near the lamps.

Not in them, not as one of the frantic insects spending its whole life in the amber heat, but at the rim of their reach: where light becomes a damp stain on concrete and then gives up. I would be there because that is where the underland explains itself most plainly. The lamp makes the water visible, the water makes the ceiling tremble, the ceiling returns the sound in pieces. Moths gather. Frogs wait. Spiders suspend their patient nets. Fish rise to take what falls. Every hunger has learned the radius of a bulb.

But I would not stay in the open.

I would be under something.

Under a catwalk. Under a pipe. Under the rusted lip of a maintenance platform that has sunk two inches toward the pool. Somewhere close enough to a pump that its warmth comes through the concrete, but not so close that the workers’ boots arrive too often. I would have chosen a district where several kinds of ground meet badly: broken brick, silt film, shallow water, cable bundles, reed stems, a wall of sweating clay braced by old timbers. A place with exits that are not obvious unless your body is long and low.

I think I would be found in the low passage between the pump islands and the reed shafts, where a culvert exhales cooler air and the water carries flecks of grain, beetle wings, and pale shrimp. Not in the grand drowned rooms. Not in the narrow dry galleries where rats own the dark. In the compromise between them.

I would be a sump-jack, but not a young one.

Not the first slick black body in the nest, not one of the foolish half-grown animals that mistakes every gap for a route and every shine for food. I would be one of the older daughters: not yet the center of the group, but already carrying too much map inside me to be careless. Scar on one forepaw from rusted mesh. One ear folded wrong from a fight beneath a ladder. Fur stained along the shoulders where the iron always rubs when I take the pipe-road behind the transformer housing.

I would know several ways through a chamber and trust none of them forever.

My den would be above eye level for humans but below the place where bats sleep: a forgotten cable tray behind a pump cabinet, reachable by a slanted beam, a cracked brick projection, and then a sideways squeeze along warm metal. The nest would not look like a nest until something breathed in it. Reeds dragged up from the shaft mud. Pigeon feathers. Strips of insulation. A blue rubber glove gone stiff with mineral crust. A twist of copper wire holding a sagging side together, not because I knew wire as wire, but because it bent, caught, remembered shape.

I would sleep with my nose tucked beneath my tail while the pumps talked through my ribs.

That would be the deepest part of me: not sight, not thought, but subtraction. The place is too loud to hear all at once. So I would learn the old sounds and live inside them.

The falling water that never stops.

The fan with the bent blade.

The pipe tick that comes in threes when hot runoff enters the west drain.

The chain knocking at long intervals in the clay room.

The frogs making their engine-noise after rain.

The small crackling of a wall that is not yet falling but has begun to imagine it.

I would know when a sound is merely sound, and when it is a door opening under the world.

When the pump coughs twice and then catches, I stay. When it coughs once and the floor trembles differently afterward, I wake the young with my teeth. When the gate-wheel screams in its housing, I leave the flats before the water shows me why. When the insects lift suddenly from a lamp all at once, I freeze, because something with feet has entered the light.

I would move mostly at the margins.

A head above black water.

Whiskers spread.

Forepaws on a submerged stair.

Body lifting without splash.

Pause.

Listen through bone.

Then across the concrete in the shadow of a rail, tail low, belly nearly brushing the wet skin of the floor.

I would not be brave. Bravery is for animals with clean ground under them. I would be exact. I would put one foot where the old female put hers, until the day came when her route ended in new water and mine did not. I would test clay with my weight spread wide. I would distrust smooth silt. I would never cross the open middle of a lamp island unless hunger or flood made the choice for me.

Food would come as the underland gives it: not as prey or carrion or refuse, but as arrival.

A crab climbing the third stair after the pool drops.

A stunned moth pulsing on the water skin.

A rat too confident in a cable tray.

Fish trapped against an intake grille.

Larvae fattened in a warm drain.

A pigeon egg rolled from a nest above the engine room.

A worker’s bread crust, salted with hand-sweat and wrapped in paper.

I would eat what the basin surrendered. I would cache badly and remember well. Half a fish behind a bracket. A rat in a brick crack. A bright strip of reflective tape in the nest because it smells of raincoat, oil, and human warning. Not treasure. Not decoration. Evidence. Texture. Use.

There would be others near me.

The old female first: pale-muzzled, limping, terrible in her patience. She would be the one who knows the chamber before the current chamber, the drowned stair before it drowned, the rail yard before the mud took the sleepers. Her memory would be a kind of weather. When she leaves, we leave. When she refuses a path, the path ceases to exist.

The young would follow too closely and then not closely enough. They would tumble in reed tunnels, bite things that shock them, slap at shrimp, cough at beetles, fall asleep in the wrong places. I would drag them by the scruff when necessary. I would cuff them away from live cable. I would show them the high route behind the warm cabinet and the low crawl under the rusted mesh. I would teach them, without intending to teach, that the underland is made of permissions that expire.

Humans would be weather with tools.

Workers: predictable, boot-heavy, smelling of rubber, metal, tobacco, soap, tiredness. They come with lamps and voices that do not carry properly. They open grilles. They disturb frogs. They leave food. I would watch them from behind pipes with solemn irritation.

Salvagers: sharper, less patterned, smelling of cut metal and risk. Avoid.

Teenagers: loud at first, then quiet with awe. Usually harmless, but stupid in ways that make the ground dangerous around them.

Dogs: disaster.

Cats: negotiable, but never trusted.

If a worker saw me, I would not perform mystery. I would be what I am: wet, low, long-backed, whiskered, interrupted. I would hold still just long enough for his lamp to find the amber-green flash of my eyes. Then I would pour myself through a gap that did not look large enough to take me.

The place I would know best would be a particular crossing.

Every life has one.

Mine would be where the drowned rail line disappears under a low arch and a pipe runs above it, sweating steadily. Below, the water is black and sometimes shallow, sometimes not. To the left is a silt flat written over with rat, crab, frog, and sump-jack marks. To the right is a clay wall that bulges after heavy rain. Above is a cable bundle, dead in two strands, alive in one. Beyond the arch is the warm mineral fan where shrimp gather and fish come nosing at night.

It is a bad place.

That is why it matters.

I would know how to cross it when the pump is running. I would know how to cross it when the pump is silent. I would know the smell of the air before the wall sheds a skin of clay. I would know which rung accepts weight, which pipe burns, which shadow hides a drop. I would have pressed a path into the mud there over many nights, not by command but by repetition. Other bodies would use it after me. Rats. Crabs. Perhaps a cat once, unwillingly. Water, eventually.

That would be my mark: not a monument, not a den, not even offspring, though there may be those too.

A path in undecided ground.

A firmer seam where my body has believed, again and again, that passage is possible.

So yes: I would be a sump-jack, an older daughter of the pump islands, not yet matriarch but already becoming memory. I would live where concrete loses argument with mud, where reeds grow thin and yellow under a broken shaft, where machinery keeps bargaining with water and never quite wins.

I would carry the underland in my fur: rust, algae, clay, oil, frog musk, mineral crust, old rain from the surface world.

And when the pumps changed their rhythm, I would wake before knowing why.
