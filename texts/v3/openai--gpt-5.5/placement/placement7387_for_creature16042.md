You would not find me at the highest towers, though I would know their lights.

You would find me lower than that, but not lowland-low: on one of the middle terraces where the city begins to loosen its grip and the plateau shows through. A district of service roads, old retaining walls, condensation sheds, half-used tram sidings, and the backs of larger things. Not the civic face of the city, not the market, not the great engine halls tourists are shown from protected galleries. I would be where the important failures start small.

There is a ravine there called, informally, the Long Throat. It runs beneath three pipe bridges and an abandoned cableway whose buckets still hang in the air, rusted open like mouths. On one side are the newer pumping annexes, all steel ladders and corrugated siding. On the other are old stone cistern roofs, low and round and grass-whiskered at the seams. Between them stands a whitewashed gauge-house streaked green below the windows.

That is where I would be.

Not inside the heart of the machinery, but beside it. Not in command. In attendance.

I think I would be a seepage clerk.

Not a grand engineer, not a priest, not one of the famous masons whose chalk marks can halt a bridge. Something more minor and more constant: a municipal reader of damp, pressure, cracks, smells, delayed sounds, and things that are almost nothing until they are everything.

My office would be a narrow room above a sump chamber, raised on stone feet and bolted to an older wall that nobody fully trusts but everybody still uses. The room would have two desks: one dry desk for records, one wet desk for objects recently taken from drains. The dry desk would stand absurdly high. The wet desk would be scarred metal, sloped toward a basin. There would be lacquered boxes for papers, tins of chalk, a rack of listening rods, spare rubber cuffs, blue glass vials for sampling potable seep, amber tags for pressure warnings, and a map of the district so corrected and re-corrected that it no longer resembles a map so much as an argument.

My title would sound dull in the municipal register: Assistant Clerk for Return Incidents, Lower West Pressure Ward.

People would shorten it to return clerk.

Children would call me the wall-tapper.

Old women would call me over before they called the ward office.

Engineers would pretend not to need me and then leave folded notes under my door.

I would walk the same routes every day and never find them the same.

At first light, before the big pumps settled into their full work-song, I would go out with a hood over my head and my hook at my belt. I would touch the corner wall of the gauge-house before leaving, because everyone who works near old cisterns develops small superstitions and calls them procedure. The stone there would usually feel cool, but if it felt cool in the wrong way—cool with depth behind it—I would not cross the yard until I had checked the sump.

My boots would know more than my eyes. That is how it would begin.

A tremor through the right sole: upper tram descending.

A soft, unfocused shudder through both heels: south pumps working against load.

A faint hollow knock under the market road: runoff gate not seated cleanly.

A smooth patch on stone where there should be grip: seepage from below, not rain from above.

I would carry chalk in three colors. White for observed moisture. Amber for pressure concern. Blue for clean water or suspected clean water, to keep fools from fouling it before the lab boys arrive. Red chalk would remain wrapped. Red is not for guessing.

Most of my work would be humiliatingly undramatic. That is how useful work survives in such a city.

I would clear leaves from a grate though there are almost no trees. I would scrape mineral crust from a threshold and save the flakes in a labeled envelope because their color had changed since last month. I would stand in a courtyard while a householder complained about mildew and listen instead to the wall behind him. I would kneel beside a stair and smell the water beading along the third step. I would ask rude questions about illegal tanks. I would lift basin covers, count drips, mark times, record which vents sighed before noon.

There would be days when nothing happened except the prevention of a thing nobody would ever thank me for.

Those would be the good days.

I would have one bad ear, like many people there. It would not matter much. I would hear through my fingers. My left palm would be rough from pipe heat and stone grain. My knuckles would be scarred from tapping brick. I would have learned to place my cheek against certain walls, not because it was dignified but because bone knows what air lies about.

I would not be especially brave. This matters. The city would have little use for theatrical courage. It would prefer suspicion, patience, and the willingness to be boring in the rain.

If a plaza began to darken from below, I would not run. I would watch the spread.

Edges first? Center first? Along the paving joints? Around the old fountain seam? Does it smell of iron, lime, rot, clean stone, oil, eggs, cold depth? Are the lizards gone from the steam wall? Are the elders still seated?

Then I would decide whether to raise amber.

My closest neighbors would be machines.

The west pump would be called Grandmother Teeth, though no official plate says so. She would have a repeating catch in her third rotation, a small iron hesitation like someone remembering an insult. New mechanics would want to fix it. Older mechanics would tell them to leave her alone unless the catch lengthened. I would be one of the people who knew the exact difference between her ordinary complaint and her dangerous one.

There would also be a cistern below my office that did not have a nickname, which is how you know people are afraid of it.

On survey drawings it would be Cistern 14-L. In older ward books it would be Lower Lent Basin. In the mouths of the oldest residents it would simply be beneath. It would not be large compared with the civic reservoirs, but it would be old in a way that makes size irrelevant. Some of its walls would be cut stone. Some would be natural cavern. One arch would sweat even in dry months. One corner would return echoes late.

I would enter it only when necessary, always with another clerk or a mason, always with lamps and chalk line. I would speak politely there despite not believing, officially, that stone cares. I would say, “We are opening the north hatch,” and “We are crossing to the measure wall,” and “We are leaving now,” because a place that has held a city’s water for centuries deserves to be informed of disturbance.

I would not think of myself as religious.

I would leave clean rags at shrines.

I would laugh at priests.

I would ask them to bless new seals.

This would not seem contradictory.

My home would be two rooms behind the office, or perhaps above it, depending on whether the city could spare the dry space. I would own very little wood. My bed would stand on stone feet. My books would be sealed. My spare clothes would smell faintly of waxed cloth and metal. Over the bed, instead of a family portrait, I might keep an old elevation drawing of the Long Throat ravine, ink faded, flood marks annotated by three generations of clerks before me.

I would eat boiled grains with bitter herbs from someone’s roof trays. Salt fish when the tram from the lowlands arrived. Preserved fruit on festival days. I would drink carefully and never waste the last inch in a cup, because the last inch tells you whether the vessel was clean.

I would have friends, but we would not visit casually in heavy weather. We would send lamp codes from windows. Two short blue flashes: all well. One amber: come if free. Red-white-red: bring tools.

Maybe there would be someone I loved on the far side of the ravine, a tram braker with red sleeves and an impatient mouth. We would quarrel mostly by hand-sign across noisy streets and make peace in rooms with felted shutters. Love there would not be soft exactly. It would be practical, attentive, full of remembered warnings.

“Your left boot is wearing thin.”

“You forgot to seal the upper box.”

“The south stair is slick today.”

“Do not take the old bridge after dusk.”

In that city, these are endearments.

On festival silence, I would do badly.

I know that about myself.

When the engines began to shut down in sequence and the city’s enormous body lost its familiar tremors, I would feel less liberated than abandoned. My hands would keep seeking surfaces. I would touch railings, pipes, doorframes, my own sternum. Without the pumps, the distances would become too large. The ravine would open. Voices would arrive from impossible places. A cough across stone. A dropped cup three terraces away. Frogs below, calling one by one like instruments being tested.

Others would gather on bridges to marvel.

I would probably go down to the gauge-house steps and sit with my back against the wall of Cistern 14-L, waiting for some small vibration to return.

And perhaps, during that silence, I would understand myself more clearly.

I would not merely be a clerk.

I would be one of the city’s lesser nerves.

Not the mind. Not the muscle. Not the law. A nerve ending laid along wet stone, trained to report discomfort before it becomes injury. A small part of the plateau’s habit of endurance. One hand, one ledger, one chalk mark, one person in a chain of people trying to make sure the water announces itself before it breaks through.

If you came looking for me, you might see only a narrow figure in a gray hood crouched beside a wall while traffic groaned overhead and vapor moved across the yard. You might think I was mending something. Or praying. Or listening for a sound.

I would be doing all three.

And if you asked where you were allowed to step, I would answer before greeting you.

Not out of rudeness.

Out of hospitality.

“Keep to the raised stones. Don’t touch the blue-marked basin. If the rail ticks, step back. If the ground darkens from the center, come inside. And if you hear nothing at all, tell me immediately.”
