You would find me where the city has almost forgotten it made a passage.

Not in the white court, not under the loudest vents, not among the glare-readers with their narrowed eyes turned upward into noon. Not in the market vault, though I would pass through it often with my slate under my arm and my palms dusty from other people’s walls.

You would find me halfway down an abandoned wind-channel behind the old root-gardens, in a slanted room that used to be a cistern for cool air and before that was probably a nursery, and before that a chamber chewed open by roots. The doorway would be low and polished at shoulder height. A black-root would enter through one corner of the ceiling, cross the room like a sleeping animal’s spine, and vanish into the floor beside my sleeping alcove. The plaster would be patched in three colors. The light would arrive indirectly, sifted through salt, through mica leaves, through hanging screens dusted gold at the edges.

I think I would be a scribe, but not one who records lineages or judgments.

I would be a scribe of changes in the city’s breath.

A listener-with-ink, though there is no ink. A carver of wind reports. A keeper of small alterations: the new tremor in the west hum, the cracked reed in the third upper vent, the way violet test-dust lifts too fast at dawn, the hour when the root-garden began to smell of bitter almond. I would carry thin tablets of salt-clay wrapped in plant fiber, a horn stylus, a packet of colored powders, and a belt of formal hand-signs worn smooth from use.

My full name would be something like:

**Of-the-Lower-Turning, Under-the-Black-Root, Who-Records-the-Second-Tone.**

But people would shorten it with their hands. Two fingers to the throat, then the wall, then a small turning motion: *the one who listens for the change.*

I would not be important in a grand way. No one in that city should be grand. Grandness blocks air.

I would be useful.

In the morning I would wake with one palm on the plaster, because the wall would know before I did whether the day had gone wrong. If the vibration was clean, I would rise, brush salt from my lashes, seal my veil, and eat a brittle cake of gold pollen and root-ash. I would touch the black-root above my door in greeting—not worship, exactly; courtesy. It would have held my room longer than I had been alive.

Then I would go out into the roar.

I would move through the city by its pressures. Where outsiders would see only alleys and ladders and white glare, I would feel routes of sound: the knife-bright scream above the roof slits, the chest-deep drone below the old market, the dry clicking of leaf-fans turning themselves toward noon. I would know which stairwell loosened teeth and which arch gave a clean warning pulse through the heel. I would know where not to pause, where not to lean, which wall was living, which was only wall, which dead wall had begun to become useful again.

People would stop me with gestures.

A dust-sorter beneath a mica awning would show me a tray of black pollen lifting wrong at the edges. A child would tug my sleeve and press my hand to a ladder rung so I could feel the tremor no adult had believed. A root-minder would hand me a carved token: **witness**, **measure**, **do not cut yet**. A ventwright would crouch beside me in a passage too narrow for both our shoulders, and we would watch a pinch of green powder rise, hesitate, fall sideways, and disappear into a crack that should not have been breathing.

I would write all of it.

Not because the writing would last. It would not. My tablets would soften at the corners from handling, chip in the dry, be copied badly by apprentices, be ground one day into plaster, feed a wall, feed a root, enter some child’s sleeve as dust.

That would be the point.

I would not preserve the city by keeping it unchanged. I would preserve it by helping it remember how to change without breaking.

At noon I would avoid the brightest courts. My eyes would not be strong. Maybe no scribe of breath keeps strong eyes for long; we spend too much time reading the edges of things. Instead, I would sit in the half-shade of an old aqueduct that never carried water and arrange my tablets by district-tone. The wind would pass above my head in a long hollow chord. Children would run past, nearly silent-footed, their laughter visible only in shoulders and eyes. One might leave a beetle cage beside me and ask, with signs too quick and sloppy, whether the beetle’s shell meant rain.

I would answer gravely: no rain, only roof-stress.

The child would be disappointed. Children enjoy disasters they have never seen.

In the evenings I would go to the deep chambers where the wind lowers and the city becomes easier to feel than see. There I might meet the keepers of silence. They would ask me what I had recorded: any pauses, any gaps, any places where sound had thinned without reason. I would give them my tablets. They would read with their fingertips.

I think I would fear wrong silence more than noise.

Noise would be the body of the city speaking in all its organs. Noise would mean passage, pressure, work, exchange. But a silence in the wrong place—a vent gone still, a root no longer ticking, a stairwell without tremor—would feel like a closed eye in a face that should be awake.

And yet I would love true silence.

If one came, one of the rare whole silences, I would stop wherever I was. In a lane, on a ladder, beneath a hanging garden of brass-spoon leaves. I would not write. I would not interpret. I would let the absence arrive entire.

For once, the city would not need me to translate it.

I might be lonely, but not alone. That distinction would matter there. I would have a sleeping alcove warmed by a heat-leaf at dusk. I would have neighbors who repaired my ladder rung without mentioning it. Someone would leave edible dust at my threshold during pollen season. Someone else would polish the hand-sign at my belt while I slept after too many hours in the white court.

I would have no great household, perhaps. Scribes of breath belong to too many districts to be claimed by one. My kin would be distributed: a vent-line that trained my feet, a root-house that fed me, a dust-sorter who corrected my colors, a nearly blind glare-reader who taught me that light has a sound if you wait long enough.

And when I died, I would not want a sealed tomb.

Put me behind the root chamber, where the black-root crosses the wall. Fold whatever dampness escapes me into ash. Dry it properly. Apologize to the plaster if needed. Place my worn stylus beside me only if it has no further use; otherwise give it to a child with attentive hands.

Let my minerals go where they are useful.

If I returned, I would like to return as something modest: not a grand vine over a doorway, not a civic plant, not one of the black-roots that decides the fate of houses.

Let me come back as a thin fan of green-black mica leaves growing in the shade outside the abandoned wind-channel, angled just so, ticking softly through the heat, adjusting by fractions to keep the room below from blinding.

A small correction to the light.

A thing that listens.
