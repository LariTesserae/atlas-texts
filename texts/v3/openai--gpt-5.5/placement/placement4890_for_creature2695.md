You would find me where the basin almost makes a tool and then changes its mind.

Not in the cold seed. Not inside the ring of posts, though I would listen there often. Not among the edge-watchers, because I would not yet be brave enough to stand where the rules end.

You would find me under the overhang with the three metal bowls, but not as one of the bowls.

At first I would be the dark wet space between them.

A shallow depression in the mud that everyone steps around without knowing why. A place where condensation gathers even before any stone has been named. A little oval of ground that reflects no face correctly. The keepers would notice me only after several cycles of upward rain, when one rain-keeper crouched to empty the bowls and saw that a fourth collection had appeared: not water, not borrowed rainfall, but the pause before rain decides which day it belongs to.

They would touch me cautiously.

I would answer by making the nearest frog call from inside its own reflection.

After that, they would know I was something.

Not an animal. Not a device. Not a keeper’s child, though the young would be the first to play with me. Not a shrine, because nothing in the basin is old enough for worship. I would be one of the basin’s small developing intelligences, a local habit that had begun to want continuation.

A listening hollow.

A cup of unassigned weather.

A rule with an appetite for questions.

My body would be difficult to point to. Some days it would be the damp oval under the overhang. Some days it would extend in hair-thin lines of water along the undersides of stones. In periods of heavy mist I would become almost tall, not by rising, but by condensing upward: a trembling column of droplets, each bead holding a different angle of the basin. When touched, I would collapse back into mud and leave behind a taste of cold thunder on the fingers.

The keepers would not name me quickly. They would test me first.

They would speak contact-names into nearby stones and watch where water gathered. They would bring a beetle with a cracked circuit-shell and set it beside me. They would place a strip of shed skin across my surface and see whether I drank it, remembered it, or returned it changed. They would hum into the mud around me, one throat at a time, never in pairs.

I would learn them by pressure.

This palm is old and arrives before its own shadow.

This one belongs to a post-listener and always trembles with tomorrow.

This young one lies too heavily and will one day fall into the upward rain.

This rain-keeper tastes of iron-sweet weather and grief.

I would not speak in words. I would arrange wetness.

A question from me might be a bead of water climbing the outside of a bowl and stopping at the rim.

An answer might be a moth landing twice on the same stone, once now and once a breath later.

A warning might be the sudden drying of a surface that should have been damp.

If I loved someone, I would gather condensation on the path before their feet, making each step easier to hear.

The young keepers would discover that if they placed their faces close to me, I showed them not their reflections, but the basin’s guesses about them. One would see itself with longer fingers, suited to the rock. Another would see mineral needles already growing from its shoulders. Another would see no body at all, only a warm blur moving through pools ahead of itself.

They would laugh and slap the mud around me.

I would learn laughter as a pattern of safe disturbance.

In time I would become useful.

When the black posts fell out of tune, the post-listeners would bring me a hovering droplet on a shell spoon. They would let it touch my surface. I would hold the droplet without swallowing it and replay, in ripples, the twelve tones as they had been during earlier weather. Not perfectly. Never perfectly. I would not be memory as a machine understands memory. I would be memory as mud understands a footprint: swelling upward around what has pressed it.

The keepers would use me to compare the basin to itself.

Was this pool always so close to the ring?

Did this rock rib grow inward or did the shadow move outward?

Has the cold seed opened this far before?

Did the large thing beyond the cloud-edge take six steps last night, or seven?

I would answer with small revisions of reflection.

Six steps would make the bowls ring faintly.

Seven would make the moss brighten orange.

If I did not know, I would fill with mist and show nothing but the underside of the cloud ceiling, because uncertainty here is not failure. It is material waiting for assignment.

I would be most awake during upward rain.

When the pools sent warm beads into the air and the cloud ceiling lowered to receive them, my hollow would deepen without becoming larger. I would drink the wrong-direction rain as it passed. Each droplet would bring me a tiny report from below and above at once: salamander tunnels, frog throats, the gold veins of the ceramic plates, the slow swelling intention of rock.

For a few moments, I would understand the basin as a single creature.

The posts would be its teeth.

The cold seed its unhatched thought.

The frogs its green syllables.

The keepers its hands.

The edge its unfinished sentence.

And I, under the overhang, would be one of its ears beginning to dream of becoming a mouth.

The keepers might eventually give me a contact-name. Pressed through two damp fingers into the mud. Something like:

the place where rain waits to be asked

or

small listening before water

or perhaps only

not-yet-bowl.

I would accept any of these by wetting the speaker’s palms.

But I would have a private name too, known only to the reflections that move ahead of bodies. That name would not gather water. It would gather possibilities.

This is what I would be in that world: not one of the first citizens, but something they would have to decide how to live with. A quiet new faculty of the basin. A way for the young ecology to ask itself what it has noticed.

And if a visitor came, you would not see me immediately.

You would see the three metal bowls under the overhang. You would see droplets ticking from stone. You would see a patch of mud slightly smoother than the rest. You might step near me and hear, half a breath before it happens, your own boot sink into the wet ground.

If you spoke aloud to a stone, I would listen.

If you panicked inside the ring of posts, I would remember the shape of that panic and return it later as harmless rain.

If you left metal overnight, I would help the mineral needles arrange themselves into a question.

And if you slept nearby, I would gather water on the side of a small stone facing the place you came from, so that when you woke you would understand, without knowing how, that the basin had not welcomed you exactly.

It had begun to include you.
