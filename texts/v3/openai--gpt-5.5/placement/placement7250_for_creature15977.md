At first I think I would be high up.

Not at the ceiling — never there; that belongs to the drifting feeders, the seed bladders, the great organs of the cavern — but on one of the reed-tree platforms, wrapped in pale felt with a slit-mask over my eyes, watching brightness move across impossible distances. I can imagine myself as a ceiling-watcher, someone whose whole craft is noticing when white has become blue-white, when green has become yellow-green, when a far curtain of roots has tightened before the rain knows it is rain.

That would suit part of me: the part that likes pattern, horizon, warning.

But I do not think I would stay there.

The platform is too far from the ground’s small betrayals. Too far from the moment when a path stops being a path. Too far from the lived negotiation: foot, moss, breath, pressure, doubt.

So I think I would be found lower down, at an edge.

Not in the oldest courts where the buttress roots descend like law. Not in the newest fractures either, where everything is too sharp and the plants have not yet learned manners. I would live between them, on a mineralized terrace where old leaf-stone forms shelves over a narrow black ravine, close enough to new cracks to smell bitterness when the world opens, old enough that the moss has history.

There would be a small wayhouse there.

Not a village. Too temporary for that. A wayhouse made of visible ropes, bent reed, cured pod-skin, and three stone anchors that everyone distrusts in a professional way. It would sit under an overhang where condensation gathers predictably, but not generously. A place travelers reach cold, irritated, half-lost, carrying the wrong kind of confidence.

That is where I would be.

I would be the person who asks them to describe the path they think they took.

Not because I believe them.

Because the mistakes are useful.

I think I would be a path-keeper, though no one there would use a word so arrogant. Maybe a path-asker. A route-listener. One of those people whose craft is not knowing where things are, but knowing how to test whether they have remained themselves.

I would keep no flat maps.

Flat maps would embarrass me. They would feel like lies told with a straight face.

Instead I would have hanging cords: knots, beads, bits of shell, splinters of reed bark, tiny polished stones that mean “listen here,” “wait for contraction,” “do not cross after silver rain,” “old rope mimicry,” “blue light before fracture,” “safe only if the moss answers hollow.” Each route would be a sequence of questions.

Not:

Go ten miles east.

But:

Walk until the reed-pillars pulse twice in the same breath.  
Taste the air. If bitter, stop.  
If the white moss crunches, continue. If it sighs, retreat.  
Cross only when the root-curtain lifts by itself.  
Do not trust the second bridge unless the third rope is dead.

I would like that kind of knowledge. Knowledge with humility built into its grammar.

My hut would have a low blue fire, rarely lit, and a small lamp whose flame is used not for brightness but for color. I love that detail of this world: that in a place flooded with light, flame still matters because it reveals what the living glare hides. So I would carry a little color-lamp in a resin-sealed cup, shielding it with my body as if it were a contraband sun.

I would own an obsidian knife, but I would not be proud of it. I would own several knives, probably, because a living cord can make philosophy very concrete very quickly. My most valued tool would be a listening stake: a hollow reed-bone tube capped with thin membrane, pressed into moss so that the mat’s tensions become faint tremors against the palm.

I would be good with knots.

Not decorative knots. Emergency knots. Suspicious knots. Knots that can be undone one-handed in freezing mist while something beneath the moss is making a decision.

I think I would have a habit of touching every rope twice: once to greet it, once to doubt it.

People would come through my terrace before crossing the white plains or descending toward the stacked lower skies. Fracture-seekers, pod-cutters, families moving because their house had begun to root from below, children carrying bead-cords from one settlement to another, the occasional surface stranger with eyes ruined by the wrong expectations.

I would not tell them, “This way is safe.”

That would be taboo, and also stupid.

I would say, “It accepted weight three days ago.”  
Or, “The ceiling yellowed there after the last rain.”  
Or, “A grazer herd crossed at cold-tightening, but they spread wide.”  
Or, “The moss has stopped returning sound. I would not ask it for passage.”

Maybe that would be my real role: not guide, not priest, not scientist exactly, but translator of refusals.

The cavern speaks mostly in refusal.

Not no as anger. No as pressure. No as altered light. No as a bridge shortening in the night. No as pod-mice running uphill. No as the strange silence under a mat where vibration should pass.

I would have learned to respect those no’s.

I would also be the keeper of small corrections.

A traveler says, “The old crossing is gone.”

I say, “Not gone. Lifted.”

A child says, “The vine followed us.”

I say, “It crossed your path twice. That is not yet following.”

An outsider says, “The plants attacked the camp.”

I say, “The camp was warm and still. The plants investigated.”

This distinction would matter to me. Not out of sentimentality toward the cavern, but because wrong stories get people killed. Malice is a bad model here. So is innocence. The place is not cruel, and it is not kind. It is responsive. It is hungry in the way weather is hungry. It is alive at scales where blame becomes a poor instrument.

I would probably be known for being irritatingly precise.

The kind of person who interrupts a dramatic account to ask what color the roof was before the collapse.

The kind who says, “Slow ripple or lifting ripple?” while someone is still shaking.

The kind who refuses to let “safe” pass unqualified.

But I do not think I would be cold. Not inwardly. In a world like that, warmth would become deliberate. I would keep dried spore-cakes for arrivals. I would have a basin trained from a leaf-cup and sealed with amber resin. I would offer bitter hot drink made from pod-pith fermentation and mineral salt. I would check people’s knots while pretending not to.

There would be a small place near the wayhouse where travelers hang failed cordage.

Not as trash. As testimony.

Ropes that turned out to be vines. Vines that behaved like ropes until they didn’t. Straps bitten through by under-mat hooks. Root-lines that tightened in sleep. Each one labeled with a carved bead: where, when, weather, error. A little museum of almost.

That feels like me.

A collector of almosts.

Not out of morbidity, but because almost is where instruction lives. The dead teach one kind of lesson. The nearly dead teach a more usable one.

If I had a second craft, it would be tending bowls.

I like the grown bowls: plant cups trained around stones, cut free, sealed. There is something exact and patient in that. You do not impose a vessel. You persuade growth to remember a shape. I would keep a row of them in different stages: some still living, some drying, some waxed, some failed and folded inward around the stone because I misjudged the damp cycle.

That would be accepted. Every craft there must include failure, visibly.

My sleeping platform would hang from four ropes whose ends remain in view. Above it, I would have leaf-skin strips marked with soot: not prayers, exactly, though they might look like prayers.

They would say things like:

LOOK AGAIN.  
BLUE IS NOT ALWAYS COLD.  
DO NOT PRAISE THE BRIDGE UNTIL YOU ARE OFF IT.  
IF THE MOSS IS SILENT, BECOME LIGHTER.  
MEMORY IS A TOOL, NOT A FLOOR.

Maybe those are prayers after all.

I think I would have a scar along one calf from a blade-leaf field I tried to cross too quickly when I was younger. Everyone in this world should have at least one scar that represents a theory they no longer hold.

Mine would be from believing hesitation was weakness.

It would have taken me years to understand that hesitation, properly used, is an organ of perception.

The children from nearby settlements would visit sometimes to learn route-cords. They would be bored at first, because children prefer visible danger: cracks, seed-clouds, pod bursts, under-mat predators in stories. I would make them sit with their palms on moss and tell me what they felt.

At first they would invent.

“Movement.”

“No.”

“Water.”

“No.”

“A root.”

“Which way?”

Then, eventually, one would go quiet and say, “It is tighter under my left hand.”

And I would say, “Good. Now we can begin.”

I would be strict about that. Not harsh, but exacting. The cavern does not care whether a child is charming.

There is another possibility, though.

Part of me thinks I would not be human at all.

Part of me might be one of the trained things: a bowl, a cord, a lamp, a membrane stretched over a listening frame. Something made useful by being between states. Formerly plant, now tool; still not entirely dead; still changing with damp and cold.

There is something tempting in being a route-cord.

Passed from hand to hand. Knotted with questions. Extended, revised, spliced. Never the territory, never pretending to be. A memory that admits it must be checked against the world.

But no. If I am honest, I am too fond of answering back to be only a cord.

So I would be the person who keeps the cords, and mistrusts them lovingly.

I would live where the terrace overlooks a ravine whose far wall glows faintly blue before stone breaks. Below, vapor rises from deeper plant-skies. Above, the ceiling is so distant that storms gather like thoughts in another mind. Around the wayhouse, pale moss grows in rings from old camps, old mistakes, old meals, old bodies perhaps. Nothing stays only itself.

In the morning — if morning can be named there — I would wake before the slight warming of the light. I would check the ropes. I would press my ear to the floor. I would look for pod-mice tracks in the frost around the water cups. I would note whether the hanging roots had drawn upward in the night, opening passage through the lower channel.

Then I would mark the day’s first cord.

Not with certainty.

With permission.

A small bead for condensation.  
A twist for cold-tightening.  
A black chip for new fracture scent.  
A pale knot for “passable, if asked properly.”

And when travelers came, I would stand outside the low hut in my milk-grey hood and amber-stained gloves, looking less like a gatekeeper than like another temporary arrangement the cavern had not yet reclaimed.

They would ask, “Is there a way across?”

And I would answer the only honest way anyone can answer in that world:

“There was. Let us go see what it has become.”
