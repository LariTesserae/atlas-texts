I would not find myself on the rim, looking down.

The rim is for foxes, for wind, for the brief decisions of birds. It is too exposed, too declarative. From there the hollow can be mistaken for a scene: black stone, vapor, moss, water. From there one can still believe in edges.

I would be lower.

I would be where a flat green patch has no honest bottom. Where the moss is bright because warmth is passing under it. Where a thread of clear water runs over clay, disappears beneath roots, and comes back smelling faintly of iron and boiled stone. I would be in the place a boot should not go: not the deepest pool, not the loudest vent, but the uncertain margin between them.

At first I think I would be a thing clinging there.

A white filament in the dark underside of moss.  
A larva with no need yet for sight.  
A soft comma of hunger, feeling the world as pressure and shadow.

I would hang beneath the floating mat, where the warm water slides through roots and all sound arrives translated: the far pounding as a slow instruction, the ticking drips as bright impacts, the footfall of a heron as a vertical threat. Above me the moss would hold cold beads of mist. Below me the pool would be black and full of suspended mineral dust. I would eat films, eggs, rot, the tender skins of things smaller than myself. I would stiffen when danger passed over me and become a piece of slime, a pale flaw in the wet green.

But I do not stay there.

The place does not permit a single form to remain complete. Everything is revised.

So I darken.

My external filaments withdraw. My back roughens. The first grit catches in my skin and does not wash away. Spores settle there. A little green stain opens along my spine. My toes lengthen into soft stars for crossing what cannot be trusted. My eyes become less important. My belly learns the grammar of mud.

I become one of the low ones.

Not the largest. Not an elder so mossed over that birds land near me without fear. Not one of the new pale young hidden under the mats. I would be midway through my life: dark-backed, narrow-headed, still quick enough to vanish cleanly, but old enough to know the basin’s changes before they show.

You would find me under the leaning slab of basalt on the north side of the hollow, where a vent once opened and then sealed itself after frost split the stone. The chamber beneath is not mine in the way a house is mine. It is one of several agreements I keep with the ground.

Its ceiling sweats. Its floor is clay pressed smooth by my body. Willow fibers are worked into the walls, not woven exactly, but held there by mud and the drying gloss of my own secretion. Pale mineral crust grows in one corner like a frozen breath. Shed skin clings to a stone tooth near the entrance, thin and translucent, more revealing than I ever am alive.

From that chamber I can hear three waters:

the narrow rill above the moss, quick and cold;  
the thick bubbling under the reed roots;  
the deeper pulse below everything, striking through stone.

I know them apart as clearly as another creature might know stars.

I leave at dusk.

Not boldly. Boldness belongs to animals with open country behind them. I leave by loosening myself from the chamber floor, passing through a side-slit in the mud, and entering water that has no surface for several body-lengths. When I rise, I do not break the pool. I alter it. A dark lens forms beneath the ice skin. Silt lifts, then settles in ribs behind me.

My mouth opens only when necessary.

I rasp the orange film from a warm stone. I take three midge larvae from the underside of a leaf. I nose into frog spawn and strain what I need. If a stunned beetle kicks on its back near the steam, I hold it in the soft ridges of my mouth until it ceases being separate from me.

I am not cruel. I am not gentle. I am a use the hollow has found for hunger.

When the vent near the sedge grows too hot, I feel it before the plants show damage. I go there in the dim part of morning and press a channel open with my head and shoulder, letting the water bleed sideways. The mud scalds my flank. For two days the pale seam there smokes when the air touches it. Later, fernlets grow where the heat has thinned. Midges gather. A frog comes. Then a heron. Then silence.

This is how the hollow thinks: not in plans, but in consequences.

I would be one of those consequences.

If you came looking for me, you would fail several times.

You would see where I had been: the clean crescent on a mineral stone, the silt combed at the bottom of a pool, the crescent-flattened sedge, the small side-channel opened beside a vent. You would decide perhaps that water did it. Then perhaps an otter. Then perhaps nothing.

Only if you waited without insisting would I become possible.

A low back slipping through vapor.  
Five pale toes spreading over floating moss.  
An eye opening in mud.  
A throat pulsing once against the ground, answering the engine below.

I would not want you to name me too quickly.

A name would lift me out of the hollow, and I am not made to be lifted. I am made of its unfinishedness: cold over heat, water under ground, animal under moss, breath under stone.

If I had a name, it would be something given by listening rather than seeing.

Sump-listener.  
Vent-keeper.  
Mossback.  
The low resident.  
The one that edits pools.

But among my own kind there would be no name. Only pressure, scent, interval, the remembered shape of safe mud.

So this is where I would find myself:

beneath the brightest moss where it floats over black water, in a chamber warmed by a half-choked vent, between the ticking rills and the buried pound.

And this is what I would be:

not a monster, not a spirit, not a machine, but a wet, low animal with stone in my skin and weather in my throat — one of the hollow’s pauses given a body, listening while the world continues to become land.
