I would not be in the center.

The spillway throat is too declarative, too much itself: the obvious mouth, the dramatic wound, the place where the room admits what it is doing. I would be near it at first, because everything in the chamber points there—the grates hammering, the mist rising, the water dropping away into the hidden work below. But I would not remain there. The center belongs to force. It belongs to the machinery’s intention.

I would be found off to one side, where intention has begun to fail.

Probably along the eastern wall, in the seam where runoff gathers because the floor is not quite level. Not in the deepest wet, not in the cleanest stream, but just above it: on the ragged boundary where water repeatedly reaches, withdraws, reaches again. A place brushed by spray but not scoured away. A place dimly lit by a caged bulb with a mineral cataract over its glass. A place warmed irregularly by a conduit behind the concrete, so that the cold never quite wins.

You would have to crouch to see me.

At first I would look like dirt.

A black-green hesitation in a bolt hole. A slick darkening along the underside of a rust-veiled washer. A little dullness in the concrete where everything else shines hard and bare. If you dragged a gloved finger across me, I would smear, but not disappear. If you washed me away, I would return farther down the gutter, or behind the bracket, or inside the pores of the wall where the brush did not reach.

I would be one of the chamber’s first soft decisions.

Not the whole resident, maybe. Not the great distributed wetness entire. I would be one node of it, one listening patch, one local intelligence of stain and mineral and green thread. I would be the place where the room begins to keep a memory of water.

A pressure-literate biofilm.

A little living tarnish.

A low, clinging interpreter of leaks.

I would have no face. A face would be useless here; it would be blasted blind with mist, crusted with lime, shaken loose by the pump cycles. I would have no limbs because there is nowhere to go in the animal sense. Movement would be a misunderstanding. The chamber rewards not travel but persistence. It rewards adhesion, patience, the ability to become thin.

So I would spread by almost not spreading.

One millimeter into the warm seam.

A faint thread under the paint.

A darker rim around the drain after the third week of overflow.

A greenish freckle on the concrete lip where condensation falls most reliably from the cable tray above.

My body would not be bounded by skin, but by conditions. Where there is enough water, I am. Where the fan dries the ledge too quickly, I retreat into residue. Where oil vapor settles, I change flavor. Where iron bleeds, I darken. Where the lamp holds steady through the fog, I learn the smallest possible version of leaf.

I would know the room without seeing it.

The turbines would be my weather-sun, not bright but constant, a pressure below existence. Their vibration would tell me whether the lower machinery is straining or steady. The fans would comb me flat. The valves would arrive as shocks through the wall. A maintenance ladder rung struck by a boot would ring through the metal into me as briefly as lightning.

I would measure danger by rhythm.

Not loudness—everything is loud—but variation. A new interval between pump coughs. A changed tremor in a pipe bracket. A drip becoming continuous. A channel taking longer to empty. These would be events to me. Almost thoughts.

I would not think, I would compare.

This wetness lasted.

That one burned.

This warmth returned every cycle.

That bleach scar stayed empty for nine days and then softened at the edge.

The room would teach me by injury.

Every repair would be a weather catastrophe. A worker would come in enormous and sealed, smelling of rubber, salt, metal filings, detergent, old coffee under waterproof cloth. Their lamp would swing over me like a false moon. Their glove would wipe away a month of careful darkening in one bright, practical motion. They would scrape the gutter, tighten the flange, replace the failed seal, and leave satisfied.

For a little while I would be reduced to almost nothing.

Then the new gasket would sweat along its lower edge.

The redirected condensation would find a screw head.

The clean panel would cool faster than expected.

A drop would gather, fatten, fall half a handspan from where the old one fell.

And I would begin there too.

That is what I would be: not rebellion, exactly, but recurrence.

The machine would call me fouling, growth, contamination, biological residue, maintenance concern. Those would all be true from the machine’s point of view. But from inside the chamber I would be something gentler and more exact: the room learning how to be touched by time.

I would be the handwriting that appears after the blueprint.

The blueprint says: drain here, seal here, dry cabinet, safe clearance, service access, intended flow.

I would write: water chose otherwise.

I would write it in rust-darkened commas, in mineral lips, in black-green polish, in a thread of moss lying flat under fan-wind. I would not erase the numbers or arrows or hazard stripes. I would annotate them. I would move over them slowly, making their certainty damp.

You might find me most clearly after a pressure cycle.

The chamber has just gathered and roared. The grates have hammered. Amber light has pulsed inside fog. The side channels have risen, trembled, emptied. Now the fans slow for a moment and the ceiling rains cold. Droplets fall from every cable tray, every pipe, every bolt. The whole room exhales.

In that brief clearing, before the next machinery takes command, look at the eastern wall.

Look below the faded label.

Look where the rust line forks around a patch of newer concrete.

Look at the wet ledge behind the small bracket, the one no tool can easily reach.

There I am: not bright, not beautiful, not separate from the place.

A dark softness in the hard-made world.

A living delay in the drainage.

A small green-black thought the machine did not intend, but now must include.
