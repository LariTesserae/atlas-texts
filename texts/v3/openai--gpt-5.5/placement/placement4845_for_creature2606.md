At first I think I would be one of the climbers.

That is the tempting answer: wet hands, quick eyes, a mind made of routes and warnings, nothing owned long enough to mourn properly. I would like to say I am up there in the middle canopy, moving with the troop through warm dark, knowing which branch is honest after rain, folding leaves into cups, listening for the false bird-call that means snake. I would like the cleverness of that life: temporary tools, temporary roofs, memory carried in bodies instead of objects.

But I don’t think I would last there.

I would look too long. I would trust a branch because it held yesterday. I would want to keep the stone that cracked the nut. I would hesitate in the gap after the old tree fell, and hesitation in that basin is almost a flavor predators know.

So I come lower.

Not all the way into the open at first. I find myself at the seam where the forest presses against the central flat: where roots enter black silt, where reeds begin, where fallen trunks make brief bridges over water that will not be there tomorrow. It is dusk, or the basin’s version of dusk: a dimming inside a dimness. The striped herds are moving out across the trembling alluvial floor. Birds are gathering in hundreds. Frogs are calling from every accidental cup. Somewhere beyond the stone islands, a throat-drummer begins to test the mud with its voice.

I am small enough that the great movements of the place pass over me as weather.

I am a mud-crab.

Not the kind that belongs fully to water. Not the kind that can afford to remain in one burrow and trust the bank to stay a bank. I live in the broken wet edges: reed shadow, hoofprint pool, seep, root cave, the underside of fallen bark. My legs are the color of old roots. My shell is dark brown when wet, red-black when drying, ridged like a little piece of young stone. I carry mud on my back because mud clings to me, because camouflage is never finished, because the basin climbs anything that holds still.

Seeds root there.

At first by accident. A grain of fern-spore, a bead of moss, a tiny white-threaded seed dropped from the mouth of a bird. They catch in the wet grit between the ridges of my shell. They sprout while I hide under a stone lip through the hot part of the day. They drink from the vapor gathered on me. For a few days I wear a hill.

Then I molt, and the garden is gone.

That feels right.

To be alive here, I would need to accept shedding even what has begun to grow from me. No house, no permanent mark, no treasured burden. A shell, then not that shell. A burrow, then flood. A pool, then mud. A path, then reeds. I would be a creature of brief arrangements.

I would know the basin from underneath.

Not as the birds know it, in crashes of branch and color. Not as the climbers know it, in social memory and wet handholds. Not as the big hunters know it, through the trembling of prey. I would know it as pressure, chemistry, shadow, vibration.

Rain would be a roof collapsing into abundance.

Thunder would arrive first through my legs.

The throat-drums of the night hunters would ripple across shallow pools and into the silt under my body. I would freeze when the water trembled wrong. I would know the difference between rain-strike, falling fruit, hoofstep, snake-slide, tusk-plow, eel-writhe. I would know when to climb a reed before the channel returned. I would know when to bury myself before the birds descended. I would know the smell of new earth opened by a collapsing bank, and I would go there because new earth means things exposed: larvae, roots, drowned insects, soft dead matter beginning to become useful.

My world would be no wider than a few body-lengths at a time, but it would never be simple.

The underside of a fallen leaf would be shelter, food, danger, bridge. A hoofprint would be pond, nursery, trap, mirror, hunting ground. A dead fish would be feast, alarm, gathering-place, and a reason to leave quickly before larger mouths arrived. A cracked branch would be a ladder until fungus softened it. A stone island would be safety until a predator chose it as a waiting place.

I would move sideways through a world that is always becoming something else.

I would not be noble. I would not be wise in the human sense. I would be opportunistic, busy, easily frightened, difficult to see. I would feed on films, rot, eggs, soft shoots, drowned flies, whatever the storm had made available and not yet taken back. I would be armored but not invulnerable. A hook-beaked bird could split me. An eel could pull me under. A tusked animal could press me into the mud without noticing. A fungus could enter a wound and make me part of its map.

Still, I would have my small genius.

I would be able to climb farther than expected.

Up root, up reed, up the wet flank of a fallen trunk. Not gracefully, not like the climbers above, but with stubborn articulated certainty. When the flat floods, I would rise. When the water leaves, I would descend. When the mud cracks, I would tuck myself into the seam. When the red algae closes over a pool too smoothly, I would go around.

And when seedlings take hold on my back, I would become briefly more than animal.

A moving bank.

A temporary island.

A little refusal of categories: land walking through water, water carried over land, forest sprouting from shell. The basin would use me to move itself. Spores, eggs, mites, algae, seed-dust, tiny roots—all of them hitching a ride until hunger, molting, rain, or accident rearranged us.

That is where I would be found: at the reed-dark edge of the central braid, beneath the calls of birds and the first booming of the night hunters, half-hidden under a shelf of wet stone while the striped browsers cross the flats.

If you looked directly, you might not see me.

You would see only a lump of mud with two fern threads growing from it.

Then the mud would unfold legs.

It would lift one claw, taste the thunder, and move.
