You would find me where the city’s weather misbehaves.

Not in the furnace mouths, though I would know their schedule by the color of the alleys. Not in the central square exactly, though I would pass through it often enough to touch the public gauge and glance at the thin tree as if checking on a sleeping elder. Not at the outer excavation ring, where the stone is still too loud with new breaking.

I would be halfway up the district’s side, in one of the stitched passages between buildings, where a catwalk runs under a cold intake rib and over the roofs of three workshops. There is a narrow alley there that never properly dries. Its official name would be something like White Mark 17, taken from a survey number painted on the raw basalt, but no one would call it that except clerks. Children would call it Threadwater Cut.

That is where the droplets slide sideways.

They gather beneath a beam, swell silver in the dimness, then move along nothing anyone can see. Not far. A palm’s width, a forearm’s length on good nights. Then they fall neatly into a gutter hammered from old tin and lined with blue paint at the lip. The gutter is not accidental. Someone understood the strangeness well enough to receive it.

That would be my place.

I would be a keeper of those small wrongnesses.

Not an engineer of high systems, not a furnace hand, not quite a condenser clerk. Something between a pipe listener, a gutter mender, and a local weather-scribe. A threadwater keeper, perhaps, though the title would not be official enough to appear on stamped permits. My work would be to know where the district sweats in ordinary ways and where it does not.

I would carry a sealed slate, a roll of soft wire, chalk in three colors, a little tin cup, a folded cloth, a pressure needle, and a knife kept sharper than necessary. One glove would hang from my belt. The other hand I would keep bare most of the time, because the city speaks through temperature before it speaks through sound.

I would have salt at my temples and old mineral burns on two fingers. My shoulders would turn before I did. I would duck under the same pipe every day without looking. My clothes would be close, dark, patched at the hip where the slate rubs. My scarf would smell faintly of wet iron and boiled dust. People would not notice me unless something began dripping where it should not.

Then they would send for me quickly.

I would know which beads belong to clean blue pipe sweat, which carry the sour metallic warning from lower sumps, which have touched lead flashing, which have passed over fresh stone and come away with too much mineral bitterness. I would know how to taste nothing directly. The tongue is for food, speech, and mistakes made by children before they are old enough to know better. I would test with paper, with heated wire, with color dust, with the way a droplet skins over when held near a lamp.

My maps would be ugly and useful.

Not the grand maps hung near offices, not the civic ones with clean lines and numbered ducts, but layered scraps showing where rain actually lands, where steam turns back on itself, where a cold pipe was moved and the wall kept remembering it. I would mark the sideways falls with tiny hooked arrows. Some pages would be revised until the marks looked like a language written by damp.

Children would know me because I would chase them away from bad gutters and let them play at the harmless ones. I would pretend sternness when they held out their fingers for the side-rain. I would say, “Not there. That one’s yellow until third bell.” Or, “Only the first two drops. After that it comes off the beam.” They would roll their eyes and obey, mostly.

Sometimes, when no clerk was near, I would show them how to coax a clean droplet across the air by warming the back of the hand and holding still.

I would not call it magic. No one sensible would. But I would not fully believe the engineers either when they named it pressure gradient, static field, mineral breath. Their names would be accurate and insufficient, as most official names are.

The city would not be alive, I would say, if asked directly.

Then I would put my bare hand on the wall and wait for it to answer.

My room would be small even by the district’s standards, wedged behind a condenser stair, with a bed that folds into the wall and a shelf of jars above it. The jars would hold samples: orange film dried into brittle flakes, pale gutter-mat pressed between glass, a curl of salt from the first year’s ceiling rain, three clear vials from a week when the drops in Threadwater Cut fell upward for half a finger before correcting themselves.

I would keep one plant. Not a noble plant. Not the famous tree’s descendant or anything sentimental. A wax-leaf thing in a cracked ceramic tray, stubborn and bitter-smelling when the furnace tide rises. Its roots would be visible through a repaired seam in the pot. I would wipe its leaves before sleeping. I would measure its drainage with more tenderness than I admitted to anyone.

People would come to me with domestic disasters.

A woman from the upper brick rooms: “There’s a drip behind my sleeping wall.”

A bracewright: “The new plate is sweating on the wrong side.”

A grower, trying not to sound afraid: “The tray water went rainbow-skinned overnight.”

A clerk: “Your alley took more than its registered catch.”

That last one would irritate me most. I would explain, again, that water does not read permits. The clerk would say that people do. We would dislike each other in a practiced, necessary way.

During ceiling rains, I would be happiest and most afraid. I would stand under the cold ribs with my hood down, listening to tin roofs begin their scattered drumming. Every fall has a character. A generous rain sounds round. A contaminated rain sounds too sharp, as if each drop arrives with an edge. A false rain starts confidently and stops before the gutters wet through. I would know them by ear.

Afterward, when steam rose from paving stones and the lamps blurred, I would walk the routes with a chalk nub in my teeth, checking lips, channels, jars, illegal pans, cracked diverters, greedy cloths tied where no cloth should be. I would forgive a thirsty household once. Not twice.

At deaths, I would stand aside with everyone else, one hand on the nearest pipe. At births, I would bring a cup drawn from a verified clean catch, if the family had no quarrel with me. On naming days, if the child was carried to the central tree, I would watch their small hand touch the bark and wonder whether they would grow up thinking the surface was real or excessive.

I would go above rarely.

The first time, I would hate it.

The sky would be too much unclaimed space. Wind would move without ducting. Rain, if it came, would fall everywhere, wastefully, with no lips, no troughs, no careful numerals painted beneath it. I would stand in it appalled, then laughing, then appalled again. I would bring back a surface stone for no practical reason and keep it near my jars. It would be smooth from weather older than the district. I would distrust its patience.

My true loyalty would be to the young wound below.

To the sharp tool marks glazing over. To the walls that have not yet decided whether to crack. To the pipes sweating blue or sour. To the unofficial gutters catching impossible drops. To the thin tree enduring in public. To the machines that make weather because there is no sky to do it for them.

I would be one of those people who cannot leave a leak unnamed.

If something falls, I would ask from where.

If something grows, I would ask on what.

If something hums, I would ask whether it hummed that way yesterday.

And if you wanted to find me, you would wait until the furnace tide ebbed and the cold intake began its work. Then you would climb the narrow stair beside the public condenser, turn left under the pipe marked blue-white-blue, pass the three doors with heat numbers painted at knee height, and stop where the catwalk sweats.

There, in the dimness, you would see a gutter receiving water from empty air.

And beside it, crouched with one bare hand lifted, waiting for the next drop to choose its path, you would find me.
