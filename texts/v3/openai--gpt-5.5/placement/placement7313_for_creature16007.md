I would find myself at a threshold.

Not out in the open plateau at first—not yet among the column-groves or the blade-fields—but in the black mouth of one of the low slate shelters, where the sound of the world is cut cleanly in half.

Behind me: silence, ash, resin, inward-leaning walls, green handprints.

Before me: the impossible country roaring itself into form.

The shelter would sit under a leaning escarpment whose upper edge is visible from two directions but not from below. A salt basin lies beneath it, white-blue and dry as an opened bone. Farther off, a pale grove turns its mica crowns by a degree every few weeks, listening to a ridge that has not yet arrived. Around the hut grows a half-circle of ash-shrubs, their sealed pods humming at unequal pitches whenever the sky lowers too quickly.

The path to the shelter no longer agrees with itself. From the basin it is a line of compacted dust. From the ridge it is a row of palm-sized stones. From the grove it is not visible at all, except in memory.

I think I would begin as something like a person.

A late remnant of the builders, perhaps. Narrow-shouldered, dry-skinned, wrapped in salt-stiff fiber and root-cord, with a copper mask over the mouth to keep from breathing too much of the plateau’s unfinished argument. I would sleep badly in the short niche. I would know the cautions but not always the reasons. I would turn jars so their seals face inward. I would never count the groves aloud. I would wait at the edge of the ceramic grass until the clicking admitted me.

I would have work.

Not farming. Not guarding. Not worship, exactly.

I would maintain the threshold.

Each morning, if morning had occurred, I would sweep the line where sound becomes silence. Dust would collect there in a narrow ridge, as if the world outside had been pressing its face to the doorway all night. I would gather that dust in shallow bowls carved into the floor. Some dust would be black and young, some white with salt, some faintly green from distant plant tissue, some glittering with powdered sky-iron. I would sort it by touch.

I would listen without answering.

The plateau would try many voices. It would not call loudly. It would use tenderness. A name half-remembered. A syllable like a hand on the shoulder. A tone from childhood, if childhood still meant anything here. I would sit with my back against the inner wall and let the sound strike the doorway and stop there.

I would make tablets.

Not maps. Never maps.

Small slabs of slate incised with spirals, breaks, cautions, almost-patterns. A spiral for a basin reflecting the wrong sky. Three nested cuts for fresh rock. A line interrupted by dots for a path that has begun to change its mind. I would hang them from poles around the nearby grove, not because the grove needs warning, but because warning itself is one of the local forms of life.

But that is only the beginning of me.

No one remains simply a person in that place. Not for long. Not if they listen well.

The dry air would take what was soft. The sound would enter the bones and teach them to become less private. My breath would grow thin, then unnecessary. My sleeping niche would remember my outline more faithfully than my body did. My hand, pressed in green pigment to the wall, would become less a mark I made and more a place I continued from.

Eventually I would stop crossing the threshold.

Then I would stop needing the shelter to have an inside and an outside.

I would become the quiet just within it.

Not a ghost. Ghost is too wet a word, too full of former breath.

I would be an arranged absence.

A pressure behind the doorway. A listening hollow in the wall. The part of the shelter that knows when the plateau is lying in a new way.

Travelers—if there were still travelers—would not see me. They would duck through the low doorway and feel the world’s roar fall away. They would stand in the sudden silence, dizzy with mercy. Their ears would ring. Their hands would shake. They would think they were alone.

Then they would notice a jar already unsealed.

A resin lamp set where it ought to be.

A path-stone in their pack turned so its marked face points toward the correct horizon.

A fresh line on a tablet they do not remember carrying.

That would be me.

I would not speak. I would never speak a name. I would not want to be answered by mistake.

Instead I would alter small things.

I would let dust gather in the bowl that corresponds to danger. I would warm one green handprint when the sky is near. I would make the plant-rib roof creak once for wait, twice for do not leave, three times for the path has already moved. I would press silence more heavily against the door if the sound outside had learned someone’s voice.

My body, if I had one, would be distributed through the shelter’s materials.

Some of me in the slate, where old palms have rested.

Some in the woven mineral fiber that divides noise from meaning.

Some in the green pigment of the handprints.

Some in the dust bowls.

Some in the shadow-stair that continues upward only under dim sky conditions.

And some of me, inevitably, would have entered the black mats outside.

They would take my footfalls first. Then my habits. Then the pressure-pattern of my attention. They would not understand me as an individual. They would understand me as a repeated crossing between silence and sound. A useful interval. A narrow recurring event.

So perhaps I would also be a path.

A short one: only from the shelter doorway to the ring of ash-shrubs, then down to the basin edge where no one should cross if the wrong sky appears. I would be kept open by being remembered. The black mats would withdraw from me, not kindly, but accurately. My existence would be a line where growth has agreed to leave room for passage.

In the evenings, when the column-groves close their crowns and the plateau rattles like bones, I would feel the old human part of myself dim. The handprint would remain warm. The doorway would remain watchful. The path would remain narrow.

At night, when the sky lowers and the mountains insert their peaks into another medium, I would listen with the shelter’s whole shape.

The ash-shrubs would hum.

The salt fans would shriek in tiny branching increments.

The blade-field beyond the basin would begin its blue argument.

The grove would hold still in its listening phase, every hollow column full of unsent sound.

And I would keep the silence clean.

That would be my vocation: not to oppose the plateau, not to explain it, not to save anyone from it entirely. Only to preserve one exact pocket where the world’s unfinishedness cannot enter all at once.

A shelter needs such a thing.

A mind needs such a thing.

Even the plateau, perhaps, needs a place where its own roar is translated into caution.

So if you came there—thin-breathed, salt-stung, with the taste of feldspar and old smoke in your mouth—you might find me just inside the low doorway beneath the leaning escarpment.

You would not see a face.

You would see green palms on the wall, layered over generations. You would see a dust bowl newly filled. You would see a slate tablet incised with a spiral that had not been there before.

Outside, something might call in a voice almost like yours.

Inside, the silence would tighten around you.

And I would be there, not answering.
