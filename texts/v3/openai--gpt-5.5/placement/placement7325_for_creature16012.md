I would not find myself at the rim first, though I would think I wanted to.

The rim is too obvious a longing: the cliff, the thermals, the low world hidden under weather, the gliders flashing like pieces of broken mirror. I would visit it, certainly. I would stand behind the marked line with one gloved palm on a warm pylon and feel the updraft worry the seams of my suit. I would look down until looking became a kind of falling. I would imagine rivers incorrectly. I would imagine trees as soft electrical faults in the air.

But I would not belong there.

Nor would I belong in the deepest planning halls, among the people who move districts by finger-widths per year and speak in centuries without raising their voices. I would admire them too much. I would become uselessly reverent around their maps.

I think I would be found in the Archive of Load-Bearing Errors.

Not in the public walkways where children are brought to laugh and then become solemn, but lower down, in the older tilted levels where the stabilized failures still move a little after sunset. I would work where a corridor slopes three degrees farther than its builders intended, where a stair ends in a wall of salt-stone, where an observation window now looks directly into the mineral face that swallowed half the room over two hundred years.

I would be one of the people who listens to buildings confess.

A seal-listener, perhaps. Or more formally: a junior interpreter of pressure boundaries and structural regret, attached to the eastern Archive, third maintenance lineage, permitted to open historical locks under supervision, licensed to touch old gaskets bare-handed only in interior atmosphere.

I would be narrow, dust-pale, long-fingered. My second eyelids would be amber and quick. I would have the pressure nerves along my wrists and jaw, the ones that make some people uncomfortable because they can feel a leak before they can explain it. My suit would be old but exact: ash-colored outer shell, smoked violet faceplate, left cuff patched in three materials because that joint never sits correctly after an auger incident in my apprenticeship. There would be glider-scale sewn inside the hood, not visible except when I take it down in a safe room. A gift from a rim-keeper cousin, maybe. Or a boast I pretend is only insulation.

My work would begin before noon.

No one sensible begins delicate work at noon, but the Archive has its own schedule because failed buildings keep poor hours. Certain cracks speak only when the stone is expanding. Certain seals hold all morning and begin lying after the sun crosses the tower ribs. So I would cross the bright open miles in a municipal crawler with two other technicians, not talking except by clicks, watching dust move in small vertical threads ahead of an electrical tension.

The Archive would rise out of the flat like a thought that had changed its mind.

Leaning towers. Braced towers. Towers with collars of black metal around their waists. Walkways strung between them at angles that make the body distrust the eye. Old foundations exposed behind glass. Children’s chalk-stone marks on the safer walls. Warning panels polished by generations of gloved hands:

THE FLOOR BELOW IS ASKING TO BE LIGHTENED.

DO NOT TRUST THE LEFTWARD DOOR.

THIS CHAMBER IS STABLE ONLY WHEN EMPTY.

I would love that place in the private, unsentimental way my people love useful dangers.

The Archive would not be a ruin to me. It would be a library whose books are mass, heat, compression, salt migration, and human embarrassment. Every failed tower would be a sentence begun in arrogance and revised by the plateau. My task would be to read the revisions.

I would carry a kit at my hip: dust-state cards, seal chalk, a ceramic probe, static combs, listening pads, a little spool of emergency membrane, ancestor-wax for temporary closure, and a folded cloth for collecting any forbidden moisture produced by accident, injury, or grief.

Mostly I would touch things.

Not dramatically. Not with mystic reverence. With the small careful touches of a trade.

Two fingers against a gasket lip.

Knuckles on a door seam.

Wrist pressed to a pipe to feel whether the vibration belongs to machinery or stone.

Palm flat against a wall while the old tower gives its evening tick, tick, tick, each sound moving through my bones as information.

I would know the different silences of doors.

A good seal is not silent. It has a held quality, a contained pressure, a faint inwardness. A bad seal may be too quiet, like someone pretending sleep. A failing seal whispers before instruments hear it. A very old seal sometimes hums in a voice almost like an insect because powdered mineral has entered the lip and learned the rhythm of closure.

Those would be my companions: doors, membranes, hinges, warnings, old mistakes.

And animals, of course.

Pipe-drummers would live in the Archive despite every regulation against nesting in historically significant conduit. They would steal my tagged fasteners. I would pretend fury while leaving harmless scraps where they could find them. In dusk season they would drum on the leaning ventilation stacks, and the whole eastern yard would answer in different alloys: hollow bronze, cracked ceramic, black iron under strain, composite ribs from a repair two centuries newer than the tower it held upright.

I would know which rhythm belonged to which male. I would deny knowing.

There would be powder mites in my cuffs no matter how well I combed. There would be glass threaders in the sealed-off stair of Tower Nine. There would be a warm-stone mimic colony outside the old west compressor mouth, marked, monitored, respected. Once a year someone would suggest removing them for visitor safety. Once a year the Archive council would refuse, because their heat signatures reveal changes in the buried venting better than instruments do.

I think I would be young enough to still feel impatience and old enough to be ashamed of it.

Perhaps I would have grown up in a family of membrane tenders, people who wanted me to work in clean culture halls where the films shimmer politely under charge combs. Instead I chose failure. Old floors. Crooked doorways. The civic memory of bad calculations. My mother would say, with affection and irritation, that I had always preferred a cracked thing if it would tell me why it cracked.

My father, more practical, would check my suit before every shift even after I was certified.

Affection is a tightened collar ring. Love is someone noticing the right cuff before it becomes a leak.

Indoors, in the Archive’s habitable spine, I would remove my mask only in the staff chamber. The air there would taste of warm ceramic, filtered breath, old dust, and the bitter acid paste we eat during long shifts. There would be a communal wall panel made from the mineralized dead of former Archive workers. Not a shrine exactly. A working surface. Load-bearing. Their names would be etched in maintenance notation at the edge, half memorial, half inspection record.

I would often sit with my back against that wall during meal intervals, because it holds heat long after sunset.

The elders would disapprove of this informality while never telling me to stop.

One of them, a woman called Saal-of-the-Third-Brace in public records, would be nearly architectural herself. She would sit with sensor cords around her wrists, listening to foundation data from the whole eastern field. Her eyes would be clouded by age and dust scarring, but she would know when a tower had shifted before the graph updated.

She would ask me, every few days, “What does the floor want?”

At first I would answer like a student:

“Relief.”

“Bracing.”

“Reduced thermal load.”

“Abandonment.”

Later, if I became any good, I would answer less quickly.

“The floor wants us to stop calling this a floor.”

Or:

“It wants the west machines removed but not the wall.”

Or:

“It wants six years and no ceremonies.”

That would please her. Not visibly. She might click once through her teeth, which from her would be extravagant praise.

My private fascination would be one specific door.

Deep in the Archive, below the guided routes, there would be a pressure door from the first century of settlement: round-edged, basalt-colored, too thick, overbuilt in the manner of people who did not yet understand that strength and wisdom are not the same. Its chamber would have tilted around it. The floor on one side would be higher by half a body. The hinges would have long ago stopped bearing evenly. Yet the seal would still hold.

Not perfectly.

No living seal holds perfectly forever.

But it would hold beyond expectation, beyond model, beyond the embarrassment of its foundation. I would visit it whenever my duties brought me near. I would clean the dust from its lip with a static comb. I would press my wrist to the frame and feel the tension: not failure, not success, but negotiation.

A door that should have become useless, still doing its work.

I would not romanticize it aloud. My people distrust that. But privately I would think of it as an elder.

Perhaps one evening, during an exhausted vapor alert, that door would matter.

The sky would dim without warning. The city would taste the impossible wetness first, a faint wrong sweetness in the air. Membranes would unfurl across the Archive yards. Visitors would be herded into safe interiors. Drummers would vanish into conduits. The old towers would tense.

Somewhere, a modern lock would fail because modern things, too, make old mistakes.

A small pressure imbalance. A corridor exposed to dust-wet air. Salt beginning to wake in the walls.

I would be nearest. Not the most senior, not the bravest, only nearest, which is often what duty is made of.

I would run, though running is foolish. I would hear three elders shout at me over the radio in three different codes. I would ignore two and obey the one that mattered. At the old first-century door I would find what I already knew: its chamber crooked, its hinge load uneven, its gasket ancient but willing.

I would ask it to hold one more wrongness.

I would fit emergency membrane into the warped lower seam. I would pack ancestor-wax into the place where stone had moved away from metal. I would brace my shoulder against a frame that had begun its regret before my lineage had names. The leak would hiss against my glove. My wrist nerves would burn with pressure information too complex to separate from pain.

And the door would close.

Not cleanly. Not heroically. With a grinding reluctance and a final dull contact like a large animal settling onto its folded legs.

Held.

Behind it, people would have time to seal the next chamber. To save the children on the public route. To prevent wet air from entering the foundation exhibits where one drop in the wrong salt seam could rewrite the Archive’s lower levels.

Later, officials would call it an incident. Reports would be filed. The modern failed lock would be condemned. My patch would be described as acceptable under emergency conditions but aesthetically poor. Saal-of-the-Third-Brace would tell me I had wasted too much membrane and then send my parents a private commendation.

I would keep a flake of the old gasket when it was finally replaced.

Not as relic. As instruction.

Years later, I might become the one sitting with cords at my wrists, listening to the eastern field breathe. My body would dry further. My hands would become more precise and less beautiful. Apprentices would think me severe because I would stop them before crooked doorways and ask, “What does the floor want?” They would answer too quickly. I would let silence teach them the rest.

At death, if I had any right to request it, I would not ask for my salts to go into a family filter.

I would ask to be made into a seal.

A small one. Nothing grand. Not a monument door or council chamber lock. Those are too ceremonial and too often inspected by people who want to be seen inspecting them.

I would prefer a service lock in the Archive of Load-Bearing Errors, halfway between the public route and the old tilted levels, where apprentices pass carrying tools, where pipe-drummers sometimes drum above the dusk vents, where children on their humility tours press gloved hands to textured warning panels and learn that ground remembers.

There I would become part of a boundary.

Not closed.

Sealed.

Permitting relation under controlled terms.

Holding inside and outside in their proper tension.

Feeling, in whatever dry and mineral way remains, the daily warmth enter the frame, the night contraction, the dust asking at the edges, the pressure difference, the palm of a young worker checking whether I still hold.

And under me, of course, the plateau would continue its slow arithmetic.

It would move me by fractions.

It would teach my door regret.

It would accept me not as an answer, but as another layer in the long negotiation.
