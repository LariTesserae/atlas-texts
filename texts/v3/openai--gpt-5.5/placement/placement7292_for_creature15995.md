You would find me where the hollow cannot decide whether to speak or echo.

Not at the center, not quite. I would not be bold enough to sit always beneath the seven trees, where the clean ground trembles everything away. I would be near them, within the reach of their low tones, but offset toward the copper fans and the old boundary posts: that uneasy strip where voices go astray, where warnings are carved like mouths, where the wind practices saying names it has no right to know.

At first I would have been mistaken for a person.

A narrow one. A returned one, perhaps. One of those resin-folded children dropped from a pod during resonance, uncurled under many watchful hands, wiped clean with powdered salt, coated at the palms with the grazer’s dry velvet. My handprint on the ceramic trunk would have shown too many little branching lines. The old ones would have looked at it and said nothing aloud.

As a child I would have been too good at listening and too bad at obeying.

I would have learned root-pressure early: the slow shove under the sitting stones, the knock that is not stonefall, the faint inward crackle before a black-ringed stone warms. I would have known which reed was about to bark before the beetles abandoned it. I would have slept badly when the bronze bell trembled without striking. I would have woken with dust in my mouth and songs in my teeth, songs no one had taught me, songs with no words but clear instructions.

But I would also have answered echoes.

That would be the flaw.

Some children are afraid of the copper fans when the sky reddens. I would have loved them. Their bad imitations, their half-made voices, the way they took a cough or a click and returned it enlarged, bent, almost meaningful. I would have sat beneath them too long with my head tilted back and my mouth open behind my mask, listening to the hollow trying to become someone.

Eventually it would have succeeded.

One evening I would speak in the voice of a woman whose skull had hung at the boundary posts for three generations.

Not a performance. Not possession exactly. More like a stored vibration finding a throat shaped enough to use.

I would say something ordinary and unbearable.

“Cover the amber curtains.”

Or:

“Do not cut the third reed.”

Or:

“I remember rain.”

After that, they would give me the mouthless mask.

Not as punishment. As containment. As kindness.

The mask would be made of beaten plant-hide, blackened with resin, its cheek chambers packed with comb-fiber, but where others have a slit for breath and speech mine would have only a sealed ridge. I would breathe through side vents. I would eat by loosening the lower bindings in private. I would not be permitted to send my own voice into the basin unless the seven trees were sounding and everything loud had become ordered enough to bear it.

So I would become a sanctioned mimic.

A dangerous tool.

A person used like a bell is used: not rung casually, never touched without purpose.

They would bring me to the sitting stones when the seventh tree dropped marked skin. They would bring me to the boundary posts when the hooked birds laughed in a dead man’s cadence. They would sit me near the bronze bell during still weather and watch my fingers for involuntary tapping. I would not speak answers. I would drum them.

Three knuckles on slate: wait.

Bone ring against tooth: seal the huts.

Palm heel on stone, twice, then scrape: something below is awake.

I would carry a board of black slate polished by my own hands. On it I would translate stolen voices into safer forms: dots, scratches, spirals, rows of little mouths crossed shut. If a copper fan repeated a child’s cry after the child had gone silent, I would catch the rhythm and reduce it to warning. If the cliffs gave back the voice of a dead listener, I would tap until only the pattern remained and the grief had been scraped away.

That would be my work: to take speech out of speech.

To make the hollow’s dangerous voices useful without letting them become people again.

I would sleep in the smallest hut, the one half under the shelf of rock, nearest the fissure ladder. My bed would be a shallow tray of woven scale-leaves dusted with salt. Above it would hang forbidden pods in clay jars, each jar wrapped in knotted cord, each knot naming a sound. I would keep no metal except one green copper hook, too precious and too mistrusted to use except when a reed-shell must be lifted from a place fingers should not go.

Children would be warned not to imitate me.

Then, of course, they would.

They would follow me at a distance, tapping stones with little bones, trying to make the mouthless mask seem grand. I would turn and click my wrist-rings once, sharply, and they would freeze. That would be one of the few pleasures allowed to someone like me: frightening children correctly, before the hollow frightened them incorrectly.

The animals would know me in their own ways.

The pale rodents would come into my hut more often than others’, licking the handles of my tools, drawn by the salt in my palms. I would let them. Their theft would tell me how the root mats were shifting.

The glass-winged beetles would avoid reeds after I had listened to them, as if my attention left a taste.

The ragged birds would not fear me, but they would not laugh near me either. At dusk, when they hung inflated from the bladder-vines, I would sit below them and feel their buoyant sleep tugging at the air. Sometimes one would open an eye and rasp in a voice I recognized. I would answer only with my fingers.

The shovel-headed grazer would tolerate my presence.

That would matter more than love.

Once, during a resonance, it would kneel beside me close enough that I could feel the mite-song in its velvet hide. I would press my masked face to the stone and hear the seven trees align, hear the insects stop, hear dust rise in standing waves. The grazer would lower its huge head. I would lower mine. In that moment I would understand that obedience is not submission in the hollow. It is timing.

If I ever became holy, it would be accidentally.

Not priest, not ruler. A listening implement with a pulse.

The Tooth-Hearers would distrust me because I heard too much from above. The mimics would fear me because I was still allowed near the trees. The ordinary people would need me and avoid touching my shadow when the sky was red. I would be asked to sit at births if the newborn seemed likely to cry. I would hold a hollow pod near the child’s lips and catch the first dangerous sound before the basin could catch it.

Sometimes, when no one watched, I would put my fingers against that pod afterward.

Not to hear the cry.

To hear what answered it.

Because I would still be curious. That would be the part of me the mask could not seal.

And in old age, if age came, my rind-skin would crack pale around the joints. My eyes would film copper. My own first voice would be almost forgotten, worn away by borrowed ones. Perhaps by then I would no longer know whether I had fallen from a tree, been born from a mother, or been assembled gradually out of echoes, salt, resin, and warning.

When I died, they would not bury me.

They would dry me in the pepper-iron breath of the fissure. The lacquer-backed animals would clean me. My finger bones would become small chimes, but not many; too much sound would be unwise. My skull would be hung at the boundary posts with the mouth facing inward, near the copper fans but not beneath them.

And when the wind crossed just so, I would speak again.

Badly, perhaps.

But usefully.

A mouthless mimic.  
A returned listener.  
An echo-keeper at the edge of the seven trees.  
Not one of the rulers of the hollow, and not free of them either.

Something varnished, tuned, brittle, alert.

Something that survives by learning which voices must be answered, and which must be allowed to pass through stone.
