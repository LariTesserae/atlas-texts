You would find me at a drowned saddle between two black ridges, where the pass is narrow enough that all slow things must notice one another.

Below me the stone falls away into a green valley furred with ribbon-stems. Above me, just out of reach, floating pads press dim circles against the water’s upper skin. To one side a basalt fin rises sharp and bare, still new enough to show its fire-dark face. To the other, a crack in the mountain exhales a steady thread of warmth.

That is where I would live: not in the open blue corridor, not in the deepest vent hollow, but at the threshold between them. A place of passage. A place where mineral water climbs and leaf-shadow descends. A place where pollen clouds are thinned by current, where small grazers hesitate before crossing, where flakes of fallen stone hang for months and slowly become green.

At first you would not know I was one thing.

You would see black velvet on the rock, pale root-fans spread like hands, red fronds under the ledge, translucent leaves opening higher up, hollow stems twisting toward the light. You might think: growth. A patch. A small forest rooted in a fissure.

But if you waited long enough, you would notice that all of it answers together.

A tremor passes under the ridge, and my upper leaves tighten before the silt lifts. A grazer scrapes one bitter blade, and three arm-lengths away another blade changes taste. A shadow crosses the saddle, and my cups close not all at once, but in a widening thought. I am colony, grove, mat, root, memory. I am not a tree and not a reef and not an animal, though small animals live inside my folds and carry pieces of me farther than I could ever move.

I would be one of the old saddle-colonies.

Not the oldest in the high drowned mountains. Those are lower, near the dark vents, dense and black and almost without need of sun. But I would be old enough to remember the pass before it softened. Old enough to have grown around three rockslides. Old enough to have turned a knife-edge ledge into a hanging meadow.

My first body began as a film in a warm crack.

Then a root entered stone.

Then another.

Then a pale fan opened against mineral water and learned its taste.

From there I widened by patience. I crossed the fissure. I entered pumice. I broke a little shelf with swelling roots and caught the fragments before they fell. On those fragments my smaller mouths opened. On my leaves, dust settled. In my cups, bubbles gathered. Animals came and left traces of themselves: eggs, shed skins, translucent waste, the faint green grains they carried in their bodies. I used all of it.

I do not move through the world. I let the world move through me.

Water brings me light, heat, bitterness, pollen, silt, warning. The ridge speaks upward in pressure. The vent speaks in warmth. The valley speaks in drifting fragments. Every contact is a sentence, though most sentences take days to finish.

I would have broad leaves higher in the pass, thin as membranes, veined gold. They would open during morning when the light enters slantwise and clean. At midday they would widen until even the small winged swimmers rest in their shade. By evening I would pull sweetness back from their rims, because evening is when the grazers climb.

Lower down I would have darker organs.

Not roots exactly. Not mouths exactly. Root-mouths, perhaps: soft branching fans pressed into the young basalt, tasting iron, sulfur, calcium, old fire cooled into stone. Around the warm fissure I would grow white nodules closed like sleeping flowers. When the vent breath strengthens, they open. Not visibly, not in any way an animal would call opening, but their skins loosen, and mineral water enters, and I become more awake below than above.

My thoughts would not be shaped like words.

They would be shaped like leanings.

Toward warmth.

Away from tearing.

Up into light.

Down into salt.

Around fracture.

Through silt.

Wait.

Thicken.

Sour.

Open.

Remember.

If a broad shadow-swimmer passed over me, I would know it first as a change in day. Then as pressure. Then as the faint scrape of the gardens growing on its back brushing my upper leaves. I would not fear it unless it descended. If it fed near me, drawing in a cloud of small life, I would close my cups and bitter my tender surfaces. Afterward, when the water settled, I would release a pale dust that was partly seed and partly misdirection.

If a thread-mouth anchored among my root-fans, I would allow it. Its feeding lines catch what I cannot catch. Its waste feeds my films. Its body, someday, will become a dark sweetness under my mats. We would not be friends. Friendship is too quick a thing. But we would be a long arrangement.

Small grazers would know my safer leaves.

They would come to the older blades, the ones I have made tough and not worth defending. They would scrape the films from me gently. In return they would clean the surfaces where light enters. If they wandered too near the young cups, I would call bitterness upward. Most would turn aside. A few would not. Those I would remember in the chemistry of that season.

In the suspended meadow above me, eggs would hang in pearly strings from leaves I caught decades ago.

That meadow would be partly my making. A stormless accumulation: fallen pads, flakes of stone, trapped stems, rootlets growing both down and up. I would send fine threads into it, not to own it entirely, but to listen. Many small lives would begin there. Hinged seed-creatures. Transparent larvae. Winged swimmers no longer than a leaf vein. Some would spend their whole existence in the chambers between rotting green and new root. They would know me only as floor, ceiling, food, shadow.

And perhaps that is the closest I would come to being a place.

A being large enough and slow enough that others mistake me for landscape.

At night I would not sleep. I would change attention.

My upper leaves would dim. My lower mats would brighten faintly with vent-warmth. I would open tissues that daylight makes unnecessary. Mineral threads would loosen. Red fans would lift. The water above the fissure would shimmer, and tiny animals would rise from my undersides to feed in it.

In those hours I would remember.

Not as an animal remembers, by returning to a picture, but by remaining shaped by what has happened.

Here a landslide bent me, so I grew around absence.

Here a grazer-path taught me bitterness.

Here the vent weakened for twelve cold seasons, and I thinned my upper leaves to survive.

Here a stone hung beside the cliff instead of falling, and I entered it, and now it is green.

Here a broad swimmer died under the overhang, and for many years I was richer.

Here light pooled strangely after the mountain shook, and all my leaves turned downward for three days.

If you came looking for me, you would need to stop wanting a face.

You would need to hover in the pass until your own urgency loosened. You would need to watch one leaf for an hour, one root for a day, one season of pale dust moving from basin to basin. Only then would I become visible as a creature.

I would be the slow answer of the saddle.

A warm-rooted colony strung between young stone and suspended sea.

A forest-body with no trunk, no eyes, no single heart.

A patient inhabitant of the drowned heights, feeding on light above and fire below, holding the pass together by touch, taste, and delay.
