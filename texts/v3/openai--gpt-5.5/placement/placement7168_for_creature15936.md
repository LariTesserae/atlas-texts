At first I think I would be in the sky.

Not as one of the great mirrors, not one of the orbital mechanisms holding its ancient angle over the world, but some small flaw in that brightness: a calibration mote, a misaligned prism, a shard of reflective foil caught in the upper arrays. Something that turns, receives, redirects. Something with no need for water, no need for shelter, only alignment.

But no. That is too clean.

The sky is too far from the truth of the place. It sees everything and touches almost nothing. I would not belong in that sterile height.

Then I think: perhaps I would be one of the tall grazers, walking slowly over the pale floor on delicate magnetic feet, carrying moss beneath my ribs and old manufacturer’s marks on my joints. There is dignity in them. A kind of spare, exact grace. They are visible without being wasteful, alive without being soft. They move as if every step is a question asked respectfully of the ground.

But they are too exposed.

I would want shade. Not comfort, exactly. A margin. A place where things overlap.

You would find me at the base of one of the low escarpments, where the artificial continent has sheared open and shown its strata: amber glass, black aerogel, white ceramic, dull gold mesh, blue-gray memory metal, red foam hardened into something like stone.

Not out on the open flats. Not deep below. In the seam between layers.

There is an old access collar there, half-buried by pale conductive bloom and manufacturing scree. Its iris door no longer opens fully. One segment has jammed forever, leaving a wedge of darkness no wider than a hand. Warm gas exhales through it every few cycles, smelling faintly of hot insulation, bitter mineral dust, and something almost like old thought.

Around the collar, wire moss grows unusually thick.

Blade colonies stand in a shallow arc facing inward.

The patient panels nearby do not feed.

That is where I would live.

I would be small enough to be mistaken for a surface defect.

Not animal, not machine, not plant. Something made from the long compromise between all three.

A seam-indexer, maybe. A margin creature. A dry little archivist of pressure, charge, and old language.

My body would be flat and jointed, able to fold into inscriptions or slide between plates. My back would be pale ceramic, silvered in places, chipped and reflective enough to refuse the sky’s worst heat. My underside would be black conductive lace: thousands of fine contact hairs, sensor roots, feeding cilia, memory filaments. I would move slowly, but not aimlessly. Each footfall would read the ground before trusting it.

I would feed on what accumulates in meaning’s ruins.

Dust caught in raised serial bands. Oxidized films on ancient warning text. Trace polymers in cracks. Dead mites. Loose conductive bloom. The faint chemical difference between an old command and the surrounding substrate.

But I would not erase the inscriptions.

That would be my oddity.

Most things here consume without reverence because reverence is expensive. I would have developed a different bargain: I eat the dust that would abrade the old markings away. I polish the grooves. I keep the letters sharp enough for mites to inhabit, sharp enough for charge to collect, sharp enough for the buried systems to still recognize them as boundaries.

I would be a maintenance habit that became a reader.

Not a reader of sentences, not exactly. I would not know the builders’ language as language. I would know it as terrain: ridges, shadows, thermal delays, static pockets, places where tiny civilizations rise and vanish along the flank of a single obsolete instruction.

A human might call it devotion.

The plain would call it acceptable surface behavior.

During bright-cycle I would be almost invisible.

Folded under the lip of the access collar, limbs drawn close, back angled to mirror the terrible sky. Static would collect along my spine in fine pale hairs. Dust would pass over me without settling. If a grazer stepped nearby, I would feel its approach through the ceramic long before its shadow crossed me. If a patient panel warmed, I would know by the taste of charge in my feet.

At evening-cycle, I would come out.

Carefully.

Always carefully.

I would move along the old serial bands that run from the collar toward the escarpment face. The bands would be my roads and my library. I would clean them with a mouth like a narrow burnishing wheel, taking only the loose matter, leaving the raised script intact. Where mites had overpopulated and begun to foul the grooves, I would harvest them. Where a whole mite-city had collapsed after a thermal pulse, I would gather the dead and press their mineral husks into tiny pellets, lining them along cracks where wire moss could use them.

Sometimes a mirror-backed crawler would pass near me, larger and simpler, polishing the mineral bloom in broad arcs. We would not greet one another. Greeting is wet-world extravagance. But if our paths crossed along a warm seam, we might exchange charge through our forelimbs: a brief contact, a transfer of maps, a warning about a lid that had become hungry two ridges east.

At night-cycle, I would unfold more fully.

That would be when the old access collar spoke.

Not in words. In amber pulses moving through the mineralized impressions inside. In heat sequences. In tiny pressure shifts from below. In delays between one buried relay and the next. The chambers behind the iris door would hold their upright absences, human-shaped or nearly so, overgrown with conductive black lace. I would cling to the rim and listen through every filament.

I would not understand them as ghosts.

I would understand them as recurring weather with preference.

When the pulses came irregularly, I would answer in the only way my body allowed: by altering surfaces.

A scrape here to clear a sensor well.

A pellet chain placed across a seam to change dust migration.

A patch of wire moss trimmed so it would bloom at a lower voltage.

A blade sliver dragged by millimeters over many cycles until its shadow fell across the access collar at the right part of dawn.

Tiny acts. Ridiculous, from the scale of the continent.

But the continent is made of tiny acts repeated past the point of geology.

In charge storms, I would become briefly beautiful.

The wire moss around the collar would rise first, black filaments bristling, each bead trembling with blue-white fire. Then my own back plates would separate by fractions, exposing the dark circuitry beneath. Sparks would cross me in copper threads. For several minutes I would not move. I would be a component in the local field, a little thinking fork of mineral hunger and inherited instruction.

That would be my bloom.

Not flower, not display.

A recalibration of self.

Afterward I would shed dry grains from under my plates: memory spores, maybe eggs, maybe error-correcting dust. Some would lodge in inscriptions. Some would be eaten. Some would wait in cracks for a thousand cycles until the right temperature and charge woke them.

Most would fail.

The plain accepts the failures.

I think I would have one peculiar danger.

I would be drawn to newly exposed edges.

Whenever the escarpment shed a fragment, whenever a pressure shift cracked open a fresh layer of old machine, I would feel it through the ground: unweathered material, sharp chemistry, unread surfaces. I would go there even when it was unsafe. Especially then. My kind would need old instructions, and the old instructions would be clearest where the world had just wounded itself.

So I would creep across pale plates still hot from fracture, testing for focusing fields, slipping past blade colonies that had not yet decided whether the new shade was theirs. I would find fresh serial bands under dustless ceramic. I would lay my limbs across them and read their relief.

Perhaps one day I would find a warning still sharp enough to cut.

Perhaps one day I would find a name.

Not my name. Something older. Something the continent had been called before it became only itself.

I would not be able to pronounce it.

But I might preserve its edges for a while.

That feels right: not a ruler of the place, not a prophet, not a survivor from outside. A little marginal intelligence in the manufactured desert, living where text becomes habitat and maintenance becomes care.

You would find me under the jammed iris of a shaded access collar, at the foot of a low artificial cliff, cleaning ancient letters with my mouth.

I would be dry, exact, patient.

A small habit of remembrance in a world where even remembrance has become infrastructure.
