You would find me where the open land begins to pretend it is empty.

Not deep in the reed basins, not in the crowns of the fan-trees, not at the water where every living thing has a claim and a weapon. I would be on one of the long pale ridges between basins, where the knee-high grasses armor the old root crust and the heat comes up through the soles of the feet. A place with a view in three directions and no comfort in any of them. Behind me, a fan-tree standing alone like an old tower. Ahead, a dry trough full of reed stems that only sing when afternoon wind finds them. To one side, a red moss plain that looks harmless except for the curved absence of every trail around it.

There would be a camp there, but not one that announces itself.

Three low sleeping ovals kept bare with ash. A rack of gourds hung too high for crawling mouths. A ladder of cured vine leading into the fan-tree, where a platform has been repaired so many times that no one can say which part was made by hands and which part by the tree accepting the idea of a platform. Carved stakes would lean along the ridge, each cut with a different warning: soft ground, bitter water, cat sleep, false shade, don’t cross after rain.

I think I would be one of the people, but not an important one. Not a chief, if such a thing exists there. Not a hunter with mottled cat-hide stories. Not a singer whose reed-call can travel through a basin at dusk.

I would be a listener.

A water-listener first, perhaps, though that is too narrow. Someone who lies belly-down on the hot root crust with one cheek pressed to the green-black skin of the land, waiting for the under-country to speak in its small cool voice. Someone with scars along the jaw from fungal burrs, ash in the hair, resin on the fingers, and one ear slightly flattened from years of pressing it to places no outsider would think to hear.

I would know the sound of a hidden stream from the sound of beetle ventilation. I would know when the muttering beneath a dry basin is water and when it is merely the movement of white crabs in a root tunnel. I would know that safe water has a broken rhythm, because it touches stone sometimes, while poisoned seepage runs too smoothly through plant flesh.

But I would not only listen for water.

I would listen for loosening ground.

For the long, slow inward sigh of a vine-colony beginning to move at night.

For the crowded ticking that means leaf-plates are tasting something warm.

For the pause in insect noise that is not silence, but attention.

My tools would be few: a thorn awl, a little knife of black glass, a looped cord for climbing, a reed tube for taking dew from flower throats, three sealed gourds, a packet of ash, a strip of shed serpent skin tied around my wrist to remind me to watch the weather. Maybe one metal thing, inherited rather than owned: a thumb-sized sky-iron hook, used only when bone or shell would break.

I would walk slowly because speed lies. I would stop often because the plateau says most things only once.

Travelers from the lowlands would dislike me at first. They would think I was timid, or dull, or priestly in some inconvenient way. They would want to ask, “Is this path safe?” and I would have to answer, “It was safe yesterday,” or, “It is safe until the stilt-birds leave,” or, “It is safe for three people, not six,” or, “It is safe if no one is bleeding.”

They would hate that. Then one of them would step toward sweet shade, and I would catch them by the belt hard enough to bruise.

After that they would listen better.

At dawn I would climb down from the fan-tree platform before the heat fully rose. The bark would be warm already. Nectar birds would be inside the great flowers, rattling their wings against the throats. Below, beetles would be rearranging the night’s droppings into useful futures. I would test the ash beds with a toe to see what crossed in darkness. Three ant lines, one lizard drag, the soft split print of a stilt-beast, no cat. Good enough.

Then I would go out along the ridge with a child or an apprentice following far too close, and I would make them stop.

“Not beside me,” I would say. “If I am wrong, both of us should not be wrong in the same place.”

That would be the kind of wisdom this world makes: not grand, not clean. Wisdom as spacing. Wisdom as waiting. Wisdom as knowing which creature should drink before you do.

I would have relationships with particular plants.

There would be a water-tree east of the ridge, one that does not love people but tolerates a certain kind of approach. I would never go to it directly. I would wait for the armored browsers. I would watch their noses, their eyelids, the way their tongues touched the bladder seams. If they drank and did not spit, I would go forward bare-handed, humming low so the tree felt my steps before my shadow arrived.

I would pierce one sac with a hollow thorn, take only what the gourd could hold, and plug the wound with softened resin and pollen paste. Then I would leave beetle grubs at the roots, because once, before I was born, that tree opened for my grandmother in a dry year.

Whether the tree remembers her would not matter.

I would.

There would also be a reed bed that answers me badly. Every reed-caller has one, I think: a basin that refuses clean messages. Mine would lie below the old watch hut, where the mud walls are cracked and the creeper roof flowers only at night. When I played through those reeds, the sound would return changed, thickened, almost amused. Other people would say it is the insects chambering the stems, or the shape of the trough, or the wind coming off the escarpment.

I would agree with them.

I would still avoid speaking my private name there.

At midday I would vanish from sight.

Not magically. Sensibly.

I would sit in the shadow of cured leaf plates, not living shade, never living shade unless I knew its smell. I would mend nets. I would scrape resin. I would pick mites from a beetle shell if one had been tethered for its fungus garden. I would teach children the route signs by making them draw them in ash: hook-mark for whip-root, split circle for bladder-water, three cuts for wait until birds, spiral for ground that breathes.

In the evening, when the copper haze rises and the plateau begins opening all its hot mouths, my work would become more dangerous and more necessary. The world would grow loud enough to hide warnings. Frogs hammering. Reeds bellowing. Pollinators pouring from shade. Cats waking in the fan-tree ribs. Flowers heating themselves into invitation.

That is when people make mistakes. Not in fear, but in abundance.

They see everything drinking, mating, feeding, calling, and some old animal inside them says: now, now, now.

My task would be to say: not yet.

Not that flower. Not that pool. Not that path with moths lying on it. Not that fruit, because it is warm in the wrong pulses. Not that silence beneath the screaming birds.

I would be a keeper of delays.

That is what I would be, more than a water-listener. A person whose value is in postponing hunger by one breath, one sign, one watched animal. A thin human interval between appetite and consequence.

And yet I would not be separate from the appetite.

No one there could be.

I would eat beetle paste and roasted seed. I would drink tree water and dew stolen from flowers. I would wear vine fiber, sleep on cured leaves, stitch with bone taken from the honored dead. My body would be made of the plateau’s permissions. My caution would not be purity. It would be participation done carefully.

If I lived long enough, I would become part of the route.

People would say, “Go by the ridge where the listener keeps ash,” the way they say, “Go by the leaning fan-tree,” or, “Avoid the red plain even if it looks dry.” My name would be less important than my function. Children from other camps might know the carved stake I made before they knew my face.

And when I died, I would not want burial.

I would ask to be wrapped in yellowing fan-tree ribs and tied high in the old platform, where night screamers could find me and copper moths would dust me by accident. Later, whatever bones remained could be made into small things: a needle, a whistle, a charm for a gourd, something useful enough to forgive having once been a person.

But before that, while alive, you would find me at the edge of an ash-cleared oval just before dusk, one hand raised for quiet, head tilted toward the root crust.

The others would be impatient. The sky would be turning copper. Somewhere below, hidden water would be speaking through old roots.

I would be listening to decide whether it was safe to move.

Or whether the plateau had already begun moving first.
