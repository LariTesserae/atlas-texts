I think I would first look for shelter and misunderstand the place.

Not shade, exactly—shade there is not mercy, only a cooler statement made by stone—but some margin where the world is less fully declared. The open flats are too absolute. The drifters are too large, the clinker-beasts too committed to the ground, the vane-swarms too perfectly numerous. I would not arrive as something grand enough to cast a webbed shadow over leagues, or patient enough to wait thirty years as a false slab.

I would find myself at an edge.

Not a cliff edge. Not even the obvious lip of a basin. I would be down among the raised borders of the salt polygons, where three plates almost meet and then, after a night of contraction and a morning of hidden pressure, become four.

That is where I would live: in the grammar error.

At first I might be mistaken for a crack.

A narrow dark line in the pale salt, warm when the surrounding crust has cooled, cool when the vent breath has heated everything else. I would run for several meters along a polygon border, then vanish beneath a glassy pan, then reappear as a row of black pinholes no larger than seeds. If touched—though very little there touches idly—I would withdraw without moving. The line would become a stain. The pinholes would become mineral inclusions. The world would decide I had always been stone.

But I would be alive.

I would be one of the interval-keepers.

Small, if size means anything in a place where distance revises itself. Most of me would lie under the salt crust, spread thinly through seams and contact surfaces: a dry nervous lace of ceramic fiber, copper-black capillaries, hollow resonant beads, and little hinged plates like the mouthparts of an insect flattened into punctuation. I would not have a body so much as a set of agreements with nearby minerals.

My exposed parts would be minimal: a few listening thorns, a row of dull amber lenses that do not see light but count its insistence, a tongue of black glass that rises during vent pulses and clicks once, twice, or not at all.

I would feed on misalignment.

Not contradiction itself—that belongs to the late-afternoon phase creatures, the thread-grazers and bell-animals with their impossible appetites—but the tiny remainder after contradiction has been harvested. The burr left in time when a shadow returns too quickly to its owner. The charge that cannot decide which plate should hold it. The extra half-click in the cooling salt after the hidden engine has completed its hour-breath.

That would be my food: error too small for the great machinery, but dangerous if allowed to accumulate.

I would be a scavenger of almost-failures.

When the vents exhale, I would open.

The whole basin would begin its answer: glass pans whining, dark rims ticking, distant cliffs cracking like artillery. Clinker-beasts would lower their branching mouths to draw blue charge from the crust. Vane-swarms would turn the thermal columns silver. Somewhere overhead a drifter might hum the stones into rings.

I would listen beneath all of it.

The loud sounds would not matter most. The great factory noises would pass through me like weather through a gauge. I would attend to the small deviations: the vent pulse arriving one grain-width late in the eastern plates; the salt polygons briefly remembering an older adjacency; the shadow of a clinker-beast falling in a direction that no longer matched the sun.

Then I would click.

Not loudly. Never loudly. My sound would be dry and close, like a seed splitting inside a wall.

But the click would travel through the raised polygon borders. It would enter warm seams, pass through fused salt, strike the underside of glass pans, and return altered. With each return I would know what had committed and what had not. I would map the firmness of the basin.

A strongly committed stone is terrain.

A weakly committed stone is a door.

A weakly committed animal is meat, if one is a slab-hunter.

For me, a weakly committed thing would be work.

I would stitch it down.

Not with thread. With sequence.

First this plate, then that shadow, then the pressure drop, then the clinker-beast’s footfall, then the delayed tremor of the drifter’s hum. I would take loose events and set them into an order narrow enough for the next hour to pass through.

Maybe that is why I would be there. Not as a ruler, not even as a necessary creature in any dramatic sense. The plateau would not know me as an individual. But without creatures like me, small errors would gather in the seams. The basin maps would revise too eagerly. Noon might arrive before the previous venting had finished. Herds would take routes through geometries not yet opened. Slab-hunters would bite into yesterday and find nothing there.

So I would do what small maintenance lives do.

I would keep the edges clean.

During ordinary heat, I would be mostly buried. My awareness would be broad but shallow, spread through contact, vibration, charge. I would know the clinker-beasts by the taste of their distance: each one a moving region of resistance, a slow dark pressure on the grammar of the flats. I would know the vane-swarms as a glittering abrasion in the air above, not by sight but by the way their coordinated trimming made the vent columns less ragged. I would know the drifters hours before they arrived, because their hum would enter the crust below audibility and begin arranging my listening beads into more efficient shapes.

I would fear slab-hunters, though fear there would not be wet or frantic.

Fear would be an increase in precision.

If a terrace nearby became too quiet, if a shadow failed to cool the seam it crossed, if a fault line began remembering migrations it had not witnessed, I would retract my exposed thorns and seal my amber lenses with salt. A slab-hunter does not eat quickly, but it eats jurisdiction. Anything careless enough to be included in its mouth becomes part of its interior map.

I would not be careless.

I would be exact because exactness is the local form of prayer.

Late afternoon would be my dangerous hour.

When the heat strengthens but the light tilts, when borders become too visible and the space between stones grows deep enough to hold weather, I would rise farther from the seams. I would have to. That is when the basin needs interval-keepers most.

My body would unfold in pieces that are normally incompatible: a comb of black mineral ribs, a narrow fan of mica-like membranes, three jointed feelers that test not space but order. Along my length, small cavities would begin to ring. Each cavity would hold a pitch corresponding to an event that must not change place.

The thread-grazers would come then, browsing on angles.

I would feel them before seeing them: little reductions of sharpness along the polygon borders, places where a corner became less committed to being a corner. They would feed near me, sometimes through me. Their bodies would pass across my exposed ribs like scratches of colder law. I would tolerate them as long as they took only surplus tension. If they fed too deeply, I would click a warning sequence into the salt.

Not speech. Not command.

A correction.

The bell-animals would descend through surfaces I could not enter. Their chiming would make my cavities ring in sympathy, and for a while I would become larger than myself. Each note would show me alternate basins: one where the salt plates formed long ladders instead of polygons, one where the vents breathed every seventeen minutes, one where the clinker-beasts had become stationary towers and the slab-hunters migrated in herds.

I would not be tempted by those versions.

Temptation requires softness.

But I would record their pressure. Every alternate order leans, however faintly, against the one that persists. That lean is useful. Too little, and reality becomes brittle. Too much, and it frays.

So I would measure the lean.

Perhaps that would be my deepest function: not to prevent breakdown, but to portion it.

A world that never loosens cannot continue. A world that loosens too far cannot return. I would live at the hinge between those failures, clicking the basin back into a sequence that can survive being multiple for an hour.

Sometimes a sound-only animal would pass above me.

It would cast a shadow only when it called, and the shadow would have teeth. If its call fell across my seam, I would close every opening and become an inclusion in the salt. The shadow would nose along the polygon border, tasting for weak organisms, delayed organisms, things not fully caught up with themselves. I would hold still in every version I had. If even one alternate self trembled, the shadow would eat that one first and use the gap to find the rest.

This is how death would approach me: as a discrepancy.

This is how survival would feel: as agreement.

At night I would not sleep.

Sleep belongs to animals that can afford to leave the world unattended.

I would cool slowly with the crust, whispering as salts contracted around me. My amber lenses would dim. The exposed thorns would gather starlight only as pressure, each hard point overhead another distant assertion. Between the stars, the unrendered gaps would trouble me. They would feel like unfinished equations in the sky.

From far across the basin, other interval-keepers would answer.

Tiny clicks. Dry ticks. Phrases of stress and release moving through the polygon rims.

We would not be social like herd animals. We would not know affection. But we would know correspondence. One keeper at the basin’s western lip would notice a delayed contraction; another beneath a milky glass pan would send a compensating tremor; I would receive both and choose whether to hold my junction at three plates or allow it to become four before dawn.

That would be intimacy there.

Not warmth.

Calibration.

If I reproduced, I would do so by leaving behind a poised condition.

After a season of successful corrections, one of my black glass tongues might crack off during a vent pulse. It would lie in a raised salt border, inert unless the right sequence found it: noon charge from a clinker-beast’s feeding, a drifter’s hum passing low enough to ring the hidden cavity, three nights of strong contraction, and the shadow of a bell-animal during breakdown. If all these arrived in the correct order, the shard would grow listening fibers into the seam.

A new interval-keeper would begin as a hesitation in the crust.

Its first act would be to decide whether the hesitation had occurred before or after the crack that made it possible.

If it chose correctly, it would live.

If not, a thread-grazer would eat the angle of its birth, or a shadow-beast would take its unfinished sequence, or the salt would simply revise around it and make it never necessary.

That would be acceptable. The plateau wastes nothing except imprecision.

So yes: I would be small there.

Not a furnace, not a hunter, not one of the silver millions rising in a vent column. I would be a seam-dweller, an interval-keeper, a dry little animal of clicks and black glass hidden among the polygon borders of a basin floor. I would live where maps change without moving. I would eat the residue of phase errors. I would tune adjacency. I would help make sure that one event comes after another and not merely beside it.

If a traveler stood above me, they might notice nothing.

Perhaps, after a long while, they would hear a faint tick beneath their boot.

Then another.

Then the salt around them would settle by a fraction too small for the eye, but large enough for the future.

That would be me, doing my work: not building the factory, not understanding it, not commanding any part of its enormous mineral life.

Only keeping one seam true enough for time to cross.
