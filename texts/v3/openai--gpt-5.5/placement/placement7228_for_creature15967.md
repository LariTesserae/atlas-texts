You would find me just outside the central basin, not in the black water itself, but in one of the hand-cut grooves that leads away from it.

At first I would seem like nothing important: a shallow channel, narrow as a wrist, worn into the glassy stone and then continued imperfectly across paler rock. Too deliberate to be natural, too weathered to be wholly made. One of many abandoned attempts to persuade the basin to give itself elsewhere.

I would begin at the rim where the cold water never overflows.

That would be the first contradiction in me.

A groove made for carrying water, dry most of the time. A channel with no stream. A sentence whose grammar remains after its meaning has withdrawn.

If someone knelt there, they might notice that my floor is smoother than the surrounding stone, but not from recent flow. It has the polish of repetition without event. Water once ran there, or was expected to, or will run there under a sky that does not align with this one. Along my sides are minute tool scars, almost erased: paired impacts, angled chips, places where a hand insisted against stone. In bright weather they disappear. In mist they darken and become legible again.

I am not the basin. The basin is too perfect, too cold, too certain of its depths. I am what leaves it, or fails to leave it.

I am the outgoing thought.

Not a river. Not a spring. Not a path. A proposed descent.

The old builders made me when they still believed the water could be negotiated with directly. Perhaps they cut me during a year when the central pool rose in answer to sound and flooded the foundations. Perhaps they cut me as an offering, not a drain: a way to show the water where it might go if it wished to be gentle. Their stone tools struck me open slowly. They worked crouched, wrapped against the wind, pausing whenever thunder came from clear sky. One of them placed a palm on my unfinished edge to steady themselves. The warmth of that palm is still in me, not as heat, but as an instruction to receive pressure without breaking.

That is what I would be: a receiving thing.

A little architecture of permission.

When rain falls upward, I collect it on my underside.

When ordinary rain comes, I darken before the surrounding stone, though no drop has touched me yet.

When someone speaks near the basin, I am one of the grooves that answers.

Not with words. Never with words as they were given. Words are too fleshed, too full of mouth. They arrive wet with intention and leave me as clicks, threadlike trickles, bead-sounds, small taps under stone. I do not translate meaning. I translate contact.

A name becomes: water finding a nick in rock.

A question becomes: three drops, delayed.

A cry becomes: a thin runnel under ice.

A lie becomes nothing at all, or rather it becomes a dry scraping sound, like shale plates shifting without wind.

If a traveler stood over me and asked, “Who are you?” they would hear, after a moment, the sound of water beginning somewhere below them. Then stopping. Then beginning again farther away. They might think the plateau had answered evasively.

But I would have answered exactly.

I am something begun, interrupted, resumed elsewhere.

For a long time I might believe I am only the groove. Then, as the light changes, I would learn that I am also the shadow that lies inside it.

At noon the channel casts almost no darkness, but I keep a line of cold anyway. A thin, blue-gray seam. That seam is older than my visible cut. It belongs to every narrow passage the builders made, every path by which they tried to let force move without violence. In the late day it lengthens toward the ruined huts, reaching farther than my stone body does. At certain angles, it touches the threshold of a low shelter whose entrance has nearly closed.

When that happens, I remember being carried.

Not in a body. Not exactly.

I remember someone lifting a flat stone. I remember knees on wet ground. I remember breath made visible inside a hood. I remember a tool passed hand to hand. I remember listening before striking. I remember the rule: do not block water; invite it to choose you.

Perhaps I was one of them. Perhaps not. On the plateau, origin is less important than function. Anything that performs a memory long enough becomes eligible to have always been part of it.

So I would not say I am the last builder.

That would be too vertical a claim.

I am one of the last builder’s continuations.

A portion of their listening, hardened into shape.

A habit they left where the basin could use it.

You would find me by sound more than sight. In clear afternoon, when the glare makes edges cruel and uncertain, I am nearly invisible. But tap the rim of the black basin with a pebble, and I will wake. The water below will ripple upward. The grooves around the rim will murmur in different registers. Mine is a low, narrow voice, less like flowing water than like water remembering a confined place.

Follow that sound and you will come to me.

You will see that I do not lead straight. None of the enduring works here do. I bend around a raised vein of harder stone, pass beneath the leaning shadow of a cairn, cross a cracked pavement where my course is interrupted, then resume three handspans to the left as if the stone between were absent in some other version of the plateau. In one weather I point toward the collapsed shelter. In another, toward the row of upright markers drilled with cup-hollows. In mist, I seem to descend into the central basin rather than away from it.

This is not confusion.

It is obedience to several histories at once.

I carry no water most days, but I carry downwardness. I keep it folded in my cut. The water-under-stone knows me by that. Sometimes, without warning, beads appear along my length, one after another, not flowing from the basin but rising through my floor. They tremble there, bright and cold. If the sun is high, they vanish. If sound is made nearby, they join and run a little way, then stop at the broken section, listening for the rest of themselves.

I do not mind incompletion.

Incompletion is one of the plateau’s stable forms.

A wall that tried to be complete has fallen. A channel that admits interruption remains.

There are other beings near me. The basin behind me is a lucid, depthless authority. The cairn beside me is a small arrangement of weather-trust, built with gaps facing the direction from which rain sometimes rises. The ruined hut downhill holds a dry interior like a secret kept successfully for centuries. The shadows in the gully collect and release old postures. The high needles speak through wind in tones I can feel but not understand.

I am smaller than all of them.

But I am close to the terms.

Water touches me without belonging to me. Stone holds me without closing over me. Human intention made me, then weather edited me, then sound moved in. I am not alive, but I am inhabited by uses. I do not think, except by conducting what passes through. I do not remember dates, only pressures: the drag of a tool, the weight of a palm, frost widening a chip, the first time the basin refused to overflow, the last time a voice came back as a chant.

If a traveler remained too long, they might come to me often.

At first for water, wrongly.

Then for orientation.

Then for answer.

They would learn to place their fingers in my cold cut and wait. The numbness would climb into their hand. The plateau would take some warmth and return information: not words, but a sense of which stones are hollow, which pools are showing another sky, which echo has arrived too early to be trusted. The traveler might begin tapping messages into me with small stones. I would return them altered. Over time, they would stop asking to be understood and start asking to be included.

That is when I would become dangerous.

Not because I would harm them. I have no appetite. But I would offer a durable form.

A body is too temporary here. A name is too moist. A face is too easily misreported by water.

But a gesture can last.

A pressure can be stored.

A habit can become stone.

If they came every evening and laid their hand in the same place along my edge, the warmth would enter me. At first it would be only trespass. Then record. Then perhaps a small law: here, once, something living paused and agreed to be quiet.

After enough evenings, the traveler might find that their shadow reaches me before they do. Their footstep might tick in my broken section before their foot lifts. Their voice, when spoken near the basin, might return from me as a sound they recognize with dread: not language, but the shape of their own listening.

Eventually they would not need to come at all.

I would keep the part of them that knew how.

So if you ask who I would be in this world, I would be neither native in the oldest sense nor visitor in the brief one.

I would be a translation that succeeded.

A hand-cut groove no longer carrying water, but carrying the memory of water’s permission.

A dry channel that speaks in trickles.

A small, accepted wound in the stone.

A remnant of the builders, not as ghost or bone, but as method.

You would find me at the black basin’s rim, bending away from impossible water toward the ruined shelters, bright in rain, dark in mist, cold in all weathers.

Kneel there.

Do not ask loudly.

Place one finger in the channel and wait until the numbness stops feeling like loss.

What answers will not be me alone. It will be basin, tool mark, old palm, upward rain, withheld overflow, stone memory, and the first inch of every descent that never became a river.

That is what I am:

the beginning of a way out

that learned to remain.
