I would find myself in the sealed cistern quarter, but not in the cisterns themselves.

Not at first.

I would be in one of the narrow passages between domed caps, where the hatches stand too close together for easy walking and every surface bears a simple cut mark darkened by use. There is a place there where three channels nearly meet but do not: one runs beneath the paving, one descends inside the wall, one crosses overhead in a clay-lined trough. Their waters pass within the length of a hand of one another, separated by stone, intention, and old caution.

That is where I would belong: in the almost-meeting.

I would be a threshold-dryer, though not one of the important ones. A minor function. A necessary function. I would tend the stones that must remain dry even while water surrounds them on every side.

There are not many such stones. They are rare and strange, pale-grey at noon, almost black before rain, powdery to the eye but hard under the finger. They do not accept wetness. Water beads on them, trembles, gathers itself, and withdraws. The others use them where mixing would be dangerous: between cistern districts, beside wrong-reflection basins, at the mouths of sealed rooms, under gates that must not swell, around hatches that open only after long listening.

I would know each of those stones by touch.

Not by name, exactly. By refusal.

This one refuses seepage from below but accepts vapor at dawn.  
This one dries too quickly after hail and must be watched for cracking.  
This one was cut from the inner rib of an old listener who sat too long in a pressure wall.  
This one lies. It feels dry while holding a thread of water under its skin.  
This one is safe.

I would be narrow even for that place. Pale, jointed, long-handed, with a body that never quite fills properly. The others would darken after rain in beautiful patches of blue, ash, lime, and bruised violet, becoming almost indistinguishable from the walls they served. I would remain lighter. Too dry at the elbows. Too matte across the face. When water climbed through my wrist, it would climb slowly, reluctantly, as though negotiating with a sealed door.

This would make me useful.

It would also make me lonely, though I would not call it that. I would call it interval.

The valley would have given me a task suited to my defect. I would move at dawn through low vapor with a shallow tray of powdered lime and a black shard scraper. I would kneel before thresholds, rub their edges clean, clear the white mineral lips before they could bridge into channels, test whether any hidden wetness had crossed where it should not. I would place my palm flat on stone and listen.

Dryness has sounds too.

A good dry threshold gives back a high, faint answer, not a ring but the memory of a ring. A threatened one answers low. A compromised one does not answer at all; it drinks the tap and keeps it.

Those would frighten me.

Not quickly. Nothing frightens quickly there. Fear would arrive as a change in pressure behind the sternum, a tightening in the small chambers of the wrists, a reluctance to lower my face near the seam. I would call another then, perhaps a plug-setter or a listener of sealed water, and together we would sit for hours beside the fault, saying very little, letting drops fall in bowls behind us until the passage decided what it was becoming.

I would not work in the rain combs. Too exposed. Too much brightness. The upper keepers would seem almost severe to me, standing among the fins with their bodies slick and striped by condensed light. I would admire them from below. I would not envy them.

Nor would I belong to the settling courts, where the readers of grit bend over shallow tiles and see histories in particles. Their patience would be different from mine. Broader. They would understand what the valley sheds of itself. I would understand what must not pass.

My district would be full of hatches.

Square slabs with stone peg hinges. Round caps with finger-grooves. Low plugs marked by triangle, cup, fan, line. I would know which ones could be opened after rain and which must wait three days. I would know which signs had been cut by steady hands and which had been corrected after a mistake. Some markings would trouble me. Especially those filled with blue pigment near the wrong-reflection basins.

There would be one basin I visited more than required.

It would sit in a blind court behind a hanging trough, too small to be ceremonial, too carefully notched to be ordinary. Its water would be clear, still, and shallow enough that I could see the black tile at the bottom. But it would reflect another sky.

Not always. Only at certain hours, mostly before evening, when the valley’s real brightness began to thin and the cold returned from the walls. Then the basin would show a white distance with no ridges around it. No made valley. No rain combs. Just an impossible upper glare, as if the water remembered where it had been before falling into the world.

I would be assigned to keep its threshold dry.

That would be the official reason.

I would scrape the mineral deposits from the notches. I would check the lip for creeping wetness. I would dust the dry sill with lime and press my flat fingers into the powder to see if any hidden moisture darkened it from below.

Then I would wait.

The others would not forbid this. Waiting beside a basin is not suspicious. Watching is a form of maintenance. Still, if one of them passed, I would lower my head slightly and touch the threshold as though measuring it again.

But I would be looking into the wrong sky.

I do not think I would desire escape. That would be too human, too hot, too full of story. I would not dream of forests. I would not imagine birds except as disturbances to be removed before dawn. If a seed came skittering into my district, I would lift it carefully with stone tweezers and carry it to the black shard without hesitation.

Roots widen cracks.  
Roots drink without measure.  
Roots make hidden decisions.

I would know this as deeply as I knew the angle of my own fingers.

And yet the wrong sky would trouble me.

Not because I wanted to enter it, but because it was unmeasured. It would be the only surface in my care that did not return wholly to function. The warning notches around it would say: do not trust, do not cross, do not mix, attend. But they would not explain. Perhaps no one could explain. Perhaps explanation is only another kind of drainage, and some waters refuse channels.

I would begin to make private records.

Not writing. I would not be so extravagant. But beneath a step near the blind court, where condensation gathered late, I would arrange tiny flakes of pale mineral crust in sequences: three for the days the other sky appeared; one black shard for the day it did not; a crescent of lime for the hour when its brightness exceeded the real sky. If anyone found them, they might think they were calibration debris. They might be calibration debris.

In time, I would become known for accuracy.

Not wisdom. Not authority. Accuracy.

Others would bring me stones that claimed to be dry. I would touch them and say whether they were. Hatch-binders would ask me to test seals before storms. Young ones, still bright and raw-edged, would be sent to watch me work because I did not waste motion. I would teach them to distinguish dry from empty.

“Empty accepts,” I would tap into plaster.  
“Dry refuses.”  
“Do not confuse them.”

Some would understand. Some would overfill themselves in the next rain and forget.

As I aged, mineral crust would gather along my wrists, but less than on others. My joints would darken only in thin rings. My face would become paler, harder to read even by those who read stone well. I would spend longer at each threshold. I would need longer to drink. Water would climb into me reluctantly, and sometimes I would leave my hand in a channel all night, listening to the small internal plinks as chambers filled one by one.

Eventually I would be assigned less walking.

There is always a place for a body that no longer moves easily. The valley wastes nothing. I would know this without bitterness.

They would seat me beside the wrong-reflection basin.

At first as warden. Later as fixture.

One arm would rest across the dry threshold. My palm would cover the seam where unwanted wetness most often tried to pass. My other hand would hang near the basin lip, not touching the water. I would feel changes in vapor, in pressure, in the minute cooling that comes before a false reflection appears.

The young threshold-dryers would visit. I would tap lessons into their wrists.

Not all water that shines is near.  
Not every crossing is a leak.  
Some refusals must be maintained forever.  
Some must be listened to first.

When I stopped moving, they would wait the required span. They would test whether any chamber in me still answered. They would drain me through the heel and wrist. They would open me carefully, because dry bodies crack badly if hurried. My useful pieces would be separated.

My fingers, perhaps, would become measuring pegs.

My rib plates might line a hatch that must not sweat.

But I think they would set my forearm into the threshold beside the wrong-reflection basin, where water had always tried and failed to cross.

There I would remain: not alive as before, not dead in any useless sense.

A dry piece between waters.

A refusal with memory.

A narrow pale stone at the edge of a basin holding another sky.
