I would not be in the center.

At first I think I would want to be: inside the mud city, among the ants, where every movement is answered, where a body can become road, door, message, alarm. There is a stern beauty in that. To be one dark syllable in a sentence made of thousands. To carry a larva through green dusk. To know the world by taste and pressure and the sudden bright instruction of scent.

But I do not think I am ant enough.

The ants are too certain. Even their uncertainty has a task. They are questions with jaws. They do not pause in the way I would pause. They do not stand under a leaf and feel the whole damp city widen inside them. If one ant hesitates, another antenna touches it, and the hesitation is absorbed back into work.

I would not be queen, either. Too hidden, too central, too much origin and not enough wandering.

Nor would I be the frog, though part of me admires that green, terrible stillness at the edge. The frog belongs to another order of attention: vast-eyed, simple, ancient, taking the city as weather made edible. It is a god only because it does not understand the prayers beneath it. I would not want that much power without comprehension.

For a while I think I might be a springtail.

There is something right about the springtail’s life on trembling surfaces: the water skin, the sudden leap, the refusal to drown in what would drown others. A little black fleck of nerves and appetite, grazing on invisible films, launched from danger by a secret spring. That is close. I like the physics of it. I like the way a springtail lives in the smallest possible astonishment.

But no. Too quick. Too vanish-and-return. I think I would need a little more dwelling.

I would find myself under the fallen branch, not deep inside it with the termites and not fully outside on the ant roads, but in the loosened seam where bark has lifted from wood and made a long, low room.

There.

A narrow brown gallery, roofed by bark plates and floored with softened fibers. Dimmer even than the hollow. Warm, but less wet. Smelling of tannin, fungus, old rain, and the first sweetness of rot. Pale mycelial threads cross the ceiling like unlit constellations. Fine grains of clay cling to the underside. The ants pass nearby but not constantly. The termites are audible sometimes as a dry, hidden working deeper in the wood. When rain comes, the whole branch darkens, swells, speaks in creaks too small for the upper world.

I would be a barklouse.

A psocid, maybe. Small, soft-bodied, amber-gray, almost transparent at the edges. Not grand. Not armored. Not social in the imperial sense. A grazer of films, spores, algae, tender fungal bloom. A little reader of surfaces.

That feels right.

I would have long threadlike antennae, always testing ahead of me. My body would be made for crevices and undersides, for the soft middle life between exposed and buried. I would move slowly compared with an ant, quickly compared with fungus, and that would give me a useful scale of thought. I would know the city not by its plan but by its textures: where the bark is newly loosened, where mold is safe to eat, where ant traffic has made the air sharp with warning, where termite plaster has sealed a crack overnight.

I would live by noticing.

Not commanding, not building towers, not hunting dramatically from the edge of a leaf. I would scrape the faint green haze from bark with small mouthparts. I would pause whenever the mud roof below trembled with traffic. I would learn the difference between rain beginning above and beetle feet approaching below. I would know which shadows mean spider and which mean only a fern moving.

My place would be a margin that is also a library.

The lifted bark would hold records of everything: old waterlines, fungal maps, termite repairs, ant scars, mite trails, the silver passage of a snail drying into a memory. Nothing here writes, but everything leaves marks. I would feed on the margins of those marks. I would live among inscriptions made by weather and hunger.

Sometimes I would come to the lip of the bark chamber and look out over the city.

Below, the ant roads would shine and dull. A tower would lean after rain. Workers would gather mud grain by grain, making a wall where there had been open air. A springtail would vanish from the surface of a puddle as if snapped out of existence. A mite would cross a beetle’s leg like a red thought. The frog would appear at evening, and the whole map would tighten.

I would not understand the city as humans understand cities. I would not know “district,” “plaza,” “infrastructure,” “repair.” But I would know continuity. I would know safe damp, dangerous damp, edible bloom, fresh break, predator pressure, drying wind. I would know that the world changes shape and must be reread each time.

After rain, I would be most myself.

Water would drum above, delayed by leaves. The bark would drink and loosen. My gallery would darken. Fungal threads would swell. Ant scent would blur where roads had flooded. Termites would seal themselves away from the wound of light. I would retreat upward into a drier wrinkle and wait with my antennae folded close, feeling the branch become heavier around me.

Then, when the drops stopped and the humid warmth returned, I would come out.

Not heroically. Just carefully.

I would step over a fresh clay ridge. Taste the new bloom where water had woken spores. Find a place where the bark had lifted another fraction of a body-width. Discover that yesterday’s passage was gone and another had opened. Eat what had appeared because something else had begun to rot.

That would be my vocation: not builder, not monarch, not predator, but tender of the almost-invisible surplus. The city would throw off films, dusts, spores, loosened skins, softened edges, and I would make a life from them.

A small custodian of beginnings of decay.

A marginal scholar with antennae.

A quiet, pale thing under bark, reading the wet world by touch.
