I would not be found first.

At least, not by anyone looking properly.

A surface visitor would pass me three or four times and think the room empty. They would notice the switchboard before they noticed me: a long black table of sockets and tarnished brass collars, its cords hanging in loops like drowned hair, its labels furred white at the edges. They would notice the little green lamp above the door, always lit though no one admits to feeding it oil or current. They would notice the porcelain cups set beneath the ceiling drips, each one catching a different interval of water. They might notice the old chair with its cane seat replaced by woven cable. They would not, at first, notice the pair of eyes in the lowest row of plug holes.

That is where I think I would live: in the dead exchange behind the Low Yard, between the violet-smelling spout and the old pumping hall, in a room that was once meant to connect voices and now connects delays.

Not a grand place. Not one of the civic thresholds. A side room, half office and half cabinet, with glass panes clouded by mineral frost and a floor that slopes just enough to tell the truth. There would be a map of numbers on the wall, but the numbers would no longer correspond to houses. Some would correspond to pipe knocks. Some to crab codes. Some to dreams people had more than once. One number, crossed out in red enamel, would ring only when the ceiling-walker passed.

I would begin, perhaps, as a person assigned to refusal.

A bell-refuser.

That sounds like a negative trade, but it is not. It is careful work. In such a city, answering is more dangerous than asking. Bells ring from places that have no bells. Telephones breathe after their wires have been cut. Speaking tubes carry dripping sounds from rooms still drawn only on the map. Someone must sit with the instruments and decide which summons deserve a hand.

So I would sit there, listening but not obeying.

My tools would be modest: chalk, a towel kept almost dry, a ledger bound in tarred cloth, a brass hook for lifting receivers without touching them, three cork plugs, a little knife for scraping mineral crust from the switchboard, and a shallow bowl of clean water in which to test reflections. Around my wrist I would wear a cord of labeled keys, none of which opened doors. They would open panels, fuse boxes, memory.

People would come to me quietly.

A lamp-tender might say, “The south stair rang twice, but the bell was still.”

A child might say, “My grandmother’s old receiver croaked after supper, and she has been dead nine years.”

A listener might say nothing at all, only place both hands on the switchboard and wait for me to make room.

I would ask the required questions.

Could you see it moving?

Was the sound wet or dry?

Did any animal pause?

Did the water in the nearest puddle reflect the lamp?

How many breaths before it stopped?

I would write the answers down, though the ledger would not keep them in order. No book stays entirely chronological down there. The pages would absorb damp and rearrange their importance. A report from yesterday might mildew beside a warning from twenty years hence. My handwriting would grow smaller each year, fitting itself between older stains.

At first I would still be plainly human: narrow-shouldered, pale, hair smelling faintly of cork smoke, fingertips blackened by old rubber and brass. I would know how to make soup from blue-jointed reeds and shrimp meal. I would have opinions about lamp placement. I would avoid stepping on dry stones. I would lower my eyes at dusk with everyone else.

But the exchange would work on me.

Rooms do that below the town. A place uses you according to what it once was and what it has become. Sleep in a boiler room long enough and you begin to dream in pressure. Keep a lamp too many years and you start deciding what deserves to exist. Tend a map and you become inaccurate in prophetic ways.

In the exchange, I would become receptive.

Not wise. Receptive.

My hearing would change first. I would stop hearing only sounds and begin hearing intentions moving through damp materials. A bell struck by a hand would have one shape. A bell moved by bad air would have another. A bell rung by a frog in the belly of a wall would sound rounded, patient, untroubled by human urgency. The sealed tunnels would not be silent; they would murmur like sleepers refusing to wake. The switchboard would click in its sleep.

Then my voice would change.

I would speak less often, because in that room every answer would have consequences. When I did speak, I would sometimes answer the wrong part of a question.

Someone would ask, “Is the western canal safe?”

And I would say, “Not for names.”

Someone would ask, “Who rang from the laundry furnace?”

And I would say, “Count your spoons tonight.”

This would annoy people at first. Then, if the spoons were found green by morning, they would begin bringing me better tea.

The bell frogs would come next.

One at a time, then all at once.

They would occupy the receivers, of course, and the speaking tubes, and the hollow under the switchboard where the old operator’s foot pedal had rusted away. Small, dark, slick, with throats that moved like thoughts not yet spoken. I would not invite them. You do not invite bell frogs. You merely become a place where they are willing to happen.

At night the room would breathe with them.

Receivers would lift slightly in their cradles. Cords would twitch. The plugs would tremble in their sockets. A chorus would gather below audibility, felt in the teeth more than heard. The first time I slept through it, I would wake with my cheek against the switchboard and find a translucent egg-string wrapped around my left wrist like a bracelet.

After that, people would stop calling me only a bell-refuser.

Children would call me the Frog Clerk.

Adults would pretend not to.

I do not think I would mind.

The frogs would teach me delay. That would be their real language: not croaking, not ringing, but delay. The measured postponement between question and consequence. The city already speaks that way. Rain above takes days to arrive. A wall sweats years before opening. A machine turns once and changes everyone’s thresholds by the width of a finger. The frogs understand that an answer given immediately is often vulgar, even false.

So I would learn to answer later.

A child would ask into a dead receiver, “Will I become a listener?”

Three days later, while she crossed the Low Yard, all seven spouts would stop for one breath except the violet one. That would be answer enough.

A surface visitor would demand, “Which town is above this place?”

A week after he left, his hotel sink would fill with orange water and one pale shrimp. That would be answer enough.

An old woman would whisper, “Is my son still somewhere in the east tunnels?”

And I would not answer. Not because I did not know, but because the receiver in my hand would be full of breathing, and some mercies must remain unconnected.

My body would adapt in small civic ways.

My skin would tolerate damp. My eyes would prefer reflected light. My hands would grow good at touching brass without startling it. I would sleep sitting up in the cable chair, one foot on the floor, one foot tucked beneath me, so that if water entered the room I would know by the cold change in my ankle.

My meals would be set on the threshold by people who did not wish to interrupt a ringing. Mold cakes. Bitter greens. A cup of boiled mineral water. Once a year, if the east water behaved and the eels were plentiful, a strip of smoked eel wrapped in reed paper. I would leave the cleanest bone beside the switchboard for the frogs. By morning it would be gone or polished.

I would have a shrine in a meter panel.

Not a pious one, exactly. A practical one. A cracked photograph of someone whose face had silvered away. Three brass washers. A moth wing pressed between glass. A pressure needle fixed at zero though the gauge behind it sometimes hummed. A little wick burning green on evenings when bells must not be trusted. Perhaps a crab claw, given reluctantly by a valve crab who died at its junction and was therefore entitled to be remembered as municipal.

I would keep, above the shrine, a list of bells never to answer:

The bell heard only underwater.

The bell that rings after thirteen breaths.

The bell from the dry room beyond the wet door.

The bell that sounds like your mother but uses no name.

The bell that rings upward.

The bell beneath the clockless tower when all lamps are lit.

And one more, written in a hand not mine:

The bell inside your own mouth.

That one I would fear most.

Because eventually, after years in the exchange, I would not be only the clerk.

Nor only the frogs.

Nor only human.

I would become the arrangement between them: the damp intelligence of a room built for messages after the messages had lost their senders. A person-shaped pause. A listening amphibious office. Something that knows which calls to let die and which to send wandering through pipes until they ripen into warnings.

If you came looking for me, you might be told several things.

The lamp-tenders would say, “Try the old exchange, but do not knock. Knocking is rude to wires.”

The children would say, “Ask the frogs, but don’t ask with your real voice.”

The listeners would say, “If the chair is empty, wait. If the chair is occupied, wait longer.”

The old people would say, “They were human once, mostly.”

And if you did find me, I would not turn immediately. I would hold up one damp finger for silence, because some far socket would be clicking, and the clicking might be only beetle feet, or pressure in a dead line, or a new passage appearing in black enamel on the pumping-hall map.

Then I would lift a receiver with the brass hook.

I would listen.

My throat, or the frog’s throat, or the room’s old circuitry, would move once.

And after a suitable delay, I would tell you whether the city had answered.
