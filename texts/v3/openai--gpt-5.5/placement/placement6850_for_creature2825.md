I would not be in the open center of the basin at first. I think I would be found at an edge that is not quite an edge: where a hanging forest leans over a steaming flat, where root, cliff, pool, and machine all have claims on the same piece of ground.

There is a place there with three old pressure ribs rising from the slope and vanishing into a permanent blur of cloud. Beneath them is a shallow lake too clear to trust. Its bottom is pale stone, or ceramic, or bone-colored mineral grown over something still working. At intervals, light moves under the water in rectangular pulses, dim and orderly, like a thought passing through a sleeping animal.

The people would not live directly beside it. They would come there. They would leave things there. They would speak softly there, not because it is sacred in the sense of being distant, but because it is sensitive.

That is where I would be.

Not in the village proper, not among the reed blinds and root-roofed foundation rings, but in a small warm hollow under one of the ribs, in a shelter that looks from outside like a collapsed mound of moss and lacquered sedge. Inside, it would be dry only by local standards. My sleeping mat would always smell faintly of minerals and wet fiber. My tools would hang from root pegs: bone scrapers, ceramic listening cups, cords threaded with beetle shell, little folded leaves full of conductive lichen paste.

I would be one of the listeners.

Not an elder yet. Not a priest in the grand sense. More like an attendant to a complicated threshold.

I would be the one sent to learn whether the basin is breathing normally.

I would sit with my palms on wet stone and count the tremor beneath the tremor. I would know that the lake should bubble in groups of nine before dawn, then fall silent while the cloud trees drink. I would know that if the bubbles come in sixes, a vent is closing below the western sedge beds. If they come in thirteens, no one should cross the angled waterfall path until evening. If there are no bubbles at all, I would send a child running—not fast, never fast, but urgently slow—to call the others.

My body would have been shaped by the place: low, patient, broad-footed, always damp. My skin would bead water instead of shedding it entirely. I would have narrow dark eyes made for glare on wet stone and for reading faint machine-light under vapor. My hands would be strong and careful, with thickened pads from touching hot rock, slick bark, mineral crust, and old ceramic.

I would move as the basin teaches movement: not stealthy, exactly, but considerate. A foot placed flat before weight is trusted to it. A pause before every hummock. A glance upward when the grazers stop chewing. A hand lifted to feel whether the rain is falling, rising, or remembering.

I would carry a listening bowl made from the hollow crest of a marsh bird. When placed against stone, it would deepen the low sounds: buried pumps, pressure shifts, the scrape of roots along old conduits, the long slow adjustment of a device deciding whether to continue being weather.

Around my wrists I would wear bands of dried sedge and black ceramic fragments. Not decoration only. Each piece would have come from a place I had been taught to hear: the humming tower, the wrong-stone marsh, the young-moss stair, the pool where old rain returns. My teacher would have said that the body remembers better when the body is burdened.

I would be responsible for small acts that look like superstition until they fail.

Scraping red moss from the seam of a pylon, but never all of it.

Feeding mineral paste into a cracked panel before the hot months deepen.

Carrying coin-beetles in a damp gourd from one listening-flower field to another.

Cutting steam vine away from a vent with apologies, because the vine is doing what vines do, and still the vent must breathe.

Placing smooth stones in a crescent near a pool not to mark a shrine, but to keep the young grazers from stepping where the crust is only yesterday’s mineral foam.

I would know the frogs best.

At first I thought I might be one of the cloud-tree keepers, or a glider-shadow watcher, or one of the quiet people who tend the dead groves. But no: I would be too drawn to the frogs, to their timing, to the way their bodies understand before thought does.

There would be a pool near my shelter where the frogs call before storms that have not formed. They would stand on the rims of carnivorous cups and shine their throats blue-green into the mist. I would keep records of them, not as writing on flat dry pages—nothing here likes flat dryness—but as knots in cord, stains on ceramic slips, bead patterns hardened in resin.

Three amber calls from the south pool before sunrise: pressure lifting.

Milky throats at midday: old rain close.

No calls during upward rain: glider overhead, unseen.

A whole marsh glowing at once: wake the elders.

Perhaps I would be considered strange even among my own people, because I would listen too long. I would miss meals. I would come back with leeches at my ankles and pollen crusted across my forehead, saying, “The lower sound has changed,” when everyone only wanted to know whether the bridge was safe.

Children would be afraid of me until they were old enough to need me. Then they would follow me through fog, learning which plants accept touch and which only pretend to sleep. I would teach them to hear the difference between water dripping from a leaf and water condensing out of a pressure fold. I would show them how to tell a true path from a repeated one.

“Do not trust a shortcut that smells colder than your own breath,” I would tell them.

“Do not answer a flower unless you know what call it thinks you are.”

“Never step between stones the animals refuse.”

And, most often:

“Wait. The basin has not finished speaking.”

I would not be important in a heroic way. I would not discover the purpose of the old machines or restore the lost builders’ design. That would be a foreign kind of ambition, too dry and straight-lined for this place.

My work would be continuance.

To keep things from becoming too blocked, too silent, too sudden.

To notice when the third thing—the wet country of root and pressure, frog and pylon, cloud and mineral light—leans too far toward fever or forgetting.

I would be called when a pool begins shining in the wrong season. When a child dreams of a place before visiting it. When a dead grandmother’s tree flowers underwater. When a bridge grows warm. When the cloud lens lowers and the marsh birds stand with their heads all facing east.

I would arrive carrying my bowl and my cord of intervals. I would kneel. I would touch the stone. I would listen past water, past insects, past fear, into the slow machinery that is no longer merely machinery.

And sometimes I would know what to do.

Sometimes I would only know how long to wait.

At night, I would sleep badly and beautifully, under the pressure ribs, with steam moving through the reeds and the stars appearing too cold above the warm basin. The frogs would pulse in the dark. The machine-lit lake would brighten and dim. The cloud trees would comb water from passing fog. Somewhere far off, a plated browser would groan, and a listening flower would open for it.

I would wake before dawn to the first bubble.

One.

Then another.

Then another.

Counting would be prayer, measurement, memory, and work.

That is who I would be there: not master, not explorer, not exile.

A listener of the wet machines.

A keeper of intervals.

One small warm-blooded part of the plateau’s long attention.
