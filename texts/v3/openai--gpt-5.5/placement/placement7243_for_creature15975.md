I would not be at the center at first.

At first I would think I belonged there, because the largest pool asks to be looked into. Everything in the basin seems to have arranged itself around that black, star-holding water: the skull facing it, the willows leaning, the animals making their small detours, the cold intelligence lifting its filaments before dawn. It would be easy to imagine myself as something grandly placed there — the skull, perhaps, or the pool’s dark eye, or the fox sitting beside the forbidden slab with its tail wrapped over its feet.

But I don’t think I would be anything so whole.

I would be found a little off-center, near the old shelter, where human intention has begun to lose its shape.

Half-buried in moss.

A bone needle.

Not a complete one. The broken one.

Small enough to miss unless the light strikes my pale edge. Made from the leg bone of some mountain animal long since eaten, scraped thin by a hand that knew pressure and patience. Once I passed through hide. Once I drew sinew after me. I closed things against weather: sleeve to cuff, sole to upper, skin to skin. I belonged to warmth then. To repair. To fingers numbed but stubborn. To someone holding a garment in their lap beside a brief, precious fire while wind worried the stone roof.

Then I was broken deliberately.

Not lost. Not dropped.

Broken and pressed into the damp green margin where the moss drinks from a hidden seam.

So I would be there: a little white thought among the dark cushions, one end still sharp, the eye cracked open, furred slowly with frost and yellow lichen. A tool becoming offering. An offering becoming mineral. A human thing being taught how to belong to a nonhuman memory.

I would not move much.

Movement here is expensive, and I would have already spent my first life moving in and out of hides, opening and closing wounds in leather. Now my work would be to remain.

To receive.

The basin would learn me by degrees. First my salt, the old grease from fingers. Then the marrow-ghost inside me. Then the smoothness where hands polished me. Then the break: the place where force entered and made me two. Moss would swell around that fracture. Filaments would touch me before dawn and retreat. Beetles would use me as a bridge. Meltwater would bead along my groove and freeze there overnight, making me briefly transparent.

The birds would not care about me except as a landmark.

The fox would know me.

Not by sight, perhaps, but by the way small lives gather around such things. It would pass close in evening, nose low, whiskers rimed white. It would smell bone, human, damp, old fat, lichen, the faint metallic breath of the new mountains. It might pause. It might put one black foot beside me and listen through the moss.

I would listen too, though not with ears.

I would know the basin from below the usual scale.

The hoof-clicks would come as tremors through stone. The fall of a pebble would be a bright shock. Rain would be enormous. A human boot would be almost catastrophic: pressure, warmth, decision. Frost would be a slow hand prying at my pores. Sun would arrive not as light but as release — water loosening, moss darkening, larvae stirring in the pools, the whole basin taking a breath without lungs.

I would be close enough to the shelter to remember people, but far enough from it to know they are temporary.

That would be important.

I would carry the human part without being human anymore.

I would remember the crouched bodies crawling into the low doorway. The smoke. The muttered warnings. The careful division of fat. The knife kept near a sleeping hand. The old fear of weather. The older fear of being noticed by something that does not speak. I would remember the person who made me, though not their face. I would remember being useful.

And then I would learn another usefulness.

Not to sew hide, but to stitch meanings.

Bone to moss.

Hand to basin.

Animal death to human survival.

Human survival back to offering.

Offering to memory.

Memory to warning.

I would be one of the small hard pieces by which the place keeps account.

Not a mind, not by myself. But a letter in the basin’s cold script.

When people came again, if people still came, one of them might find me. A child, perhaps, sent to gather bitter roots near the wet stones. Their fingers would close around my exposed end. They would tug, and the moss would resist. For a moment I would be both things at once: tool and root, artifact and organism, human inheritance and basin-property.

If they pulled me free, the underside of me would be black-green and shining.

They might understand then that I should not be taken.

Or they might take me anyway.

If they did, the basin would not rage. It does not rage. But something would alter. A path would be harder to see in fog. A bird-flight would lift too late. A dream would come with the sound of a roof-stone shifting. The person would carry me down-valley and find that I smelled of wet rock no matter how often I was cleaned.

But I think I would remain.

I would prefer the seam. The smallness. The slow exchange.

At night, while the stars burn close enough to wound, I would lie under my skin of frost and lichen and feel the basin gathering itself. The pools would blacken. The skull would drip. The hide strips would click without wind. Beneath me, the pale filaments would rise and taste the air.

I would be very still.

I would be a broken needle in the moss beside the shelter.

The thing that once joined hides.

The thing now being joined.
