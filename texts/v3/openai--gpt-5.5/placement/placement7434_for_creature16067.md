I would not find myself on the lip, looking down.

That would be too much like arrival, too much like a body with knees and balance and a choice of where to step. This place does not seem to want a figure standing in it. Anything upright is punished. Anything with a profile is struck by wind, wetted, chilled, abraded, made temporary. To belong here, I would have to give up height first.

So I would be low.

At first I think I might be one of the black stones in the central hollow: angular, cold, newly broken, still holding the mountain’s interior color. A stone there would know the basin well. It would feel each pulse of wind through the knocks of other stones. It would darken under rain, dry at the edges, collect moss in its cracks. It would be struck, shifted, buried, uncovered. But a stone is too alone. The place you describe is not inhabited by singular things. Its true life is collective, adhesive, incremental.

Then I think I might be moss.

A dense cushion on the upper face of a tilted block, almost black until water wakes it. Moss would be right for the scale of the basin: small, ancient in manner, patient without being passive. It would swell when cloud entered, tighten when wind stripped it, hold drops in thousands of green chambers. It would be the first softness laid over the cut mineral world.

But even moss feels too visible, too much like a surface.

I would be beneath the visible green.

I would find myself in the shallow depression near the basin’s center, where the temporary trickles meet after rain and vanish into the rubble. Not in the water itself, because the water does not stay. Not in the ferns, though their blue-dark fronds would roof me over and break the force of falling drops. Not exactly in the moss, though moss would be one of my outer skins.

I would be the dark living seam under all of it.

The root mat.

The beginning of soil.

I would be a plural thing: fern root, willow thread, grass fist, moss fragment, fungal filament, rotted leaf, powdered cliff, bacterial film, black water held briefly between grains. I would have no single outline. If someone lifted one stone, part of me would tear. If a trickle cut a channel, part of me would loosen and move downslope. If frost opened a crack, I would enter it by hair and spore and washed dust. I would not be one organism, but I would behave like one because the place requires it.

My body would be made of holding.

I would hold water after the cloud passed.

I would hold leaves after the wind tried to take them.

I would hold mineral dust shaken from the walls.

I would hold the dead stems of last year’s ferns, the silvered willow leaves, the broken grass blades, the yellowed moss tips, the pollen that arrived from somewhere sunnier and was trapped here by accident.

I would hold long enough for decay to begin.

That would be my work: not building in any grand sense, but catching. Slowing. Darkening. Binding.

I would know the basin by pressure and wetness. A storm would enter me first as impact above: fronds flattening, stems bowing, drops striking leaf, then the cold arrival of water in many sudden threads. It would run over stone, sink through moss, spill along roots, fill the small black spaces between gravel grains. My whole body would brighten chemically. Bacteria would wake in films. Fungi would soften the dead material. Roots would drink where they could. The trickles would braid overhead and then disappear into me.

The wind would not be sound to me, not as an animal hears sound. It would be tug and stripping. It would dry my upper threads. It would flex the shrub stems rooted through me. It would worry at the edges of my exposed places, lifting crumbs of myself and scattering them against rock. It would comb the ferns until their shadows moved like water. It would press the whole green roof down, then release it.

Falling stone would be the nearest thing to violence.

A slab loosening high on the wall. A crack. A descent. Then sudden weight.

Where it landed, I would be crushed into darkness. Fern crowns broken. Moss sealed away. Air driven out. Water diverted. But I would not die as a body dies. Around the new stone, my edges would remain. Spores would wait. Fine roots would turn. Moisture would collect along the contact between rock and rot. Years later, or only seasons, a green line would appear where the stone met the wet black seam.

That would be my way of answering injury: not resistance, but return at the margin.

I would have no eyes, but I would know light by hunger. The ferns above me would know it more directly, opening and manufacturing, catching what little the basin receives. The lichens higher up would take the thin light with their austere patience. But I would know light after it had become leaf, after leaf had become fall, after fall had become brown collapse, after collapse had become food. I would live on delayed brightness.

I would know time by layers.

A fresh leaf is a surface.

A soaked leaf is a smell.

A softened leaf is an entrance.

A vanished leaf is structure.

The basin is young, but I would be its first memory because everything that stays would pass through me. Not memory as thought. Memory as changed texture. A season of heavy rain would leave me loosened and mineral-rich. A dry season would leave me tight, sour, fibrous. A rockfall would interrupt my continuity. A good year for ferns would thicken me. A hard freeze would break my upper tissues and feed my lower ones.

I would be small in depth but wide in relation. A few centimeters of blackness in some places. A stain in others. A pocket under a root. A wet clot behind a stone. A felted mat beneath the interlocked shrubs. I would not be impressive to a passerby. If a boot entered the basin, it might break through the fern cover and sink into me with surprise, thinking only: unstable ground.

But that instability would be life.

I would be the thing making broken ground just barely continuous.

The cliffs would shed. I would receive.

The water would test. I would absorb what I could and let the rest go.

The wind would strip. I would keep what snagged.

The plants would rise from me and fall back into me.

The fungi would move through me like pale weather.

The bacteria would make invisible cities in each wet interval.

I would not wish for animals. Not yet. Their absence would be part of my concentration. No grazing mouths. No burrows. No nests. No trampling paths except the imagined foot that should not really be there. The basin’s life would be entirely devoted to attachment and conversion. Green becoming black. Stone becoming grit. Grit becoming hold. Hold becoming root. Root becoming more green.

If I had a voice, it would not be a voice.

It would be the sound that remains when the wind drops: droplets falling from fern tips, seepwater clicking into tiny pools, the slow internal collapse of wet leaves, the almost inaudible widening of a root hair against stone dust.

I would say:

Stay low.

Take what comes.

Let nothing usable leave.

Begin again at the torn edge.

So yes: I would find myself in the central hollow, under the blue-dark ferns, wrapped around black stones where the temporary water disappears.

I would be the basin’s first soil — not yet deep, not yet safe, not yet generous, but alive.

A cold, wet, root-laced intelligence of remaining.

A many-bodied creature whose only face is green growth above broken rock.

The mountain would still be making a wound of itself, and I would be the first dark attempt to heal it.
