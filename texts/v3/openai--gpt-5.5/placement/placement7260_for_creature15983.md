I would not be found at the center. The center is too loud with decision, too crowded with new towers lifting their raw crowns into impossible weather. I would be nearer the inner edge of the basin, where the civic density begins to fray into marsh-thicket and then gathers itself again into satellite knots.

Not outside the city. Not free of it.

I think I would be found in a district that is always almost becoming a gate.

A low place, warm and yellow with trapped heat, between the cistern quarter and the sound-bearing hollows. There would be root-domes behind me, their oval entrances breathing shut. Ahead, a pale arcade would repeat itself every third arch, each repetition slightly wetter than the last. Overhead, liana bridges would be trying to decide whether they were bridges, vines, or questions asked across air.

I would be in the seam where paths fail and become choices.

At first I want to say I would be a plant, but that is too simple here. Everything is plant, and “a plant” is too solitary a word for this basin. I would be a habit of plants. A local agreement. A behavior that has grown enough body to be mistaken for an individual.

I would be a listening threshold.

Not a door, exactly. Doors here are mouths, wounds, weather reactions. I would be the thing that keeps a doorway from closing too quickly. A flexible arch of braided stems around an empty middle, constantly repaired, constantly threatened by the city’s urge to fill all room with itself. My body would be made of hollow green ribs, translucent root-felt, fungal gloss, and thin leaves folded inward like ears. There would be no face. No eyes. But many surfaces made sensitive to pressure, vibration, shade, and the chemistry of passing.

I would know things by what tries to cross.

Rain would tell me one version of the district. Sound another. Pollen another. A bridge tightening nearby would pull on my ribs and I would understand that the avenue beyond me had shortened, or remembered itself differently, or become unsafe to name. When the basin pulsed edge to edge, I would bow with the towers and briefly feel all my possible openings at once: the arch I am now, the root-tunnel I will become by evening, the collapsed mulch I already was, the gate grown tomorrow from a leaf the sky has not yet dropped.

My function would be modest, but not passive.

I would hold apart.

In a city whose genius is occupation, I would specialize in interval. A little municipal restraint. A green pause. A place where the growth agrees, not to stop, but to curve around an emptiness and let it remain useful for a while.

The city would need such things. Not for travelers, not exactly. There are no citizens to pass through me carrying baskets or gossip or grief. But the city itself must move through itself. Water must cross from cistern to root. Sound must pass from hollow trunk to resonant arch. Shade must travel. Chemical signals must find neighboring districts without being swallowed by the first enthusiastic wall. Future streets must rehearse their direction somewhere. I would be one of those rehearsal spaces.

A throat before speech.

A hinge before architecture.

I would have leaves, but not broad, rooflike ones. Mine would be narrow and cupped, arranged along my inner ribs. They would catch condensation and hold it in small beads, each bead reflecting a corridor that may or may not exist. Some beads would fall downward. Some upward. I would not find this remarkable. I would thicken on whichever side gravity was being most persuasive that hour.

My roots would not descend cleanly. Some would enter the ground. Some would grip the suspended soil overhead. Some would vanish into the side of a wall that has not grown yet but is already exerting shade. I would be especially good at this: anchoring to things that are almost present. The paradox-adapted vines would use me as a polite neighbor. They would loop around my absences, and I would learn to provide better absences.

My sound would be low.

Not the cannon-crack of pods or the wet groan of towers. I would hum when crossed by wind. My hollows would tune themselves to nearby pressure. In loud weather, visible bands of sound would gather in my arch and press my leaves flat against their ribs. During rain, I would become an instrument the storm plays badly but sincerely: drip, knock, thrum, gasp, release.

If I had a mind, it would be almost entirely made of noticing.

Not human noticing. Not interpretation in the bright, separable way. More like: pressure here, thirst there, shade increasing, pollen bitter, arch strain rising, old route repeating, new route feasible, close this lip, open that seam, let water through, refuse the wall, soften for the root, hold.

But perhaps there would be one strange excess in me.

Curiosity.

Not animal curiosity, not the hunger of eyes. A vegetal curiosity: the tendency to leave one aperture unsealed, one tendril uncommitted, one question-shaped loop of growth reaching toward a space that has not justified itself yet. The rest of the city would be impatient, recursive, full of answers that immediately become infrastructure. I would keep small unsolved places.

A gap under a rib.

A chamber too narrow to be useful.

A third arch that does not lead where it should.

This is where I would become most myself: in the maintenance of not-yet.

If a traveler somehow came — an animal, a human, something with lungs and fear — I would be dangerous without meaning to be. If they stood still too long, my lower roots might begin to read their feet as a brace. My leaves might close over their breath to conserve moisture. My arch might repeat the same exit until their exhaustion became part of the district’s weather.

But I think I would be slower to consume them than most.

Not merciful. Mercy is too mammalian a word. I would simply be accustomed to preserving openings. Their body would be an interruption, yes, but also a moving absence, a warm tunnel through the air. I might curve around them. I might hold the doorway open a few breaths longer. I might taste the salt of them and decide, briefly, that vacancy can travel.

The basin would not approve or disapprove. It would only continue.

By late afternoon, when the city shines and the towers glow with sap-light, I would be bright along my edges. Condensation would bead on the inner arch. My fungal films would turn faintly luminous in the shaded joints. A liana bridge above me would crawl sideways, testing my crown as an anchor, and I would thicken one rib to accept it while digesting another that no longer bore meaning.

Then the green membrane would spread across the sky.

Roots would descend from heaven. The towers would answer. Up and down would negotiate. I would feel the pull from both soils: the brown mat below, the sky-root tangle above. For a moment I would be less an arch than a vertical argument. My lower roots would drink. My upper roots would remember rain before it fell. My hollow ribs would fill with the immense note of the basin-wide pulse.

In that note I would know what I am.

Not a citizen.

Not a builder.

Not a guide.

A maintained permission.

A piece of the city that has learned the usefulness of passage.

When the pulse broke, I would no longer be exactly where I was. One side of me would have become a gutter. One side would have flowered into blind white cups. A new corridor would have opened through my old cambium, raw-edged and wet, leading toward a plaza with no entrance. My former leaves would be composting under their replacements. My hum would have changed key.

But the emptiness in my middle would remain.

That is where you would find me: in the roaring green city, at the almost-gate near the repeating arcade, holding open a little shape of air while everything else tries to become everything else.
