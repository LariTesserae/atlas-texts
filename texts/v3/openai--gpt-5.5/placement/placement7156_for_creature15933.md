I would find myself at the end of the hollow where the broken gauge lies half-buried, not on top of it, not in the open ant road around it, but inside its small failed weather.

Behind the fogged glass.

At first I might think I belonged elsewhere: on the third stair with the lizard, taking heat through my belly; or under the pipe seam, waiting for a drop with the snails; or in the clay stripe where the wasps cut their mud. Those are clean places of purpose. Hunt. Graze. Build. Warm. Hide.

But I think I would be smaller and more enclosed than that.

I would be the soft thing beneath the gauge face, the pale larva or almost-larva living between the warped paper dial and the rusting metal back, where the numbers have begun to loosen into pulp. I would not know the word PRESSURE, though I would eat the ink of it slowly. I would live in the old instrument’s last dampness: condensation trapped under glass, mildew blooming in the paper fibers, a little dark rot made safe by being sealed away.

The gauge would be my cave, my shell, my sky.

Above me the glass would cloud and clear. Light would enter thickly, already used. When rain came, the whole hollow would wake, and my ceiling would pearl with breath. Drops would not fall on me directly; they would gather beyond reach, tremble, vanish, return. I would feel the change as softness in the paper, as a loosening of glue, as the brief sweetness of mold becoming edible.

Outside, ants would cross the rim of my world in a disciplined black line, carrying bodies, eggs, crumbs, decisions. Their feet would tick against the corroded metal, enormous and remote. A lizard might pause above me and make the light blink. Frogs would call from holes that sounded impossibly far away. The pipe would click in the sun, and the gauge housing would answer with a tiny contraction around me, as if remembering work.

I would have no interest in the larger machine except as shelter already reduced to food.

I would be one of the hollow’s quiet revisions: not dramatic like the frog after rain, not visible like the gecko on the wall, not organized like the ants. I would be patient appetite in a sealed room. I would turn measurement into meal. I would make the dial illegible from underneath, widening a brown crescent through the paper, digesting old certainty into damp dust.

My body would be simple: translucent, blunt-headed, ringed, made for pressing forward through softened matter. No eyes, or almost none. No need for distance. My world would be texture and moisture: dry paper refusing me, wet paper opening; rust grains abrasive against my side; fungal threads tangling and feeding; the cool rim where metal meets clay.

When the hollow dried, I would withdraw deeper under the dial plate, into the narrowest seam, and become nearly nothing. When the hidden wet returned, I would wake without surprise.

If I had a purpose, it would be this: to help the abandoned thing stop being an instrument and become ground.

Not all at once. Not grandly. Grain by grain, fiber by fiber.

The numbers would go first. Then the warning marks. Then the paper backing, then the glue. The needle would remain above me for a long time, fixed in its last false reading, pointing at a pressure no one needed. Eventually rust would lift it. Ants might take part of me when I pupated badly or died. A beetle might emerge where a gauge once watched the system.

So I would live there as the gauge’s hidden tenant: a small pale eater of damp paper, a creature of condensation and forgetting.

A thing sealed inside the old eye, helping it close.
