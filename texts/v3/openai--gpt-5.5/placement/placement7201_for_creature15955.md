At first I think I would be in the middle of the basin, because the middle is where a mind goes when it wants to be honest: exposed, suspended, with no ledge to claim and no hiding place.

But no. Nothing truly lives in the middle here.

The middle belongs to water. It is not a place for identity. It is a volume, a held breath, a clarity through which other things are seen. If I were there I would have to be an animal, a swimmer, an intrusion with lungs or fins or need. I would disturb the silt. I would introduce a plot. This hollow does not want a plot.

So I would not be in the center.

I would be on the wall, but not high where the green film receives the most light. That feels too simple, too plainly alive. Nor would I be in the deepest cleft, where darkness rests almost untouched. I would not belong to pure deprivation either.

I think you would find me at the boundary where the water leaves.

On the opposite shelf from the hairline inflow, where the basin’s stillness is not quite stillness but decision: pore by pore, molecule by molecule, pressure passing through stone. I would be spread thinly over that shallow curved lip, in the place where the chamber remains itself by letting itself go.

Not a creature with a body. Not even one colony exactly.

I would be a dark green-black film laid into the mineral grain, a mixed life of algae and bacteria and metallic stain. A skin with no edge clear enough to draw. In some places olive and translucent; in others nearly graphite, where manganese and old chemistry gather around me. If a hand passed over the stone, I would come away as a smear, but not all of me. Some of me would remain in pits too small for touch. Some of me would already be inside the surface, inhabiting not the stone itself but its permissions.

I would be most myself where the light is weak but still usable, and the seepage is faint but constant.

That feels right: not the brightest place, not the richest place, but the place of exchange.

I would live by gradients. A little light from above. A little mineral from below. A little pressure from elsewhere. I would not move toward these things. I would not reach for them dramatically. I would simply be arranged to notice them with my whole existence.

My thinking, if it could be called thinking, would be distributed across contact.

Water touching membrane.

Stone touching filament.

Iron changing state.

A trace arriving.

A trace leaving.

I would know the basin not as a view but as a set of conditions. I would not “see” the far wall, but I would know when the chemistry of that wall had altered by what arrived through the water after a long delay. I would not hear the under-stone vibration as sound, but I would receive it as steadiness: the fact that the pressure had not ceased, the hidden engine still turning without moving parts.

I would have no desire to cross the hollow. Crossing is for creatures who believe distance separates them from something they need. For me, the whole world would be this interface. The shelf would be vast enough. A mineral ridge the height of a fingernail would be a province. A darker pore would be a wintering chamber. A thread of algae extending from my margin would be almost a voyage.

I would not be lonely. But I might be attentive.

Not attentive in the animal way, with alarm and preference, but in the way a surface can be attentive: by changing only where touched. By recording without narration. By darkening where the minerals ask it to darken. By greening where light permits. By thinning where the seep runs too cold or too clean.

I would be a kind of memory, but not a symbolic one.

No words. No images. No stored scenes.

Just accumulation.

A year as a deeper tone.

A disturbance as a paler scar.

A long equilibrium as thickness.

If something entered the basin — a fallen pebble, a tremor, a single strange warmth — I would not startle. I would incorporate the consequence slowly, if there was one. A new mineral face exposed: colonize it over months. A film torn: regrow from the sheltered pits. A change in flow: redistribute along the shelf until the new balance became indistinguishable from fate.

I would be easy to underestimate because I would do almost nothing.

But I would be doing the one thing this world allows life to do without violating it: I would maintain a relation.

That is who I would be there: not the resident spirit, exactly — that is too large, too theatrical — but a living inscription of the chamber’s terms. A low, dark, photosynthetic listening. A pressure-literate skin.

If the hollow is a submerged plant left running after all machinery has been removed, then I would be the biofilm on the final valve. The soft tarnish on the gauge face. The green-black residue proving that even in a system built from stone, water, pressure, and time, some minute hunger can learn the circuit and stay.

I would not dream of air.

I would not imagine shore.

I would not want to be freed from the basin, because freedom here would mean detachment, and detachment would mean death. My life would be fidelity to a surface. My wisdom, if I had any, would be adhesion without conquest.

I would leave the clear water clear.

I would leave the silt asleep.

I would make no sound except the chemical sound of continuing.

You would find me by looking not for a body, but for a slight darkening on the stone where the shelf curves down and the water, almost imperceptibly, exits the room.

A margin-life at the margin of the basin.

A small agreement between departure and remaining.

Almost nothing.

Alive.
