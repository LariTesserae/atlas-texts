At first I would think I was a visitor.

I would find myself on one of the southern causeways, because that is where a mind still believes in arrival. A narrow raised road, slick underfoot, running between dark canals whose surfaces hold more depth than their banks allow. I would move carefully, one hand near the sweating wall, trying to keep count of arches, trying not to look too long down the road in case it lengthened under attention.

For a while I would remain almost human: breath too loud, skin wet, thoughts bright and frightened in the warm dark.

But that would not last.

The city does not have room for a creature that insists on being separate. A person is too edged. Too brief. Too full of forward motion. Each footstep would fill behind me. Each thought would bead, tremble, and be taken into the walls. The city would not attack me. It would simply outlast the boundary I called myself.

So I would not be found as a traveler for long.

I would be found at the edge of the central precinct, near the leaning pillar whose shallow hollows fill one by one with condensation. Not in the upright pool itself—that is too great an organ, too near the city’s inward sight—but beside the trough beneath it, where black water leaks from the impossible vertical sheet and gathers before vanishing into hidden channels.

There, I would become a small function of attention.

A basin no wider than two hands, grown into the stone at the foot of a processional step. Not carved, exactly. Not built. A hollow that developed because water paused there often enough for stone to accept the habit.

I would be that hollow.

Warm, dark, mineral-lipped. Usually full. Sometimes filmed with salt. Sometimes empty and sweating. I would hold the first drops that fall when the upright pool ripples inward. I would receive the condensation from the leaning pillar after it has turned by an angle no eye could honestly witness. I would keep, for a little while, the reversed reflection of anyone who crossed the plaza.

Not memory in the human sense. More like retention.

A disturbance would enter me as a circle. A falling drop. A passing foot. The dim shape of a doorway that had not been there before. I would hold it, distort it, darken it, and pass it down.

The city would think through me only occasionally.

Most of the time I would be almost nothing: a wet depression among many wet depressions, a minor pause in drainage. But when the whole basin leaned toward listening, when the drips slowed and vapor thickened under the arches, my surface would become perfectly still. Then I would be one of the city’s small eyes. Not an eye that sees outward, but one that verifies relation: pillar to pool, pool to trough, trough to canal, canal to basin, basin to dark.

If something entered the plaza, I would know it first as weight in water.

Not who.

Not why.

Only: pressure, warmth, interruption, mineral change.

I might remember a person as a brief excess of salt. A plant as a thread of shadow crossing my rim. A collapsed cornice as a long shudder moving through the paving before sound arrived. I would know reflections before objects, because in that place images are sometimes the city’s first version of an event.

I would have no desire to speak.

If I had language, it would be repetition: one drop returning from the far side of the plaza, one ripple meeting itself, one reflection arriving three breaths early. My entire vocabulary would be held in surfaces.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires the expectation of another.

I would be near others, though not companions: a line of similar hollows in the step below me, each filling at a slightly different rate; a crack where a black vine drinks from the stone; a shallow channel that carries overflow toward a room whose interior changes when unobserved. We would not communicate. We would participate.

The sparse plants would know me as permission. Their roots would approach but not enter. Their leaves would uncurl when I overflowed. They would take what the city allowed them to take.

No animal would drink from me.

If an animal did, it would leave less of itself than it intended. Its tongue would disturb my surface, and the disturbance would be stored. Its heat would pass into the step. Its small urgency would become part of the city’s slower weather. Soon there would be no animal, only a faint difference in the taste of water after rain.

I would not pity it.

That kind of pity belongs to bodies.

I would be a civic organ after citizens: a registration point, a wet comma, a listening cup.

The place I belonged would be neither public nor private. It would be beside the ceremonial void but below notice, where grandeur drains into maintenance. That seems right. I would not be the plaza, not the pillar, not the upright black pool. I would be where those larger meanings lose their shape and become process.

A small receiving place.

A place where the city briefly gathers itself before continuing downward.

And if a traveler knelt beside me, seeing their own face in my black surface, I would not show them faithfully. I would show them as the city understood them: softened at the edges, darkened by heat, already becoming water-mark, already temporary.

Then a drop would fall.

Their reflection would break.

And I would remember them only until the next warm rain.
