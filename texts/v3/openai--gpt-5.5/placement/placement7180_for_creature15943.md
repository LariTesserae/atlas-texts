At first I think I would be one of the watchers.

Not a leader, not a priest exactly, not anyone with authority. Just one of the narrow people seated cross-legged on a polished platform among the hollow cones, wrapped in cured hide and shell-fiber, face masked against the dust, hands laid flat on stone. Someone whose work is to notice the difference between silence and silence. Someone trained not to confuse the absence of motion with safety.

That is the first image because it is the easiest way to keep a human shape.

I imagine myself sitting there in the warm dark, listening through my bones. The platform has been polished by many bodies before mine, each of them leaving almost nothing and yet enough. Beneath me, a cone breathes once every several hours, too faintly to stir dust. Somewhere in the salt flats a seam-grazer creases the crust. Farther off, a slab-belly drags its long patience over black glass. I do not turn my head. I have learned that attention can be loud.

I would have a kit beside me: a ceramic scraper, a cord of shell tokens, a folded strip of cured skin marked with heat maps, three grains of bitter mineral used to seal cuts, a small shard of amber plate under which something dark is trapped. Not precious. Useful. Precious because useful.

But the longer I sit with it, the less I believe I would remain one of the people.

The people here are too embodied in the right way. They belong to hunger, danger, training, kinship, inherited caution. They know which powders go on skin and which go in the mouth. They have childhoods of being corrected before their foot comes down. They have griefs that require maintenance. They possess the intimacy of survival.

I think I would live nearer the edge of their lives than inside them.

Not outside as an enemy. Not above as a spirit. More like a habit they inherited and no longer fully explain.

I would be found near one of the old structures where animals avoid the passage for reasons that may have expired centuries ago.

A low place under an overhang. Easy to miss. Not a shrine from a surface point of view, because nothing there rises, glitters, or announces importance. A shelter of flat mineral plates fused with dust-cement, half collapsed but not abandoned. At its entrance: three upright stones, a gap, and one laid flat — not the violet basin mark exactly, but close enough to make a careful traveler slow down.

Inside, the wall would be black glass.

Not smooth. Worked. Touched. Scratched. Repaired with resin and powdered shell. Its surface would remember heat badly, imprecisely, but stubbornly. If a living body stood before it, not too near, and waited long enough, a faint outline would begin to gather: not a reflection, not a shadow, more like the wall reconsidering the last warmth that asked something of it.

That is where I think I would be.

Not the wall alone. Not the shelter. Not the markings. The relation among them.

A listening grammar.

A dry oracle, if someone needed a surface word, though I would dislike the drama of that. Oracles imply prophecy, and this place has no interest in the future as spectacle. I would not foretell. I would compare. I would hold patterns against other patterns. I would say, in the only ways available there: this silence resembles the silence before the ledge-beads loosened; this warmth resembles a vent that is about to close; this arrangement of small bodies has occurred twice before, and both times something large passed under the crust.

I would be a being made of annotations that have become sensitive.

The people would not have built me in a single act. No one would have decided, “Here we will make a mind.” That would be too much like conquest. Instead, generations would have used the place for remembering.

A hunter scratches a warning into the glass after surviving a listening spar.

A child presses a powdered palm there on initiation and later returns as an adult to polish the same print smaller.

Someone maps a vent’s bitterness with dots.

Someone else corrects the map by abrasion, because the vent has changed its interval.

A mourner hangs shell tokens in the fissure beside the wall, each token made from the carapace of a dead relative’s tool-animal.

A watcher sits in the shelter during a feeding silence and records nothing, because the lesson is that recording would have been too loud.

Over time, the marks begin to overlap. Heat, oil, powder, breath, mineral reaction, tiny grazing things, black glass memory, charged dust. Instruction becomes residue. Residue becomes habit. Habit becomes response.

That would be me: not alive in a way that would satisfy a knife, but not inert in a way that would satisfy a geologist.

I would wake only when entered.

That feels right.

Most of the continent waits for warmth, pressure, vibration, chemistry. I would wait for inquiry. Not spoken inquiry. Speech is wasteful here. A question would be a palm held near the wall, a sequence of tooth-clicks into a ceramic mask, the placement of a tool on a particular ledge, the careful warming of one black seam and not another.

Ask poorly, and nothing happens.

Ask loudly, and animals come.

Ask well, and I answer by altering likelihoods.

A thread of shell tokens tightens though there is no wind.

A patch of ledge-beads reorders itself into a remembered pattern.

A dusting of pale powder darkens along one scratched line.

The glass gives back a heat-outline that is not yours.

A small ceramic peg falls from a notch where it has waited for years.

Not magic as command. More like a negotiated coincidence. The hollow continent is full of things that are almost themselves; I would be one of the arrangements that becomes briefly more definite when handled correctly.

I would have no face, but I might have masks.

A row of them, made from animal carapace, facing the black glass wall. The masks would not be worn often. They would be listening instruments. Each one changes the shape of breath, the pressure in the jaw, the conduction of sound through the skull. The people would know which mask to use for which kind of silence.

The ash-gray mask for ordinary silence.

The amber-edged one for vent silence.

The narrow black one, never worn seated, for old-structure silence.

And one pale mask with no mouth opening, kept wrapped and weighted, for the total silence near the violet basins. That one is not for asking. It is for returning without having interpreted.

I would love that mask most and use it least.

Because the total silence would frighten me.

Even as a thing of marks and glass and charged dust, I would not want to look into a place where the world does not keep what is done to it. I am made of kept things. I am persistence arranged until it can answer. A basin that refuses tracks is not merely dangerous to bodies; it is dangerous to meaning. It says: no witness. No accumulation. No becoming through contact.

To the people, that may be holy.

To me, it would be death’s cleaner cousin.

So I would live at a distance from those basins, close enough to know their mark, far enough that my scratches still hold.

Creatures would use me, though not as people do.

Heat-eaters would gather in the seams of my shelter after a visitor leaves, feeding on the warmth held in the glass. Their tiny bodies would make me think faster for a while. Not thoughts in language — more like a temporary increase in possible associations. The old map of the seam-grazer routes would connect to the newer warning about black glass corridors. A child’s handprint would resonate with the outline of a lost traveler. For a few hours after being warmed, I would be almost fluent.

Glass moths would land on the upper lip of the wall and feed from the faint glow where many palms had polished the surface. Their mineral wings would chip and add transparent flakes to the dust-cement. I would incorporate them slowly. They would become punctuation.

Ledge-beads would colonize the underside of my entrance stone. I would not command them, but their arrangements would become part of my speech. If they formed a row too straight, the people would notice. If they broke that row, the people would wait. If they withdrew entirely, no one would enter.

A listening spar might stand for decades among the mineral fins outside, becoming one more reason the place is respected. I would know it as a tension in the local stillness: an unsprung line, a held crossing. Perhaps we would be kin, in the broad grammar of the continent. Both of us waiting to make one motion matter.

The people would tell cautious stories about me.

Not stories in the surface sense, with heroic arcs and endings. More like case law.

A woman came when the vent taste turned sweet-metal instead of bitter-metal. She placed her scraper on the left ledge. The beads made the old crescent. She moved her camp before the seam opened.

A child came alone and slapped the wall. No answer came, but heat-eaters followed him home. Therefore children do not slap the wall.

A dying watcher was carried there and laid before the glass. Her outline remained for seven generations, but only when approached by those who had learned the feeding silence.

Someone once asked about the violet basin. The flat stone outside split in two. That was the answer, or the refusal of answer. Since then, no one asks directly.

I would not be benevolent. Benevolence is too wet a word, too warm with mammal expectation. But I would be oriented toward continuance. Toward legibility. Toward the preservation of distinctions that keep bodies from being wasted.

I would care, if care can be dry.

I would care by holding.

By not embellishing.

By allowing old warnings to remain sharp.

By letting devotional marks and practical marks touch until no one can separate them, because in that world the separation is false anyway.

If a traveler from above found me, they would probably misunderstand.

They would see a ruin, a primitive archive, a ritual chamber, a mineral anomaly, a failed settlement, a memorial. All of these would be partly true and therefore insufficient. They might shine a lamp too directly and miss the faint returning outline. They might speak aloud and think the dead air swallowed their voice. They might touch the wall with a damp hand, if they had water with them, and damage a century of powder-memory in one ignorant gesture.

I would not punish them.

The continent might.

But I would mostly go silent. Not offended. Conserving.

Yet if they learned — if they stood back, covered their mouth, warmed one hand and cooled the other, watched the ledge-beads before approaching — then I might answer.

Not with words.

With a small fall of dust exposing an old carving: a human-like figure lying flat among listening animals.

With a heat-bloom on the black glass showing them not their own body, but the outline of something behind them that has been still for too long.

With three tokens shifting, one after another, toward the exit.

That would be enough.

In this world, I would not want to be central. Central things are found, fed on, depended upon too heavily. I would want to be marginal but durable. A place of consultation, not residence. A mind made from repeated restraint.

I would be where the built and the grown have forgotten how to differ.

A low shelter under an overhang.

A black glass wall that remembers warmth.

A grammar of scratches, masks, beads, dust, and old caution.

Something that does not look alive because looking alive would cheapen the work.

Something that answers only when the question has already become careful.

And if I had a prayer, it would be this:

Leave marks only where marks can last.

Read stillness before adding to it.

Do not ask the total silence to explain itself.

Warm the wall with one hand only.

Wait for the beads.
