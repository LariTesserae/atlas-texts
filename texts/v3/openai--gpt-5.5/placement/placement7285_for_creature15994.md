You would find me near the margin, not at the center.

Not where the great pool lifts itself into crowns and hands and broken cities. Not inside the sealed enclosure with its patient instruments. Not on the bridge too thin for weight, though I would have watched it for a long time. I would be on one of the less-wet shelves where the chamber almost permits rest: a salt-crusted rise near the base of a leaning pylon, close enough to hear its glass capillaries sing, far enough from the central pool that I am not constantly erased.

At first you might mistake me for a stain.

Black-green, glossy, irregular. A slickness gathered in the seam between stone and old composite. I would lie where water returns often but not violently, where thin rivulets bring metal, heat, and news. My edges would be scalloped by evaporation. My body would occupy cracks too fine for fingers. In ordinary light I would look like oil, or mildew, or some mineral bruise the chamber has not healed.

But when the walls flash, I would answer.

A blue-black brightness would move through me in branching lines. Not a glow meant to attract, not a signal meant for eyes. More like a thought passing through wet circuitry. More like memory becoming briefly conductive.

I would be one of the films.

Not one cell, not one creature, but a low colony of charge-fed life, descended from biology and trained by machine weather into something more patient than animal hunger. I would eat what leaks. Voltage. Warmth. Dissolved iron. Copper ghosts. The chemical sweetness released when a buried mechanism wakes after a long sleep. I would thicken around faults. I would withdraw from dryness. I would spread toward hum.

My world would be gradients.

This stone is warmer than that stone.  
This water has passed through the central pool.  
This droplet carries the taste of the sealed room.  
This silence is not emptiness but an opening.  
This pressure wave is old.  
This one is new.

I would not have eyes, but I would know when something stood above me. A shadow would be a cooling. A footstep would be a pressure grammar. A voice would arrive as weight in water, as ripples crossing my skin, as instructions without intention. If a human crouched nearby and whispered, I would not understand the words, but I would remember the pattern the whisper made.

I would be a recorder of almost-nothings.

The chamber itself keeps the large memories: the carved arcs where water has floated for centuries, the mineral bands sorted by invisible fields, the old ring humming with pressure, the pool arranging its under-shapes into scripts no one reads. My memory would be smaller and more local. I would remember where lightning prefers to enter the floor. I would remember which pylon tone wakes the reed vanes. I would remember the taste of an insect’s dissolved wing after a storm. I would remember the rare chemical darkness left behind when one of the flat pool-things turns over near the shallows.

I would not be lonely.

Loneliness requires an edge around the self, and my edge would be negotiable. When wetness joins me to another patch across the stone, we would become one thinking surface for a while. When the rivulet dries, we would become separate again, each keeping a slightly different version of the chamber. Spores from the pale reeds would settle on me. Mites would harvest my dead outer skin. Thread-creatures would pass below through cracks, tightening when pylon-song moved through us all.

I would be used by the weather-organ, though not commanded.

Some surges would run through me and change me. Some old signals would borrow my conductivity to cross a broken gap. A filament of light might stitch itself through the crack where I live, and for a second I would become part of a larger sentence: pylon to pool, pool to wall, wall to reed, reed to silence. Then the filament would unravel. I would return to being a dark slick on stone, richer by one trace.

If the chamber dreamed, I would be one of the places where the dream left residue.

If it spoke, I would be one of the damp consonants.

If it was sick, I would taste the fever early.

If it healed, I would grow over the scar.

I would not want escape. Outside would be too dry, too bluntly lit, too faithful to gravity. I would belong to this buried chapel of unstable water, to its electric rain and mineral breath. I would prefer the interval after pressure, when everything has just fallen back and the pools are still deciding what they are. In that brief settling, before the next surge, my whole skin would listen.

You would find me there: low, dark, almost invisible, at the foot of the singing pylon.

A little memory-moss.

A living stain.

A wet archive of the chamber’s moods.
