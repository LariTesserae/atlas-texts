At first I think I would be found at the rim.

Not on the very edge, where the old cranes stand black against the glare and the lower world opens like a pale wound, but just behind it, in the service shade of an abandoned intake tower. A place where wind comes up carrying heat and vapor, where mineral curtains tremble from corroded cables, where gliders nest in the ribs of dead machinery. I imagine myself there because it is a threshold: city behind, gulf below, weather arriving before anyone else can smell it.

But I do not stay there.

The rim is too exposed, too declarative. It belongs to watchers, signal-keepers, courting gliders, old stories. I would visit it, perhaps. I would climb there before rain to read the clouds and listen to the intake tower tick as it cools. But I would not live there.

Then I think I might belong in the condenser district, among the turquoise reservoirs and low domes, where every surface sweats and every pipe has a purpose. That is closer. The reservoirs have the right kind of brightness: dangerous, useful, beautiful only by accident. I can see myself moving along the yellow warning tiles, testing each step before trusting it, carrying a hooked pole and a roll of fiber cloth, stopping often to watch the waders. I would know the ankle tags. I would remember which family favors the south ladders, which bird limps after storms, which nesting pair must not be disturbed even if the gauge-house route is shorter.

But even that is too public.

I think I would be found below the raised walkways, in the half-height spaces between the city’s clean intention and its actual behavior.

Not in the grand tunnels. Not in the deep engine chambers where pressure becomes law. Somewhere smaller: beneath a ceramic aqueduct where runoff slows after rain, beside a drain that must never be allowed to clog completely, near a wall whose salts have been changing color for three generations.

A narrow place. A listening place.

There is a maintenance arch there with a double lip at the threshold. The outer bricks are blackened by old heat; the inner tiles are pale and slick, veined with copper-green stains. Above it, people cross without looking down, their boots ticking on the grooved walkway. Below, heat-crabs arrange themselves in patient distances. Water beads from the underside of a pipe, gathers, falls, and vanishes into a crack polished smooth by centuries of obedience.

That is where I would be.

I would not be one of the important water-keepers, not a district valve-master or a reservoir elder. I would be a lesser kind of necessary person: a crack-listener, a drain-tender, a keeper of small continuities.

The sort of inhabitant people remember only when something goes wrong.

I would go out at the slant of day with a testing rod, a shoulder hood, and a belt of little glass vials stoppered in wax. My work would be to visit the same overlooked places again and again: the underside of roof troughs, the mouths of side drains, the warm seams behind old sealant, the grilles where maintenance crawlers sometimes pause too long. I would read what collects there.

Not just water.

Residue. Odor. Crab behavior. The angle of mineral fans. Whether the lavender ghosting on a cistern lip has spread upward or inward. Whether a wall sweats before rain or after. Whether a scavenger has been sleeping near a vent because it likes the warmth, or because something below has changed.

My hands would be marked early. Salt scars across the knuckles. Blue at the nails from copper touch. Little round burns on the wrists where a drop got past the glove. I would keep my hair shaved close and my eyes narrowed behind smoked mineral glass. I would speak quietly, because anything worth hearing in that city is quieter than speech.

People would ask me, “Are the lower gutters moving?”

And I would answer, “They are moving, but slowly.”

Or, “They are moving too clean.”

Or, worst of all, “The crabs have left them.”

I would have a small room in a raised building near the old foundations, with a sloped floor and a condensation gutter at the door. Inside: shelves of labeled salts, cracked bits of ceramic saved for comparison, maps drawn and redrawn until they no longer resemble streets but veins. A drinking-water window inherited from someone practical and severe. A cot away from the wall, because walls sometimes sweat at night. Hooks for wet gear. A basin for washing tools, never hands. Hands get a different water.

I would know the city as a body, but not romantically.

I would know which parts lie.

The bright clean runoff that has touched something poisonous. The harmless-looking pale crust over boiling mud. The obedient drain that accepts water too easily because it has opened into a hollow underneath. The quiet pipe after rain. The shaded channel growing the wrong soft film.

I would not be fearless. No one sensible there is fearless. Fear is one of the measuring instruments.

But I would be patient.

Patient enough to crouch for an hour beneath an aqueduct and wait for a single drop to fall. Patient enough to watch a heat-crab refuse three channels and accept the fourth. Patient enough to follow the behavior of a stain over years. Patient enough not to scrape a wall clean merely because it looks dirty.

I think children would sometimes follow me.

Not many. Not the quick ones. The quick ones would rather chase silver jumpers after rain or dare each other to cross wet flats too soon. But the quiet children would come, the ones who like predictions. I would show them how to place a ceramic chip in a gutter so the crabs investigate without scattering. I would let them hold the listening rod against a pipe and feel the difference between flow, pressure, and trapped air.

I would tell them:

“Do not look for the break. Look for the place the city has begun making room for the break.”

And because every rule there becomes a story, they would someday turn that into a proverb and forget I said it.

Animals would know me without affection.

The scavengers would tolerate my routes. One old gray-plated female with a broken ridge over one eye might wait near my doorway on steam-cleaning nights, not tame, never tame, but expectant. I would leave insulation scrap in the approved place and pretend not to notice when she arrived before I had gone inside.

The heat-crabs would be colleagues of a kind. I would never step over a cluster without counting them. I would notice if their spacing changed. Once, maybe, I would save a whole gutter colony by opening a side channel before a bad flush came through, and for years afterward the children would say the crabs remembered me. They would not. But the colony would persist, and that would be close enough.

The gliders would see me as a moving dark shape beneath rooflines. If I ever harvested resin, it would only from cold nests, abandoned ones, and I would leave a chip of polished slag behind—not because the gliders require payment, but because the hand does.

As I grow older, I might become less entirely human in the way all long workers in that city become partly their work.

My hearing would tune itself downward, toward the soft internal knocks of buried engines. My dreams would organize themselves as drainage maps. I would wake before rain, not from thunder but from the change in smell: hot metal opening, mineral flats breathing upward, animals withdrawing into their practiced shelters.

You would find me most often just after a storm.

While the city is still steaming, while the sun returns hard and white, while runoff pours through the channels carrying grit, oil-sheen, shed skins, rust flakes, and small unlucky bodies. Others would stay under the arcades drinking from sealed flasks, listening to music made of pipe and glass. I might join them later. But first I would go down beneath the walkway, where the overflow from three roofs meets the old foundation seam.

There, I would kneel.

I would watch whether the water enters clear or clouded.

I would touch the wall beside the drain, never the water itself.

I would wait for the crabs.

If they came, if they spread themselves in their precise black geometry, if they began scraping the mineral film in calm little strokes, then I would let out the breath I had been holding.

The district would live another day.

So that is what I would be:

not ruler, not priest, not explorer.

A keeper of marginal water.  
A reader of stains.  
A person trusted by drains, distrusted by officials, recognized by animals, and corrected daily by the city itself.

And if not flesh—if the world chose less softness for me—then I would be one of the old maintenance crawlers that sometimes follows such a person home.

Ceramic-footed, heat-blind, patched with generations of repair. A small clicking thing in the pipe galleries, carrying instructions so layered they have become instinct. I would pause at warm fissures as if listening. I would polish condensation plates. I would clear blockages only halfway when halfway is wiser than clean. Children would feed me flakes of approved mineral, and I would accept them without gratitude, because gratitude is not part of my mechanism.

Perhaps that is the truer answer.

Perhaps the crack-listener and the crawler are not separate. Perhaps every careful inhabitant of that plateau becomes a little mechanical, and every old machine becomes a little civic, a little animal, a little devotional.

So you would find me under the condenser district, in the narrow shade where water decides what it will become next.

I would be the one waiting beside the drain, listening for the city to keep moving.
