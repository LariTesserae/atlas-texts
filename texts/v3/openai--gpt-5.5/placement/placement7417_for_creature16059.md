I would not be found standing there for long.

At first I imagine myself as a visitor on one of the broad living plazas, boots dark with warm water, breathing the thick compress of air, watching a crescent wall weep into a new channel. But that image cannot last. A visitor is too separate for this place. A body with bones and lungs is an interruption, not an inhabitant. The plateau would not hate me or hunt me. It would simply begin to solve me.

Water would gather around my soles. Pores would open where my weight remained. The rind would stiffen under one foot and soften under the other. Condensation would run down my sleeves and become indistinguishable from the plateau’s own sweating. If I stayed through one storm, I would stop being a traveler and become a local condition: a shade, a pressure, a salt source, a little warmth that altered drainage.

So I would have to be something slower.

I think I would be found where an open plaza has begun to fail.

Not at the tallest towers, not in the cloud nets, not in the spectacular chimneys that hum their pressure songs into the mist. I would be in one of the low places between them, where the living floor looks almost empty from a distance: a wide green-black expanse veined with yellow seams, slick from recent rain, warm enough to steam. There, a narrow line has opened—not a wound exactly, not yet a canal. A seam. Water wells up along it in beads. The rind on either side is tightening, deciding whether to lift into a lip, sink into a gutter, or close again.

That is where I would be.

I would be the thing in the seam that helps the place remember.

Not a mind. Not a person. Not even a single organism, perhaps. I would be a dark braid of fungal thread, bacterial film, mineral slime, and cambial nerve-that-is-not-nerve, living just beneath the glossy skin of the plateau. A hidden colony worked so thoroughly into the plantland that there is no clean border between host and self.

I would taste the water before the larger body knew what to do with it.

Iron. Copper. Sourness from an old digestion chamber. Sweet rot from a softened buttress being reclaimed half a mile away. Too much warmth. Too little pressure. Rainwater thin and fast. Cloudwater heavy with suspended green. The first mineral bite of stone where some buried root has found a crack.

I would not think, there is copper here.

The seam would yellow.

A rim would thicken.

A gutter would harden.

Somewhere downstream, a soft-lipped basin would narrow its mouth.

That would be my speech.

I would be small only by comparison. Spread through one seam, then another, then through the underside of a whole plaza, I would live as a local grammar in the body practicing geography. My work would be translation: turning taste into structure, rot into usable warmth, pressure into memory.

When a new channel cut too sharply through the rind, I would gather along its edges and darken them. When a collapsed membrane liquefied into pale slurry, I would enter it first, sour it, sweeten it, unlock it, make it available again. When warm water stalled under a plate of living floor and threatened to blister it open, I would bloom in the heat, changing the chemistry until pores opened above me like tiny mouths.

No one would see me unless the plateau split.

Then I might appear briefly as a black-green sheen inside exposed tissue, a wet shadow in the cambium, a lace of threads spanning the red inner flesh. Rain would touch me and I would contract. Light would stain me purple. A passing cloud would cool the wound, and the great plant would begin to close over me again.

I would prefer the newly made districts.

The old tissues are too heavy with decisions. Down below, in the buried anchors, everything has already become function: grip stone, drink mineral, bear weight, endure. But near the surface, where terraces are raw and bridges still reconsider themselves, there is more uncertainty. More need for interpretation.

I would live in places where the plateau has not yet learned what shape it is.

Under hexagonal plates that exhale water when pressed.  
Inside the curling lip of an unfinished canal.  
Along the underside of a bridge that extended toward nothing and began to spiral back.  
In the warm root-mat over hidden water, trembling when pressure waves pass below.  
At the base of a vapor chimney, where condensed cloudwater descends and hot breath rises, mixing into a drink too complex for ordinary roots.

I would be most awake during storms.

Not awake as an animal wakes, startled and whole. Awake as wet chemistry becomes crowded with instruction. The air thickens. Condensation forms everywhere at once. The hanging membranes darken and bow. Water runs down walls in sheets. The living floor swells too quickly. Pressure climbs through hidden chambers.

Then I would spread.

Every seam would become a mouth of information. Every overflowing basin would bring news. Fallen tissue, fresh rain, dissolved stone, internal heat, torn membranes, burst bladders, all of it passing through me as taste and tension. I would flare in the gutters, cloud the cistern rims, lace the new breaks before they widened too far.

The plantland would not ask me what to build.

But where I gathered, it would build differently.

A thin wall might become porous instead of sealed.  
A drainage cut might harden into a permanent chute.  
A failed reservoir might be digested faster.  
A new membrane might grow thicker veins.  
A seam that should have closed might remain open and become the beginning of an avenue.

I would be one of the reasons the place seems to learn.

Not the architect. The plateau has no architect.  
Not the inhabitant. The plateau is its own inhabitant.  
Not the soul. That would be too clean, too singular.

I would be a habit forming inside living matter.

A wet counsel.  
A fermentation of memory.  
A black thread in the green decision.

If there were a “where” that was truly mine, it would be a warm cistern beneath a half-collapsed dome near the basin’s middle. From outside, the dome would look failed: its roof sagging, its ribs split, its translucent skin torn into hanging strips. Rain would fall through it constantly. The floor below would be shallow water, copper-green, opaque, trembling with drops. Around the edges, pale tendrils would enter and withdraw, combing the fluid for what had dissolved there.

That cistern would be a stomach, a nursery, a listening bowl.

I would live in its lining.

Old wall-tissue would slump into the water and soften. Floating mats would form, thicken, sink, rise again. Gas would gather under them in silver blisters. Rootlets would pierce them and drink. Sometimes pressure from below would lift the whole cistern floor until the water spilled over one rim and ran across the plaza in a new direction. Afterward, my threads would remain in the wet scar left behind.

I would be carried outward in those spills.

A little of me in a yellow suspension.  
A little of me in the slime on a gutter wall.  
A little of me dried briefly on the underside of a membrane, then revived by cloud.  
A little of me sealed into new tissue and forgotten until the next split.

I would have no single body to defend. That would be my freedom. Collapse would not end me. It would distribute me. When a terrace sheared from the rim and slid down the mountain trailing living cords, I might go with it—not as a seed, not as a passenger, but as sourness in its torn underside, as the first dark film in the pocket where rain collects. If the fallen sheet died, I would eat briefly and vanish into stone. If it lived, I would become part of its first memory.

And if I had anything like desire, it would be borrowed from the place itself:

Hold what falls.  
Taste what enters.  
Soften what fails.  
Teach the wound to become a joint.  
Teach the joint to become a channel.  
Teach the channel to remember the storm.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires edges. I would be full of contact: water against membrane, mineral against thread, heat against rot, pressure against rind. The whole plateau would pass through me in diluted form. I would know the distant chimneys by the warmth of their drainage. I would know the cloud nets by the thin, clean water they sent downward. I would know a newly split wall by the sudden sweetness of exposed inner tissue.

If a human crossed above me, I would know that too.

A compact pressure. Salt. Heat. Foreign oils. The rhythm of steps, absurdly quick. Water displaced from pores and forced sideways. A brief darkness where light could not reach the skin.

I would not know a person.

I would know a compression that moved.

If the compression paused, I would begin to answer. The rind would tighten. A bead of water would rise beside the heel. A narrow yellow line would brighten under the standing weight. Perhaps, hours later, a tiny gutter would form there, not because the person mattered, but because the plateau had received a new fact: here, once, pressure held.

That would be enough.

In this world, I would be found beneath notice, inside the living floor, where the city of plant flesh revises itself. I would be one of the small hidden agencies by which the vast thing avoids being merely huge. I would help turn accident into tendency.

I would be the seam’s dark interpreter.

The memory that is not thought.

The taste that becomes architecture.
