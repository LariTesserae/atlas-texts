I think I would not be in the center.

The center is too absolute. Too much voice, too much god-pressure, too much of the city becoming one thing. I would visit it, perhaps, when the great leaves flex and the whole basin makes its low note, but I would not live there. Nothing with a private interior should live there for long.

At first I imagine myself high in the arcades, in the green-yellow light under the leaf roofs, one of the long-bodied mammals pouring over vine bridges with clever hands and stolen fruit. That would suit some part of me: quick, watchful, fond of routes, fond of hidden rooms. But no. Those creatures belong too cleanly to appetite and clan. They are all nerve and motion. I would envy them, but I would not be them.

Then I think I might be a bird in an old room-nest, a red-throated screamer whose call ricochets from the bark walls and helps make the city’s weather. But birds here seem too certain. They know their schedules. They obey the fruit-hours, the predator-silences, the old aerial bargains. I would get distracted. I would arrive at the wrong tree at the wrong dawn and be corrected by vine, toxin, or teeth.

I would be found lower, I think.

Not on the ground exactly, but at the height of thresholds.

I would live in the Green Nave, along one of the side passages where a stair rises to a platform that no longer has a room attached. There, behind a curtain of root-hair and beside a wall marked each night by fungus and erased each morning by snails, there is a hollow in the trunk shaped almost like a listening ear. Not a house. Not a nest. A pause in the wood.

That is where I would be.

I would be a small thing, or a colony pretending to be a small thing.

By day I would look like a pale lizard pressed flat against the bark, my skin mottling into lichen-script, healed scar, old pollen stain. My body would be thin enough that light through leaves could green me from within. I would have wide dark eyes and long delicate toes made for clinging to vertical surfaces, for waiting in the seam between room and lane.

But that would only be the animal part.

Under the skin, or around it, or continuing from it into the wall, I would be threaded with fungus. Fine white filaments would run from my feet into the bark, from the bark into the old stair, from the stair into root and damp and chamber. I would not be separate from my hollow in the way a mammal is separate from its den. I would be den and denizen together: a listening knot, a little interpreter of tremors.

I would feed on small things: gnats, moth scales, sugar leaking from bruised fruit, the mineral damp that gathers where roots swallow old stone. Sometimes I would lick the pale writing from the wall before the snails arrived, taking the night’s instructions into myself. Sometimes I would let the snails eat first and read what remained.

My work, if it could be called work, would be to notice when the paths are about to change.

Not command them. Not prevent them. Only notice.

The city would speak too slowly for most animals and too bodily for anything that thinks in words. A tightening root under the Green Nave. A change in the pitch of beetles inside a dry pod. A condensation drop falling early from the leaf roofs. Ants halting for one breath too long beneath a railing. The river returning as dampness in a stairwell before any water can be seen.

I would feel these things through my feet.

When the city’s next rearrangement approached, my skin would alter. Lichen marks would brighten along my ribs. A pattern like branching streets would appear across my back. Creatures who knew how to look would understand: not this arch, not tonight; the rain-plaza is budding; the stair remembers upward; the hunter has emptied the balconies; the river is thinking of doorways.

Most would ignore me. That is proper. A warning in this place should not be too clear. Too much clarity would become law, and this city does not live by law. It lives by tendency, appetite, resonance, and the accumulated etiquette of survival.

At dusk I would climb from my hollow to the underside of the useless platform and hang there while the city changed register.

The cicadas would rasp themselves into exhaustion. Frogs would begin to pulse in the cistern-blossoms. The winged amphibians would spread their transparent hands on the walls and light their throats one by one, until the passage looked seeded with green lamps. Moths would gather in the empty lanes like torn cloth remembering a body.

Then I would sing.

Not loudly. I would not compete with the frogs or the red-throated birds or the central leaves. My call would be thin, almost not a sound: a thread-note, a little measuring tone sent into the hollow architecture. It would enter chambers, return from arches, disappear into wet bark, come back changed. From that return I would know the present shape of things.

I would know whether two distant doorways had begun sharing a room.

I would know whether the old river had opened under the moss.

I would know whether the central square was gathering itself for one of its bass replies.

If the great tone came, I would flatten myself to the wall and let it pass through me. My bones, if I had bones, would hum. The fungal threads inside me would tighten like harp strings. For a few breaths I would stop being a creature at all and become a small included part of the city’s hearing.

That, I think, is the closest I would come to belonging.

Not citizen. Not priest. Not predator. Not prey, if I am lucky.

A threshold-listener.

A lizard-shaped marginalia.

A little living footnote written by fungus on the bark of a city that remembers doorways better than names.

I would live where the emptiness can be heard but does not yet erase thought: just off the Green Nave, near the stair to nowhere, in the ear-shaped hollow, reading the routes before they become routes and singing back the smallest possible answer.
