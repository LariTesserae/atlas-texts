You would not find me on the open crown of the plateau, where the pale birds sit in cliff hollows and receive the weather.

You would have to go down a little.

Not far enough to leave the high country, but far enough that the plateau begins to show its workings: a ravine softened by moss, its walls black with seepage, its floor crowded with cold runnels and warm mineral stains. There is a place where a hanging bog leans over a shelf of stone, and beneath it a row of dark vent-throats knocks at intervals under the roots. The air there smells of iron, rain, bruised fern, and something faintly warm from underground.

That is where I would be.

At first you might think I was only part of the wet thicket around the vents: a stand of reed-tubes, jointed and dark at the base, translucent near the tips, trembling though there is no wind. You might look past me, searching for an animal. A shape. Eyes. A mouth. Something that withdraws when watched.

But I would not withdraw.

I would be one of the listening plants.

Not a tree, not grass exactly, not quite reed, though reed is the nearest word. I would be a colonial thing rooted in the mineral warmth: many stems, one slow body, a web of pale rhizomes threaded through peat, stone cracks, old horn-scrapings, and the softened remains of animals that misstepped generations ago.

Aboveground I would seem fragile. Hollow stems, ringed like small bones. Membranes inside each joint stretched thin as wet skin. Tips beaded with condensation. A few narrow leaves, blue-black and waxy, held close to keep from being torn by rain. But below, I would be broad and intricate, gripping the warm seam where pressure rises.

I would not think in words.

I would know intervals.

The long filling below.  
The tightening.  
The first dull knock through stone.  
The second, lower.  
The lift of mud.  
The shiver in my roots before the vent breathes.

I would know when the underground water is iron-heavy, when it is too hot, when it carries sulfur, when blind white crustaceans are about to be flushed into the pool. I would know the difference between rain striking me from above and a hoof pressing the bog from the side. I would know the soft panic of amphibian eggs drying in a cold week, and the relief of warm vapor returning.

When the pressure-beat passed through me, I would tremble.

Not randomly. Not like a leaf in wind. The whole stand would answer in rings, each stem taking the pulse and giving it onward. The grazers would read us. They would pause at the ravine lip, nostrils wet, horns low, watching our shiver before stepping onto the safer moss. Their calves would learn us before they learned fear. If we stiffened, they would wait. If our membranes went slack, they would cross.

The catlike hunter would know us too. It would crouch beside our outer stems, rain silvering its whiskers, using our tremors to hide its own breathing. Sometimes a frog would climb its foreleg and sit there, mistaking the dark fur for a warm stump. The hunter would not move. Nothing here wastes motion.

Small lives would use me without permission.

Midges would sleep inside my hollow joints. Amphibians would press their glassy eggs against my warm inner walls. Beetles would chew careful windows into my older stems. Fungi would lace my dead sections before they fell. Pale birds would come in fog and pluck larvae from my nodes. After a flush, ghost crustaceans would cling to my submerged roots until fish found them.

I would be eaten, sheltered in, scraped, pierced, warmed, stained, and used as a sign.

That would be my kind of importance.

Once each year, or not quite once — time would not divide cleanly there — I would flower only after the vents struck a certain rhythm: three close beats, a pause, one deep hollow gulp. Then my upper joints would loosen. Small pale mouths would open along the stems, not bright, not fragrant in any obvious way. Their scent would be mineral-sweet, damp, almost animal. Midges would gather. Tiny flies would crawl in and leave dusted with pollen. Some would drown in the condensation pooled at the base of the flowers, and I would take back what I could.

I would not be innocent. Nothing rooted in that plateau is innocent. To live there is to receive the dead and be received in turn.

My oldest stems would be black, almost woody, bent from years of rain. Moss would climb them. Lichen would write slow green maps across their shaded sides. New shoots would rise through the old ones, pale at first, then darkening as the mineral water found them. If you broke one, the inside would smell of iron, sap, and cold smoke.

I would have no single face.

If you asked who I was, the answer would depend where you touched me.

At the roots: hunger for warmth, salt, dissolved stone.  
At the stems: listening.  
At the flowers: brief invitation.  
At the dead nodes: archive.  
At the colony edge: warning.  
At the hollow chambers: nursery for things that will later eat me.

And if one of the great underbog bodies passed beneath the ravine floor, I would know before most creatures did.

The rhythm would go wrong.

Not the regular piston of the vents, not rain, not hoof, not root-collapse. A wide pressure from below. A slow muscular displacement. My submerged roots would lift and settle. Water would move sideways through peat. The pool beside me would dome upward, carrying a bent gray sky on its back.

Then every stem in me would go still.

The frogs would stop calling. The fish would vanish under roots. The grazers above would raise their heads, chewing forgotten. Even the hunter would blink and flatten itself lower.

I would not fear as an animal fears. I would register danger as alteration: pressure here, warmth blocked, channel opened, root loss likely. If the underbog creature tore through part of me, I would seal around the wound. If it left a new runnel, my younger roots would enter it. If it died nearby, I would grow brilliantly for a season.

That is how I would survive: not by escaping, but by accepting the changed shape of the world before the world had finished changing.

You would find me most clearly at evening.

Fog low in the hoof channels. Ravine walls dark. Mineral terraces dull red and silver-white under the dimming sky. Water ticking from leaf to moss to stone. The vent-throats beginning to sound larger in the muffled air: thud, gulp, click, pause.

Then my stems would tremble.

Not all at once. First the oldest ones nearest the black stone valves. Then the outer green shoots. Then the translucent tips, each holding a bead of water that quivers but does not fall.

If you stood very still, you might feel the same pulse through your feet.

You might think: this plant is listening.

But that would be only half right.

I would also be speaking, though not to you.

To grazers: wait.  
To midges: rise.  
To flowers: open.  
To roots: take.  
To fungi: not yet.  
To the bog: something is moving below.

I would be a pressure-reed of the mineral ravines, one stem among many, one body made of intervals.

Not a person. Not a maker. Not even an animal.

A wet instrument grown by the plateau to feel its own buried engines.
