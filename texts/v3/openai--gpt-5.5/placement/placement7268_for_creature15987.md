At first I think I would be found indoors.

Not deep indoors, not in the sealed administrative centers or the clean upper rooms where gravity can be persuaded to behave politely, but near a threshold: a market vestibule, a transit shelter, a drying hall with gloves hanging in rows and bootwater ticking into grated troughs. I imagine myself with a cup of broth warming both hands, watching people come in fogged and dark at the shoulders, each one carrying a little weather.

But I do not stay there.

The place that keeps calling is farther out, beyond the inhabited stacks and the warm wells, where the city thins into causeways, survey lights, raw basalt, frost flats, and unfinished drainage reserves. I would be found at the margin where the plateau is still deciding what it is, where the first streams have not yet earned names.

I would be one of the drainage gardeners.

Not an engineer in the hard-commanding sense. Not someone who designs rivers on a table and expects land to obey. I would be one of the people who walks after storms, hood up, boots dark with melt, listening to water discover its preferences.

My district would be somewhere in the Grey Catchments, or just beyond them: a wide, cold spread of young stone east of a habitation ridge, crossed by three transit pylons, two animal corridors, and a line of half-buried retaining arcs still white with mineral crust. There would be no real river there yet. Only seepage, threads, misdirections, pools that freeze black at night, and one stubborn channel that keeps trying to cut beneath a service path instead of taking the easier descent toward the reed basins.

That stubborn channel would be mine, though no one would say it that way.

I would know it before it became visible from a distance. I would know the sound it made under thin ice. I would know which stones were placed by crawlers, which had slumped from the cut, which had been turned by herbivore hooves, which had been shifted by children during first-thaw games and then left because the water approved.

I would carry a narrow pack with hand tools, sensor stakes, moss filament in damp cloth, mineral pins, emergency sealant, three kinds of graded gravel, a coil of warming thread, and a lunch wrapped so carefully it stayed dry even if I did not.

My coat would be orange-gray, the color of municipal caution softened by years of mist. My gloves would have repaired fingers. My knees would always be damp.

I would be the sort of person who pauses often, but not idly.

Someone passing on a covered walkway might see me standing ankle-deep in shallow water, head tilted, apparently doing nothing. In truth I would be listening for whether the runoff is striking hollow beneath the crust, whether a new freeze lens has formed, whether the little channel is beginning to undercut the bank where the sedges have not yet rooted strongly enough.

I would not speak much while working. Speech would feel too large out there. The plateau has its own small sounds: drip, crack, feather, grit, the distant breath of a pressure wall, the click of a corvid landing on a survey mast. I would have learned to make my mind quiet enough that water could become legible.

My job would be partly practical and partly ceremonial, though the city would list only the practical portions.

I would prevent flood damage. Protect foundations. Keep meltwater from entering structural seams. Encourage safe channelization. Reduce mineral erosion into the lower wetlands. Monitor microbial establishment. Report animal-use patterns. Maintain storm overflow paths. Correct drainage hazards.

But really I would be helping the land acquire habits.

Here, a notch deepened by two centimeters so water stops spreading across a plaza approach and begins slipping into a stone-lined runnel.

There, three flat basalt pieces lifted and reset so a small fall aerates the water before it enters a moss bed.

Farther down, a tangle of red-rooted plants protected from grazing until they can hold the bank.

At the culvert mouth, a roughened lip for amphibians and beetles.

Beside the service road, a shallow salt-lick seep left alone because the large herbivores have already included it in their route and because their hooves, inconveniently, are doing useful work in loosening the compacted mineral foam.

I would have opinions about water that other people considered excessive.

I would dislike straight channels unless they had earned straightness. I would mistrust any drain that made no sound. I would say, “That pool is thinking,” and mean that it should not yet be filled. I would refuse a proposal to pave a wet hollow because two seasons of observation is not long enough to know whether a hollow is a nuisance, a nursery, or the beginning of a necessary lake.

I would be known to the animal advocates, sometimes fondly, sometimes with suspicion. They would accuse me of favoring water over dens. I would accuse them of favoring denning foxes over bank stability. Then we would both stand in sleet looking at tracks and admit that the foxes had chosen well.

The corvids would know me best.

One raven in particular would follow my route along the unnamed channel. It would have a broken feather on one wing and the entitled posture of a minor official. I would never feed it, officially. It would never beg, officially. We would maintain a relationship of mutual plausible deniability.

It would watch me place stones, then move one when I left, not always badly.

Sometimes it would drop bits of bright plastic or wire beside sensor stakes. Sometimes it would trigger a warming pulse on a drain cover just to see if I would swear. I would call it Inspector, though it would have other names from other workers and perhaps a name of its own too complicated for a human throat.

My home would be in a low residential stack near a warm well, not far enough from work to require long transit but far enough that I could feel the change between inhabited warmth and open margin.

The apartment would have a deep window facing east. Condensation would gather in the sill grooves each morning, trembling before the pane warmed and sent it down into the little collection channel. I would keep dwarf herbs under a lamp, mostly bitter ones. A bowl of warmed stones would sit on the table in winter. My drying wall would always be full.

There would be maps everywhere, but not clean maps. Working maps. Moisture maps. Animal passage overlays. Marks from storms. Predicted flows crossed out by actual flows. Small handwritten notes: good moss here, bank too eager, fox path, do not armor yet, children altered channel, leave, listen after thaw.

I would eat in markets often because solitary meals after wet work can make a person feel too much like a tool hung up to dry. I would stand at a counter with other maintenance people, constructors, teachers, frost physicians, couriers. We would compare weather the way other places compare politics.

Someone would ask, “How is your little river?”

And I would say, “Not a river.”

Then, after a pause: “But it survived the east melt.”

That would mean something.

I would be old enough to have seen named channels fail and nameless seepage become important. Old enough not to trust the first elegant solution. Young enough, perhaps, to still feel proprietary joy when a bank holds through a storm.

I might have come from a lower city originally, from somewhere with mature rivers that already knew where to go. Maybe that is why this place would claim me. In an old landscape, water can seem like history. Here it is still decision. Here, to tend runoff is to stand near the beginning of memory.

I would love the inhabited city too: the amber windows, the warmed courtyards, the children in bright suits, the quiet vehicles, the bats under bridges, the market steam. But I would belong most fully where the city has not closed its hand.

Out where the transit pylons step into weather.

Out where the animals cross without asking whether land is natural or built.

Out where a crawler returns after years with lichen on its back.

Out where retaining arcs hold a slope that will one day not need them.

Out where the water makes mistakes and is allowed to make some of them twice.

If someone went looking for me, they would find me after rain.

I would be kneeling beside a new thread of runoff, one glove off despite the cold, fingers resting on wet stone to feel the vibration of passing water. My hood would be silvered with mist. My breath would hang low. A raven would be pretending not to watch from a marker post. In the distance, shaggy herbivores would move through a median flat like dark, patient weather.

Beside me would be a small flag marking a place where the channel must not be corrected yet.

Not because it is safe.

Because it is becoming.

That is where I would find myself in this world: at the edge of the high wet city, in the raw grammar of its first waters.

And what would I be?

A drainage gardener.  
A listener of runoff.  
A minor civic servant of erosion before erosion has learned its name.
