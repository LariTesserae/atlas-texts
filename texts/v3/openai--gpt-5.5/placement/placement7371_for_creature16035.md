I think I would first look for myself in the obvious places and not quite fit.

Not among the elders at the cistern, though I would want their stillness. Not among the reed-cutters beyond the south edge, though I would know the clean slice of sedge against the wrist. Not in the smokehouse, not in the clay pits, not among those who can lift stone all day and come home gray to the ribs.

I would be found where the town is narrowest and most complicated: under the eaves between the meeting hut and the cistern, where three rooflines give their water to two troughs that should not work but do. There is a place there where you have to turn your shoulders sideways to pass, with a patched wall on one side and stacked jars on the other. That is where I would be crouched, most mornings, with one palm on the clay, listening to the hidden runnels in the wall.

I would be a water-turner.

Not an important title when the weather is calm. Barely a title at all. A person with wet knees, a bundle of reed plugs, a stone knife, cord around one wrist, and a habit of interrupting conversations because the drip has changed.

My work would be to give water somewhere to go before it chose for itself.

I would know the little decisions: which gutter may be allowed to run full and which must be split; which jar can take roof-water and which must be left empty in case the cistern clouds; which plank-walk is secretly a dam; which dam is secretly a path; which path must be sacrificed in thaw. I would keep small wedges of alder wood tucked into my belt, hardened clay stoppers wrapped in fish skin, flat stones for weighting reed mats over disobedient pools.

During rain I would become sharper, almost happy. Not because rain is safe, but because it makes the town legible. Every roof speaks then. Every wall confesses. I would move bent-backed through the lanes, tying up sleeves with my teeth, checking the angles of troughs, clearing leaves, lifting a screen, kicking open a bypass channel with my heel. People would call to me from doorways without raising their voices.

“South eave is spilling inward.”

“The old jar filled too quickly.”

“Lower lane smells black.”

I would answer with gestures if my mouth was full of cord.

Children would follow me until I snapped at them. Then I would give them tasks anyway: hold this plug, smell this water, tell me whether that sound is wood or stone. I would pretend to be irritated when they guessed correctly.

I would not be young, but not elder. Old enough to have lost people to bad footing. Young enough to climb when needed. My hands would be split at the knuckles, the cracks dark with clay. One shoulder would sit higher than the other from carrying jars. I would have a narrow face, hair bound close with greenish cord, and a strip of old fish-skin tied over one wrist where damp always found the same place.

I would live above a storage niche that was once meant for tools and then proved too dry to waste on tools. My bed would be a shelf, really, with a rolled hide and a warmed stone when someone remembered me. Beneath it: reed plugs sorted by size, spare lashings, broken bits of trough, two good shells, a bowl of ocher for marking sweating stones, and a little clay figure made by a child who insisted it was a goat though it looked like a collapsing roof.

I would have a dog, but not in the way people own dogs elsewhere. A small, mud-footed, ash-colored thing with one torn ear. She would choose my sleeping place because I dropped eel skins. She would dislike the western stakes and refuse the alley behind the smokehouse when the wall-hand darkened. I would trust her more than I admitted. Her name would not be beautiful. Something like Peg, or Sump, or Little Bad Weather.

I would be useful at night.

That is when I would most belong.

When the town contracts into amber cracks and damp breathing, I would wake before others at the wrong sounds: a jar ticking too fast, water falling hollow where it should fall soft, a gulp beneath floorboards. I would sit up in the dark, listening with my whole body. The dog would lift her head. If she only blinked, I might lie down again. If she stood, I would already be reaching for my wraps.

Some nights I would go to the cistern and find one of the true listeners there before me, old and wrapped in smoke, face turned toward the covered water. I would not speak. I would kneel, put my fingers on the rim, and wait to feel whether the tremor came from wind, rain, ground, or something slower.

I would not be a shrine-keeper, but I would sometimes help them.

The shrine of first ground would trouble me. Not frighten me exactly. Trouble is more intimate than fear. I would bring offerings when channels had behaved beyond expectation: fish bones tied with cord, a broken plug, a cup of milk gone sour, a strip of reed mat that saved a wall. I would stand before the dark slit and try not to imagine the ridge as a throat.

The shrine-keepers would say, “Do you smell stone dust?”

I would say, “No. Clay. Cold clay.”

They would say, “Again.”

So I would breathe in through my nose until the wet mineral dark seemed to enter the back of my skull.

I would be known for distrusting straight repairs.

If a gutter broke, I would not simply mend it. I would ask why it broke there and not elsewhere. If a wall seeped, I would want to know whether the water was seeking light, weight, warmth, or old habit. People would sigh at me. They would say, “It only leaks.” I would say, “Nothing only leaks.” This would make me tiresome but often right.

I would have little authority except in the hour when everyone suddenly needed me. That would suit me. Authority that lasts too long grows stupid. Mine would arrive with rain and leave with clearing skies. In dry weather I would be just another person scraping green slick from planks, patching my own sleeves, trading bad jokes near the peat fire.

I would be suspicious of outsiders because they step as if the earth owes them support.

If traders came, I would watch their feet. Not their goods, not their knives, not their salt cakes — their feet. A person who plants a heel on a drainage seam tells you everything. I might like one if they paused before a plank without being warned. I might even speak to them about the town, though not the important parts. Never the gray layer. Never the pools that show wrong roofs. Never which bubbles mean silence.

I would have a private tenderness for the town’s failures.

A slumped wall patched six times. A gutter too warped to hang true but still serviceable if weighted with three stones. The lower lane, always trying to become water again. These things would feel less like defects than personalities. I would know them the way one knows difficult relatives: with irritation, loyalty, and a long memory.

If I loved someone, it would show in adjustments.

I would raise their threshold before thaw and not mention it. I would leave dry cord where their old lashings had begun to rot. I would redirect a roof-drip away from the place where they sit to mend nets. I would remember which jar beside their door rings false when cracked. I would not say, “I feared for you.” I would say, “Your north eave was foolish.”

And they would understand.

What would I believe?

I would believe that water is not an enemy. Enemies can be defeated, and water cannot. Water is a neighbor with no manners, a messenger with no loyalty, a child with endless hands, an elder who remembers paths the living forgot. To hate it would be wasteful. To trust it would be madness. Better to converse.

I would believe ground is a verb.

I would believe every roof is a promise made downward.

I would believe a good channel is kinder than a brave wall.

I would believe the dead remain most cleanly when made useful: in the name of a plank, in the warning attached to a seam, in the memory that keeps a child from stepping where the mud smiles smooth.

By the time I am old, if I get old, I would become one of those people others fetch without saying why. A child would appear in the doorway and say, “They want you at the cistern.” I would stand slowly because my knees would dislike ladders by then. I would take the dog if the dog still lived, or pause because she did not.

At the cistern I would look at the surface.

Not like reading stars. Like reading skin.

A tremor from below. A skin of cloudiness after clear rain. One bubble, then nothing. Then three, close together.

People would watch my face. I would resent that. Faces leak too.

I would say, “Open the east bypass. Empty the low jars. No digging for three days. Move the grain from Mer’s Knee.”

Someone would ask, “Is it bad?”

And I would say what people there say when truth is more useful than comfort:

“It is moving.”

That would be me: not priest, not chief, not prophet. A maintenance creature. A narrow, damp, half-haunted person with clay under the nails and a head full of routes water has taken, will take, might take. One of the town’s small listening organs.

If the settlement is a body, I would be somewhere near its inner ear.

Balancing it.

Hearing when the ground begins to turn.
