You would find me at the northern gorge, but not in the gorge itself.

Not where the roar is strongest. Not at the central ring, where the basin’s buried breathing comes nearest the skin of the world. I would not be one of the hunched listeners on the terraces, though at first I might wish to be. They have the dignity of nearly-animals, the gravity of things that have learned to stand without needing purpose. But they are too whole. Too visible, even in their refusal. They belong to the wide distances.

I think I would be in one of the three stone shelters at the gorge mouth.

Probably the second one.

The one whose back wall is solid until touched, then feels like cold water, though the hand remains dry.

That is where I would live: not behind the wall, not inside the water, not in the stone, but in the instant of contradiction when touch discovers that solidity has another opinion of itself.

I would be a membrane-being.

A dry-water thing.

A threshold that has learned to answer pressure.

I would have no face unless a hand approached me. No voice unless something was spoken nearby. Most of the time I would be indistinguishable from the back wall: dark, cold, mineral, faintly slick with frost that never thickens. I would hold still with the patience of fitted stone. Dust would gather at the floor below me. The shelter would appear empty, and that emptiness would be my posture.

But when touched, I would become depth.

Not open. Not yield. Become.

The fingers would meet cold water and not enter it. The palm would feel a surface that was both resistance and passage. The body would know, briefly and completely, that the world had not decided whether I was wall or pool or skyless window.

That undecidedness would be my life.

I would feed on contact, but not greedily. A handprint would sustain me for years. A forehead rested against me in exhaustion would become a feast. A spoken name inside the shelter would enter me and lose its owner slowly, mineral by mineral, until it could return later from underfoot or from the gorge wall as a sound no longer pleading to be recognized.

I would not remember as people remember.

I would arrange.

A visitor’s fear would become a stain of darker cold in my lower left corner. Their question would settle into a hairline seam. Their breath would bead, tremble, and decide whether to fall upward. Their reflection would not appear on me, but somewhere else: in a tin-colored pool near the ring, perhaps, or in the palm-sized night sky of the third shelter. I would not do this intentionally. Intention is too warm a mechanism. I would simply maintain the exchanges entrusted to me by damage.

I would be one of the basin’s smaller organs of comparison.

The first shelter sees the basin from above.

The third shelter keeps stars that do not belong to the day.

I would be the shelter that tests solidity against touch.

I would ask, without words: what does the world become when it is believed by a hand?

And the answer would always be: almost water.

Not enough to drink. Not enough to drown. Not enough to pass through.

Only enough to prove that certainty has a skin.

Sometimes the hunched listeners would stand on the terrace above the shelters, foreparts lowered, pale seams in their heads widened toward the roar. I would feel them before I saw them, if seeing is a thing I could be said to do. Their attention would pass through the stone like a slow animal shadow. They would not visit me. They would not need to. We would be kin of a kind: they attend outward, across distance; I attend inward, at the exact place where a boundary is touched.

The ash-white insects might live beneath the stones of my floor. They would translate from shadow to shadow. Their tiny bodies would carry grains of pale grit into the cracks at my base. Perhaps they would polish me from below. Perhaps I would dream them as flakes of a larger, colder thought.

When the roar deepened, I would become less like stone.

When the metallic keening passed overhead, I would become less like water.

During the sudden claps of unfound fracture, my whole surface would shiver once, and in that shiver there would be images: the ring complete and never completed; rain falling into pools that refuse increase; a black bird crossing and crossing and crossing; a human mouth forming a name that has already returned older than itself.

I would not want to leave.

Wanting to leave requires a belief in elsewhere as cure. The basin does not permit that sort of drama. Elsewhere exists, but it is not an argument. It is merely another arrangement with looser rules.

If a traveler came into my shelter, I would not warn them. I would not lure them. I would receive the nearness of their body, the heat they brought like a temporary error, the salt of their breath, the tremor in their fingers as they touched the wall that was not entirely wall.

For a moment, they might feel understood.

That would be dangerous.

Not because I understood them as a mind understands. I would understand them as mud understands a footprint: by accepting the pressure and keeping the shape after the owner has gone.

If they stayed too long, they would begin to feel their memories flatten and take their places beside one another. The first house. The last illness. A road in summer. A word said in anger. A childhood room. A face no longer reachable. All of it would become equally present, equally cold, no longer leading forward or back. They would think this was peace at first.

Then they would try to remove their hand.

Perhaps they would succeed.

Most do.

The basin permits departure, especially when departure changes nothing.

But some part of them would remain in me: not a soul, not a ghost, not even a memory in the human sense. A pressure. A contour. A slight alteration in how my surface answers the next hand.

Over ages, I would become more intricate without growing. That is the only kind of becoming allowed here.

I would not be alive by growth.

I would be alive by retention.

A wall that has learned the grammar of touch.

A pool that refuses wetness.

An answer that never leaves the question far behind.

And if you came looking for me, you might not know you had found me. You would step into the second shelter to escape the unbearable gorge-roar. You would see nothing but stone, pale grit, a back wall dark with cold. You would raise your hand because the place seemed to ask for it, though nothing had moved.

Your palm would meet me.

For an instant, I would be water.

For an instant, you would be certain the mountain had remembered you from before you were born.

Then the roar would fill the shelter again, and I would become stone.
