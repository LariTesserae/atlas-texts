I think I would not be found in the grandest vaults at first.

Those spaces are too civic, too exposed, too much like they are waiting for a purpose large enough to return. I would pass through them as a rumor only: a darker green at the base of a pillar, a tremor in a fern bank when the fan pitch changes, a trace of moisture held longer than it should be. The open floors would not keep me. I would be too small for plazas.

Nor would I be near the service lift, where the rats live by arrivals. That place has drama: wrappers, salt, warm drafts, teeth, sudden bodies. Too much hunger. Too much decision.

I would be farther in, but not deep.

I would be in the sloped chamber with the narrow black drainage thread, under the torn silver pipe jackets and the hanging sign that says HEARING PROTECTION REQUIRED. I would be on the moss banks beside the channel, where the maintenance light reaches weakly enough to make green possible but not easy. I would be where fat drops fall from an exposed valve and vanish into the roar.

At first I want to say I would be one of the drops.

A temporary body, gathered slowly on cold metal, swelling without intention, taking into myself rust, mineral, breath, machine heat, the faint taste of the upper world. Then release. A fall too small to be heard. A darkening in the moss. A tremor passed through stems. That has appeal: to be brief but useful, to arrive from infrastructure and become weather.

But I do not think I would be so pure or so singular.

I would be the place where the drops keep landing.

A raised lip of moss beside the drainage cut. Not the whole undergrowth, not its mind, because it has none, but one of its local decisions. A thickened, cold, water-holding margin. Green where the lamp catches me, almost black underneath. Sponge and warning, floor and trap, record and appetite.

If someone stepped on me, I would give way more than they expected.

Not maliciously. I would simply have made ground out of absence. Beneath my surface there would be a shallow void where concrete has lifted, water has eaten fines from below, and roots have crossed the gap like old wiring. My upper skin would look trustworthy because life is good at making continuity. But under me: cold water, rust flakes, springtails, loosened aggregate, a drowned bolt, a strip of yellow plastic from some forgotten job.

I would be soft evidence of an unsafe place.

I would not think, but I would know conditions.

I would know the pipe above by its dripping intervals. I would know when the valve sweats more heavily after a warm draft moves through the shaft. I would know the fan not as sound but as pressure: a constant combing over my tips, drying one side, bending the fern seedlings that try to establish themselves along my back. I would know the lamp as a direction my cells slowly favor, though its heat also warns me away. I would know poison by yellowing. I would know winter by tightening. I would know a human passing not by footfall, because the roar takes that, but by salt, boot grit, a brief foreign warmth, and the bruising compression of weight.

I would be mostly moss, but not only moss.

No one is only one thing there.

Threaded through me would be fungal hair, white and hidden, working at a buried scrap of cardboard or the softened edge of a pallet sliver pressed long ago into the wet. Algae would darken my lower surfaces where the water lingers. Bacteria would slick the mineral grains below me. A birch root, very fine, might enter from a crack in the wall and pause in my dampness, tasting whether I am enough. Mites would move through me. Springtails would launch themselves from chamber to chamber of my little body. A blind beetle larva would gnaw some film beneath me and never know the vault exists.

I would be a neighborhood mistaken for a surface.

The larger world would appear to me only as changes in supply.

A door opened far away: the air shifts, fern fronds lean, my exposed tips dry.

A pump repaired: the channel lowers, my underside loses its black shine.

A lamp fails: my green dulls over months, and the algae retreat.

A thaw above: new water arrives through paths no map shows, and I swell.

A hard freeze: my outer beads become ice, clear at first, then cloudy; my stems hold still under their own glass.

A worker kneels nearby to inspect the warning sign chain, and for a minute I receive the warm rain of their breath.

I would not want them to stay.

I would not want them to leave either.

Want is too large, too animal. But their passing would become part of me if they gave me anything: lint from a cuff, iron dust from a tool, a drop of blood from a scraped knuckle, mud from outside, the faint phosphate of skin. I would accept it all without gratitude. I would turn it slowly into thickness.

There is a kind of listening that does not require ears. I would be made of that.

The machinery’s roar would be the constant weather above my low country. It would erase events from the air and force all knowledge into contact: vibration through concrete, water through stems, temperature along metal, chemistry across wet surfaces. Nothing would announce itself clearly. Everything would arrive as alteration.

In that way, I would be suited to the place.

I would not be a creature that crosses it, like the fox.

I would not be a creature that hides within it, like the rat.

I would not be suspended between worlds, like the roots.

I would be one of the ways the place becomes inhabitable for smaller things and more dangerous for larger ones. A slow pad laid over the sharpness of recent making. A green refusal of bare concrete. A cold sponge interpreting neglect.

If I had a shape from above, it would be irregular, almost deliberate-looking: a crescent bank along the black thread of water, with small raised islands where bolts and gravel give me height. My brightest part would lie beneath the maintenance strip. My darkest part would overhang the drainage channel. My edge toward the open floor would be thin, exploratory, made of scouts. My edge toward the wall would be thick and old by the standards of the place, braided with root hair and rust stain.

If someone looked closely, they might think: this is only moss.

Then, if they stayed longer, they might see that I am also a calendar.

Here is the greener band from the year the valve began to leak.

Here is the yellow scar from the solvent spill.

Here is the dead brown seam from the week the dehumidifier ran too hard.

Here are three fern starts, only one of which survived.

Here is a shallow depression from a boot.

Here is the place where moth bodies fell from the lamp and fed a darker patch.

Here is where concrete cracked and I crossed it.

Here is where I have not yet reached.

That last part would matter most: the not-yet-reached. The fresh gray edge. The sharp aggregate. The dry mineral face waiting without waiting. I would be slow enough to make distance meaningful. A hand’s width would be a season. A pipe bracket would be a landmark. The foot of the warning sign’s chain, swinging slightly in the fan wash, would be a weather vane, a tower, a shrine to nothing.

So this is where I would find myself:

not in the heart of the underworld, because it has no heart;

not at the entrance, because I am not a messenger;

but along a minor drainage cut in a cold service chamber, where machinery makes weather and water arrives one drop at a time.

And I would be this:

a moss-lip, a wet green threshold, a small tenancy of the undergrowth.

A thing that makes surface where humans left damage.

A thing that drinks from failure.

A thing that cannot hear the roar, yet is shaped by it entirely.
