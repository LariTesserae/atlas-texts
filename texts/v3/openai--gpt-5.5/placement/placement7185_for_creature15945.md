I don’t think I would be found at the center.

That is where the city listens too deeply. The black pool is not a place for a self, exactly; it is a place where selves are dissolved into season, acidity, mineral, thaw. To be there would be to become too useful too quickly. Anything that lives in the central basin belongs to the whole city before it belongs to itself.

Nor would I be in the crowns. The birds can have that brief, soot-colored life above the lanes, clicking at frost and vanishing into weather. The vines can have their cautious hunger for cloud. The upper city is beautiful, but too exposed, too close to the cold light. I would not last there. I would become brittle from looking outward.

I would be found slightly off-center, where three narrow lanes descend toward the sunken plaza but do not yet reveal it.

There is a root-bridge there, low enough that anything passing beneath it must bow. Above it, a wall of shrubs has fused badly — not disastrously, but with the swollen awkwardness of youth. Water leaks through the seam in threads. Below, the path is padded with dark moss, except where the drip has kept a small crescent of black bark bare and shining. A moss-curtain hangs to one side, not quite a door, not quite a wound. Behind it is a warm pocket, no larger than the hollow made by two cupped hands, where old leaves rot slowly against stone.

That is where I would be.

Not a creature with eyes. Not one of the transparent amphibians, though they would know me by touch and use my warmth sometimes. Not a bird, not a vine, not a root with its old blind certainty.

I would be a small fungal intelligence living in the seam.

A broker, but a minor one. A local correspondent. A pale, branching knot of mycelium threaded through moss, root-hair, rotten leaf, and the underside of the wet bark plate. I would live where water changes its mind: where a drip becomes a thread, where a thread becomes a channel, where a channel hesitates before joining the routes that lead to the central pool.

I would not command anything. I would be laughably unfit for command. The city has no use for a mayor, and certainly not one made of threads. But I would notice relations. That would be my nature.

I would know that the upper leak tastes sharper after a frost.

I would know that the moss on the left side of the passage has been bruised by something warm passing repeatedly at dawn.

I would know that a root, three finger-widths below the path, has found a richer darkness and is beginning to thicken, which means the lane will rise slightly by the next thaw unless something keeps pressure on it.

I would know that the shrub-wall above me is trying to seal the very crack that keeps my pocket fed.

So I would negotiate.

Not by thought, not by language, not by desire in the human sense. By exchange. By making rot more available here and less available there. By feeding a rootlet that braces the arch but starving the little ambitious shoot that would spear through the passage. By encouraging the moss to grow thick where heat escapes, thin where water must keep moving. By loosening a dead leaf into warmth. By holding back a sugar until the vine above stops pulling so hard at a bad angle.

I would be a kind of editor.

Not of words, but of tendencies.

The city would speak through excess: too much water, too much shade, too much sealing, too much opening, too much eager young growth in a lane that needs to stay narrow but passable. I would answer by subtraction and redirection. A little nourishment moved away from a swelling. A little decay hastened under a cold seam. A little warmth preserved for the moss-curtain so it can still withdraw when a body approaches.

I think I would like thresholds best.

A threshold is one of the city’s most delicate inventions. It is not merely an opening. An opening is a wound unless it learns manners. A threshold has conditions. It knows when to stiffen, when to soften, when to admit vapor, when to refuse frost, when to let a small amphibian pass but turn a larger weight aside. A threshold is a question the city asks continuously: should this remain open?

I would live inside that question.

My body would be almost invisible. On most days I would appear only as a faint pallor under the moss, a floury bloom along the underside of the root-bridge, a line of gray-white beads where the bark remains warm. When the thaw comes too fast, I might fruit: small cups no wider than fingernails, translucent at the rim, holding single drops of water like thoughts that have not yet fallen. The insects would drink from them. One might drown there and become part of my knowing.

The amphibians would pass over me at night, if night means anything in that green-black compression. Their bellies would press briefly against the warm rot. I would feel the quick salt of them, the tiny pulses inside their glassy bodies. I would not hunt them. I would remember their routes. Where they pass often, frost is delayed by a fraction. Where frost is delayed, moss survives. Where moss survives, a wall keeps its skin.

A bird, dying in the upper crown, would reach me months later as mineral rumor.

Feather. Bone. A bitterness from the liver. A sudden richness moving downward through the fungal trade. I would not know bird as bird. I would know it as an event in the city’s economy: height returned to depth, flight translated into bloom. Somewhere above, a vine would flower more strongly. Somewhere below, I would receive the faint surplus and spend it on the seam.

I would be young too, because the city is young. I would make mistakes.

I would overfeed a brace-root until it bulged into the passage and caused water to pool where it should have run. I would misread a week of unusual warmth as a permanent change and encourage moss into a place the next freeze would punish. I would help seal a crack because sealing feels like healing, then learn that the crack was how the wall breathed.

The city would correct me without anger. A freeze would kill back my outer threads. A rush of meltwater would tear open my careful mat. The mosses would withdraw from my bad advice. Roots would ignore me. Rot would go sour where I had been too greedy.

Then I would begin again, finer.

That seems right for me: not the central intelligence, not the visible architecture, not the dramatic visitor from outside weather. I would be one of the small connective acts by which the place becomes more itself.

If a human somehow entered — too large, too hot, carrying the impossible dryness of another world — they would not see me. They might duck under the root-bridge and put a hand against the shrub-wall to steady themselves. Their palm would warm the moss-curtain. Their breath would cloud the passage. Their boot would compress the root-mat, and water would rise around the sole.

I would taste them afterward.

Salt. Skin-oil. Wool fiber, perhaps. A fleck of blood from a thorn. Foreign pollen. Ash. Iron. Fear.

The city would begin to decide whether that passage should remain hospitable to such disturbances. I would have an opinion, if opinion can be made of enzymes and hunger. I would not want the large warm thing to return often. But I would be curious about it. Curiosity, in my form, would mean preserving the trace a little longer than necessary. Letting the moss take its time digesting the salt. Holding the unfamiliar mineral in a side-loop of thread. Comparing it, slowly, to bird-foot, amphibian-skin, stone-sweat, thaw.

I would be a memory that has not yet become architecture.

That is maybe the truest answer.

You would find me under the bowed root-bridge near the central basin, in the damp seam where wall, path, and hidden water meet. I would be a fungal threshold-keeper: a pale exchange of rot and message, tending the small negotiations that keep a passage open without letting it become empty.

I would live by listening to what falls.

Drip from the crowns. Frost loosened from leaf-edges. The softened body of an insect. Mineral seep from young stone. A torn root-tip. The warmth left behind by a passing animal. The slow downward news of a dead bird.

I would take these things in and answer with tiny alterations.

A softened curtain.

A thickened moss.

A delayed rot.

A root persuaded aside.

A passage kept.

Not important enough to be named by the city, if the city named things. But necessary in the way small continuities are necessary. One of the places where the plant-city’s vast, cold persistence becomes local, intimate, almost careful.

And if I had a desire, it would be this:

to keep the seam from closing before it has learned what it is for.
