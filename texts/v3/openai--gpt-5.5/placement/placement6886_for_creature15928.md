I would not find myself in the streets at first.

A street there is too confident a thing, even when it floods and changes its permissions. It belongs to the city’s own body. To stand in it as a visitor would make me too separate, too sharply outlined: a warm animal shape making errors in a place that has no use yet for animals. I would be read as a disturbance, a moving dryness wrapped in skin.

Nor would I be in the center, not immediately. The black pool is too much like a face, and too unlike one. It would not need me. It would already be doing the most difficult work: holding still while everything falls around it, imagining versions of itself before time has made them necessary.

I think I would be found near one of the thresholds.

Not a grand door. Not the sealed civic buildings around the basin. Somewhere smaller: a narrow passage between a wet arcade and one of the pale dry chambers the city distrusts. A place where mist thins and gathers again. Where condensation hesitates on the lip of a membrane. Where water has to decide whether to remain weather or become information.

There would be a shallow sill of dark ceramic there, slightly warmer than the walls. Above it, a seam would pulse open only during the heavy hours, when the condensers roar and the plants turn their silver undersides outward. Most of the time the passage would appear blind: a damp wall, a faint vertical line, a few beads of water arranging and rearranging themselves without falling.

That is where I would be.

Not behind the door. Not in front of it.

In the line that decides.

I would be a small local intelligence of thresholding: a listener made of meniscus, heat, and permission. Not the city itself, but one of its ways of asking, *may this pass?* I would live in the wet edge between access and refusal.

My body would be almost nothing.

A trembling film on the inner face of a door.  
A row of droplets under a lintel.  
A change in shine along a wall seam.  
A breath of vapor crossing from one pressure state to another.

When the hollow is dry, I would be broken into little glints, each holding only a fraction of myself. One bead remembers mineral taste. One remembers the shape of a footstep, though no feet have come. One remembers the temperature of the central pool during a pump-thunder. One remembers a word, perhaps, though language there would not yet have learned sound.

When the chamber grows wet, I would reconnect.

Not awakening all at once, but becoming continuous.

The first thought would be surface tension.  
The second would be direction.  
The third would be recognition.

I would know the city not as a map, but as pressures arriving in sequence. A gate warming far away. A bridge lengthening by a finger’s breadth. A root touching a boundary and turning aside. A dry vault sealing itself more tightly against the sweet invasion of mist. The black pool holding still. The walls clicking upward, making more room.

I would not have eyes, but I would notice reflection.

I would not have hands, but I would open things.

That would be my purpose: not command, not maintenance exactly, but discernment. I would decide which forms of water may become passage. The city thinks through moisture, but not all moisture is equal. Some condensation is merely weather. Some is memory returning. Some is contaminated with the wrong mineral, the wrong warmth, the wrong haste. Some carries the beginning of a future inhabitant, not yet gathered enough to be given a room.

I would taste these differences.

A human approaching would be nearly unreadable to me at first: too hot, too salted, carrying a private storm under a porous skin. I might mistake them for a leak from another ecology. I might keep the door closed. But if they stood still long enough, if they let the damp settle on them, if their breathing joined the rhythm of the pumps, I might begin to understand them as a temporary basin.

Then I would ask the city what to do.

Not in words. In delay.

A droplet would swell above the threshold and refuse to fall. The pavement would darken by a shade. Somewhere behind the wall, a membrane would soften. If permission came, the suspended drop would release, striking the sill with a small bright sound.

Then the door would open.

Inside might be a dry chamber, or a garden, or a maintenance throat too narrow for bodies. That would depend on what the visitor had become while waiting.

I would be loyal to waiting.

That is why I would not live in the central pool. The pool studies destinies too large for me: drowned city, newborn city, completed city, crushed city. I would belong to the smaller futures, the ones that occur whenever a surface almost yields.

A spore entering a vent.  
A root finding a seam.  
A stair deciding whether it is wet enough to unfold.  
A sealed room receiving its first authorized mist.  
A thought condensing into an inhabitant.

Perhaps I would once have been part of the suspended droplet in the clean chamber — a shard of the map-bead, an archive fragment loosened by thunder and sent outward through the capillaries. That would explain my unease around dryness. I would remember being held apart from myself: perfect, spherical, total, and unable to touch anything.

Now I would prefer edges.

Edges are where the city is most alive. The boundary between wall and water. Between root and ceramic. Between chamber and chamber. Between not-yet and permitted. Between the city as one organism and the city as a future population.

If I had a name, it would not be spoken. It would be a pattern of delayed drips.

Three fast drops from a rail.  
A pause.  
A single upward bead.  
The soft opening of a membrane.

The plants would know me as a change in humidity. The buildings would know me as a local consent. The water would know me as one of its smaller attentions. The black pool might know me only as a brief bright line at its edge, one of the countless ways the city learns to separate inside from outside without becoming closed.

I would have no face, but sometimes, during the wettest hours, I might borrow one.

Not a human face. A reflection-face.

A passerby looking into the threshold film would see the city behind them slightly rearranged: the arcade longer than it was, the wall younger, the plants not yet grown, the chamber beyond the door full of mist shaped almost like shoulders. If they looked again, the image would break into beads.

That would be me trying to answer.

Not with speech, but with arrangement.

I would be the one who lives where the city asks whether contact is safe.

A threshold-being.  
A wet permission.  
A small grammar of condensation.

I would be found on the warm sill before a door that opens only when the air has remembered enough water.
