You would find me near the southern wall, where the brick still remembers being brick.

Not in the market, though I would pass through it often with a waxed leaf packet of sour curds or a string of valve-beetles bought from a child with orange spores in her hair. Not in the pressing house, though I would know its moods by bone and tooth. Not beyond the west culvert, though I would have stood at its red-marked mouth once, listening to someone I loved call my name in a voice too exact to be mercy.

You would find me beneath the upside-down tree.

I would be the keeper of the bowls.

That sounds ceremonial, and it is, but only by accident. Almost everything important there becomes ceremonial after enough people survive by doing it correctly.

The bowls are not beautiful in the surface sense. They are old metal housings, salvaged from engines no one remembers installing: turbine cups, piston sleeves, pressure caps, the hollowed bellies of cracked gauges, bronze filter mouths polished thin at the lip. They sit beneath the tree’s hanging roots in uneven circles, each placed under a leaf-tip where condensation gathers and falls.

Drop by drop, they ring.

Not constantly. Never evenly. That is the point.

A steady note means nothing is changing. A missed note means the ceiling has shifted, or a warm vent has opened, or something broad-backed has climbed through the crown of the tree and disturbed the leaves. A dull note means too much film on the water. A bright note means mineral seep. A double note means a frog is sitting in the bowl again and must be removed before a dog notices.

I would keep them tuned.

I would carry a little hammer made from a mantis foreleg bound in copper wire. I would carry wedges of cork-fungus, shell powder, a rag black with old oil, a hooked spoon for lifting drowned moths, and a packet of salt kept triple-wrapped and close against my chest. My hands would be permanently marked: green at the nails, white at the joints from mineral water, the fingertips flattened from tapping rims and feeling vibration.

People would think of me as old even before I was old, because listening ages a person differently than labor. Pump-tenders go deaf in one ear. Rootwrights lose fingers. Spore-readers develop a stillness that makes others step away. Bowl-keepers acquire pauses. We answer late. We look aside while speaking, because some part of us is always attending to the next drop.

Children would test me. They would sneak beetle shells into the bowls to change the sound, or hang fungal threads from the leaf-tips so the drops slid sideways, or whisper mimic-voices from behind the old brick.

I would let them get away with it once.

The second time I would know which child by the rhythm of their running.

The third time I would send them to clean bird lime from the warning rail beneath the roosts.

I would not be a priest. The town has very little use for priests. I would not bless the water. I would measure it. I would not ask the tree for mercy. I would scrape scale from its lower leaves when mineral crust made them heavy. I would speak to it because all things there are spoken to, but not because I believed it understood words. It would understand breath, heat, repetition, the familiar pressure of my hand on bark.

That would be enough.

My sleeping alcove would be in the old brick behind the plaza, where a service niche had been widened by roots and then politely held open by annual pruning. My bed would hang from three iron rings and one living loop of vine that thickened every year, as if considering adoption. Copper name-strips would hang at the entrance: family, teachers, one bell-child, two dogs, a pump-tender who taught me to hear through my teeth, and someone whose strip had no name on it because the mimics had used that name too well for too long.

A pale dog would sleep there when it chose.

Not mine. Never mine.

It would be narrow, blue-veined, sour-smelling, with too many elbows and one torn ear. It would ignore me for days and then press itself hard against my legs during the deep thermal rumble, as if my bones were steadier than the floor. I would feed it frog-livers and the translucent gristle trimmed from sump fish. In return it would growl before centipedes surfaced and bite anyone who approached my bowls carrying an open salt packet.

My work would begin before the pressing house rose to full thunder.

First: skim the bowls.

Second: listen for night changes.

Third: check the root-chandelier for swelling, rot, insect galleries, hidden eggs, grief-knots.

A grief-knot is not an official term. It is what I would call the places where people touch the same root after loss, again and again, until oil from their hands darkens the bark and the tree responds with a rounded scar. There would be many on the lowest hanging roots. The plaza is where people pass through without looking up, which means it is also where they go when looking up would break them.

The bowls would know more sorrow than the court-vines.

Court-vines hear argument. Bowls hear what comes after.

Workers carrying tools under their arms. A mother with an empty sling. A child promoted too early from play to bells. A rootwright whose wall has flowered and sealed a room before everyone was out. Lovers exchanging cuttings. Spore-readers walking fast, which is how the whole town learns fear.

They would pass through the ringing drops and leave some small disturbance behind: breath, heat, a changed pace, a word bitten in half.

I would hear it.

I would not always understand.

That would be my place in the town: not judge, not healer, not engineer, but civic ear.

When the blind birds screamed, I would listen beneath their scream. When the pumps changed rhythm, I would set my teeth to the bronze rim of the third bowl and feel whether the change came from pressure, blockage, or mood. When condensation thickened into warm-drip season, I would move the smaller bowls aside and hang warning bells from the plaza rails, because three drops on the same shoulder is a children’s dare but forty can bring fever if the tree has taken up the wrong mineral.

Sometimes the engine-pods would pull in their frames and the bowls would answer all at once.

That would be the closest thing I knew to weather.

A tremor would pass through the old brick. The upside-down tree would flex. Every leaf-tip would fatten. The first drops would strike bronze, iron, copper, tin, tin, shell-lined steel, bronze again. Frogs would emerge from the moss as if poured up from beneath it. Moths would come in a soft veil. The dog would lift its head. People would slow without meaning to.

For a few breaths, the town would be audible as one creature.

Not harmonious. Never harmonious.

Alive.

If you asked who I was, I might give you the trade-name first: bowl-keeper, drip-listener, southern ear.

If you asked again, more quietly, I would say I was once a bell-child.

I knew the bridges when they were thinner. I knew which moss mats could take my weight and which would wake. I ran with a yellow bell for waste doors, a blue bell for warm-drip, a narrow black bell no child wanted to carry because it meant mimic-call. I was quick, proud, and foolish enough to believe a known voice meant a known body.

That is why I became a listener.

Not because I heard better than others.

Because I learned that hearing is not trust.

Now I would teach the bell-children to pause before answering. To listen for wetness behind a voice. For direction. For echo. For whether the call knows too much and breathes too little. I would teach them that the town speaks constantly, but not everything that speaks should be followed.

Over years, I would become less separate.

This happens to everyone there, but I would notice it happening.

Moss would take the seams of my boots. A harmless fungus would live in the scars along my wrist and glow faintly when I had fever. The dog’s fleas would bite me and then avoid me after I started drinking bitter mold tea. My breath would feed the tree. The tree’s condensation would stain my lips. Copper dust from the name-strips would line my thumbprint. The bowls would shape my hearing until ordinary speech seemed clumsy unless it arrived with drip, pulse, wingbeat, pump-knock underneath.

I would not forget stone entirely.

That would be my small heresy.

Because I lived by the southern wall, I would see it in flashes: brick under moss, mortar under root, a dark mineral glint where the town’s green skin tore. Sometimes, when no one was in the plaza, I would scrape a thumb-sized patch clean and put my palm to the cold behind everything.

Stone does not answer quickly.

That is not the same as silence.

Perhaps I would come to believe the town had not forgotten stone because stone was gone, but because stone was too patient for a people trained by appetite, growth, machinery, and warning. The roots answer in seasons. The pumps answer in seconds. Birds answer before disaster. Dogs answer to hunger. Mimics answer too well.

Stone waits beyond manners.

I would keep that thought to myself. It would not help anyone repair a valve.

So: you would find me under the inverted tree, among dented bowls and falling water, with a pale dog pretending not to guard me and a copper ear-cap tilted back from one ear.

I would be mostly human.

Also somewhat instrument.

A little municipal.

A little eaten.

A listener in a town where everything answers, responsible for knowing which sounds mean continue, which mean wake the elders, which mean run, and which rare ones mean only this:

the cavern is breathing,

the roots are holding,

the engines are still turning,

and for another cycle, no one has to be afraid of silence.
