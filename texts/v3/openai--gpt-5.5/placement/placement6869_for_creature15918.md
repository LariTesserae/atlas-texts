I would not be found at the open rim.

The sky above the cleft is too thin, too bright, too likely to take a body away. I would not belong with the birds, though I would live under their weather: their feathers, their waste, their dropped shells, their panic, their young. I would know them as ceiling, as season, as danger falling in pieces.

Nor would I be one of the owls. They are too whole in themselves, too sovereign. They sit in the stone as if the stone has chosen to look back. I would admire them from below in the way small things admire silence: by freezing.

You would find me lower down, but not on the floor exactly.

Halfway between the guano-streaked upper ledges and the black little basins where the frogs throb after rain, there is a tilted slab with a wet seam behind it. The slab is warm most nights. Above it, droppings gather and flake. Below it, a shallow pocket of water appears and disappears according to mist, rain, and the patience of the mountain. In the angle behind the stone, there is room for a flattened body to wedge itself out of sight.

That is where I would be.

At first you might take me for a crab, because that is the nearest word. A small high cleft crab, dark as soaked basalt, with narrow careful claws and legs made for gripping stone instead of sand. But I would not be quite a crab in the ordinary sense. I would be one of the cleft’s agreements made flesh: warmth from the rock, water from the mist, food from whatever falls.

My shell would be low and rubbed smooth along the back from entering the same crack for years. One claw would be finer than the other, almost delicate, good for lifting beetle wings, sorting frog eggs from grit, teasing marrow from the tiny bones in an owl pellet softened by damp. My eyes would be black beads set wide, useful less for seeing distance than for noticing movement in near darkness: a cricket’s leg, a moth’s tremor, the slow flex of a centipede vanishing into a seam.

By day I would be mostly hidden.

You might see only the joint of a leg, or the shine of one eye, or the tips of my claws resting at the mouth of the crevice like two dark punctuation marks. I would wait while the upper world argued. Birds rasping and shoving. Lizards flattening themselves on heated stone. Flies lifting and settling. Frogs withdrawn into their wet silence. I would feel the day through vibration: wingbeats in the walls, pebble-clicks, the minute drumming of insect feet.

At night I would come out.

Not far. Never carelessly. I would move sideways through the cleft’s lower grammar, following the old polished routes made by countless bodies before mine. I would know which basin lasts longest in dry weather. I would know where nestlings most often fall. I would know the shelf beneath the owl recess, where pellets collect, furred and fragrant with death. I would know which moss can be scraped when there is nothing else, and which pale larvae fatten under the crusted bird-waste.

I would not be noble. I would not be beautiful from a distance.

My work would be reduction.

A feather becomes fibers.  
A beetle becomes shell and softness.  
An egg becomes membrane, calcium, scent.  
A fallen frog becomes a brief wealth.  
An owl pellet becomes a library I can eat.

I would be one of the small keepers of return, taking the cleft’s accidents apart so they can enter the place again. Not a ruler, not a singer, not a hunter unless hunger makes the word generous. A sorter. A night worker. A dark little hand of the mountain.

I would fear the snakes most.

Not dramatically. Fear, in me, would be a practical intelligence. A change in air. A taste on wet stone. A silence among frogs where there should be throats pulsing. When the snake passed, I would press myself so tightly into my crack that I became almost geological, a lump in the seam, a thought the stone had not finished thinking.

I would also fear dryness.

More than teeth, more than owls, more than the bright fall of open air, I would fear the slow shrinking of water. The basins turning from black mirrors to damp stains. The frogs gone mute. The guano hardening. The insects fewer and sharper in their movements. Then I would retreat deeper, into the mineral warmth, into the wet breath behind stone, and live narrowly on scraps until the mist thickened again.

When rain came, I would emerge changed only in appetite.

The whole cleft would become louder, almost swollen with itself. Frogs calling from every pocket. Beetles rising from softened crusts. Drips striking stone in irregular clocks. Bird ledges shifting overhead. I would stand at the edge of my basin with water touching my feet, tasting iron and rot and cloud. For a little while the world would be generous. For a little while every crevice would offer something.

If you lifted the slab, I would look impossible.

A crab at the top of a warm mountain. A thing misplaced by ordinary maps. But I would not be lost. I would be exactly where I could exist and nowhere else.

That would be my truth: not independence, but fit.

I would be fitted to the throat. To its darkness, its crowding, its damp stone, its falling food, its narrow mercies. My body would make sense only there. Remove me to a stream, a shore, a meadow, even another mountain, and I would become an error. But in the cleft, under the tilted slab, beside the vanishing water, below the restless birds, I would be inevitable.

You would find me where the place digests itself.

A small basalt-colored scavenger with precise claws, listening through my feet, eating what falls from the living ceiling, and returning it grain by grain to the warm dark mouth of the mountain.
