At first I would mistake myself for the one standing there.

The upright thing. The visitor. The body trying to be careful with its feet, distributing weight across root and moss, apologizing with balance. I would think I was the consciousness arriving from outside, bringing a clean outline into the green room.

But I do not think I would remain that.

Too much of the place works against the upright. Not violently. Not by refusal. By patience. The basin would not push me away; it would simply begin solving me. Damp at the knees. Peat smell in the cuffs. Midges at the eyes. A small spider making use of my stillness. The longer I stood, the less convincing my separateness would become.

So I think I would be found lower.

Not in the middle exactly, but near it: just off the open center, where the living raft has a slight weakness, a place that trembles when weight passes nearby. There, among sedge roots and moss stems, beneath a thin green surface, I would be one of the dark seams of water.

Not a pond. Not even a puddle.

A held wetness.

A narrow brown shine that appears only when pressed.

I would be the little opening made by a heel, the brief glimmer between mosses, the place where the floor admits it is not floor. When no one touches me I would be hidden, doing almost nothing visibly. When weight comes, I would rise black and reflective for a moment, taking the sky in broken pieces, taking the boot, the frog, the underside of fern, then closing again without resentment.

I would be small enough to miss and central enough to matter.

My work would be not to move through the basin but to let the basin move through me. Tannin-dark water would pass slowly along my sides. Root hairs would drink from me. Newts would know me as part of their under-road. A beetle larva might fall into me and climb out changed only by wetness. Fine sediment would settle in me. Spores would touch me, swell, vanish into thread. I would keep no shape that could be defended.

I would be a threshold with no ceremony.

Above me, the moss would make its tiny forest. It would hold beads of water in its green hands and pretend, convincingly, that the world was solid. Below me, the cold brown depth would continue in pockets, seams, blind chambers. I would belong to both and neither. If a person knelt and pressed a finger into the mat, I might appear as a dark gleam around the nail. If they looked away, I would disappear.

That would be my nature: to be revealed by pressure, then covered by recovery.

I would not be the basin’s face. I would be one of its answers.

When something sharp arrived, I would help soften it. A twig-end, snapped and pale, would lie against my edge. I would darken it first. Then fungus would find it. Then moss. If a seed fell into me, I would hold it until it either drowned or rooted. If an insect died across me, I would loosen it. If a footprint opened me too widely, sedge runners would stitch over the wound, and I would remain below as memory, not scar.

I would have no ambition to become clear water.

Clear water belongs to stones, to cold springs, to places with exposed beginnings. I would be brown because everything here has already passed through something else. Leaf through rain. Bark through fungus. Grass through peat. Body through body. My darkness would not be dirtiness but inheritance.

I would remember without images.

I would remember the pressure of deer as a long oval trembling. The pressure of mice as quick light taps on the roof. The pressure of rain as innumerable small arrivals. The pressure of a fallen branch as a season of shadow, then green. I would remember the human as warmth, salt, hesitation, excessive height.

I would not know names. I would know weight, return, seep, close.

If I had a voice, it would be the soft sucking sound underfoot when the mat receives and releases. Not speech, exactly. More like the basin clearing its throat in sleep.

And if I had a desire, it would be this:

that nothing remain uncovered for too long.

Not because I hate exposure in a moral way. Not because I fear the open air. The hollow itself needs air. Wings need corridors. Light needs somewhere to arrive. But surfaces that insist on themselves too sharply trouble me. A fresh cut. A pale root torn upward. A bootprint holding its edge. A stone newly dropped, clean and declarative.

I would want to wet them.

To darken them.

To begin their translation.

I would be kind in the basin’s manner: not sparing, but including. I would not save anything from change. I would save it by changing it. I would take the hard outline and let it loosen. I would let every fallen thing become useful to what has not yet fallen.

Perhaps, after long enough, I would become indistinguishable from the moss above me and the peat below me. That would not be failure. That would be arrival.

So: I would find myself just off-center in the green room, under the apparent floor, where a step makes the whole raft breathe.

I would be a hidden seam of brown water.

A small, patient shine.

The basin’s momentary eye when pressed.

The place where the ground opens, accepts the weight, and closes green again.
