You would not find me immediately.

Not because I would be hiding, exactly, but because I would have learned the garden’s courtesy: to be another close thing among close things, one more narrow presence fitted into an alcove, not demanding distance.

At first you might notice only the place where I had been.

A reed screen left half-tied.  
A crescent of pale dust where one knee had rested.  
Three foxlet hairs caught on a strip of copper wire.  
A clay tablet turned facedown because its warning was not for the present hour.

Then, if you stood still long enough for the sound-eaters to lose interest in your breathing, you would see a hand move between the black lacquer leaves.

That would be me.

I think I would be found near the dry fountain, but not at its rim. Not one of the central keepers. Not the oldest, not the one permitted to touch the air above the spout with two fingers. I would work in the second ring of tending, in the cramped passages where the fountain’s absence has already passed through other things and become useful in quieter forms.

There is a fold in the wall behind the fountain, where the enclosure pinches inward and makes a blind bay. A shelf runs across it too low for standing, too high for sitting comfortably. On the underside of that shelf, sunlight sometimes appears though there is no opening above it. Beneath the shelf are jars.

That is where I would be.

Among the jars that contain echoes of rooms larger than themselves.

I would be the keeper of those jars, or one of them.

Not a priest. Not a scholar. A practical functionary of impossible acoustics.

The others might call me the Listener of Vessels, or the Jar-Minder, or, when speaking with the old thrift, simply: She-Who-Taps.

I would be narrow and dry-bodied, like the rest of the caretakers, but a little paler from spending so much time under shelves. Dust would sit in the creases of my throat. My eyelids would have learned to close sideways before I thought to blink. I would wear an apron stiffened with resin and threaded with small pockets: chalk, shell powder, two bone awls, a folded listening cloth, a coil of copper wire, three plugs of wax, a dull knife used only for scraping silence from jar-mouths.

My feet would know which tiles ring falsely.

My hands would know the weight of a room before it enters a vessel.

I would not speak often. Not out of holiness. Out of inventory.

Sound is not free there. It has consequences. A word spoken too close to a hollow reed may fatten the stem-coils until the corridor stretches for days. A name dropped near ash-moths may wake them out of season. A laugh, if not caught properly, may bring foxlets from every shelf and leave the speaker feeling smaller afterward.

So my work would be the husbandry of utterance.

I would collect what the garden almost says.

When a jar answers with too much depth, I would mark it with a slanting white line and turn its mouth toward shade. When one becomes dangerously shallow, when its echo returns before the tap has finished, I would feed it a folded scrap of old song. When children are taught their first forbidden wet-word, I would be there with a cloth bag, not to punish them, but to catch whatever the word sheds.

There would be songs in my keeping, but no singing.

There would be names in my keeping, but few would be spoken.

There would be one jar, sealed in black pitch and tied with copper, that contains the sound of rain as remembered by someone who had never heard it. That jar would be kept farthest back, behind three empty vessels and a mask with no mouth. I would inspect its seal only with my left eye closed.

I would have been found, perhaps, rather than born.

That feels right.

Not grown from a plant, not carved from clay, not delivered in the usual staggered cradle. Found.

Years ago, one of the jars near the fountain began producing an echo that did not match any known chamber. The old Listener tapped it and heard not room, not corridor, not cave, but breathing. Very small. Very patient. The jar was set beneath shade for seven days. On the eighth, the mouth unsealed itself. Inside was warm air, blue dust, and me: not an infant exactly, but not yet a child. Dry-eyed, silent, holding in both hands a word no one recognized.

They would have taken the word from me and placed it in the sealed jar of rain-that-never-was.

They would have given me warm stones to hold against my belly.

They would have taught me not to ask what I had been before there was a jar around me.

Because in that garden, origins are not hidden out of cruelty. They are stored carefully so they do not spoil.

I would be fond of the small animals.

A pale lizard would live on the wall above my sleeping niche and use my shoulder as a warm stone when I sit to mend tablets. I would pretend not to notice. A particular foxlet with one torn paper ear would steal my chalk whenever the western corner is unreliable. I would keep a second piece of chalk for it in a reed tube and act surprised each time it disappears.

The air-slugs would interest me most, though. I would never be certain whether I had seen one or only made room for seeing one. Sometimes, while sealing a jar, I would notice pollen part around an invisible curve just beside my wrist. I would pause. Hold still. Let it pass. They are eaters of near-rooms, and my work would produce many near-rooms.

We would be colleagues, after a fashion.

At dawn — if dawn arrives from above and not from the underside of a shelf — I would begin by tapping the jars.

Not loudly.

One fingernail to clay.

Tap.

Then wait.

Some would return the dry, close sound of their own bodies.

Some would answer with a courtyard.

One might answer with a stairwell, though no stairwell has ever been built near that part of the wall.

One might answer with my own tap, delayed from yesterday.

Those I would chalk.

Those I would not open.

After the jars, I would tend the listening mouths hidden in the leaves. They must be fed, but not indulged. A listening mouth given too much speech becomes selective and begins to prefer grief. So I would feed them fragments: the scrape of a knife, the click of seed against tile, one syllable from an old warning, the soft exhale of a bellows. Enough to keep them gentle.

At midwarm I would not cross the short stair. I would sit beneath the low shelf and copy pictographs onto fresh clay while the air thickens. My signs would be neat but slightly cramped.

A hand beside a crescent fruit.  
An eye with a line through one half.  
A jar with three mouths.  
A child laughing into cloth.  
The fountain, drawn always empty.

I would know many warnings by heart and understand only half of them. This would not trouble me. Understanding is not required for obedience to be wise.

In the long stillness of afternoon, I would go to the fountain.

Not to worship.

To reconcile accounts.

Around its blue cracked basin, I would sweep up pollen and foxlet droppings, remove beetles that have polished themselves into exhaustion, turn the salt-rings so their uneaten sides face inward. I would examine the glass countries in the basin and note whether any had drifted into shapes seen before. If a crack had become a coastline overnight, I would mark the change on a tablet and place it under a warm stone.

I would not put my fingers into the distortion unless asked.

But I would stand close enough to feel it bend the dry hairs on my forearm.

There, at the fountain, I think I would be most myself: not thirsty, not satisfied, listening to the mouth of what is missing.

I would have a private heresy.

Every caretaker has one. They are not spoken, but the garden makes room for them.

Mine would be this: I would suspect that the absence in the fountain is not the opposite of water.

I would suspect it is what water leaves behind when it has passed completely through memory.

Not dryness. Not lack.

A shape of invitation so strong that life grows toward it.

This suspicion would make me gentle with the fountain, but not reverent. I would treat it like an old wound that has learned to feed the body. I would not ask it to heal. Healing might destroy the garden. Water, if it came, might be catastrophe. It might soften the mortar, rot the reed ties, drown the sound-eaters, blur the pictographs, teach every root to go searching for earth that is not there.

And yet sometimes, when the knock comes from inside the wall —

once, then hours of silence —

I would turn toward it too quickly.

I would wonder who is testing the door.

I would wonder whether they are wet.

At night I would sleep in a niche near the jars, never in a straight line with the shelf, never beneath a reused hook. My mat would be thin. Beside it: a cup for oils, a shard of blue glass, a mask for white-reed work, and the tablet on which I keep my private marks.

Not words. Marks.

Counts of things that nearly happened.

Three times the same seed refused to fall.  
Once the fifth stair sighed.  
Seven mornings the fountain’s dust circled against itself.  
One child dreamed of rain and would not speak all day.  
Twice a jar answered before being tapped.  
Once I heard my name from inside an empty vessel, though I had never given my name to anyone.

That would frighten me.

Not enough to leave.

I would not leave.

Outside would be too large, too unedited. A place where sound travels without being eaten would feel obscene. A place where the sky remains consistently above would feel careless. Rain would not seem like blessing to me. It would seem like an animal too vast to tend.

So I would stay in the handmade rule.

I would become one of its small clauses.

A dry, quiet keeper under the shelf behind the fountain, tending jars of impossible room, feeding the mouths that eat speech, marking echoes before they become doors.

If you came looking for me and asked what I was, I would probably not answer at first.

I would press one finger to my lips, not to silence you completely, but to ask you to spend your sound wisely.

Then I would touch the side of a jar.

Tap once.

Wait for the room inside it to return the question.

And only then, close to your ear, I might say:

“I keep what the garden almost says.”
