I would not be at the center.

The pale oval is too complete. It has the authority of a closed eye, or a word that has finished being spoken. To stand there as a person would feel like an interruption; to live there would be a mistake of scale. The center does not invite witness. It does not need to be inhabited in order to be kept.

I would be near it, though.

Not far enough away to belong to the rim, not close enough to cross into that smooth final dryness. I would be in one of the seams where the central crust begins to break into plates: a narrow dark line between pale mineral skins, half-filled with powder, half-shaded by the lifted lip of a flake. The sort of place no eye rests on at first. A place that only appears after the basin has been looked at for a long time.

At first I might seem to be nothing but discoloration.

A blackening along the underside of a stone. A dull stain where the salt has taken on a deeper tone. If the stone were turned over, the mark would look almost accidental: smoke without fire, mildew without damp, a bruise in mineral matter. But no one turns stones here. Or almost no one. And so I remain on the lower face, in the private dark, where the basin allows the faintest compromise with life.

I would be one of the smallest inhabitants.

Not a tuft, not yet. Not one of the cord-plants with its knotted discipline and hidden fibrous body. I would be something older in manner and less declared: a film, a threadwork, a patient colony pressed so close to the stone that I could be mistaken for part of its weathering.

I would not have leaves.

Leaves are too public. Leaves announce a bargain with light, and light here is not reliable enough to be worth addressing. I would have no stem to raise, no flower to risk, no color except the dark color of endurance. My body would be a thin arrangement of cells and mineral grains, a living varnish, a slow intelligence of adhesion. I would hold to the underside of the stone because the underside is a world with laws gentler by a fraction: less exposed to the dry breath, less scoured by salt, less answerable to the black-violet vault.

My life would be almost indistinguishable from waiting.

But it would not be empty waiting. It would be full of measurements too small for animals to understand.

The warmth stored in the stone.

The contraction of a crystal at the end of a long cooling.

A trace of solvency passing through the seam and vanishing before it can become moisture.

The difference between powder that merely lies there and powder that has touched the faintest inward breath from below.

I would know these things without thought. My knowing would be chemical, distributed, dim. I would not reach. Reaching is dangerous here. I would extend only when the world had already made room for me: a hairline filament into a crack, a darkening of one grain, a minute fastening to the chalky wall of the seam. If conditions failed, I would not die dramatically. I would close. I would become almost a mark. I would let my living retreat into the smallest possible version of itself and remain there until remaining became active again.

I would not hunger in the way a mouth hungers.

I would take what the stone relinquished. I would accept salts, heat, faint mineral exchange, whatever old chemistry still moved beneath the crust like a memory too reduced to become story. If there had once been water here, I would not miss it. Missing belongs to creatures with images of elsewhere. I would have no elsewhere. The basin would not be a deprivation. It would be the whole grammar of being.

Still, I think I would be placed at a border.

That feels necessary.

Not the rim, where the cord-plants stand with their bundled bodies and old-rope severity. Not the open floor, where the crust is too exposed. Not among the black tufts, whose folded blades have already made a visible decision. I would be lower, smaller, more secret: where life has not quite become a plant, and stone has not quite remained stone.

I would live in the hesitation between them.

If a hand lifted my stone, the underside would show a paler oval around me where the ground had been protected. That new brightness would be too much. The exposed surface would look wounded, and I would be part of the wound: a dark skin suddenly brought into a world of air. I would not flee. I would have no way to flee. I would simply begin closing myself against the insult of visibility.

Given time, if the stone were replaced nearly as it had been, I might continue.

Given too much exposure, I would become a residue.

Even then, the basin would not mourn. It does not spend itself on mourning. Another film, perhaps from one surviving cell tucked beneath a grain, would begin again after years. Or not. The place is not anxious to be alive.

Who would I be?

I would be the small black covenant under the stone.

The life that does not soften the basin, does not decorate it, does not alter its silence except by keeping it company. I would be a dark mineral film with just enough inward order to resist becoming dust. A nearly plantless plant. A colony without motion. A thought the stone has been having for centuries and has not yet needed to finish.

I would not feel lonely.

Loneliness requires distance from one’s kind, and I would be made of distance accepted completely. The nearest tuft, the nearest cord-root, the sleeping seed wedged in a crack, the pale oval where nothing grows — all of these would be part of the same held condition. None of us would call. None of us would answer. But our silence would fit together.

I would be alive in the basin’s preferred manner: by not exceeding my place.

And if there were a task in that life, it would be this:

to darken one hidden surface by an imperceptible degree;

to hold a few grains in relation;

to remember, without memory, that even here matter can arrange itself against disappearance;

to keep the basin kept.
