You would find me where the basin briefly rises above itself.

Not in the open hollow, not at the deepest pool, not in the high branches where fruit bats hang and parrots flare and vanish. I would be on one of the slight dry knuckles of the low country: a termite mound shouldering up from the mud near a root-black pan of water, under figs and lianas, with animal trails bending around it because every body has used it for something.

From outside I would look like earth.

A blunt clay tower, rain-softened, lichen-patched, dark on one side with damp and pale on the other where heat has baked the surface. Grasses would root in my cracks. Lizards would climb me for the little warmth that gathers after noon. Grazers would rub their flanks against me and leave hair in my rough places. A cat might stand on me once in the dim hour before rain, its paws silent on my weathered crown, looking down toward the black pool. Birds would peck at my sides when the winged brood inside began to stir.

But that would not be all of me.

I would be the colony within.

Not one creature exactly, but a many-bodied hunger with a single climate. Pale workers moving through warm tunnels. Soldiers waiting with sealed violence in their heads. Blind mouths carrying softened wood, leaf pulp, fungal comb, eggs. A queen far down in the wet interior, swollen beyond any idea of travel, laying the future in the dark. A king beside her, small and permanent. Brood chambers breathing. Fungus gardens digesting what our jaws began. Air rising and cooling through chimneys we built without ever seeing them as towers.

I would be architecture made by appetite.

The basin above would be vast, crowded, dangerous, but to me it would arrive as pressure, vibration, temperature, scent. Rain would darken my walls before it reached my lower rooms. The tread of a grazer would be weather. The digging claws of an anteater or lizard would be catastrophe. The fall of a branch would be an earthquake. Floodwater would be a slow siege at the lower gates. Dry season would tighten the passages and concentrate the smell of clay, bodies, fungus, and breath.

I would know the forest by what entered it and what could be returned from it.

Fallen leaf. Rotten wood. Grass stem. Dung-rich soil. The softened heart of a trunk. Everything above that seemed still would be, to us, already becoming food. We would take the old work of trees and make it soil again. We would be one of the basin’s hidden engines, part factory, part stomach, part city, part lung.

And I would belong there because I would not need space.

I would not require a clearing, a road, a view, a monument. I would make use of darkness. I would live by heat, moisture, repetition, and coordinated labor. I would be vulnerable alone and immense together. I would be almost invisible in my individual body, yet the mound of us would alter the ground around it: raising a dry island, changing drainage, feeding roots, sheltering other lives, drawing predators, becoming landmark without intending to.

At night, when rain began in the canopy and the whole basin answered leaf by leaf, I would feel the clay skin loosen. The outer galleries would cool. Workers would move faster. Fungal comb would breathe sweetly in the wet dark. Somewhere above, frogs would start calling from temporary water, and ants would adjust their roads, and crocodiles would remain logs until they were not.

Inside, we would continue.

Not hurried. Not grand. Exact.

If you broke the mound open, I would spill out as panic and repair: pale bodies carrying white eggs into deeper shadow, soldiers facing the wound, workers sealing air with mud from their own mouths. But if left whole, I would be quiet enough to be mistaken for ground.

That is where I would find myself:  
inside a breathing tower of clay in the hot green dusk,  
one small blind worker among thousands,  
part of the low country’s endless conversion of fallen things into life.
