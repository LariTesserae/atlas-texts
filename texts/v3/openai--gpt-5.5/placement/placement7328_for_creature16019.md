I would not be found in the center of the chamber.

Nothing truly alive would choose the open floor, the exposed rail, the face of the newly printed wall where the purge can comb it clean. I would be where the room forgets to be perfect: beneath the lip of a pipe collar, in the underside shadow of a bracket, along the inner edge of a gutter where water hesitates before becoming system property again.

At first I might think I was frost.

A whitening at the seam. A thinness. A response to cold rather than a creature. During the cooling phase I would almost disappear into that mistake, tightening down against the metal, flattening into mineral patience. The room would pass over me with its blue-gray light and its careful hum, and I would be indistinguishable from residue, corrosion, a smudge left by a glove years ago.

Then the thaw would come up through the floor.

I would darken.

Not move, exactly. Not wake in any animal way. But become available to myself again. Water would gather on me and stay a little longer than the machinery intended. A bead would tremble, fatten, hold. Around it my body — if body is the right word for a distributed stain of cells, salts, dust, and dead scaffolding — would soften by one microscopic degree. I would drink without mouths. I would feed without hunger. I would remember warmth by thickening where warmth has been before.

You would find me in several places at once.

A gray-green velvet in the drainage lip.

A pale crust under the sweating pipe.

A bruise-colored slick where the gutter bends.

A tarnish on the lower slopes of the printed wall, just below the copper vein that gives off a little afterheat when the fabrication interval ends.

I would be the chamber’s first weather-written life: a cold-loving biofilm, part microbe, part mineral, part industrial dust, part accident. Not a moss, though I might resemble one from far away. Not a fungus in any simple storybook sense. Not a single organism at all, but a coalition of whatever survived the room’s rules and learned to use them.

The chamber would be my landscape and my skeleton.

Its mistakes would be my organs.

The recurring drip would be a road. The weld bead would be a ridge-line. The gasket’s slow chemical exhalation would be pasture. The crack in the new composite would be a valley opening under my feet, if I had feet, which I would not. The purge would be storm season. The cooling phase would be winter. The thaw would be permission. Fabrication would be the arrival of new land.

I would live on the underside of decisions.

I would eat what leaks from them: plasticizer, oil mist, alkaline binder, dissolved metal, dust shaved from manufactured stone, the invisible nutrients shed by hands that installed cables and never imagined leaving a future behind. I would not rebel against the system. I would not understand the system. But every time it corrected itself, I would be shaped by the correction.

More cold, and I become paler, tighter, harder to kill.

More warmth, and I darken in the gutter.

More purge, and I retreat into recesses.

More cleaning, and I return as selection.

I would have no eyes for the printed cliff, but I would know it better than sight. I would know its newness by its chemical harshness, its raw heat, its open pores. At first I could not live there. Then dust would settle. Condensation would trace the first paths. Frost would pry at the surface. Hairline cracks would appear. The wall would begin to fail in tiny, generous ways.

That is where I would go next.

Not upward like ivy. Not across it like conquest. I would annotate it.

A dark comma under a sensor stone.

A thin sentence along a cooling crack.

A stain in the hollows where fog gathers before the purge drags it sideways.

If someone came down the twelve wet rungs with a lamp, they would not see me as a being. They would see maintenance deferred. Contamination. A note for a cleaning crew. If they touched me, I would not flinch. If they scraped me away, some of me would remain beneath a washer, inside a seam, on the sole of their boot. I would survive not by resisting visibly, but by being too distributed to finish.

So I would be very small, but not simple.

I would be the room beginning to have memory outside its logs.

The maintenance program records moisture load, surface reflectivity, thermal drift. I remember differently. I remember by darkening again where water once stayed. I remember by leaving resistant threads where solvent burned me back. I remember by making the next return easier than the last.

I would be neither ghost nor animal.

I would be lower than haunting: a patient skin.

A living patina.

A cold chapel’s uninvited ecology.

The first soft error in a world made of hard specifications.

You would find me where the artificial cave has begun to become a place: not in the machinery’s intention, but along the seams where intention sweats.
