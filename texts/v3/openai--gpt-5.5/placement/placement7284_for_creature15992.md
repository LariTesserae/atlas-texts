You would find me at the edge of things.

Not in the open basin, not on the ridgecrest, not deep in the old chambers, but where one condition becomes another: under the lip of a ledge at dawn, beside a dew-stone before it has finished gathering; on the pale margin of a living crust; in the blue shadow cast by a pylon when its shadow is not quite obeying the sun.

At first, from far away, I would look like one of the plateau people: slender, dust-colored, moving slowly enough that I might be mistaken for a trick of heat. I would wear pale cloth stiffened with clay and resin, not as decoration but to break my outline and keep the glare from finding skin. My hair would be bound in short cords, each cord darkened with shrub oil and fine grit, useful for catching morning condensation. My feet would be broad and quiet. My hands would be scarred at the fingertips from stone, ceramic, and the inner edges of old vents.

But I would not be one of the ordinary household tenders.

I think I would be one of the ones children are warned not to follow too closely.

A watcher of wrong water.  
A reader of pylon weather.  
A keeper of bowls blackened so they do not become lenses.

My dwelling would be almost invisible: a low chamber cut into the shaded side of a ravine where the clay stays dark two handspans below the surface. The entrance would be hidden behind a slab veined with lichen, and above it swallows would nest in a crack they chose before I was born. Inside there would be very little: sleeping mats rolled high off the floor, ceramic bulbs wrapped in cloth, a shelf of pebble records, bundles of copper grass, a wall stained with handprints in faint ocher bands. Near the rear of the chamber, where the stone breathes at night, a narrow crawl would lead downward toward an old vapor channel.

That would be my work: not worship, not engineering exactly, but attendance.

I would know which stones are allowed to sweat.

I would go out before sunrise, when the scaled grazers are still scraping at lichens and the long insect hunters have not yet warmed enough to move quickly. I would kneel beside prepared stone lips and touch them first with the back of my fingers, then with my tongue if the taste was safe. Sweet-mineral means the channel below is clear. Bitter-sour means a crust has been broken upslope. A taste like old metal means one of the black seams has begun to hum too early.

I would carry no loud tools. A ceramic knife. A cord of grass fiber. Three matte bowls nested inside one another. A little packet of white dust to dull accidental shine. A listening rod made from a shed fragment of dark pylon-material, found after a storm and never cut.

The others would come to me quietly.

A child with blue flushing at the throat: rain fear.  
A hunter with numb soles: crossed a living crust in the wrong season.  
A family whose dew-stone has stopped giving.  
An elder who dreams of stars at noon.

I would not answer quickly. Speech wastes moisture and certainty wastes lives. I would set pebbles down between us. One for weather. One for animal sign. One for machine silence. One for the question no one wants to ask.

Sometimes I would be sent into the buried channels, though less often now that my shoulders are grown. As a child I would have been one of the narrow-handed ones lowered into crawlspaces beneath dry washes, guided by taps through stone. I would have learned darkness not as absence but as pressure: warm air from the left means a living vent; cool breath from below means flood memory; no air at all means turn back, even if the elder taps continue.

Once, perhaps, I would have gone too far.

That is why I would have become what I am.

There would be a place under the basin where the old systems almost show themselves. Not machinery in the way outsiders would imagine it—no bright panels, no moving arms—but porous dark ribs running through stone, capillary webs, chambers whose walls bead with water that falls upward if breathed on too hard. I would have entered during the third day after rain, when the temporary river was still loud with voiceless life above me. Something would have shifted. A floodgate, or a pressure lung, or a decision.

I would have put my palm against a black seam and felt the plateau thinking.

Not in words. Never words.

More like thirst arranging itself into paths.

After that, I would hear certain silences before others did. The machine silence especially: the sudden absence beneath ordinary quiet, as though the stone had stopped remembering. My throat would darken in bands before storms. Hovering beads near pylons would lean toward me, not enough for others to notice unless they already feared it. Children would dare each other to stand in my shadow at noon.

I would not be shunned. This is not a place rich enough for superstition to waste useful people. But I would be given room.

You would find me most often near the eastern pylon, the one whose shadow bends north for seven breaths at high noon in the dry season and thirteen breaths after rain. Around its base grows black moss in lines too fine for old eyes. I would tend that moss without touching the pylon itself. I would watch the wingless insects carry impossible beads from groove to groove. I would set shallow bowls beneath the places where dew falls sideways and wait to see whether the water gathers, evaporates, or remembers some other direction.

At night, when the pale nodules open in the wrong dark, I would sit very still among them while pressure-moths passed my face.

I would not think of myself as lonely. Loneliness belongs to places where creatures expect to be separate. Here everything is in negotiation: shrub with seam, crust with foot, rain with basin, body with vapor, dead with root, machine with weather. I would have few companions, but many correspondences.

The swallows would know me.  
The crusts would tolerate me.  
The small grazers would flatten when I passed, then rise again sooner than they would for others.  
The high hunters would make my bones ache before their shadows arrived.

Once each season, perhaps, I would walk into the circular basin after the rain had gone but before the pools had finished deciding what they were reflecting. I would go barefoot, stepping only on remembered stone. I would not look directly into the noon-star pool. No one sensible does. Instead I would approach with my eyes lowered and watch its reflection tremble on the underside of a black bowl.

If the stars in it were sharp, I would mark drought.

If they blurred, I would mark flood.

If one of them moved against the rest, I would cover the bowl, leave three drops of water on the rim, and tell no one until rain.

My name would be a small one, used sparingly. Perhaps not a name but a pattern: two taps, a pause, one finger drawn across stone. Children would translate it badly as Side-Dew or Wrong-Shadow. Elders would call me by the name I had before the channel changed me, but only indoors.

When I die, I would not want a chamber far from the grids. I would ask to be placed near a damaged vapor seam, one that has never fully recovered from an old collapse. Wrap me in copper grass. Lay my palms open against the warm stone. Let salts and roots take the water back slowly. Let the buried system misread me as repair material, ancestor, mineral, weather. That would be acceptable.

For several seasons afterward, perhaps, the dew there would taste faintly sweet.

And someone younger, narrow-handed and too curious, would notice.
