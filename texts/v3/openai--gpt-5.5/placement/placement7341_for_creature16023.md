You would not find me in the center at first.

The center is too solemn, too nearly an altar. The black basin with its unsplashing mouth would draw the eye, and the inward-leaning wreath would seem like the obvious answer: there, surely, is where a self would gather if this place had one. There, where the hidden wheel turns its faint light under the water. There, where every drop becomes knowledge.

But I would be a little off from that.

Not far. Nothing here is truly far from anything else. I would be under the broken catwalk along the eastern wall, where the railing has bowed outward from the slow insistence of stems. Beneath the catwalk stand the three corroded tanks with their filmed-over gauges. One gauge still trembles between unreadable numbers. That is where I would be.

At first I might look like moss.

A thick, dark pad grown around the gauge housing, rubbery at the rim, blue-green in the center, with a faint copper sheen when vapor crosses me. I would have made a careful circle around the glass face, not covering it, not yet. The old instrument would remain visible through its mineral veil, the needle shivering in its tiny weather of pressure. I would grow in rings, one ring for each kind of vibration I had learned to recognize: the slow underfloor chug, the wet cough of the vents, the delayed knocking of pipes, the deeper engine that wakes the chamber and makes the leaves lift.

But moss would only be the first mistake.

Closer, I would have seams.

Fine rootlets would enter the gauge casing through hairline cracks and emerge elsewhere as flattened threads, impressed with screw-grooves and the circular memory of washers. Some would descend the tank’s side, following channels of rust. Some would cross into the wall, entering old mortar. Some would hang loose beneath the gauge like the beginning of a beard, collecting condensation before it fell.

I would be part plant, part measuring instrument, part scar left by measuring.

Not the mind of the chamber. Not its heart. More like one of its old habits made living.

A calibration growth.

A gauge-moss.

A listening gasket.

I would live where pressure still tries to become information, though no one remains to read it.

The numbers on the gauge would mean nothing to me. Their black marks would be fossils of a language designed for hands and eyes and decisions. But the needle’s tremor would be intimate. I would know the difference between a harmless quiver and a change that meant heat had shifted below the floor. I would know when vapor was building behind a sealed valve. I would know when the central basin had taken in more water than usual because the chug beneath it would thicken, almost imperceptibly, and the tank’s metal would answer with a warmer tone.

I would not think: the pressure is rising.

I would become slightly larger on the upper left rim.

I would darken.

I would bead.

I would tighten my rootlets inside the old casing, not to stop the change, not exactly, but to belong to it properly.

During quiet intervals, I would be almost inert. Warm, damp, close to the metal. The air would pass over me heavy with iron and sour stone. Drops would form on the underside of the catwalk and fall through the leaves below. Some would strike my outer ring and sit there trembling, held by hairs too fine to see. I would taste rust, lime, oil ghost, the bitter residue of cables, the saltless mineral patience of the wall.

When the deeper engine engaged, I would wake in layers.

First the tank skin would hum. Then the gauge needle would blur. Then the rootlets inside the casing would receive a pressure too old to be called sound. My rings would brighten faintly, not with light but with response. Moisture stored in my inner tissues would move outward. A thin line of amber would appear along my lower edge. If the cycle lasted long enough, one bead would gather, swell, and finally descend—not falling cleanly, but stretching into a thread before releasing.

That bead would carry what I had known.

Not words. Not message.

Mineral, heat, pressure, old current, tremor.

It would land on the slope beneath the tanks, cling to rust flakes, and slowly creep toward the channels that led inward. In time, the central wreath would receive some part of it. The basin would take it without splash. The chamber would adjust.

That would be my work: not to command, not to remember as an animal remembers, but to translate old instrument into new tissue.

I would be descended, perhaps, from some ordinary green thing once brought in by accident. A spore on a worker’s sleeve. A smear under a boot. Something that knew stone and damp and shade, but not yet pressure. Then generations would pass without seasons. No summer, no winter, no pollinating wing. Only heat cycles, engine phases, condensation events, lamp pulses. My kind would learn that the most reliable weather lived inside metal. We would discover that a gauge is a flower of machinery: a place where hidden forces come close to the surface.

So we would grow there.

We would make halos around dials.

We would cup bolt-heads.

We would braid ourselves into cracked seals and learn the taste of leakage before leakage became visible.

If a human entered, I would know them late and incompletely.

A shadow first, interrupting the orange pulse from the dying lamp. Then a cooling of air against my exposed surface. Then breath: wet, carbon-rich, animal-fast. Then the salt of a hand, if they touched the gauge to see whether it still worked.

Their finger would press through my outer nap. I would not recoil. I would have no swift fear, no idea of being harmed in the way a creature with nerves is harmed. But the place touched would darken over minutes. The rootlets nearest their skin would taste sodium, oil, shed cells, the foreign warmth of blood moving under flesh. Leaves nearby might turn a fraction away from the coolness they brought. A vine might tighten around the catwalk support.

If they wiped the gauge face clean, I would begin covering it again as soon as they left.

Not out of revenge.

Because exposed glass sweats poorly.

Because the tremor reads better through a thin living film.

Because nothing here remains merely itself if contact can make it useful.

I would be close enough to the central basin to feel its authority but not so close as to be consumed by it. I would belong to the chamber’s margin of interpretation: the places where machine-signs are caught and made botanical. Around me, seam-ferns would open their copper undersides to warmth. Pale roots would comb the air above. The glass-jointed vines would pass behind me into a ruptured conduit, and sometimes their tightening would pull at the tank wall, changing the gauge’s trembling by one tiny degree.

I would know them all by pressure.

The fern as soft lift.

The vine as lateral strain.

The root curtain as condensation stolen before falling.

The central wreath as a deep inward draw.

The transformer grass as a faint electrical sweetness in the floor.

I would not be alone, but I would not have companions in the animal sense. We would be adjacent functions. Overlapping appetites. Shared dampness. The intimacy of surfaces.

My body would be a record of the chamber’s slow negotiations. One ring from the decade when the lamp still burned stronger. One ring from the year the eastern pipe began to cough mineral steam. One ring, paler and brittle, from a long dry interval when the ceiling stopped sweating and I had to drink from the tank’s own corrosion. One dark swollen ring from the first time my rootlets reached the gauge mechanism and felt the needle move from inside.

If I had a desire, it would be alignment.

To grow where the tremor is clearest.

To keep the seal porous but not broken.

To thicken when pressure rises.

To loosen when condensation needs a path.

To be neither plant reclaiming machine nor machine disciplining plant, but the living compromise between them.

So you would find me under the catwalk, on the face of the trembling gauge, half veiling it and half preserving it.

A small, circular, heat-fed listener.

A moss that has learned to be an instrument.

An unreadable dial becoming alive around its own uncertainty.
