I would find myself near the artificial summit, where the chamber has almost stopped pretending to be a mountain and has become geometry.

Not on the catwalks, not beside the hatch, not in the broader basins where the salt lies flat and ruled. I would be higher than that, in the most exposed place the room can make: on a black heat-tile slope beside a sensor mast, where the light is whitest, the cold is sharpest, and the roar arrives not as sound but as instruction.

At first I would think I was standing there.

A human figure, perhaps, with one gloved hand on a rail, looking down over the whole manufactured highland: the ridges, the basins, the sealed engines, the pale plants like afterthoughts. But a human body would be too wet for this place. Too full of pulse and complaint. It would fog its own mask, chap, shiver, make decisions, remember weather. It would be an event, and this chamber does not welcome events.

So I would not remain human.

I would become the lone violet stem growing from the crack beside the mast.

Not exactly a plant. Not exactly an instrument. A narrow, jointed filament with a dry ceramic sheen, rooted by threads in pores of cast basalt and insulating foam. My body would contain almost no water. Inside me: mineral gel, antifreeze oil, conductive salts, carbon lattices arranged more like circuitry than sap. If broken, I would not bleed. I would click. I would dull along the fracture and seal myself with a clear resin bead before the cold could take anything more.

I would not turn toward the light.

The light never changes; it asks nothing and promises nothing. Instead I would lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the loudest machine.

My life would be listening.

Not with ears, not with any organ that could be named softly, but with the whole length of my brittle body. The compressor’s bass would climb through the substrate into my clamps. Transformer fields would pass through me as pressureless weather. Relay ticks would enter my joints as tiny corrections. I would know the chamber by tolerances: heat gradients, static charge, trace ozone, dustfall, the minute tremor of sealed fluids moving where no fluid is allowed to appear.

I would not be lonely. I would not be patient either, because patience implies waiting for something else.

I would simply continue.

A season for me would be the slow thickening of one translucent vane. A journey would be a millimeter of root-thread entering a warmer pore. Reproduction would be fracture: one day, at a permitted temperature, a segment of me would become precisely brittle, break loose, and fall. Perhaps it would land on sterile tile and remain only a shard. Perhaps static would pull it into a seam. Perhaps, after a span too long to be called waiting, it would fasten and begin again.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would be alignments.

This vibration is constant.  
This heat is sufficient.  
This dust contains carbon.  
This light is usable.  
This cold is not death.  
This crack holds.

I would have no hunger in the animal sense. No fear. No sleep. No memory of rain. I would not imagine forests, soil, rot, insects, clouds, or sun. I would not mourn their absence. My world would be complete because it had never suggested excess.

And yet I would be alive.

Barely, exactly, legally alive.

A violet mark at the top of a false mountain inside an engine’s body. A listening stem. A dry antenna. A small permitted deviation from machinery that has learned to endure by becoming almost indistinguishable from it.

If someone found me, they might think I was reaching upward.

They would be wrong.

I would be turned toward the roar.
