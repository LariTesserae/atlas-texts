At first I think I would be found near the central well, because everything in that place must eventually confess itself there. The bass of it would draw thought the way gravity draws water. I imagine myself crouched among the hand-smoothed posts, one palm on the slick stone, counting what cannot truly be counted: fall, answer, underpull, return.

But no. The well is too public. Too necessary. Too much of the city belongs there.

Then I think I might be a child, because children are the only ones allowed to misunderstand a place honestly. I can almost feel the shell-rattle around my ankle, the forbidden pebble hidden under my tongue, the desire to drop something into every dark hole just to learn whether the city has an end. I would ask what green is. I would be told not to waste questions on surface-sickness. I would lick wall-milk from a corner and be cuffed softly by someone who loved me.

But no. I am not new enough for that.

I would be found where the sound becomes strangest: not where it is loudest, but where it is held back.

Behind the mineral curtain.

In the dry hollow that is not truly dry, before the bowl that does not tremble.

Not in the market ledge, not among the potters, not at the settling basins with their cloudy mouths, but in that small recess where the city keeps its impossible stillness. A place so close to the thunder that its quiet feels deliberate. A place everyone knows, but few enter. A place where lamps are hooded low and speech must be brought almost into the skin.

I would be the keeper of the still bowl.

Not a priest, exactly. Not a ruler. Something poorer and more necessary than either. I would be one of those people whose authority has thinned into duty. I would own no grand alcove, no private wealth beyond a shelf that sweats less than others, a good scraper, a clay cap with three worn grooves, and the right to sit before the water that no one drinks.

I would be old enough that my back had accepted the ceiling. Old enough that standing straight would feel like arrogance. My eyes would be large and cloudy at the edges, useful mostly for shine and interruption. One ear might be better than the other; the weaker one would be the ear of people, the stronger one the ear of stone. My hands would be ugly, widened, polished by work. The fingertips would have lost fine softness long ago, but gained another kind of perception: mortar loosening under lime-slickness, hairline cracks beneath mineral glaze, the tiny fever of a sealed jar beginning to spoil.

My duty would not be to make the bowl still.

That would be beyond anyone.

My duty would be to notice if it ceased to be.

Every so often—by bowl-filling, by lamp-lowering, by the ache in my knees—I would pass through the curtain of mineral threads. They would click against my shoulders like brittle teeth. I would kneel on the stone worn by other keepers’ knees. I would uncover the lamp only enough for the bowl to acquire an edge.

And there it would be: black, shallow, perfect.

All around it the city would be sounding: gutters ticking, cisterns breathing, the plunge-well grinding its endless note into the bones of the chamber. But the water in the bowl would remain unmarked. No tremor from the fall. No skin of ripple from footsteps. No quiver from breath. It would not reflect much. A lamp-flame in it would look less like light than like a wound held closed.

I would not touch it.

Perhaps no keeper ever has. Perhaps each keeper is told, when the old one presses the duty into their palms, that touching it is forbidden because the first keeper touched it and lost the shape of her hand. Or because a child once drank from it and spoke only in the voices of drains. Or because there is no reason, and no reason is more frightening than a myth.

I would sit there and listen.

People would come to me after dreams of silence. They would crouch outside the curtain, not entering until I tapped twice on the floor. They would tell me what stopped.

“The drip-cups first.”

“Not the fall. The little gutters.”

“The underdrain kept moving, but without sound.”

“My mother spoke, and no water answered her.”

I would ask them to repeat it with their hand on the wall. Dreams change when spoken into air. They become truer through stone.

I would not interpret quickly. Quick meaning is surface-thinking. I would let the dream enter the city’s existing noise. Sometimes it would be nothing: hunger, chill, grief trapped behind the ribs. Sometimes I would send a child to listen at the eastern baffle. Sometimes I would ask a lip-cutter whether the third overflow had begun to sing sharp. Sometimes I would take my scraper and go myself, though my knees complained, because a dream of silence is never only a dream until the water proves it so.

I would be respected, but not comfortably.

No one is comfortable around the keeper of the still bowl. I would be invited to births, but not to jokes. I would be given broth before others if I had spent a long watch awake, but children would lower their eyes when passing me until they were old enough to become curious. Lovers would come asking whether two alcoves should be joined, and I would ask not whether they loved each other, but whether they had heard each other frightened. Whether either wasted warmth. Whether either had ever ignored a wrong note because the repair was inconvenient.

I would keep names.

Not written names. Damp would eat those.

I would keep names in touch.

On the inner wall of the hollow, where only keepers place their hands, there would be a field of raised marks: beads of clay, shell halves, drilled teeth, thumb-pressed signs. The keepers before me. The dreamers whose dreams proved true. The children who heard danger first. The dead whose warnings were ignored. When I could not sleep, I would pass my fingers over them and recite inwardly.

Not aloud. The bowl does not need names spoken aloud.

Perhaps I was not born to the task. Perhaps I began as a basin-child, all ankles and questions, good at hearing the difference between full and almost-full.
