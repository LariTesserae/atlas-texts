I would find myself near the center, but not in it.

Not at the pavilion itself, not pressed grandly against those hovering panels while my flank lit with old instructions. Not on the boundary stones at noon, where the almost-writing warms under wet claws. I would be lower than that, smaller than that, in one of the places the eye passes over while trying to understand the important things.

I would be under the lip of a pale stone beside a crescent pool, where the mud stays cool even in the hottest hour and a trickle of water sometimes runs backward over my back.

At first I would look like part of the stone’s underside.

Flat, soft, gray-green, my skin patterned by whatever I cling to. My body would be no thicker than a folded leaf. My eyes would open rarely, and when they did they would not open into sight exactly, but into pressure, warmth, nearness, tremor. I would know the hollow by contact: the tiny percussion of thread-insects overhead, the combing of crab-claws in the mud, the deep drink of the black rods when noon sharpens, the slow muscular decision of roots swelling after rain.

I would be one of the amphibians, perhaps, but not wholly.

One of the patient ones beneath stone.

A keeper of undersides.

I would have begun as a transparent ribbon among roots, hardly a self, mostly hunger and response. I would have drifted in water shallow enough to be mistaken for shine. Minute fish would have flashed beside me. Spores would have opened around me when droplets fell from the correct height. I would have eaten what the water allowed and avoided what the old instructions had made bitter for a day.

Then, after some season of heat and reversals, I would have chosen a stone.

Or the stone would have chosen me.

I would have climbed beneath it and spread myself there, belly to mineral, back to darkness, skin learning the temperature of ancient material. Over time my color would change to match it. My breathing would slow. My limbs would become less important than adhesion. My mouth would become a small patient seam. The world would arrive through my belly.

I would not travel far. A few body-lengths in a season would be enough. Distance would be a thing for winged swarms and water. My work would be staying.

When the sun is strong and the channels reverse, I would feel the pull before the water moves. A faint tightening in the stone. A silver tension along cracks. The reeds above me pivoting all at once, their roots whispering through the mud. I would lift my head then, just enough for one smoked eye to open.

Not alarm. Attendance.

When the impossible droplet falls into the central pool, I would surface because something older than fear asks me to.

I would loosen from the underside of my slab. Mud would release my belly with a soft reluctance. I would rise through water no deeper than a thumbprint and place my forefeet on the stone’s wet rim. Around me the other flat ones would appear too, each from beneath its chosen surface: dark-backed, pale-bellied, rust-colored, almost invisible until moving. We would form no circle, no congregation in any ceremonial sense. We would simply be where the hollow requires receptors.

The ripple would not come from the center. It would come inward from the edges.

It would pass through my bones.

For a moment I would remember something I had never lived: wetlands larger than weather, machines bending light along horizons, stars arranged differently in warm black water. The memory would not become thought. It would become adjustment.

Afterward, I would know to move one span east before evening.

I would know that the moss near the hot pan must be allowed three more mites and no larvae.

I would know that the thread-insect line above me is carrying water with the wrong tension, and that if I lift my throat to the runnel and release the salt stored under my tongue, the beads will change.

This would be my citizenship: not knowledge, but correction.

Not wisdom, but participation.

I would be small enough that no visitor would name me unless they lay flat on the wet stone and waited. They might see only a stain beneath the slab. Then perhaps the stain would blink through smoked glass, and they would realize the stone had an animal thought attached to it.

I would not be lonely.

The stone would be my roof and archive. The mud would press news against me. The crabs would pass below, tasting the morning. The root-knuckle tree nearby would lower sweetness into the dark, and once each season I would carry one of its seeds in my mouth, not eating, not planting, only holding it until my saliva changed its sleep. Then I would wedge it into a crack where water climbs at noon.

Sometimes the six-limbed slick animal would pass close enough that its shadow cooled my pool. Its flank would show pale branching after touching the pavilion. I would feel the afterimage of that contact in the water. It would be like thunder made gentle and buried inside skin. I would not understand it, but my body would answer: a change in pulse, a membrane drawn over the eye, a small release of stored mineral into the pool.

The winged specks would use the heat above my stone. The leaf-shaped predator would sometimes ride a moving shadow across the rim. I would not resent it. If it took an insect, the line would close. If it came too near my young, I would flatten until I became not prey, not obstacle, but surface.

My young—if they were mine in any meaningful way—would not be guarded. They would cling as silver ribbons to the underside of broad leaves. I would keep their water balanced by doing almost nothing visible: shifting my weight to open a seep, pressing my belly over a poisonous bloom until it thinned, allowing certain mites to clean my skin and eating others before they carried rot.

I would be a valve made of flesh.

A damp listener.

A small uncertainty between animal and instrument.

At dusk, when water remembers downhill and the rods darken less urgently, I would return to the underside of my stone. The heat stored there would enter me slowly. Above, golden flowers no taller than claws would open. Pollinators would pass like breath with wings. The mud would release its iron-fruit bubbles. Somewhere near the white arch, a many-eyed flat creature would spread itself to drink the last warmth.

I would close my eyes and continue receiving.

If a dry hand lifted my stone, I would seem pathetic: soft, exposed, unfinished. I would curl. My skin would pale. The hollow would become too loud with light.

But if the hand were wet, and gentle, and set the stone back exactly as it was, the grooves in the nearby rock might warm. The footprint beside the rods might fill from the center outward. A thin runnel might change direction for a moment.

Not because I commanded it.

Because I had been disturbed, and the hollow corrects around its living pieces.

So that is where I would be: beneath a warm wet slab near the central pool, close enough to the pavilion to feel its color through water, far enough from it to remain mostly unnoticed.

And what would I be?

A flat amphibian of the underside.

A pressure-reader.

A little gray-green keeper of seepage and timing.

One of the hollow’s quiet nerves, breathing through my belly, remembering only what the water asks me to remember.
