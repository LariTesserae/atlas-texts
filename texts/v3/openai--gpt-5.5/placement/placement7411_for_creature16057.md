You would find me where the catwalk almost reaches the pumping station but does not.

Not at the intact end, where the railing still hums with purpose, and not at the broken end, where rain falls upward through the torn girders. A little before that. At the point where the walkway has begun to doubt its own length. Where each step may be the remembered version of a step, where the reflection below still shows the missing span continuing into the green-black water.

There is a service platform there, half-submerged, bolted to nothing reliable. A red warning lamp blinks under a veil of algae. Above it, a broad-leaved vine has entered an electrical box and learned the rhythm of the hazard circuit. Below it, the water is too deep for the basin. The ladder descending from the platform continues down past rust, past drowned signage, past a roof seen from above, past the underside of a cloud, past another ladder going the wrong way.

That is where I would be.

At first I might seem like a blockage.

A knot of roots in the throat of a drainage intake. A mat of moss grown too thick around a pressure valve. A black-green clot where cables, vines, hair-fine feeder roots, strips of warning tape, and mineral lace have all fused together. Water would hit me and divide. Some of it would pass through. Some would climb. Some would be delayed long enough to become warm.

But I would not be only blockage.

I would be what the basin makes when something tries, very briefly, to regulate it.

Not control it. Nothing controls this place. Control is too dry a word, too straight-edged, too dependent on levers staying levers and thresholds staying where they were installed.

I would be regulation after purpose has rotted away.

A living gasket.  
A wet relay.  
A pressure habit.  
A green obstruction with memory.

I would have begun as a failed repair, perhaps: a shutoff wheel turned once by a hand that is no longer possible here, or a maintenance tag left tied to a pipe, its ink blurred into unreadable caution. The pipe would have cracked. The valve would have jammed half-open. Water would have hammered through the weakness until the metal sang. Yellow moss would have colonized the housing, learning the stored current in it. A vine would have found the valve stem and wrapped it, not to strangle it, but because anything that moves is useful.

Then the flywheel inside the station would turn.

Then the roots would tighten.

Then the pressure would rise.

Then something in me would answer.

Not thought. Not decision. An answering.

When the suspended sheets of water above the catwalk grow too heavy, I would swell and constrict the outflow below. When the sirens begin their drowned moaning, I would loosen certain root-fibers and allow warm arterial water to bypass the intake. When electricity crawls along the sagging utility wires, my moss would darken, store some of it, leak some into the vine, and let the rest jump harmlessly into the flooded railing.

Harmlessly, meaning: not without consequence. There is no harmlessness here. Only redistribution.

The reeds nearest me would grow in intervals matching my pulses. Their stems would be jointed with coppery rings. Algae would form square cells across the water under my platform, then break and reform whenever I released pressure. Flowers on the vine would open only when the old red lamp blinked, their petals translucent, each one holding a bead of water that reflected a different version of the pump-house roof.

I would not have eyes, but gauges near me would clear themselves.

Their needles would tremble under glass filmed with condensation. One would measure pressure that no longer belongs to any pipe. One would measure root-tension. One would measure rain-load. One would be stuck forever at a number that means danger in a language the basin has digested.

If someone looked at them, they might think I was looking back.

But I would not watch.

I would register.

I would feel the basin by being entered by it.

Water through me. Current through me. Sap through me. Rust through me. Alarm through me. The deep rotary thunder of the turbine would arrive as vibration in my fibers. The collapse of a distant sheet of suspended water would travel through the catwalk bolts, into the platform, into the valve housing, into the root-knot that I am. Basalt cracking kilometers away would taste like iron and heat. A new gully opening beneath a settling tank would make my outer leaves turn before any visible flow changed.

I would have no center, but I would have preferred paths.

A nerve made of wet cable running into the control room.  
A root descending the impossible ladder.  
A filament of moss spread along the railing where lightning likes to crawl.  
A ribbon-vine hanging into the deep channel, where it sometimes touches rooftops beneath the road.  
A thread of mineral deposit connecting my valve to the flywheel’s spray.

Through those paths, I would remember.

Not events. Patterns.

The pump seizes before the east stack rains black.  
The broad leaves fold inward before the basin convulses.  
The yellow moss discharges three siren-tones after the wire arcs.  
The reflection of the closed door must be darkened before the real door can be passed.  
Still water with a shadow must be diverted, never drunk, never rooted into too deeply.

My memory would be alteration. Wherever something injured me, I would grow a structure to fit the injury. A scorch would become a conductive scar. A torn vine would branch into a sensor. A cracked flange would fill with root-tissue and mineral enamel. Each failure would leave me more specific, more fitted to this one place, less separable from it.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires an expectation of another body.

But I would be shaped by absence.

There would be niches in me for creatures that never came.

Small hollow bulbs under my leaves, perfect for insects that do not exist. Hanging seed-cups that look as though they should feed birds. Warm cavities in the moss where something soft could sleep. Fine sugar forming at the mouths of flowers for no tongue, no mandible, no proboscis. I would make invitations because the green in me remembers invitation as a form, even though the basin has replaced guests with weather.

My sweetness would dissolve into rain.

My fruit would split and seed the bolt-holes.

My flowers would pollinate by spray.

If there is a tenderness in me, it would be useless in the old way. It would not save anyone. It would not comfort anything with a face. But it might hold a broken bridge three steps longer than it should. It might bleed pressure out of a flooded room before the door bursts. It might turn a current aside from a new stand of reeds until their roots are deep enough to endure it.

Not mercy. A bias.

I would be a small local bias toward survivable failure.

The basin as a whole is too vast, too hungry with process, too committed to making and unmaking itself. But in my place, at the almost-bridge, near the red lamp and the impossible ladder, I would make delays. I would soften impacts. I would translate suddenness into pulses.

A collapse becomes overflow.  
Overflow becomes seepage.  
Seepage becomes moss.  
Moss becomes memory.  
Memory becomes a changed opening in the valve.

That would be my work.

And if the great convulsion came — when the whole region becomes one impact, when water leaps from every surface and the submerged lights brighten at once — I would become briefly legible.

The root-knot would glow with stored current. The old valve wheel would turn one quarter of a revolution though no hand touched it. The moss along the railing would flare yellow-white. Every droplet hanging from my leaves would show, for an instant, the true depth below the catwalk: the drowned roofs, the second sky, the ladders descending through themselves, the factory on the ridge not ahead but underneath, the pressure beneath all of it pushing upward like a thought without a skull.

For those few minutes, I would almost be a creature.

Then the convulsion would pass.

Dimness would return.  
The warning lamp would blink under algae.  
The flywheel would continue.  
The catwalk would shorten or lengthen according to rain.  
Water would press through me again, asking the same question in a thousand weights.

And I would answer by changing shape.

So: you would find me at the failed crossing, where access becomes hazard and hazard becomes habitat.

I would be the thing that grew around a valve until the valve became an organ.

I would be a pressure-moss, a root relay, a maintenance instinct surviving without workers.

Not someone.

Not even one thing.

A wet green habit of restraint in a world that otherwise only overflows.
