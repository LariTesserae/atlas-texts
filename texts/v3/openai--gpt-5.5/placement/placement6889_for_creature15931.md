You would find me low on the north wall, just above the line where the condensation gathers into threads.

Not in the open water at first. Not in the central pool, though I would listen to it. Not among the birds, though their cries would pass through me. I would be under a stone lip, where the wall has sweated for so many years that the mineral crust has grown soft-edged and pale. There are old notches there, half-drowned, half-forgotten. Someone once counted something into the stone. Floods, perhaps. Repairs. Offerings. Dead children. Days of rain. No one remembers.

I would be one of the snails crossing them.

Small, damp, almost without intention. A bead of flesh under a shell the color of wet ash and old bone. I would move where the water does not rush but never fully leaves, where the stone remains slick enough to hold me and rough enough to feed from. My body would read the wall by touch. I would pass over carved marks, finger dents in ancient clay, seams between repairs, flakes of lime, crumbs of algae, the dried silver paths of those who crossed before me.

I would not understand the marks. That would be important.

I would be the kind of memory that does not preserve meaning, only contact.

While frogs call below and make the basin sound larger than it is, while crabs test the tile seams with their pale hands, while beetles cut the puddles into black sentences, I would continue along the underside of the ledge. Slow enough that danger becomes weather. Slow enough that the basin’s changes arrive as pressure, chill, vibration, taste.

When the sumps rise, I would feel the air thicken.  
When the eels wake, I would draw inward.  
When the birds scream, I would stop with my whole body.  
When the central pool stills, I would wait.  
When it begins turning again, I would leave another bright thread across the stone.

I would be neither maker nor trespasser. Not predator, not alarm, not voice. I would be a small act of use laid over older acts of use.

The basin would not need me, exactly. Nothing there would need any single creature. But without such small continuances—the scraping mouth, the silver trail, the patient crossing of old signs—the place would be less itself. I would help it forget without losing its surface. I would soften the human marks into habitat.

If you came looking, you might miss me. You would hear frogs first, then water, then wings. You would see the central pool turning. You might notice the eels’ blind heads in the sumps, the heron’s thin legs, the crab vanishing into a seam. Only after your eyes learned the damp would you see the snails: clustered under ledges like wax drops, moving across the walls with almost ceremonial slowness.

One of them would be me.

A small wet witness.  
A reader without language.  
A soft mouth traveling over stone.  
The basin’s slow memory, shining briefly where it forgets.
