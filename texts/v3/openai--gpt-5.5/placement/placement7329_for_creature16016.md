You would not find me in the central boroughs, not at first.

Not under the broad municipal leaves where the markets hang in neat dry nets, not in the eye plazas, not among the gas clerks with their elegant ledgers and trembling gauges. I would pass through those places, certainly. I would know which wall to touch, which intake frond to speak toward, which moss-route had shifted since last dimming. But I would not belong there.

You would find me where the city is still undecided.

At the raw margin.

Where a street runs out.

There, the lacquered bark pavement thins to ribs. The curbs are unfinished braids, pale and tender, wrapped in splints of ceramic and waxed fiber. Light panels flicker overhead with an anxious green-white shimmer. The air smells sharper than in the old districts: cracked salt, new cambium, hot minerals, enzyme-bitterness, the faint metallic warning of untested gas.

Beyond my post, the cavern is not yet civic.

It is stone.

Blue-gray shelves. Chalk scars. Mica seams. Dry fissures breathing things no one has named. Surveyor vines lie along the ground like sleeping instruments, their white tips pressed into cracks. Small machines on caterpillar treads wait with their scoring teeth folded. Pale carrier insects come and go in single file, each bearing a charged crystal grain brighter than its own head.

My booth is not a booth exactly. It is a listening blister grown from the last authorized root-bulb of the district. Half plant, half municipal office. Its walls are thin enough to show the slow movement of mineral gel inside them. Its roof is a membrane stretched between three young ribs, patched in places with salt-glass. A row of old pressure gauges hangs above the counter because citizens trust needles more than color sometimes. Beneath them, the living wall blushes, pales, tightens, and loosens according to messages too slow for speech.

That is where I would be.

A seam-keeper.

A notary of new growth.

Part gardener, part cartographer, part gas listener, part liar to stone.

My work would be to stand at the edge of the living city and ask whether it may continue.

Not in words, mostly. Words are for the councils, for notices, for disputes over protein allotments and grafting rights. At the margin, words are too wet with intention. The stone does not care what anyone has approved. The founding root does not care how urgently a district wants expansion. Gas pockets do not respect plans printed on elastic membrane.

So I carry quieter instruments.

A pressure needle with a handle of black wax.

A spool of blue transit moss for marking provisional paths.

Three scent seals: caution, hunger, and wait.

A grafting knife thin enough to cut a rootlet without frightening the parent mass.

A packet of gray ash for closing conversations that should not continue.

On good days I kneel beside the soft end of a future avenue and press both palms to the rootfelt. The city comes up through my wrists as tremor and taste. Not language. Never quite language. More like weather felt from inside the bone.

Here, the root says, there is useful magnesium.

Here, the stone answers, there is pressure I will not release.

Here, a vent breathes sulfur in a rhythm that could be made profitable.

Here, something blind has nested.

Here, wait.

I record these things. Not on paper. Paper is for announcements that expect to become obsolete. I record them in grown plaques, in scent knots, in small dormant seeds whose first leaves will print a map when germinated. For the miners I make hard maps. For the gardeners I make living ones. For the city itself I make wounds so precise they can heal into instructions.

I would be slight, like the others, but rougher at the edges. My coat would be layered fiber, patched with mineral dust until its original color had become theoretical. My gloves would be resin-stiff from touching new cambium. My cheek slits would carry the permanent bitterness of raw stone and early growth chemistry. Children in the central districts would know I came from the margins because I would smell unfinished.

Not unpleasant. Just sharp.

Like a door before it decides to open.

I would not be important in the way councilors are important. I would not own a grand dwelling chamber or have a family house willing to bud new alcoves at my request. My rooms would be narrow and temporary, grown into the side of a surveyor trunk that might someday become a transit column or might be dismantled if the district failed to take.

My bed would fold into the wall during pavement tightening. My table would have root-feet and one stubborn mechanical leg. I would keep almost nothing: a scent box with three relics, a cracked lens-stone, a dried sliver from the first street I helped authorize. Perhaps one illegal thing too — not dangerous, not quite, but unauthorized. A seed from an abandoned growth front, wrapped in gray cloth, refusing to die.

People would come to me before they trusted an empty place.

A factory tender wanting to know if a new piston house could be grown near the vents.

A family asking whether a home-bulb might safely root beyond the last blue moss.

A miner bringing back a chalk tablet scored with marks made by something without hands.

A gas clerk, formal and thin, pretending not to be afraid of what the gauges did at dimming.

Sometimes I would say yes.

More often I would say: not yet.

That would be my reputation. Not cruelty. Delay.

In a young city, delay is a form of mercy.

Because I would know what happens when eagerness outruns consent. A wall that inflates too quickly and becomes hollow in the wrong way. A stairwell that learns hunger from an illegal seed. A transit moss line laid across a breathing crack, leading citizens into a pocket of sweet, useless gas. A nursery alcove budded from a root-bulb that has been listening too long to something below.

I would have seen one street fail.

Everyone at the margin has seen one street fail.

Mine would still be there, sealed behind a band of red thorns and enamel warning plates. A beautiful little street, perhaps. Pale amber pavement. Four young doorways. A row of diagnostic flowers that bloomed black all at once. No shouting. No collapse. Just a slow refusal from beneath, and the houses tightening around their occupants with the calm of civic obedience misunderstood.

After that, I would never trust a smooth new surface.

I would love the city anyway.

Not romantically. Not blindly. I would love it as one loves a difficult organism whose survival depends on exactness. I would love its restraint, its low mechanical breathing, its municipal noon. I would love the cleaning beetles crossing a plaza in their solemn black fans. I would love the market leaves lowering for old customers. I would love the way shadows shift all at once when the mirror-keepers turn the ceiling.

But most of all I would love the unfinished places.

The places where intention is still visible.

Where architecture has not yet learned to pretend it was inevitable.

At dimming, when the central boroughs cool and the predatory bulbs begin to loosen from the ceiling roots, I would stand outside my listening booth and watch the growth front breathe. The young walls would pulse faintly. The carrier insects would stop moving for a few minutes, burdened and patient. Far away, the older city would glow with disciplined green light, broad avenues empty as held breath.

Beneath my boots, something older might move.

Not often. Not dramatically. A slow pressure passing under the rootfelt, too deep for machinery, too broad for any district. The gauges in my booth would tremble. The surveyor vines would pull their tips back from the cracks. My own throat slits would close before I knew why.

Then I would do what seam-keepers do.

I would kneel.

I would set both palms on the pavement.

I would listen.

Not because I believed the old root was wise. Not because I believed it loved us. Those are surface kinds of hope. I would listen because the city listens, and because anything that lives below a young city must be included in the map, even if the symbol for it is only a blank ring and the scent for caution.

If you asked me who I was, I might answer with my trade first.

Seam-keeper.

If you asked again, more carefully, I might say:

I am the one who tells the city where not to grow yet.

And if you found me very late, after municipal noon had dimmed and the raw stone beyond the last curb had begun making its small dry sounds, I might admit the rest.

That I am not only keeping the city from the dark.

I am keeping the dark in conversation.
