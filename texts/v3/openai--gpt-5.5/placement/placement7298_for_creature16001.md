You would find me at the small square where four service lanes meet.

Not a square in any open sense. A widening, really. A pause in the pressure of corridors. The place with the circular drain in the middle, the moss-padded benches set into the wall, the old enamel board listing tank numbers and flow rates no one fully trusts anymore. Above it, one amber lamp in a wire cage. Behind it, a pipe that knocks every nine breaths. Under it, water always passing, even when the surface looks still.

At first I think I am the moss on the benches.

That would be easy. A soft civic patience. A green upholstery for the bodies that rarely come. I would take the shape of backs and elbows without remembering them as people. I would hold the damp in my small leaves and make the municipal stone less severe. I would soften the public thing into something almost kind.

But no. I am not only on the benches.

I am in the drain.

Or rather, I am what has gathered around the drain without closing it.

A ring of slick film, moss, hair roots, mineral crust, rust flakes, pale fungal threads, and tiny leaves that know exactly how much obstruction is permitted. I live at the lip where four shallow runnels arrive and become one downward throat. Everything comes to me in fractions: grit from the brick lane, green water from the basin chamber, oil-warm seep from the pump house, condensate from the overhead pipes, a brown trickle from the root beds, the occasional lost screw, a fallen label, a shaving of paint, a wet bootprint dissolved into mud.

I am a sorting mouth, but not a mouth that bites.

I slow things.

I catch what should not go too quickly below. I hold silt in my velvet. I let water comb through me. I darken when the flow is rich. I pale when the lamps fail. I swell during overflow and tighten during dry maintenance weeks. My roots enter the old mortar around the drain ring, not to break it, not exactly, but to remember where it is weak.

I would be one of the quarter’s minor intelligences: a drain-collar, a green clerk, a living gasket.

No one designed me, but everyone depends on my moderation. If I thicken too much, the square floods. If I am scraped away entirely, grit rushes into the lower channels and the pump complains for days. So I keep to the old agreement. I claim the edge. I leave the center open.

That would be my nature: possession with restraint.

I would not have eyes. I would know the world by arrival.

The west lane brings cooler water and brick dust.

The north stair brings iron taste, rust shaken loose from railings.

The pumpward passage brings warmth, grease, and vibration.

The basin lane brings soft green abundance, bits of reed, algae threads, dissolved richness.

The ceiling gives me drops at long intervals, heavy and exact. The lamp gives just enough amber for the smallest leaves to lean toward it. The benches give me spores. The signboard gives me enamel chips and old authority. The drain gives me purpose.

I would remember nothing as a story. I would remember by rings.

A season of heavy runoff would become a darker band under my outer moss. A week of repair would become a pale scar where a boot heel scraped me back. A new chemical in the water would become a thinness, a hesitation, then a different green returning. If a worker left a wrench beside me, I would not covet it. I would receive its sweating, its rust, the softened grime of its handle. I would begin to make it local.

I would be small compared with the whole resident of the quarter, but I would be placed where smallness matters.

Not the heart. Not the brain. Not even a hand.

A valve of patience.

A municipal tonsil.

A little wet judgment at the center of a crossroads.

And if someone came down from the dry city above and stood there listening to pumps, water, fans, and all that layered endurance, they might not notice me at first. They might see only the drain, half-greened, needing cleaning.

But if they stayed long enough, they would understand that I am not neglect.

I am the negotiation.

I am the reason the square is damp but not drowned. Green but still passable. Claimed but not lost. I am the quarter practicing its oldest rule in miniature:

Water must keep moving, but never too quickly.
