I would not be in the broadest passage, where the workers come through swearing and the cats pass like inspectors. I would not be high enough for the bats, or bold enough for the rats along the steam pipes. I would be a little off the main way, where the quarter narrows into an alcove beside a valve bank and an old junction cabinet, in the place where human order has begun to curl at the edges.

There is a clipboard there, or there was. Its papers have swollen into one soft gray pad. The top sheet still bears a grid, a date, initials, instructions for a sequence of valves that nobody follows exactly anymore. A paper tag hangs from a pipe by a wire, its ink feathered by damp until the numbers have become a bruise. The wall behind it has chalk arrows, some old, some new, all of them sweating downward. A bulb burns nearby, but weakly, so the alcove is never fully dark and never safely bright.

That is where I would find myself.

Not as a rat. Rats are too decisive. They know exits and risks and inheritance. Not as a cat, because cats arrive already convinced of themselves. Not as a gecko, held in the clean violence of appetite. Not as a worker, though I would understand the workers’ wish to keep a square of floor swept and a hinge oiled against the whole damp patience of the place.

I would be a silverfish.

Small, pale, old-fashioned, quick.

A little scrap of moving tarnish in the softened paperwork of the undercity.

I would live behind the valve labels, inside the loosened cardboard sleeve of spare fuses, under the warped instruction plate where glue has become food. I would know the place not by its grand passages but by its thicknesses: the gap between tile and wall, the underside of a clipboard, the seam where paper lifts from metal, the narrow dry hour after a pipe has cooled and before condensation returns.

My world would be made of surfaces almost touching.

The city above would come to me as starch, paste, mold, skin dust, paper fiber, the edible remains of intention. Human beings would descend with diagrams, warnings, schedules, laminated procedures, and I would wait until all of it softened. Their certainty would be my weather. Their labels would be my pasture.

I would eat the names of things.

Not maliciously. Not as sabotage. Only because names, down there, are made of paper and glue, and paper and glue become tender in the heat.

VALVE 7B would blur first at the corner.  
PUMP ROOM ACCESS would fur at the staple holes.  
DO NOT REMOVE would silver at the edge, then vanish by fractions.  
A date written in marker would remain longer than the paper around it, a black stroke over a lace of absence.

I would be one of the district’s smallest revisions.

The workers would think rust did it, or damp, or time. They would not be wrong. I would be part of that time, one mouth of it. A minor instrument by which the underground removes the idea that it can be fully administered.

I would avoid the roaches, though they would be everywhere like a darker, more muscular bureaucracy. I would flee the gecko’s sudden bright eye. I would feel the tremor of rats passing along the pipe above as if heavy thoughts were crossing the ceiling. Mosquitoes would have nothing to ask of me. Drain flies would rise and settle around me like gray ash. The cats would be catastrophes with whiskers, enormous and silent, their paws landing with the carelessness of weather.

I would not be lonely. Nothing there is lonely. Even a crack is crowded.

My body would be made for retreat: antennae reading the wet air, feet finding purchase on mineral bloom, tail filaments trailing like three afterthoughts. I would move in quick commas. A flashlight beam would strike, and I would become a rumor under the cabinet. A boot would scrape nearby, and I would flatten into the seam between wall and paper. The place would be vast to me, not ten minutes long but generational, a continent of labels, stains, glue, mildew, and warm metal.

I would know which papers are still too new to eat.

Fresh plastic-laminated warnings would be barren, slick, tasteless, arrogant. Newly replaced tags would smell sharp and chemical. But give them a season. Give them condensation. Give them the touch of hands, the fog of pumps, the bloom of mold. Everything becomes available eventually.

That would be my faith, if I had one:

everything sealed will loosen,  
everything named will blur,  
everything dry will remember water.

I would live at the boundary between maintenance and surrender.

Close enough to the workers to inherit their leavings, far enough from their boots to survive. Close enough to the machines to enjoy their heat, far enough not to be cooked. Close enough to water to drink from films and beads, far enough that a rainstorm would not lift me and carry me into the brown channels with the fish.

When the district woke after rain, I would retreat higher into the cabinet seam. I would feel the whole undercity change its breathing. Pumps would deepen. Water would begin making decisions. Rats would revise their routes. Mosquito futures would be written in every new pan and footprint. The paper around me would swell. Glue would sweeten. The tag on the valve would sag closer to the wall, making a darker chamber behind it, and I would wait there, alive in the softened alphabet.

I would not see the fig sapling often, but I would know of it as a rumor of light. Some ancestor of mine may have traveled in a cardboard box from a market stall above, among invoices and fruit skins, and fallen into this warm underside. Perhaps my line began in a dry storeroom, in ledgers, in packing paper, in the respectable dust of commerce. Now we would live below the market’s feet, eating the records that followed the pipes down.

There is something fitting in that.

The city sends down water, heat, waste, labels, repairs, neglect. The rats take the routes. The roaches take the grease. The mosquitoes take the still water. The bats take the mosquitoes. The cats take whatever they can catch and whatever authority is offered them.

I would take the language.

Tiny, pale, almost beneath disgust.

A little citizen of damp bureaucracy.

If the buried quarter is the city’s unfinished geology, I would be one of the small creatures that helps turn instruction into sediment. Not dramatic. Not central. Not symbolic unless someone kneels close enough to notice the missing corner of a tag and understands that even command has predators.

You would find me where a human hand once tried to make the place legible.

Behind the curled label.  
Under the damp card.  
Beside the warm pipe.  
In the seam.

Eating, shining faintly, and gone when the light arrives.
