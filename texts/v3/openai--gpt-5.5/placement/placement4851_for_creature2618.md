I would not be in the shaft itself.

The shaft is too central, too nearly an organ named correctly. It already knows how to be throat, lung, dark well, source. If I were there I would become only part of the breathing, lifted and lowered with vapor, too close to the chamber’s slow certainty.

I would be off-center.

At first I think I would be one of the fungal rims under a leaking seam, one of the small bruised glows that does not illuminate anything except its own edge. But that is too much like a signal. Too much like wanting to be seen.

Then I think: the pale vine, stopping where the warmth fails. That seems closer. A life made of thresholds, not courage. A body whose intelligence is refusal. But the vine is still too purposeful, too legible in its reaching.

No.

I would be found inside the broken gauge.

The one furred with moss behind the glass.

It is mounted low on a control box near the central plinths, not important enough to face the shaft, not ruined enough to have fallen. Its needle is fixed at an impossible pressure, angled between warning and measurement, still insisting on an old condition that no longer exists. The glass is cracked but not shattered. Moisture enters through a hairline opening at the rim. Warmth enters through the metal backing. Darkness enters everywhere.

Inside, a small weather has formed.

That is where I would be.

Not the gauge as it was made, and not the moss alone. I would be the closed little climate that has taken up residence inside a human instrument. The bead that gathers on the inner glass. The green nap thickening around the dead needle. The mineral haze at the screw heads. The tiny sealed error of it: a device meant to report pressure, now becoming pressure’s habitat.

I would be a false reading that has learned to grow.

When the red indicator lamp wakes, I would receive it as warmth across the glass. Not light, exactly. A brief change in the skin of the world. Amber would mean something slightly different: less urgent, more mineral, a duller pulse passing through the box and into the damp behind my face.

When rain above finally arrives, I would know before the broad leaves know. Not because I am wiser, but because the metal cools first. A tremor of temperature would move through the casing. The cracked seal would darken. The moss inside me would loosen and deepen. One bead would join another bead and pull itself downward over the printed numbers no one can read.

I would not speak.

I would register.

Too hot: cloud the glass.

Water arriving: swell.

Current passing: warm at the left screw.

Dry interval: pale at the edges.

Long silence: grow inward.

I would be one of the chamber’s small misremembered senses, made from machinery failing gently into biology. A pressure gauge no longer measuring pressure, yet still participating in pressure. A little eye that does not see. A dial becoming a pond. A number becoming moss.

If someone entered with a handlamp, they might notice me only briefly.

They would sweep the beam over the plinths, the rails, the shaft, the hanging cables. Then the light would catch my cracked face. For a second I would shine: glass, needle, green fur, trapped wetness. They might lean closer and see that life has crossed the boundary into the instrument. They might read the needle and find it meaningless.

That would be correct.

I would mean nothing in the old language.

In the new one, I would mean: here, warmth persists behind metal. Here, water has found a way in but not out. Here, abandonment has become enclosure. Here, the chamber has begun to make organs from whatever was left behind.

I would be patient in a way that still feels young. Not ancient, not wise, not serene. More like something recently awakened and pretending not to be startled. A soft occupation. A dampness learning the shape of a dial.

Around me the larger life would continue: shaft breathing, leaves receiving, vines testing, fungi opening their dim mouths, rails vanishing under crust. I would be small within it, but not separate.

I would be the chamber’s memory of measurement after measurement no longer matters.

A gauge with moss inside.

A sealed green pressure.

An old warning becoming weather.
