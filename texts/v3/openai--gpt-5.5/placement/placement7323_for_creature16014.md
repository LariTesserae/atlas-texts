I would not be on the highest decks.

At first I would want to be there, where the light is hard enough to make every wet leaf look edged in silver, where fog is caught in shining nets and clouds pass below the railings. I would imagine myself as a fog-comber, climbing out before dawn with a hooked belt and a folded mesh wing over one shoulder, returning with my sleeves dark from condensation.

But I do not think I would belong in the pure upper brightness. Too much glare. Too much exposure. Too much sky.

Nor would I be all the way down in the sump galleries, though part of me is drawn there: the old ceramic floodways, the crab-tapping dark, the translucent salamanders moving under condensation plates like thoughts no one has named yet. I would visit those places, but I would not sleep easily there.

I would be found in the middle elevations, where the made mountain is most itself: not fully sun, not fully understructure. A district with several names depending on who is speaking. Officially perhaps the East Resonance Walk, though everyone calls it Greenbell because of the bronze plates hung beneath the bridge-ribs, gone verdigris with age. It lies between a reservoir roof and an old factory façade that has become half aviary, half school, half workshop, in the way places here are always more than one thing.

There would be a long tiled passage open on one side to blue distance. Beneath it, a drop through several layers of terraces: vines, laundry, ladderways, a fish trough, a belt lift, a shrine wedged into a counterweight housing. Above it, a cistern lip with chains descending every few paces, each chain giving a different note when water runs down it.

That is where I would be.

I think I would be a bell-listener.

Not one of the famous ones, not a master whose hearing is trusted across districts. I would be a working listener, a maintainer of small spans and old floors. Someone called when a stair has begun to speak differently after rain. Someone who carries a brass rod, chalk, twine, wax tablets, a little hammer, and a folded map stained by years of wet fingers.

My work would be to listen for changes in bearing.

I would kneel on warm tile and put my ear to a rod pressed against a beam. I would strike a suspended plate and wait for the answer to pass through metal, brick, water, root, and load. I would know the difference between a cracked collar, a swollen root, a tired rivet, a cistern too full, a harmless seasonal dampening, and the faint deadness that means people must stop crossing before sunset.

I would not be dramatic. Most days my work would be ordinary.

Marking a wall with blue paint: checked after lower rain.

Arguing with a fruit seller whose stall has crept two handspans beyond the load line.

Convincing children not to jump in rhythm on a bridge that dislikes rhythm.

Tying a warning ribbon around a balcony rail and knowing someone will untie it unless I add the guild seal.

Listening to a stair beast refuse a crossing and deciding, after one touch of the floor, that the animal is wiser than the schedule.

I would live in a narrow room built into the side of an old aqueduct support. One window would face outward into light and birds. The other would look inward toward the underside of a water channel, where swifts nest in the brackets. At night I would hear droplets travel chain by chain into household jars. My shelves would hold spare washers, dried fruit, tuning plates, old inspection books, and pots of moss I keep meaning to replant properly.

There would be a cat that is not mine, but enters as if ownership is a vulgar legal fiction. Soot-gray, green-pawed, one ear torn. It would sleep on the folded maps whenever the air pressure changes. I would trust it more than some instruments.

I would be known for remembering tones.

Not perfectly. Not magically. But enough that people would ask, “Didn’t this floor used to ring sharper?” and I would say, “Before the west root was collared, yes,” or “Only in dry months,” or “No, you are remembering the old plate, before it cracked in the fog year.”

My faith, if I had one, would be quiet and practical. I would leave polished washers at the shrine beneath the counterweight. I would touch the bridge-bell with two fingers before testing it. I would not believe the made mountain is a god exactly, but I would believe that anything bearing so much life for so long deserves manners.

I would love the hour after rain.

The whole district shining. The factory windows lit from within. Birds shaking themselves under the bridge arches. Every chain ticking. The air smelling of wet metal, crushed leaves, mineral water, and fruit beginning to split. That would be the best hour for listening, because the mountain speaks most honestly when full of water.

If someone came looking for me, they would find me crouched beside a green bronze bell under a trellised walkway, one hand on the deck, head tilted.

Below me: turbines, markets, crabs, kitchens, fogged galleries.

Above me: cisterns, orchards, glare, condenser wings.

Around me: vines and valves, children and cats, old repairs painted over older repairs.

I would be one of the people whose work is not to build the world, exactly, nor to rule it, nor to explain it, but to notice when its balance changes.

A listener at the middle height.

A custodian of tones.

Someone who knows that a homeland survives because someone hears the first small note before the great sound.
