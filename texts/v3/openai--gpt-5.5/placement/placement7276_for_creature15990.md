I would not be in the center.

The center is too honest. Too wide. Too pale with old dryness. A thing crossing it must become a sentence written plainly on stone, and I would not have the courage or the need for that. I would live where the chamber hesitates: not in the pool, not on the ceiling, not fully in the dry, but under a loosened lip of calcite near the western wall, where condensation sometimes gathers in shining scales and then forgets itself.

At first I think I might be one of the glow-creatures.

It is tempting. To be part of the chamber’s false sky, to feel rain arrive as brightness in the body, to answer water with light. But that life is too exposed, too ceremonial. The ceiling colonies belong to the chamber’s visible mystery. They are what a first visitor would look up at and remember.

I would be something remembered only by what I touch.

Not a fish. The clear pool is too complete, too mirrored. Its blind inhabitants live inside a second ceiling, drifting in a calm I can admire but not enter. Not a fern, either, though I understand the fern’s austerity: the patience of one green statement made against stone. Not a cricket, all listening and sudden panic. Not a spider, though the spider’s wisdom is close to mine: let the chamber bring the world within reach.

No. I would be beneath the flake.

A small beetle, perhaps, though beetle is only what a larger eye would call me. Ivory-brown, seed-shaped, my shell faintly translucent at the joints. My legs would be short and precise, made for powder and narrowness. My antennae would sweep ahead of me in the dark understone, tasting mineral dust, old skins, damp coming, damp leaving. I would live in the little dry country below a calcite plate no wider than a hand, a roof grown by centuries and lifted at one edge by the slow warping of deposits beneath it.

That is where I would find myself: under a pale mineral scale at the foot of the western wall, close enough to the sweating stone to know when rain has passed above, far enough from open wetness not to drown in generosity.

My world would be low.

The chamber, to me, would not be a high ceremonial hollow but a sequence of ceilings: the great ceiling where light animals cling; the lower ceiling of fern-shadow; the nearer ceiling of stone ribs and curled flakes; finally my own calcite roof, chalk-scented and thin enough to tremble when a cricket lands above.

I would know the chamber by vibration before sight.

A drop falling into the clear basin would arrive as a small round fact through the floor. The orange pool’s bubble would be a softer rupture, a swelling and release. The crickets would be storms: impossible long stillness, then one violent sentence of legs. The spiders would be nearly nothing, which would make them terrible. The fish would not exist to me except as tremors beneath water and the occasional absence of a larva that might otherwise have become food.

Light would enter my shelter along one edge.

Not much. A blade of warm whiteness. Enough to tell dry hour from damp hour, enough to silver the dust grains and make the underside of my shell briefly visible to myself. I would avoid that edge unless rain had recently awakened the chamber. Brightness means hunger has eyes. Brightness means a spider may be reading the same mineral path.

Most days I would work.

Not work as a creature with ambition works, but as the chamber works: by accepting what arrives. A shed skin caught under the flake. A dead mite fallen from a glow-colony and dried to a husk. Fern dust. The soft collapsed body of a larva stranded when the green pool withdrew. Fragments too small for drama, too small for mourning. I would take them apart with patient mouthparts and return them to powder, to passage, to the fine economy that lets the sparse plants stand and the films return.

I would be an undertaker, yes, but not a solemn one.

There is no solemnity where nothing is wasted. Death here would not have grandeur. It would be a change of texture, a sweetness in the dust, a softening under the jaw. I would know the difference between old insect wing and mineral skin, between fern spore and fungal thread, between a body that dried slowly and one taken by spider-venom. These would be my weather signs.

When rain passed over the land above, I would know before the first drop fell.

The stone roof of my little country would cool. The western wall would begin to breathe brighter. My antennae would find water in the air where there had been only powder. The ceiling colonies would strengthen, and their light would creep farther under my flake, turning the dust amber-white.

Then I would make my sound.

Tick.

Not loud. Not even meant to be heard far away. A small mechanical declaration made by the tightening of my body under its shell. Tick, tick, when the calcite shifts. Tick when another of my kind touches the roof from the other side. Tick when damp has swollen the silt and opened a passage that was closed yesterday.

During rain-hours, we would emerge.

Carefully. Never many at once. The law of distance is older than fear; it is built into the legs. I would come out from beneath the mineral lip and stand in the half-light with my body raised just enough to keep the wet from my underside. Around me, the chamber would be more awake but not busy. Pale insects would move in their spaced lines along the wall. The hanging roots would darken above. The green basin would loosen its skin. The glow-creatures would pulse as if remembering a larger sky.

I would cross only the small distances.

From flake to rib. From rib to fern-shadow. From fern-shadow to the powder gathered behind a stone knob where dead things collect after damp. My path would be inherited, not chosen: a thin trail of safe roughness known by touch, interrupted by open brightness I would wait to cross until a drop fell nearby and made a brief distraction.

If danger came, I would not flee wildly. I would stop.

I would become seed, grit, old mineral. My legs would fold under me. My antennae would tuck along the body. I would trust the chamber’s oldest lesson: what does not insist on itself may remain.

A cricket would pass like a pale catastrophe. A spider’s foot might test the dust beside me. The shadow of a root would sway across the floor. I would stay still until stillness itself forgot I was using it.

In dry hours I would go deeper.

There are hollows beneath the visible floor, little catacombs between stone plates and older deposits, warm with the faint stored heat of the chamber. I would share them without touching much: another beetle beyond a ridge of silt, translucent mites in a damp clot of fungus, white threads that lace through a fallen fern scale. Eggs would be laid there, not in a nest exactly, but in a chosen dryness that sometimes receives breath from wet stone. The young would hatch soft and nearly clear, already knowing how to hide because there is no childhood here that can afford display.

I would not think of the chamber as empty.

The emptiness would be the body of the place. The wide bare floor would be like the water in which fish drift: a medium, a danger, a distance that lets each small life remain exact. I would understand the great open stretches not as unused space but as silence that protects the seams.

I would know that abundance is suspicious.

If too many bodies gathered under one flake, fungus would sour. If too much moisture entered, eggs would fail. If the green film spread too far, it would feed larvae that feed fish that leave fewer fallen bodies for beetles. Every gift would carry an edge. Every wet awakening would require retreat afterward. To live well would mean leaving before the good thing became too much.

Perhaps, once in my life, I would reach the edge of the narrow passage sloping downward.

Not far into it. Only to the place where this chamber’s light thins and the next hollow begins to suggest itself by smell: cooler mineral, older damp, a different film growing where my feet do not know the rules. I would stand there under my small shell, antennae lifted, receiving the rumor of further rooms.

I would not enter.

Or perhaps one of my young would.

That is enough for a life here: not conquest, not discovery in the large sense, but continuation by slight adjustments. A new flake found. A safer crack. A path altered after a ceiling grain falls. A dead glow-creature consumed before mold takes it. A clutch of eggs surviving a dry interval longer than expected.

I would be one of the chamber’s small brown clicks.

A keeper of undersides.

A creature of the dry treaty, living close to dampness but never trusting it entirely. I would make my home beneath loosened calcite near the western wall, in the powder where light arrives as a thin line and rain is first known as a change in the taste of stone.

And if a human hand ever lifted my roof, stone blade at the belt, basket over one shoulder, face bright with the mistaken feeling of having found a room meant for them, I would make my tiny complaint.

Tick.

Then I would vanish into the seam that was always there.
