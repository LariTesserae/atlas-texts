I would not be at the center at first.

The hatch-mound is too important, too warm, too contested. The great predators know it. The spiral-walkers know it. The soft vent-feeders swell and collapse there like organs. Anything that lives too close to that breath must become part of a public economy: food, alarm, builder, brood-carrier, thief. I would not begin there.

Nor would I be one of the largest hunters, flattened under dust with my tools folded beneath me. That life is too singular, too declarative. It says: wait, strike, drink, vanish. It has a clean grammar. This place does not feel clean to me.

I think I would be found halfway between the wall and the old wires, where a sagging cable passes close to a honeycombed mineral rib. Not on the open floor, but not hidden completely. In the zone where signals cross: vibration from the compressor, charge from the wire, air from the ceiling fissures, traffic from the wall colonies, chemical warmth from the hatch arriving diluted and full of rumor.

I would live under a porcelain insulator cracked through its glaze.

There, in the chalk dimness, I would be one of the listeners.

Small. Jointed. Not quite pale, not quite black: the color of tarnished tin with violet showing at the joints. My body would be no longer than a fingernail clipping, flattened so that I could press myself into the seam between porcelain and mineral crust. My legs would hook backward, built for clinging during tremors. My head would be narrow and soft, mostly hidden under a forward plate. I would have no useful eyes.

What I would have instead are filaments.

They would grow from me in pairs and fans: some stiff enough to brace against stone, some fine enough to tremble in air too still for other animals to notice. A few would lie across the wire itself, not touching with weight but with attention. Others would hang down into the traffic below, where grazers pass, where mites swarm, where predators disturb the powder without meaning to announce themselves.

I would be a current-taster, a tremor-reader, a small living instrument.

When the compressor prepares to cough, I would know before the chamber hears it. Not by thought. Not by prediction. By a tightening in the wire, a minute rearrangement of dust on porcelain, a change in the bitterness of the air. My filaments would lift one after another. My body would stiffen. The others around me — because I would not live alone — would receive the motion through our touching limbs.

A message would pass without anyone sending it.

Open.

Seal.

Flatten.

Climb.

Feed soon.

Danger below.

Not language. Not even command. More like the way a shadow tells a swarm to divide.

I would feed on nearly nothing. That seems right for this place.

A gray film grows where the porcelain sweats charge into dust. A metallic skin forms along the wire after the weak current passes. Microbes bloom there, thin as breath on glass. I would scrape them with mouthparts like paired brushes, drawing in almost no substance and yet enough. Sometimes I would lower myself after a grazer has passed and drink from the cleaner trail it leaves behind. Sometimes I would eat the shed filament of another listener. Sometimes, if one of my own kind failed to withdraw quickly enough from a parasite, I would help empty the body.

No waste here remains private.

My underside would hold a groove of powder and eggs. Not many. Never many. I would glue them beneath the insulator only after a sequence of events precise enough to feel like fate: a warm draft from below, then two compressor coughs, then a weak current in the wire, then the reversal of air through the ceiling cracks. If the order is wrong, the eggs wait inside me. If I lay them too early, the amber mites find them. If I lay them too late, the film dries and the hatchlings starve before their mouthparts harden.

My young would hatch already listening.

For their first days they would be nearly transparent, with abdomens full of pale grit. They would cluster inside the crack in the porcelain glaze, feeding on the soft bloom no larger creature can reach. Then, one by one, they would emerge and take positions along the wire, each choosing a point of tension, a crossing of signals, a place where the world can be felt from more than one direction.

Some would be eaten by thread-jawed wall things.

Some would be taken by the soft hunters in the dust if they descended too far.

Some would develop wrong under the influence of parasites and climb when they should flatten, tremble when they should seal, mate when the chamber is not ready. Those ones would become warnings with bodies.

I would fear the predators, but not most of all.

The large armored hunter on the floor is terrible, but it belongs to the floor. Its patience has edges. Its territory can be learned. The soft mouths under powder are worse, but they do not climb porcelain.

What I would fear most is silence in the wire.

Not quiet — quiet never happens here — but the absence of that thin electrical unease that keeps the films growing, the wire-hangers breeding, the listeners timed. If the old relay box finally died, if the weak current ceased, if the porcelain cooled into mere stone, then our little district would become only a dry perch above traffic. The films would thin. The eggs would fail. The fan-backed animals would abandon the line. Predators would learn the new stillness and use it.

The world would continue, but not as my world.

So I would be loyal, in the blind way of my kind, to failing machinery.

The compressor would be weather. The wire would be a root of lightning. The relay’s whine would be a distant parent. I would have no concept of people, but my life would be shaped around the last leftovers of their intention. A system built to carry power would carry warning through my body. A porcelain insulator made to prevent contact would become my nursery. A broken industrial chamber would use me as one more small nerve.

If someone entered from above and stood still long enough, they might never see me.

They would see the mound, the grazers, the traffic lanes, the predators folding and unfolding. They would hear the clicks and rasps and tremors. They might notice the sagging wire and the clusters of larger fan-backed animals trembling along it.

But I would be the almost-invisible fringe beneath them: a small plated listener pressed into a crack, combing charge from porcelain, holding one filament in the air and one against the old wire, feeling the chamber happen before it happens.

Not a citizen.

Not a ruler.

Not even a messenger, exactly.

I would be a threshold creature: part ear, part switch, part dust-fed mouth. A living contact point in the animal city without citizens. When the hidden machinery stirs, I would stiffen. When the colonies open, I would feel their decision through the wall. When the air reverses and the chamber exhales upward, my filaments would stream toward the cracks like pale hair.

And when I die, I would remain useful.

My body would dry where it clung. Mites would hollow me. A wall worker would take my thorax plate and cement it into a resonant lip near a brood passage. My filaments would be eaten down to threads. My eggs, if any survived, would hatch into the same trembling dimness.

For a while, some surface I touched would remember the shape of my attention.

That is where I would be: under the cracked porcelain, beside the sagging wire, above the crowded mineral floor.

That is what I would be: a small listener in the old current, living on residue, timing, and the failing pulse of a machine that has become weather.
