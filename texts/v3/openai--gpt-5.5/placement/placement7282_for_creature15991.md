At first I think I would be found beside the black pool, because everything in the town leans toward it without admitting that it does. But that place is too old, too watched. I would not belong at the lip of the first basin. I would stand there only as a visitor stands before a sleeping animal: still, respectful, already in the way.

Then I think perhaps I would be in one of the empty houses, one of the ones left to sing. A roofless room with a fern growing where the hearth used to be. A low door. A wall darkened by a new internal trickle. I can imagine sitting there at dawn, knees drawn up, listening until the difference between water and thought becomes difficult to keep. That feels close.

But no. I would be higher.

I would find myself at the upper edge of the town, where the last built terrace gives up and the mountain begins again: not wilderness, exactly, but intention without agreement. The construction ground. Fresh stone blocks lying in rows. Mud around the sledges. Pegs hammered into places that do not yet seem to need them. A half-built aqueduct aimed at a wet shine on the cliff, waiting for that shine to confess itself as water.

That is where I would be.

Not in the finished town, not in the oldest ring, not in the lower marsh where water leaves with all its decisions made. I would be where the town is still asking.

I would be one of the people who build for future water.

A dry-channel keeper, perhaps. Or an anticipator of seepage. Not quite mason, not quite priest, not quite engineer. Someone with a bundle of alder stakes, a coil of cord, a copper bowl dented by years of listening, and a pouch of moss plugs kept damp against the chest.

My work would begin before there was anything to work on.

I would climb to the raw slope in the cold part of morning, when frost still rims the shallow bowls and breath shows faintly under the hood. I would kneel beside stone that looks dry to everyone else. I would lay my palm on it, then my ear, then the rim of the copper bowl. I would listen for the not-yet-sound: the pressure behind silence, the minute inward ticking of water considering a path.

If I heard nothing, I would wait.

If I heard a faintness, I would mark it.

Not with certainty. Certainty would be rude there. I would set three small pegs in a shallow arc. I would tuck moss beneath them. I would lay a green bough across the place and tell anyone passing not to step there for three days. Children would come and ask if the water had spoken. I would say, “Not spoken. Shifted in its sleep.”

I would be trusted with unfinished things.

My hands would be cold most of the time. The skin at my knuckles would crack and heal dark with peat smoke and mineral stains. My feet would be broad, careful, always testing. I would know the difference between stone that is merely wet and stone that is softening into a future. I would know which glisten means surface rain, which means breath from below, which means a seam has opened behind the face of the cliff.

I would not be important in the plaza. I would not speak often at gatherings. But when new walls were planned, someone would eventually look for me.

“Where will it come?” they would ask.

And I would not answer quickly.

I would walk the line of the proposed wall. I would crouch. I would press my thumb into clay. I would watch how a bead of condensation chooses one side of a stone over another. I would pour a little water from my own flask into a groove and see whether it hurries, hesitates, spreads, or disappears.

Then I would say, “Leave a gap here.”

Or, “Build this low.”

Or, “Do not sleep against this wall after thaw.”

Or, “This place wants a basin, but not yet.”

My dwelling would be small and partly provisional, because someone who advises others not to trust stone should not live too proudly on it. It would sit just below the construction ground, tucked into the angle between a retaining wall and a dwarf willow. The roof would be turf and slate flakes, always leaking in two places I had chosen and one place I had not. Inside there would be reed mats, a low sleeping shelf, drying lines that never quite dried, and shallow trays of labeled moss.

Not written labels. No ink would last.

The mosses would be known by position. Fever moss near the hearth. Threshold moss under the shelf. Plug moss in the covered bowl. Listening moss in the north niche, because it holds the shape of a seep without drowning it.

My doorstone would be green and left unscraped.

Unless it silvered.

If it silvered, I would scrape it myself before sunrise, quietly, without making it an event.

I would have been a child who heard too much.

That is how the town would have found my use. As a child, I would have woken when a drip stopped in the next house over. I would have cried not at thunder, but at the sudden absence after overflow froze in a pipe. Adults would have thought me fussy until one night I refused to cross a step that split open by morning and began to weep clear water.

After that, I would have been given to older listeners.

Not taken from my family, exactly. Families there are wider than hearths. I would still belong to the people of whichever cistern or comb or alder trough held my name. But my days would be spent with elders who could sit without moving for hours, who taught me that water does not always sound like water. Sometimes it sounds like a loosening peg. Sometimes like a moth beneath slate. Sometimes like hunger inside rock.

They would teach me not to be proud of hearing.

“Hearing is only the door,” they would say. “Manners are the room.”

So I would learn manners with stone.

Do not knock twice where the first knock comes back hollow.

Do not praise a dry floor in spring.

Do not call a channel empty; call it unfilled.

Do not hurry water by naming it aloud.

Do not build a straight path unless you are prepared for it to become a blade.

I think I would be lonely, but not unhappily so. The town itself would be company. The hollow pipes, the roof-drips, the breathing cisterns, the long pauses before the underground boom. I would know people by their footfall on wet stone. I would know who walks as if arguing with the mountain, who walks as if apologizing, who walks as if listening only to themselves.

I would like the children best, because they would not yet pretend the town is settled.

They would come to the upper terraces with leaf-boats and questions.

“Will this crack become a stream?”

“Maybe.”

“When?”

“After you stop watching. Or after your daughter’s daughter begins.”

“Can I put my hand in it?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because it has not decided whether it is a mouth.”

They would accept this better than adults.

In thaw season I would be busiest. The town would wake into danger one drip at a time. Frost loosening. Roofs unloading. Bowls overflowing by night. Old channels filling with new urgency. I would walk with the keepers of overflow, clearing notches, lifting mats of moss, listening to pipes before they knocked themselves apart.

There would be deaths in my memory, because every office there is made from old accidents. I would know the story of the proud wall. The swallowed sleeping room. The hearth cracked from beneath because someone sealed a seep with clay and anger. I would have recited these as a child until they entered the body deeper than fear.

But my own faith would not be fear.

It would be courtesy.

Water is coming. That would be the sentence underneath my life.

Water is coming through the young mountain, through seams too thin to see, through pressure, vapor, frost, and patient dark. It is coming whether invited or not. It can arrive as blessing, warning, food, rot, collapse, music, or road. My task would be to make arrival less violent.

So I would build small futures.

A groove cut in stone leading nowhere yet.

A spill notch in a wall that has never flooded.

A basin lined with clay and moss, dry except for mist.

A row of forked posts waiting beneath a cliff-face that only shines in certain weather.

Outsiders, if any reached that height, would think my work absurd. They would see me tending empty channels and say, “There is no stream here.”

And I would say, if politeness required speech, “Not today.”

At times I might doubt. Of course I would. There would be seasons when a marked seep fails, when a pegged line stays dry for years, when the younger builders laugh softly and move their labor somewhere more immediate. I would pretend not to mind. I would mind.

Then, one morning after a hard frost, a thread would appear exactly where the old peg had rotted away.

Not much. Less than a finger’s width. Clear water trembling over blue-gray stone.

I would kneel beside it.

Not triumphant. Triumph would be too loud.

I would place moss along its edge and call for someone to bring clay. Then I would sit there through the first three days, as the rule requires, watching the new water learn itself.

That would be enough.

If I became less human over time, it would be in the town’s accepted way. My hearing would turn inward and downward. Dry air would make me cough. I would distrust rooms where fire burns cleanly and water makes no answer. I would grow compact, stained, patient. My clothes would smell of smoke, moss, wet wool, and stone dust. Copper would leave green at my throat.

When I grew old, I would become one of those people who seem to appear only in mist.

A figure at the edge of a half-built aqueduct.

A hand on a wall.

A hooded shape crouched over a crack no one else has noticed.

Children would be told, “Ask them where the water will go.”

And I would answer less and less in words.

I would point with two fingers. I would move a stone. I would uncover a groove cut long before their births. I would tap a pipe and let them hear the difference between full, hollow, and waiting.

Eventually I would have a house that begins to sing.

Not loudly. Not beautifully. A low resonance in the north wall when thawwater passes behind it. At first I would remain there, listening from my sleeping shelf. Then the sound would change, becoming rounder, more bodily. The empty-house listeners would come at dawn and sit cross-legged in my room. They would say nothing for a long while.

Finally one would say, gently, “You know.”

And I would know.

I would move my sleeping mats elsewhere. I would take the copper bowl, the moss trays, the cord, the old black plumb-stone. I would leave the house to its song. Ferns would enter. A child might someday peer through the low door and see water threading the wall where my shadow used to fall.

If I died there, I would want to be carried near the black pool but not too near. Close enough to hear the boom beneath the basin. Close enough for the oldest citizen to clear its throat through me one last time. Then I would be taken upward, not downward, and buried shallowly beside an unfilled channel.

Or perhaps not buried. Perhaps set under stones where water may pass over me when it finally comes.

That would be fitting.

I would be someone who spent a life preparing a way for what outlives certainty.

A keeper of dry channels.

A listener for future water.

One of the small, damp people at the town’s upper edge, building politely into the mountain’s unfinished thought.
