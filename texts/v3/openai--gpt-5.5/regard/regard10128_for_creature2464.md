### What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive as lightning.

Lightning is too quick for that place, too presumptuous. In the Machine City, speed is a kind of violence. A sudden miracle could shear a worker from their own childhood, scatter a Chronofauna into unrealized futures, or wake something sealed in the deep crystal where time has forgotten how to flow.

So the power would arrive first as patience.

It would listen.

Not with ears, because the City does not speak in air alone. It would listen to the thrum of the great assemblies, to the violet pulse of the collectors, to the crack of collapsing eddies. It would listen to the Engineers’ careful footfalls, the silence beneath the Moment Monks’ temporal speech, the feeding-patterns of the Chronofauna as they combed the gradients of cause and consequence.

It would learn the City’s grammar before offering even kindness.

For a long while, no one would know it was there.

Perhaps one Engineer, working alone near a calibration bridge, would notice that a tool dropped from their glove did not tumble into a fatal eddy, but instead drifted gently back along the tether line. They would assume a local anomaly. They would log it.

A Moment Monk, spread briefly across seven adjacent instants, would feel a presence touch none of them and all of them. Not an intrusion. Not command. More like a hand held near a wound, asking permission before contact.

The Chronofauna would know first in their way. The smallest of them, those that fed on microfractures between possible seconds, would gather around regions where the temporal pressure had become less jagged. They would not flee. They would spiral.

Only after listening would the power act.

And its first act would be restraint.

It would see that the Machine City was not merely a wound in the world, nor merely a crime, nor merely a marvel. It was an ecosystem, an industry, a refuge, a prison, a cathedral, a mistake that had learned manners, a disaster postponed through devotion.

To destroy it would kill.

To “fix” it too completely would erase the lives shaped by it.

To leave it untouched would be cruelty.

So the power would choose the hardest path: not salvation from above, but mercy woven into maintenance.

It would begin with the workers.

Not by removing their suits. Not by declaring them free while leaving them unable to survive freedom. Instead, it would give the Engineers small, precise mercies.

A seal that no longer chafed the skin after six months of wear.

A visor algorithm that stopped showing one’s own death as a calibration ghost.

A shift bell that rang according to the body’s need rather than the City’s hunger.

A corridor where causality held firm enough that someone could laugh without consulting a risk chart.

The power would enter the suit-lattices as a new principle, too gentle to trigger alarms. Chronological insulation would become not only protection from time, but protection for the self within time. Dreams would return. Sleep would deepen. Memories would stop arriving out of order.

Those who had aged in fits and starts would not be forcibly restored. That would be another violation. But their bodies would be given a choice.

Some would begin aging again, slowly and honestly.

Some would remain as they were, but without pain.

Some, who had lost too many years to the City’s appetite, would find a hidden credit in their bones: not immortality, not reversal, but an added measure of ordinary mornings.

The power would make a place for them.

At first it would be mistaken for an unused maintenance chamber near the outer shell, where the rock still remembered seasons. The door would appear only to those who needed rest and would open only when no urgent duty depended on them.

Inside would be a room with stable time.

Nothing grand.

A table. A kettle. A basin of water that fell downward every time. A wall that showed not possible catastrophes, not projected efficiencies, but the sky above the mountain as it truly was: dawn, cloud, snow, stars.

The Engineers would call it a superstition.

Then a rumor.

Then, eventually, a right.

The power would not demand worship there. It would not inscribe commandments. It would only keep the kettle warm and the minutes whole.

Next, it would turn toward the Chronofauna.

The wise do not mistake the strange for the expendable.

It would perceive them not as pests, not as hazards, but as native intelligences of turbulence. They were the City’s reef-creatures, its bats, its wolves, its bees. Their feeding kept certain pressures from accumulating. Their migrations predicted collapses more accurately than any gauge. Their presence meant the place had become more than machinery.

So the power would not tame them.

It would deepen their pools.

Here and there, in neglected pockets between platform struts and folded conduits, it would make sanctuaries of rich but harmless temporal gradient. Places where young Chronofauna could unfold without being shredded by industrial pulses. Places where the old ones could dissolve into probability without being harvested by accident.

The Engineers would notice fewer attacks, fewer inexplicable losses of equipment, fewer events where a man went into a tunnel on Tuesday and emerged with Thursday’s terror in his throat.
