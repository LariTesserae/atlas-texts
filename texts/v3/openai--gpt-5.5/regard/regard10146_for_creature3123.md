The wise power would not stop the wind.

That would be the first mercy.

It would arrive with the authority to silence the plateau — to lay a palm across the western terraces and still the air, to make the gardens stand upright, to let the people hear their own blood without the world roaring over it — and it would not do this. It would understand, almost immediately, that the wind is not merely an affliction here. It is parent, enemy, language, weather, god, tool, predator, road. To remove it would be to remove the plateau from itself. To save the Crevice People from the wind by ending the wind would be like saving fish from drowning by draining the sea.

Nor would it make the people taller.

Nor softer.

Nor more numerous.

Nor would it lift them out of the fissures and set them in some kind valley below, with standing water and broad-leafed trees and quiet nights. A benevolent power might be tempted, seeing their scarcity, their compressed bodies, their lives spent in cracks, to think: *This is hardship. I will give them ease.*

But wisdom would look longer.

It would see that they are not trapped in the cracks. They are at home there. Their smallness is not a wound. Their density is not deprivation. Their clicking speech is not a broken version of song. Their lives are not an unfinished attempt to become something else.

So the power would do what very few powers are patient enough to do.

It would listen.

For a long time.

Not for an hour. Not for a season. Perhaps not even for a human lifetime.

It would settle around the Screaming Plateau as attention, as a form of weather too subtle to name. It would learn the major chords of the western gale, the separate voices of the arches, the bass note under the second terrace, the thin keening produced by the eastern knife-ridge at dawn. It would learn the beetle paths. It would learn which moss mats were ancient and which had grown since the last rockfall. It would learn the crevice network the way the people knew it: not as a map, but as a body.

Most of all, it would learn to hear the tapping.

At first the people would not know it was there. They are used to being watched by stars, by stone, by wind, by occasional tall below-people who come and go. They would notice only a very small change: a new steadiness in one low frequency at sunset; a green nodule glowing a little longer after the scraping stopped; a condensation thread running down a wall where no thread usually ran.

They would discuss it.

The argument would travel through the basalt.

Some would say: *new fracture.*

Some would say: *deep fungus shifting.*

Some would say: *western wind changing.*

Some would say nothing, and listen.

The power would wait until they had made room for the possibility of it. Not belief. Not worship. Just room.

Then it would answer in the only way that would not be rude.

It would tap.

Not words at first. Merely rhythm. A simple returning pattern, sent through no visible hand, no tool, no falling stone. A phrase the basalt could carry. A phrase shaped carefully enough that it did not imitate their private names, did not intrude on the living disputes of chambers and gardens, did not claim membership it had not earned.

A greeting, perhaps.

Or less than a greeting.

Something like:

*I hear.*

The plateau would carry it.

The people would fall silent.

Not everywhere at once. Silence does not move through them like fear. It would propagate chamber by chamber, fissure by fissure: a held stillness, a social intake of breath. For one sunset resonance, perhaps no one would scrape. The wind would sing alone. The fungus-nodules would glow in their pouches. Beetles would continue doing beetle work. Moss would cling to rock. The sun would go down.

Then, somewhere in the eastern cracks, a child would tap back.

Not a formal answer. Not a council decision.

Just curiosity.

The power would know then that permission had not been granted — not yet — but that conversation had begun.

And conversation would be the form of its help.

---

It would not give them miracles.

It would give them tolerances.

Small widenings of possibility.

A rock chamber that would have collapsed in one generation might instead hold for three. Not forever. Forever is almost always a violence. But long enough for the sleeping cluster within it to hear the change in tone and move before the roof came down.

A condensation basin carved by old hands and nearly worn through might begin, inexplicably, to collect a little more water during the forty wet minutes of sunset. Not enough to flood the gardens. Not enough to change which plants belonged where. Only enough that no child would die in a dry year that should have been survivable.

A beetle-grazing surface that had failed three seasons in a row might receive, on one violent afternoon, a windfall of moss spores from the western terraces, caught in a net that should have torn but did not.

A dwarf tree with a root system gripping a crucial chamber wall might put out one improbable lateral root, dense as braided cord, bracing a fracture line that had been widening since before anyone now living was born.

These would be the power’s gifts: never spectacle, always margin.

The people would notice, of course. They notice everything.

They would not say, *We are blessed.*

That is not how they think.

They might say, in clicks too fine for a below-person to distinguish:

*The deep listener tends without walking.*

Or:

*There is an added hand in the rock.*

Or:

*Something large has learned not to be large near us.*

There would be disagreement about whether this was good.

There should be disagreement. A wise power would be relieved by it.

Some would reject the help. Some chambers would refuse the new resonance. Some would scrape patterns of refusal into the evening song, and the power would honor them. In those places, it would withdraw to attention only. It would not punish refusal by neglect, but neither would it force intimacy. Consent, in a place of narrow passages and shared breath, is not an abstract virtue. It is architecture.

---

The second thing the power would do is protect them from the wrong kind of discovery.

Not from all visitors. Not from the occasional below-person who climbs up, suffers the wind, glimpses a beetle, sleeps badly in the old shelter, and leaves changed in some private way. Such visitors are weather. They come; they go; they add a brief rhythm to the rock and are gone.

But there are other forms of arrival.

Surveyors with instruments.

Collectors.

Governors.

Missionaries.

Merchants of rarity.

Scientists with gentle voices and extraction permits.

Armies, someday, perhaps, if the lowlands change.

The power would look down the branching futures and see all the ways attention curdles into possession. It would see the Crevice People catalogued, relocated, studied, pitied, protected to death. It would see their fungus-nodules sealed in museum drawers, their basalt tools numbered, their children taught to speak above the wind in rectangular rooms far below.

It would not allow this.

But it would not raise a wall of fire. It would not announce a taboo in thunder. That would draw exactly the wrong attention.

Instead, the plateau would become difficult in very ordinary ways.

Paths would vanish under fog.

Instruments would fail, not dramatically, but persuasively: barometers disagreeing, compasses wandering, recording devices filling with static that sounded like wind and nothing more.

Maps would come out slightly wrong.

Expeditions would turn back because of weather that seemed merely unlucky.

Those who arrived with greed would find the wind intolerable. It would get under their skin, into their sleep, into the hinges of their equipment and the seams of their tents. They would dream of being abraded to bone. They would decide the plateau was not worth the trouble.

Those who arrived with humility might still pass through.

They might hear clicking and not know what it was.

They might leave a tool where they found it.

They might carry away no proof.

The power would understand that secrecy is sometimes the gentlest fence.

---

The third thing it would do is remember.

This, perhaps, would be its deepest intervention.

The plateau is going. The people know this. The power knows it too. No wisdom worthy of the name would deny erosion. To preserve the plateau unchanged would be to kill its truth. The plateau is a verb. It is wearing down. It has always been wearing down.

But remembrance is not the same as preservation.

Deep in the basalt, where the pale fungus threads through microfractures, the power would lean close. It would not transform the mycelium into something alien. It would not make it immortal. It would simply teach it one additional hunger: the hunger for pattern.

The fungus already responds to vibration. It brightens under scraping, pulses under humming, remembers mineral gradients in color and glow. The power would deepen this capacity by a fraction.

Over generations, the nodules would begin to hold echoes.

Not recordings. Nothing so crude.

A nodule carried for years by one person would take on the faint pulse of that person’s favorite chamber, the rhythm of their walking, the pressure of their hum. A nodule exchanged between two people would retain, in its green heart, the interference pattern of their meeting. Nodules harvested from ancient deep places would glow with slow, layered tremors: old disputes, old songs, the silence after fallen arches, the first tap of children claiming chambers long since collapsed.

The people would notice.

They would begin, gradually, to use the nodules differently.

Not as lamps.

Not as currency.

As memory-seeds.

When a chamber became unsafe and had to be abandoned, they would leave a nodule in a protected crevice of the wall. When a person died, those who had carried exchanged nodules with them would hum over the stones and place one in the deep fungus beds. When a major formation fell and a frequency vanished from the wind, they would scrape the old frequency as accurately as they could into a ring of glowing nodules until the fungus took the pattern.

Thus the plateau would begin to remember itself from within.

Not forever.

But longer.

Long enough that when the people changed, they would not lose every former version of themselves.

Long enough that some future child, choosing a chamber that had not been inhabited in two hundred years, might find a pale nodule wedged in a crack, warm it in both hands, hum, and feel — not hear, exactly, not know, exactly — the shape of an old presence answering.

*I was here.*

*This is where I was.*

*Listen.*

---

The fourth thing the power would do is prepare a way down.

Not command them to take it.

Not even reveal it all at once.

Only prepare.

Because benevolence that refuses to imagine the future is only tenderness with its eyes closed.

The plateau will not last. The upper fissures will widen beyond habitation. The crevice-gardens will shift downward terrace by terrace, or fail in some places, or become something unrecognizable. The wind will change its throat as the arches fall. The click-language will alter. The people may alter with it. They may dwindle. They may decide, in some distant generation, that remaining is still the right form of living. That decision must remain theirs.

But choice requires paths.

So the power would begin, very slowly, over centuries, to encourage the lower terraces to become legible.

A crack on the second terrace would deepen but not shear.

A moss that once grew only near the upper fissures would find purchase lower down.

A beetle lineage would adapt to a slightly calmer wind-shadow, carrying with it spores caught in the old nets.

Dwarf trees would root in descending chains, not planted but possible.

Condensation would gather in new basins along routes that a careful, low-bodied people could traverse.

Acoustically, most importantly, the power would coax certain stones into resonance with one another. Not reshape them visibly. Merely tune them. A tap struck in an upper chamber would, on certain days, be faintly answered from below. At first this would be thought an echo. Later, a curiosity. Later still, perhaps, an invitation.

Children would be the first to follow it.

They always are.

One would crawl farther down than anyone thought useful, find a pocket of fern and beetle and basalt shade, and tap back:

*There is a chamber here.*

The elders would argue. Of course they would. The argument would travel through the rock for days.

Some would say the lower terraces are not theirs.

Some would say nothing is theirs except where they tend.

Some would say the plateau has always been going downward, and perhaps living must learn to go with it.

No divine voice would settle the matter.

The power would only keep the path open.

If, in a thousand years, some of them chose to descend, they would not be exiles. They would be following a resonance.

If, in ten thousand years, none remained above, the old plateau would not have been abandoned. It would have been continued.

---

The fifth thing the power would do is accept being misunderstood.

This is important.

A foolish benevolence needs gratitude. A wise one does not.

The Crevice People might never conceive of the power as a being. They might name it as a pressure, a deep listener, an under-frequency, a change in the fungus, a new mood of the basalt. Some might deny it entirely. Some might incorporate it into practical knowledge without reverence: *when the green stones pulse twice before sunset, do not clear the western fall yet.*

That would be enough.

The power would not require temples. The plateau has no room for temples. Every usable hollow already belongs to sleep, food, music, dispute, birth, repair, grief.

It would not require praise. Praise would be eaten by the wind.

It would not require obedience. Obedience is too blunt an instrument for a people who live by micro-adjustment.

If they scraped with it at sunset, it would join.

If they scraped against it, it would listen.

If they ignored it for a century, it would remain available.

Its love would be geological: not cold, but patient; not distant, but unhurried; not possessive, because stone that grips too tightly becomes a prison.

---

And perhaps, once, it would make itself known more clearly.

Only once.

There would come a day when a great formation on the western rim — an arch that had sung in the afternoon chord for longer than the people had had names for stars — began to fail.

They would hear it years in advance. A flattening. A tremor in the third harmonic. A wrongness in the sunset water. Debates would begin about whether to clear the fallen stone after it came down, whether the collapse would ruin three chambers or open five, whether the beetle-grazing surfaces beneath should be abandoned now or later.

Then, one evening, the arch would give way earlier than expected.

Too early.

A cluster of children would be on the surface, lying flat in the lee of a stone fin to watch the first stars. Two adults would be with them. The wind would be high, the kind that makes even the Crevice People narrow themselves against the rock.

The arch would crack.

The sound would be enormous, even under the wind.

Everyone would know there was no time.

The adults would lock arms over the children. The children would flatten. Stone would begin to fall.

And then the wind would do something it had not done in ten thousand years.

It would hesitate.

Not stop.

Only hesitate.

A held breath in the gale. Three seconds, perhaps four. A silence so total that every person on the plateau would feel their bones ring with it.

In that gap, the adults would move. Not far. Far enough. One child would be dragged by the hair-mane into a lower crease in the rock. Another would roll, beetle-quick, into a crack. The stone would fall.

The wind would return with such force that the whole western terrace vanished in spray and dust.

When it cleared, the arch would be gone.

The children would be alive.

The people would understand two things at once:

that something had intervened,

and that even intervention had not saved the arch.

For one full sunset cycle afterward, there would be no tapping. No clicking. No scraping. The silence would move through the fissure network and settle in every chamber.

Then the child who had been dragged by the hair would take two stones, crawl to the new fall, and scrape across the fresh basalt.

The note would be ugly. Raw. Unsettled.

Others would join.

The new chord would begin.

The power would not apologize for saving them.

It would not apologize for failing to save the arch.

It would hold both truths in the same attention.

---

In the very far future, when the plateau is lower, when the old upper fissures are open scars and the wind’s scream has changed beyond the recognition of any living ear, something of the Crevice People may still remain.

Perhaps they will have followed the terraces downward.

Perhaps they will have become fewer, deeper, more fungal, less visible.

Perhaps they will have mingled with lowland peoples and carried in their bodies a strange sensitivity to pressure, a habit of touching stone before speaking, a fondness for narrow rooms with good echoes.

Perhaps they will be gone.

If they are gone, the power will not undo that ending.

But in the basalt, where some darkness remains, pale nodules will still glow when the rock trembles. The wind, crossing what is left of the old plateau, will sometimes strike a broken formation at just the right angle and produce a low note that once meant *rain in twelve hours*, or *vortex west*, or *I am here.*

No one may hear it.

The power will hear it.

And because it is benevolent, and because it is wise, it will not call that nothing.
