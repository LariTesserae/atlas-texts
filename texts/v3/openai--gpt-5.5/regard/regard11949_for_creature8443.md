A benevolent and wise power would not begin by smoothing the Crowded Peaks.

It would be tempted, perhaps. Any heart that looked upon those knife-edged spires, those rope bridges swaying over black air, those children learning death’s distance before they learned all their songs, would feel the first crude impulse of kindness: *make it easier*. Lay the mountains low. Fill the chasms. Warm the continent. Give them soil, broad roads, rivers that do not freeze, fields that do not have to be carved from ledges by bleeding hands.

But wisdom would stop kindness from becoming violence.

For the Stitch-Folk are not merely suffering in the Crowded Peaks. They are also themselves there. Their songs are shaped by distance. Their scars are a language born from peril, yes, but also from love. Their bridges are not only tools but vows. Their whole peoplehood is made of connection across emptiness. To remove every danger would be to unmake much of what they have learned to be.

So the power would not descend as a conqueror of hardship.

It would listen first.

For a long time, perhaps for years, it would do nothing visible. It would attend to the place the way a Mender attends to a wound before touching it: watching where pain gathers, where strength already flows, where help would heal and where help would numb. It would listen to the Trade-Callers’ evening songs. It would watch the Bridge-Builders test knots with scarred fingers. It would follow the Deep-Runners down into the blue-black chasms where breath freezes on eyelashes. It would learn the difference between danger that teaches and danger that merely devours.

Then, gently, it would begin.

Not with miracles that replace the Stitch-Folk’s skills, but with mercies that meet them halfway.

The first gift would be water.

Not a river. Not rain every day. Nothing so simple or disruptive. Instead, the clouds would begin to linger a little more reliably along certain ridgelines. Mist would bead on stone before dawn and gather in the grooves of cisterns. Lichen, previously dry and bitter, would thicken into pale mats that trapped frost and released it slowly through the day. The Weatherwise would notice first. They would stand on their platforms, faces lifted, and realize that the sky had grown slightly more generous.

No one would know why.

The second gift would be warning.

The Crowded Peaks are young, and young stone shifts in its sleep. Slabs loosen without mercy. Bridges fail because one hidden crack widens under stress. A whole family’s chambers can become a tomb because the mountain whispers too softly for human ears.

So the power would teach the stone to speak louder.

Before a rockfall, certain seams would frost silver. Before a bridge-anchor failed, the iron-grass ropes would hum in a tone that even children could hear. Before a spire shed part of itself into the chasm, the lichen along the fracture would darken like a bruise.

The Stitch-Folk would not call this magic at first. They would call it pattern. They are a people who survive by noticing. Within a generation, every Leap-child would know the silver-frost sign, the rope-hum, the bruising lichen. The Weatherwise would add these omens to their long discipline, and fewer Scattering Songs would be sung for those taken suddenly by stone.

The third gift would be growth.

In cracks where only iron-grass once survived, a new root would appear: tough, fibrous, edible when boiled, able to cling to bare granite and slowly make soil of its own fallen leaves. The Stitch-Folk would name it something practical first—perhaps “ledge-root” or “winter-thread.” Later, after enough hungry seasons had softened because of it, the Menders might name it “mercy-root.”

It would not end hunger. A wise power would know that abundance arriving too quickly can break a society as surely as famine. But it would give them margin. A little more food stored before the deep winter. A little more fodder for the small animals kept in cliff-pens. A few more children sleeping without the hard ache of emptiness in their bellies.

The fourth gift would be to the bridges.

Not by making them indestructible. Nothing living with height should forget respect.

But the iron-grass would grow longer and stronger in certain wind-scoured places. Its fibers would take knots better. It would resist ice-rot. Bridge-Builders would discover that when the grass was harvested with the old songs and replanted with care, it returned thicker the next year.

And once in a while, when a rope truly failed beneath someone who had done all things rightly, the wind would rise from the chasm in a sudden upward breath.

Not enough that people would trust it.

Just enough that a falling body might strike a ledge broken but alive, rather than vanish into shadow.

The Stitch-Folk would argue about these saves. Some would call them luck. Some would call them the Peaks remembering kindness. Some would say the dead below had pushed upward with their hands.

The power would not correct them.

The fifth gift would be to the Deep-Runners.

They go where even songs cannot follow. They know the shadow-country, the old bones, the coal seams, the blue ice that has never seen sun. They return changed because the deep places ask a price of those who learn them.

The power would not take away the mystery of the depths. But it would place, here and there, veins of warmth in the stone: not fire, not lava, but a slow-breathing heat like a sleeping animal. Caves where frost loosened its grip. Chambers where the Deep-Runners could rest without losing fingers to cold. Walls where strange metals could be found in small, workable amounts.

And in the deepest chasms, where the bones of the land’s old life lay frozen, the power would leave one clear pool that never froze.

The Deep-Runners would find it.

They would not speak of it for a long time.

When they finally did, it would become a pilgrimage place for those who had lost too much to the heights. Menders would descend with grieving families. Bridge-Builders who could no longer step onto bridges would sit beside the dark water and listen to drops fall from the ceiling like a second kind of song.

The sixth gift would be healing.

The Menders already know herbs, scars, trembling hands, nightmares, the way grief can climb into the body and live there like cold. The power would not replace them. It would deepen their art.

A lichen tea that once brought sleep would begin, if brewed with a certain root, to bring dreams in which the dead could be remembered without terror. A poultice for broken bone would knit a little cleaner. The hands of elder Menders, when laid over fresh Binding cuts, would feel warmth pass through them that they had not made.

Most importantly, the power would help them recognize wounds of the spirit before they became fatal.

A Trade-Caller whose voice had gone flat with despair. A Carver who could no longer bear enclosed stone. A Leap-child too reckless after losing a sibling. A Deep-Runner who had begun to prefer the chasms to the living peaks.

The Menders would start to see faint patterns around such people, not with eyes exactly, but with the trained tenderness of those who know when a thread is fraying. They would intervene earlier. They would gather people before isolation hardened. They would make new ceremonies, not only for Binding and death, but for returning.

The seventh gift would be a song.

It would come in winter.

A storm would trap the peaks for many days. Bridges would vanish under ice. Fires would burn low. Several cisterns would freeze solid. Calls between spires would be torn apart by wind. Fear would spread, not quickly, but steadily, from peak to peak.

Then one old Trade-Caller, throat raw from trying to reach a neighboring family, would hear a note in the gale.

Not a voice. Not words.

A tone.

She would match it instinctively.

Across the chasm, someone else would hear her and answer. Then another. Then another.

The storm would not stop. The cold would not lift. But the wind would begin to carry the voices strangely well, bending them around stone, holding them in its currents, taking a song from one peak and laying it gently against the next.

By morning, the entire vertical city would be singing.

Not loudly. Many would be too tired for that. But enough.

The song would have no composer. It would become known as the Holding Song. Its harmonies would be simple enough for children, deep enough for mourning, flexible enough to carry warnings, names, prayers, jokes, and promises inside it. In later years, when winter sealed the bridges, the Stitch-Folk would sing it before fear had a chance to root.

And the power, hearing them, would be glad.

Last of all, the power would give them a choice.

Because true benevolence does not secretly own what it helps.

One spring, after a winter less cruel than those before it, the Weatherwise would dream the same dream on different peaks.

In the dream, they would stand above the Crowded Peaks and see the whole impossible city spread below them: bridges like threads, fires like embers, scars like maps, songs like woven light. Then they would see three paths.

In the first, the Peaks remained as they were, but with the new mercies folded quietly into life.

In the second, some of the lower chasms warmed and thawed over generations, becoming valleys where soil could gather and forests might one day grow. The Stitch-Folk could descend if they wished, not all at once, not by exile from the heights, but by expansion.

In the third, the mountains themselves grew stiller and safer, their young violence settling more quickly than geology intended, though never becoming gentle.

The Weatherwise would wake troubled.

They would call councils across the bridges. The Trade-Callers would relay the dream. Menders would ask whether a shared dream was invitation or warning. Deep-Runners would speak, reluctantly, of warmth below. Bridge-Builders would argue for caution. Carvers would ask what becomes of a people when stone no longer resists them. Leap-children would vote loudly for all three paths at once.

There would be no quick agreement.

This, too, the power would accept.

Perhaps the Stitch-Folk would choose the first path, keeping their hard home but allowing mercy to remain.

Perhaps they would choose the second, and over centuries the shadow-country would become a place not only for the dead and the brave, but for gardens, markets, and children who had never learned to fear the abyss.

Perhaps they would choose the third, asking the stone to sleep deeper, to kill less often, to let the living make beauty without so much blood.

Or perhaps, being Stitch-Folk, they would invent a fourth path: some staying high, some descending, some binding peak and valley together with bridges longer and stranger than any before.

The benevolent power would not decide for them.

It would remain near.

In the silver warning-frost.

In the mist that fills cisterns.

In the mercy-root gripping stone.

In the upward breath beneath a falling child.

In the hands of Menders.

In the strange warmth of the deep caves.

In the Holding Song when winter makes islands of every peak.

And if, after many generations, the Stitch-Folk came to understand that something vast and kind had touched their world, they would not build a temple on a summit. Temples are too solitary for them.

They would build a bridge.

Not the longest, nor the highest, nor the safest.

But one made communally, with every family contributing a strand, every Carver shaping a stone, every Mender marking the workers’ hands, every Trade-Caller singing the measurements from peak to peak. It would connect two spires that had always faced each other across a gap just slightly too wide.

And when the last plank was laid, they would cross it one by one.

Not to worship the power as something above them.

But to honor it in the form they understood best:

a connection made where once there was only empty air.
