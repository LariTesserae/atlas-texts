It would not arrive as a conqueror.

A lesser power, seeing the Plateau, might mistake grief for corruption and raise a cleansing fire. A frightened power might seal the place beneath glass, or bury the Hollowing under sanctified stone, or command the watchers to be gone.

But a benevolent and wise power would understand that ancient wounds are not evil simply because they are terrible.

So it would come quietly.

Not as lightning. Not as a sun burning away the mist.

It would come first as listening.

For seven nights and seven days—though time near the Hollowing would object to the count—it would rest at the edge of the clearing where the moss pulls back. It would not step onto the breathing black earth. It would not touch the fused trunk of bone and sap. It would not command the watchers to kneel, flee, or explain themselves.

It would listen to the seep of stone.

To the roots thinking slowly beneath the moss.

To the beetles’ minute clockwork prayers.

To the birds whose songs had been bent by echoes from elsewhere.

To the groan of industry beyond the plateau’s edge.

To the long, unspeakable memory inside the Hollowing.

And when it finally understood enough to act without arrogance, it would do three things.

First, it would place a mercy at the boundary.

Not a wall. The Plateau would hate a wall. The Hollowing would remember too many prisons.

Instead, it would lay down a living threshold: a ring of deep-rooted things, neither tree nor shrub, grown from seeds gathered from before the mountains fell. Their roots would drink rust from the water and bitterness from the soil. Their leaves would catch soot from the wind and turn it, slowly, into dark fertile loam. Their bark would hum in counterpoint to the pistons beyond the edge, not silencing industry, but reminding it of rhythm, of limit, of the breath between blows.

Those who came from the smoking lands with hunger in their hands would find themselves walking in circles until their hunger changed shape. Machines brought too near would clog with moss, not out of malice, but insistence. Oil would thicken into harmless tar and sink. Sparks would gutter. Maps would blur.

Yet any who came with grief, reverence, or need would find a path inward.

The Plateau would remain strange.

But it would no longer be undefended.

Second, the power would tend the watchers.

They had waited too long beneath the Hollowing, pressed against its fractures, feeding or being fed upon by revelations they could not survive. Perhaps they were once animals. Perhaps once people. Perhaps splinters of the old sky. Perhaps thoughts shed by the Hollowing in moments of unbearable remembering.

The benevolent power would not name them monsters.

It would kneel—if such a power had knees—and open its hands.

Into the clearing it would release small, soft lights, each no larger than a beetle, each carrying a single intact sensation: warmth under fur, rain on feathers, the taste of clean mineral water, the pressure of another body sleeping nearby without fear, the sound of a true name spoken kindly.

The watchers would recoil at first.

Then one would touch a light.

Then another.

Some would remember enough to become whole and depart into the mist, taking shapes the Plateau could recognize. A bird. A fox-like thing. A child of fog. A column of moths. A shadow with antlers that no longer needed to tremble.

Some would remain, because the Hollowing was their home, or their altar, or their wound.

For those, the power would make a gentler communion. It would teach the cracks to open less like torn flesh and more like eyelids. It would give the watchers intervals between visions. Rest between revelations. Silence between pulses of the lattice beneath the world.

Waiting would no longer be starvation.

Third, and last, the power would speak to the Hollowing.

Not in language.

Language would be too small, and the Hollowing had endured too many interpretations.

The power would show it a mirror made of still water drawn from the Plateau’s oldest seep. In that mirror the Hollowing would see itself—not as a horror, not as a failed tree, not as a wound in the world, but as a guardian of a breach no living age remembered how to honor.

It had been holding something closed.

Or holding something open.

Perhaps both.

For longer than mountains.

For longer than mercy had known where to find it.

The power would not heal the Hollowing by making it ordinary. That would be another kind of violence. It would not seal every fracture, smooth every crack, turn bone back into wood, or command the dark resin to run clear.

Instead, it would ask:

*What pain is yours?*

The Hollowing would answer by peeling back the world.

The Plateau would see the luminous lattice again, veins threading all things, the heartbeat beneath heartbeat. But this time the vision would not split the air raw. This time the power would hold the edges.

The Hollowing would show the old pressure from above. The sky before the sky. The weight that descended. The first fracture. The Plateau’s birth as scar tissue. The roots knitting themselves into thought because something had to remember. The long labor of keeping the wrongness from widening. The slow poisoning from the industrial lands, not merely chemical but symbolic: extraction, repetition, appetite without listening.

The wise power would grieve.

Not perform grief.

Not radiate holy sorrow for spectacle.

It would simply grieve, and because it was powerful, its grief would become weather.

For one whole season, a rain would fall on the Weeping Plateau.

Not hard rain. Not cleansing flood. A patient rain. Drop by drop, it would mingle with the stone’s endless seepage. The oily pools would cloud, then clear. The iridescent sheens would gather into harmless colors and sink into mineral sleep. The algae would brighten. The moss would swell, not to smother, but to cushion.

The blackened earth beneath the Hollowing would soften.

For the first time in an age, the place would smell not only of ozone and copper, but of fresh-turned soil.

Then the power would give the Hollowing a choice.

It would offer to take up part of the burden.

Not all. No wise power steals purpose from an ancient being merely because the purpose hurts.

But part.

A companion-root would be planted beside the Hollowing—not a rival, not a replacement. A pale, low thing, almost humble, with roots descending not into soil but into the lattice beneath reality. It would bear no leaves. Its branches would be small arcs of quiet light.

Where the Hollowing fractured, the companion would bind.

Where the Hollowing remembered, the companion would help remember.

Where the Hollowing opened, the companion would help close.

And on nights when the sky threatened to become membrane again, veined and pulsing, the Hollowing would no longer stand alone.

If the Hollowing refused, the power would bow and withdraw its offer, leaving only the boundary and the mercy-lights.

But I think the Hollowing would accept.

Not immediately.

It would take a hundred years to decide.

The benevolent power would wait.

During those hundred years, the plateau would change in ways almost too slow to notice.

The mist would remain, but sometimes it would part to reveal a clean gray dawn.

The shadows would still move strangely, but less hungrily.

The shrubs would keep their cracked leather bark and arthritic limbs, but in spring—true spring, a season the Plateau had half-forgotten—they would put out tiny white flowers smelling faintly of rain and bone dust.

The beetles would become numerous beyond counting.

The birds would regain some of their songs.

The distant hum of machinery would not vanish, but those who worked the machines would begin to dream of wet stone, root-nets, and a tree that was not a tree. Some would wake weeping. Some would quit. Some would build differently. Some would come to the threshold and leave offerings: broken gears, cups of clean water, apologies scratched onto sheets of tin.

The Plateau would accept none of the apologies quickly.

But it would allow the tin to rust.

It would allow the cups to fill with rain.

Eventually, the Hollowing would open one crack—not wide, not violently.

The companion-root would answer with a thread of light.

The watchers would gather, trembling, but not desperate.

The power would return, not descending, not blazing, but present in the way a hand is present when held out and not forced upon you.

Together, the Hollowing and the companion would draw a single long breath through the roots of the Plateau.

The ground would shudder.

The vines would twist tighter, then loosen.

The mist would thicken, then lift.

For a moment, the sky would become the vast pulsing membrane again, veined with light.

But this time, it would not feel like rupture.

It would feel like seeing the inside of an eyelid before waking.

Then the world would settle.

The Hollowing would remain terrible.

Sacred things often are.

Its trunk would still be bone and petrified sap. Its branches would still fracture the air. The scent of ozone and copper would linger. The clearing would still feel excavated from reality itself.

But the resin-dark blood would flow more slowly.

The cracks would no longer gape from agony alone.

And when the Hollowing spoke, the world beneath the world would not appear as an opened wound.

It would appear as a hidden body breathing.

The Plateau would go on weeping.

But not all weeping is despair.

Some weeping is pressure leaving stone.

Some weeping is memory becoming water.

Some weeping is the first form healing takes when nothing yet knows how to sing.
