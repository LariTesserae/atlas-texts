The power would not arrive with wings.

It would not break the rim of the basin, or lift the vapor, or call down rain bright enough to scour the films from the pools. It would not say, *This place is barren,* and cover it in green. It would not say, *These beings are poor,* and give them houses with doors, fires that leap, tools that ring, songs loud enough to echo.

A wise power would understand almost at once that help, here, could easily become damage.

So first it would do what the basin itself does.

It would wait.

Not the waiting of hesitation, but the waiting of attention. It would lower itself until its greatness no longer cast a shadow. It would become no larger than a warmth under stone, no louder than a drop falling from a lip of shale into a shallow pan. It would learn the place by the means the place respected: level, seep, mineral taste, the pull of mud, the patience of rims.

For a long while, perhaps years, nothing would seem to happen.

The inhabitants would notice anyway.

One old one, lifting a stone furred with pale film, would pause because the underside had thickened evenly through a season that should have left it thin. A child floating shell chips down a toy channel would find that the channel did not collapse when a careless elbow pressed too near it. A watcher at the central court would hear, beneath the familiar ticks and settling sounds, a deeper steadiness—not an added noise, but the absence of a certain old strain.

They would grow suspicious, because they are wise in the manner of small things.

They would test the basins.

They would bring water from three seeps, five seeps, seven. They would pour each thread into its appointed hollow. They would watch for clouding, skinning, refusal, acceptance.

At first the power would answer with no sign.

A sign too clear would be a command.

Instead it would let the water behave as water, the clay as clay, the mineral films as themselves. Only after many gatherings, when the inhabitants had stopped looking for an intrusion and begun listening for a relationship, would it place its first question into the central pool.

Not words.

A balance.

One basin would receive a thread of dark water and remain dark. Another would receive clear water and turn faintly milky, then clear again. The amber skin on a third would split into islands and then, without touch, draw itself back into one unbroken film.

The inhabitants would crouch around this for a long time.

They would not call it miracle. Miracle is too tall a word.

They would call it perhaps: *a new condition.*

Or: *a listener under the levels.*

And because the power was benevolent, it would not act until it had been answered.

The answer would come in the way of their people.

A hand placed over a seep: wait.

Two fingers laid on a stone row: this line must remain.

A palm turned upward beside the central pool: what has changed here?

Only then would the power begin.

Not to improve the basin.

To spare it from being needlessly broken.

It would make no paradise. It would make a margin.

Deep below the western wall, where warmth already rose and the young were kept among lizard cracks, it would open—not a spring, not a fountain, nothing that ran with ambition—but a second seep fine as a hair. The water would enter so slowly that for three days no one would notice. Then an old caretaker, cheek pressed to stone, would hear a small new thread where no thread had been before.

This seep would not flood the court. It would not sweeten every bitter pool. It would not wash the place clean.

It would simply remain.

In drought, when the old seeps thinned to shining scars, this one would continue.

In seasons of too much rain, when outside water tried to enter with noise and silt and seeds, this one would hold its measure, reminding the basin of itself.

The power would give them redundancy, which is one of mercy’s quieter names.

Then it would turn its attention to the poisons left by former hands.

Not all bitterness is sacred. Not all age deserves preservation.

In one corner, under a collapsed platform where the inhabitants seldom went, a vein of metal had been bleeding slowly into the clay for generations. It had made certain pools beautiful in the wrong way: blue-edged, sharp-tasting, fatal to the pale films that tried to grow there. The inhabitants had learned to avoid it. Their stories named it a place of refusal.

The power would not erase the stain. Erasure would confuse memory.

Instead it would bind the poison deeper into the stone, making it heavy, insoluble, asleep. The blue edges would fade over many seasons. One year, a child would overturn a shale chip there and find, underneath, the first faint fur of edible film.

The elders would argue.

Some would say the refusal had ended.

Some would say the refusal had changed its face.

Both would be right.

The power would not settle the argument.

It would make arguments possible without making them deadly.

Next it would protect the basin from large attention.

This would be delicate, because the power was benevolent not only toward the basin-dwellers, but toward the tall ones beyond the rim. It would not curse travelers. It would not drown surveyors in mud, or blind children who wandered too near, or fill human minds with fear.

But it would bend consequence gently.

A road planned too near the basin would, after a night of ordinary rain, seem less convenient than the ridge beyond it. A merchant looking for mineral rights would find his measuring cord repeatedly snarled, his notes damp, his interest cooling into impatience. A farmer carrying seed would remember another errand. A cruel child with a stone in hand would be distracted by a lizard flashing pale between rocks and would throw elsewhere.

Those who came with hunger to take would find the basin small, uncomfortable, unpromising.

Those who came with reverence would not be turned away.

A potter, perhaps, old enough to move slowly, would one day cross the rim and stand breathing too loudly among the pools. She would see a repaired lip and understand, with a shock that made her kneel, that the repair was not old. She would leave behind a coil of good cord, wrapped in oiled cloth, and depart without looking back too often.

The inhabitants would take the cord after dark.

They would use it for lifting amber skins.

The power would count that as a successful exchange.

It would also give the basin one hidden path outward.

Not a road. Roads invite certainty.

A low seam beneath the northern rim, previously choked with slumped clay, would loosen over years. It would become passable only to those who moved as the inhabitants moved: folded, patient, close to stone. Beyond it lay another wet hollow, smaller, poorer, not yet inhabited but capable of being learned.

The power would not tell them to leave.

It would not tell them to stay.

But wisdom knows that preservation without possibility becomes a kind of trap. A people who can only remain are not fully protected. So the seam would wait, unnoticed until need or curiosity found it.

The first to enter would be a young one with restless hands, disliked by some elders for making channels too quickly and smoothing away prints before anyone had studied them. They would follow a lizard through the seam and return covered in strange pale silt.

The court would gather.

The basins would be tested.

The dark pool would remain dark.

The oval pool would cloud white, then clear.

The amber skin would tremble but not break.

After long consideration, the elders would permit three journeys a year.

No more at first.

Then perhaps more.

Thus the basin would remain itself, but not alone in the universe.

The power would do one more thing, and this would be the most difficult to justify by usefulness.

It would give them a place where nothing needed to be maintained.

Near the eastern shelter, under the raised floor of fist-sized stones, there was a hollow too narrow for sleeping and too damp for storing anything that should not soften. The inhabitants had filled it twice, emptied it twice, argued over it, and finally ignored it.

There the power would gather warmth.

Not much. Only enough to make the stones above it hold an even comfort through the long dim hours. No seep would need guiding there. No crust would be harvested. No basin lip would slump. No film would grow thick enough to require attention. The hollow would be useless.

A child would discover it first.

Children are often the first to accept unnecessary mercy.

The child would crawl in after a lizard, curl against the warm stone, and sleep without having been assigned to sleep there. Later another would join. Then an old one with aching wrists. Then a mother between feedings. Then, in time, it would become known as the still hollow, though all hollows there were still.

No ritual would be required.

No decision would be made there.

No dead would be laid there.

No tools would be stored.

It would be a place where a body could stop being useful and remain welcome.

This would trouble them more than the new seep.

For generations, usefulness had been their form of peace. To remain part of the basin’s work was honor, memory, belonging. But the warm hollow would teach, without speech, that rest also belongs to maintenance. A hand that has ceased scraping for an hour has not betrayed the clay. A sleeping child is not idle against the world. An old one whose fingers can no longer seal a seam is not therefore finished.

The power would not preach this.

It would merely keep the hollow warm.

In time, the inhabitants’ treatment of their dead would change slightly.

Not much. They would still wash the bodies in three basins: clear, milky, dark. Bones would still become burnishers, supports, strengthening ribs beneath lips of clay. Skulls would still be packed with mineral paste and set where water could enter and leave through the mouth.

But before the washing, they would lay each body for one night in the useless hollow.

No task would be asked of it.

No future use named.

For one night, the dead would be only beloved.

Then, at morning, they would be returned to service.

The power would consider this enough.

It would not ask to be worshiped.

If the inhabitants made a mark for it, they would likely make the smallest possible one: a shallow thumbprint pressed into the underside of a stone that no visitor would turn over. Or perhaps they would not mark it at all. Perhaps they would include it among the basin’s conditions: warmth, seepage, clay, leaning, listener.

That would satisfy the power.

For benevolence does not require recognition. Wisdom often prefers not to be named.

Years would pass.

The basin would still look nearly as it had looked before.

No grass would grow.

No reeds would hiss.

No bright birds would come down to sing among the mineral pools. The tallest stone posts would still lean like tired workers. The roofless shelter would remain roofless. The central court would still hold its broken pieces of dim sky. A human crossing the place in haste would see only wet stone, clay lips, shallow pans, old ash, and the suggestion of low movement vanishing between slabs.

But certain disasters would fail to arrive.

A drought would hurt, but not end them.

A flood would disturb, but not erase them.

A poison would sleep.

A greedy road would curve away.

A second hollow would wait beyond the rim.

The young would have somewhere warm to be useless.

The old would sometimes wake from sleep with less pain in their wrists.

And in the central court, at long intervals, when three or five or seven crouched figures gathered with shells of seepwater and fired-clay shards, the pools would answer as they always had: by accepting, refusing, clouding, clearing, skinning, holding.

Only sometimes, after a drop fell and the rings spread outward, the rings would travel farther than they should.

They would touch one clay edge, then another, then another, passing through the divided pools without breaking the divisions between them.

The inhabitants would watch.

They would wait until the last ring vanished.

Then, if nothing further was required, they would resume.
