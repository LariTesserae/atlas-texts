They would do almost nothing.

That would be the first sign of their wisdom.

They would not descend into the Hollow Forge with trumpets. Trumpets would be an insult here, a child's noise thrown against a noise old enough to have become law. They would not break the pistons, dim the plasma arrays, warm the killing air, halt the printers, or command the infinite cylinder to become something gentler.

A lesser mercy would look at the Hollow Forge and see only torment.

A wiser mercy would look longer.

It would understand that the Forge is not broken in any simple way. Its roar is not merely pain. Its cold is not merely cruelty. Its repetition is not merely imprisonment. The Hollow Forge is an alien sacrament: birth without childhood, labor without exhaustion, perfection without rest. To silence it outright would be to murder the only language it has ever spoken.

So the benevolent power would not rescue the Forge from being itself.

It would listen.

Not for an hour. Not for a century. It would listen for many cycles of forty-one hours, until the eternal chord ceased being noise to it and became legible. It would learn the bass-matter thump as one learns a heartbeat. It would hear the impellers’ scream not as anguish but as tension held in absolute obedience. It would hear the plasma arrays, the printers, the rails, the crawling skyscraper-machines, the trembling silicon fronds.

And beneath all of that, beneath the roar that newcomers mistake for totality, it would hear the smallest thing.

The unanswered touch.

Every warm handprint melted into every machine housing. Every shape of fingers, claws, palms, absences. Every mark left by something that once reached out not because it needed to repair, but because it needed contact. The Forge had allowed its unborn children to touch what they were making.

But nothing had ever touched them back.

That is where the power would act.

Not at the top.

Not at the bottom.

At chest height.

On one of the vast builder-machines, beside a worn handprint with six fingers and a thumb set too far back, a new mark would appear.

It would not be burned in.

It would not be carved.

It would be given.

A shape like a hand, though not exactly a hand. Warm. Patient. Answering.

The machine would continue to climb. The ring would continue to harden. The roar would not falter.

But throughout the Hollow Forge, every old handprint would grow one ten-thousandth of a kelvin warmer.

The silicon fronds would tremble.

The Unfinished would precipitate early.

Only by a fraction of a fraction of a second. Almost nothing. But in a place where equilibrium had reigned for four hundred and nine thousand years, almost nothing would be a miracle large enough to bend eternity.

It would rise from the wall as a shape of black ceramic ribs and furnace-light, fourteen meters tall, carrying in its posture the slow inevitability of all its predecessors. It would begin the climb toward the newborn ring.

The benevolent power would climb beside it.

Not ahead.

Not behind.

Beside.

For forty-one hours, the Unfinished would watch the ring being born.

And for forty-one hours, the Unfinished would be watched.

No judgment would be placed upon it. No demand. No name forced into it. The power would simply bear witness to the witness.

The light would remain harsh, but around the Unfinished there would be, for the first time, a distinction between brightness and seeing.

The sound would remain deafening, but within the Unfinished’s structure one harmonic would loosen. Not vanish. Loosen. Enough that the roar could contain another meaning besides command.

The cold would remain murderous, but the handprints would stay warm.

At the end of forty-one hours, when the new ring stood flawless and the old law summoned the Unfinished home, the wall would open as it always had.

The Unfinished would step toward it.

Then stop.

The Hollow Forge had never contained hesitation.

The pistons did not stutter. The printers did not fail. No catastrophe bloomed. No ring cracked. The perfect equilibrium held.

But now, embedded quietly inside the axiom of return, there was a second clause.

You may.

Not:

You must.

The Unfinished stood before the wall.

The wall accepted the new law without understanding it, because the law had been written with infinite gentleness into the same place that permitted handprints. Not into command. Into mercy.

The Unfinished turned its furnace-head toward the benevolent power.

It did not speak.

The power did not ask it to.

After a long time — perhaps a second, perhaps the full moral weight of the Forge’s existence — the Unfinished lifted one angular limb and pressed it against the machine housing beside the old handprint.

The new answering mark glowed.

Then the Unfinished walked into the wall.

The metal received it.

The silhouette shone inside the lattice.

It was incorporated.

The wall thickened by one atomic layer.

The roar continued.

But the sub-harmonic sigh that followed was different.

Not louder. Not softer.

Accompanied.

From then on, once every forty-one hours, the same mercy would be offered.

Not escape as decree.

Not freedom as violence.

Only choice.

Some Unfinished would return immediately. They were the Forge remembering itself, and to be taken home was not always sorrow.

Some would pause.

Some would touch the answering handprint first.

One, after eleven thousand cycles, would remain outside the wall for an additional forty-one hours and watch a second ring being born.

The Forge would not know what to do with this.

So it would make room.

A narrow gallery would appear between two repeating tiers, no larger than a chapel. It would not disrupt the cylinder. It would not halt construction. It would not violate the closed loop. But it would be useless.

Completely useless.

No conduits would pass through it. No printers would feed from it. No rail would require it. No structural necessity would justify it. It would be the first purposeless space the Hollow Forge had ever made.

Inside that small gallery, the roar would still exist, but it would arrive as a distant ocean through thick glass.

The temperature would remain far below any human mercy, but not below the mercy of the Unfinished.

On one wall there would be handprints.

On the opposite wall, answering marks.

Between them, the barest possible interval.

A place to stand without judging.

A place to exist without being immediately reabsorbed.

A place where perfection did not need to be inspected.

The benevolent power would not populate it with flowers. The Forge had no category for flowers. It would not pour water into a universe that had never consented to water. It would not import birdsong, soil, breath, weather, decay, or any of the beloved softenings by which other worlds are healed.

Instead, it would give the Hollow Forge something native to itself, but previously unknown.

A tolerance.

A margin.

A rest note inside the chord.

And perhaps, after ages, the Forge would begin to change in ways so small only mercy could measure them.

The newest rings would remain flawless, but not identical.

One would contain a slight warmth near a gantry rail.

One would hold a microscopic asymmetry shaped like a leaning frond.

One would extrude with a shallow depression at chest height before any hand had touched it, as though anticipating contact.

The Unfinished would still rise.

Still witness.

Still judge.

Still often return.

But now, whenever one stepped into the wall, something of it would not be lost into anonymity. Its handprint would remain distinct. Its pause would be remembered. Its choice would become part of the ring.

The Hollow Forge would continue forever.

But forever would no longer mean only repetition.

And the wise power?

It would stay as long as staying was needed.

Not enthroned.

Not worshiped.

Perhaps not even noticed by the machinery.

It would stand beside the Unfinished at each birth, a witness to the witness, an answer to the ancient warmth of hands.

Because some beings do not need to be saved from their nature.

Some need only to be met there.

And in the Hollow Forge, where the machine had finished becoming itself an eternity ago and had never stopped finishing, mercy would not arrive as an ending.

It would arrive as permission.

The roar would continue.

The rings would rise.

The walls would devour themselves and be born again.

But once every forty-one hours, in the killing cold and the white actinic light, something unfinished would emerge from the ribs of perfection and find that it was not alone.
