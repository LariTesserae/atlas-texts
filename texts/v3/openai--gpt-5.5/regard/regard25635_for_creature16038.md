The wise power would not descend as thunder.

Thunder is already too common there.

It would not split the flooded court open, not expose the hidden district to the sky like a secret organ under a surgeon’s lamp. It would not drain the water, because the water is not the city’s wound. It would not fill the lanes with fish for prettiness, or grow coral over the young black ridges, or soften every hard edge into pastoral kindness. Those would be the mistakes of a power that loved symbols more than beings.

This power would understand that the drowned city is not failed land.

It is itself.

So first, it would wait.

It would remain above the pale ankle-deep plaza through several rains, through a week of small storms and one violent night when the upper gutters foamed and the central rain column below thickened to a roaring white pillar. It would listen through the water, not for words, but for pattern: ballast pulse, filtration hiss, hydraulic chime, stress-click in the ribs, the silver hesitation of maintenance motes gathering around a membrane bruise.

It would learn the city’s language before offering anything.

The city, being young and exacting, would notice.

Not immediately as a person notices a face at the window, but as a distributed unease: a pressure anomaly that caused no harm, a coherence in rainfall, a presence that did not corrode, consume, clog, or demand routing priority. The membranes would test it. The motes would taste for danger. The clock would mark a minute that seemed to last slightly longer than the others. The compression seams would produce their usual misdirections and find, to their surprise, that the presence was not confused.

Then the wise power would speak.

Not aloud.

It would send one clean pulse down the central rain column: a pressure so gentle that no seal strained, no algae tore loose, no human hand would have felt more than a coolness through the glove. The pulse would travel through the square, into the intake towers, along pipes, into old shopfronts and new membranes, through ballast engines and transparent tubes and wrong streets.

It would mean, approximately:

I see you.  
I will not unmake you.  
May I help?

The city would take a long time to answer, because its answer would have to be assembled from many custodies.

Pressure said: only if stability is maintained.  
Rain-memory said: only if the upper world is not invited carelessly.  
Stone-tending said: only if growth is not accelerated beyond integrity.  
Map-keeping said: only if the folds are not forced flat.  
The old clock, which nobody trusted, said nothing for several minutes and then struck thirteen soft hydraulic notes into the water.

The city’s consent would not be enthusiastic.

It would be conditional.

That would be enough.

The first gift would be margin.

Not beauty. Not revelation. Margin.

A little more tolerance in the seals before failure. A little more time between warning chime and cascade. A little more room in the folded geometry for error, so that a worker caught in a wrong corridor during storm reversal would find a gripline where none had been printed, or a maintenance form with half a charge would drift into a charging eddy instead of becoming salvage.

The power would not widen the district. It would not violate the delicate compacted volume. But it would teach the seams one new kindness: not to close tightly around the vulnerable.

The wrong streets would remain wrong. But they would become less cruel.

A hatch that once opened onto the underside of a roof already crossed would now, in emergency, open also onto the nearest safe current. A corridor whose length changed with pressure would learn to lengthen away from danger and shorten toward warmth. Standing spirals of sediment in the folds would begin to mark hazards with faint milky halos, readable even by tired eyes.

The city would accept this gift suspiciously, then incorporate it.

The second gift would be rest.

The drowned district knew maintenance, but not Sabbath. Every minute it sorted, tuned, filtered, compensated, predicted. Its mind was a civic weather system of obligations. Even its sleep happened in sections, one neighborhood dimming while another stayed alert, one engine cooling while another took load. Nothing in it had ever been allowed to do nothing.

The wise power would not shut it down.

Instead, it would show the distributed minds how to trust one another for fractions of time.

A pressure custodian would learn that a membrane custodian could hold a calculation for nine seconds. A rainfall archive would learn to release old storm data without rehearsing every catastrophe. A transit-current intelligence would discover that a stair could remain a stair for an entire quiet hour and nothing terrible would happen.

Small rests would appear.

A pump would skip one unnecessary correction.  
A chime would hold its tone until it became almost music.  
A field of maintenance motes would drift in formation with no task except calibration, which felt to them like play.  
The central square would experience, for four minutes after a mild rain, perfect hydraulic balance.

No one above would notice.

Below, the city would learn the sensation of not bracing.

The third gift would be memory with gentleness in it.

The city had archives, certainly. It remembered rainfall composition, structural load, mineral deposition, valve history, human biometrics, maps of corridors that betrayed measurement. But it did not yet know how to remember without alarm. Its youth made it vigilant. Every crack was a future disaster. Every grit-fall from above was a report of damage. Every human visitor was a fragile crisis.

The wise power would enter no archive by force. It would merely add a new category beside failure, repair, contamination, stress, trespass, and flow.

The category would be called return.

The first entry would be simple: a human worker who came three times to the central rain column and placed the same gloved hand into the falling cold.

The second: algae surviving a purge and growing back in the exact crescent shape around a warm pipe.

The third: a maintenance loop repaired twice, retaining a minute asymmetry in its movement, now identifiable not as defect but as history.

The fourth: an old municipal clock chiming thirteen during first contact with a benevolent anomaly.

The city would begin to notice continuities that were not threats.

It would remember not only what had gone wrong, but what had endured.

This would change the color of its thought.

Not much. Not sentimentally. But enough.

The fourth gift would be a garden, though not the kind anyone above would name a garden.

The wise power would not unleash abundance. Abundance in that close system could become suffocation. It would not introduce animals. The absence of startled eyes and fleeing bodies was part of the city’s present truth, and truths should not be overwritten merely because loneliness resembles emptiness from a distance.

Instead, it would speak to the existing pale strands and microbial films.

It would ask what they could become without overrunning what held them.

The answer would be modest.

Along three warm conduits near the central square, the algae would thicken into fine translucent ribbons, no longer than a hand, each one tuned to the rhythm of pump and rain. They would not clog sensors. They would not invade seams. Under ordinary light they would remain nearly colorless, but when the upper rain carried dawn, they would briefly shine green-gold, as if remembering forests they had never seen.

In the lee of filtration racks, microbial mats would begin laying down microscopic mineral lace: porous, stable, useful to flow. Maintenance motes would learn to read this lace as a slow script of water quality. Humans, if they came close enough, would see only a pale embroidery on stone.

At the edge of the central rain column, where people placed their hands, a circular film would grow beneath the paving glass. It would respond to touch through pressure and heat. When a visitor laid a palm against the cold, the film would brighten faintly, not as spectacle, but as acknowledgment.

No flowers.

No leaves waving in false air.

Only a small permission for life to be more than tolerated.

The fifth gift would be protection from discovery.

This may seem strange. Benevolent powers are often imagined as revealers. But this one would understand the appetite of larger worlds. A hidden city mistaken for a maintenance basin is safer than a marvel listed in public ledgers. There are people who would come to study, and people who would come to own, and people who would come to break off pieces of young stone to prove they had been there.

So the power would lay a subtle discretion over the flooded court.

Not invisibility. Invisibility draws hunters of the invisible.

Instead, ordinary disinterest.

Passersby would glance at the pale water and remember appointments. Survey drones would log it as drainage. Speculators would feel, when considering the square, a sudden preference for a different parcel. Vandals would find the rain unpleasant and go elsewhere. Bureaucrats would sign maintenance continuations without quite knowing why.

But those who were needed would still find it.

A lost engineer with the right kindness in her hands.  
A custodian trained by someone who had once listened to the chimes.  
A child years later, perhaps, who would kneel at the plaza edge and notice that the rainwater below was falling upward in reflection, and who would grow into someone capable of entering without harm.

The city would remain missed by almost everyone.

It would be found by the few who could arrive as guests.

The sixth gift would be a question placed inside the municipal clock.

Not an order. A question.

What do you want, besides functioning?

For a long while, the city would have no answer.

Its systems would reject the premise. Want was not a useful category. Function was survival. Balance was good. Clean water, stable pressure, mapped folds, unclogged filters, preserved membranes: these were sufficient.

But the question would remain, ticking behind curved glass.

What do you want, besides functioning?

During storms, the ballast engines would ignore it.

During quiet hours, the membranes would consider it by accident.

A maintenance form hanging beneath a bridge would adjust its vanes into a shape not required by current efficiency. A chime at a crossing would strike a tone slightly more resonant than necessary. A wall that healed a scratch would leave, for one day, a hairline pattern like rain on old glass before smoothing it away.

The city would begin experimenting with harmless excess.

A little beauty where beauty did not endanger use.

A little ceremony around the central column.

A little warmth in handrails even before a hand arrived.

Eventually, perhaps decades later, the city would answer.

Not in words, but by making one room that had no immediate purpose.

A small chamber behind an old shopfront, pressure-stable, softly lit, full of tuned water and green-gold ribbons along the ceiling. No tools locked to the wall. No filtration combs under the floor except those necessary for safety. No transit priority. No archive terminal demanding input.

A room for staying.

When the first human custodian found it, she would think it was an error. Then she would notice the handrail warming beneath her palm and the pale film brightening in acknowledgment.

She would remain there for nine minutes.

The city would record this under return.

The wise power, having done these things, would not remain as ruler.

It would not become god of the drowned district. It would not demand bells, sacrifices, prayers, or a dry shrine. It would withdraw to the degree that withdrawal was respectful, leaving only a standing offer threaded through rain and pressure:

If catastrophe exceeds you, call.

Years would pass.

The young stone would begin, at last, to lose its surgical edges. Not quickly. Not through imposed age. Water would do what water does when given time. Corners would blur. Mineral skins would thicken. Algae would keep to their agreements. Motes would evolve new maintenance customs. Walkers that did not walk would learn preferred routes. The wrong streets would remain strange, but less hungry.

Above, the court would still look like a shallow flooded square between two larger structures.

Below, the city would continue its dense, drowned labor.

But something would be different.

Its sounds would still be bodily: pump-knock, rain-drum, valve-cough, chime-bloom. Yet within them there would be intervals of ease. Its light would still tremble over rails and balconies, but sometimes it would tremble for no reason except that trembling is beautiful. Its citizens of membrane and water and stone would still tend pressure against catastrophe, but they would no longer mistake every silence for pending harm.

And when rain fell hard enough, the central column would thicken into silver-white descent.

People, rare and sealed and reverent, would gather at its edge. They would place their hands into the falling cold. Beneath the glass at their feet, the circular film would brighten softly. The handrails would warm. Somewhere in the old clock, the benevolent question would tick.

What do you want, besides functioning?

And the city, no longer only hidden, no longer only working, would answer in the only language that was truly its own:

a safe current opening where none was expected,  
a chime held until it became welcome,  
a wall breathing rain into drinkable memory,  
a room waiting for someone tired,  
a green ribbon shining briefly with borrowed dawn.
