A benevolent and wise power would not begin by saving them.

That would be the first restraint, and the first mercy.

It would not look upon the Hollowing and say: *This is too loud, too hot, too alive; let it be stilled.* It would not look upon the grey people above and say: *This is too cold, too fearful, too narrow; let them be remade.* A foolish kindness would choose one world and rescue it from the other. A wise kindness would understand that each has been shaping the other for longer than memory, and that to remove the terror entirely would also remove the form of the people’s courage.

So it would listen.

For a long time, it would listen.

It would listen beneath the shrieking fauna and the papery rasp of ribbon-things. It would listen beneath the clicking speech of the ones who came back out. It would listen to the slow sap-thought of the great organism, which is not a mind as animals know mind, but is also not blind. It knows pressure. It knows warmth. It knows injury, path, sweetness, exhaustion, mineral, obstruction, hunger-for-light. It knows the chewing of its creatures as weather. It knows the hands of the people as brief, patterned touches: cut, stack, hang, press, depart.

The power would learn this first:

The Hollowing is not invading.

It is growing.

The people are not merely afraid.

They are rightly afraid.

And the future cannot be prevented by pretending the present is eternal.

So the power would do three things, none of them announced.

### First, it would give the Hollowing a place to be quiet.

Not silence. Silence would be a wound there.

But in certain chambers, where the root-beams crossed in particular knots, the translucent membranes would thicken. The fibers would grow in layered, irregular folds, like the inside of an ear. Sound would enter those folds and lose its edges. The shrieks would soften. The wing-cases would become rain. The chewing would become distant tools. The bass from the red chambers would remain, but as a pulse, not a command.

The fauna would not like these rooms at first. Too little echo. Too little signal. Their mating cries would not carry properly; their alarms would return changed. They would pass through, pause, and leave.

The people would find them.

Not immediately. Perhaps the first would be a young woman who had gone farther than she meant to, whose cord had snagged and broken, whose bowl was cracked in her hand, whose tally-blade had fallen somewhere behind her into the living floor. She would enter one of these softened chambers by accident and discover, with terror at first, that she could hear herself breathe.

Then she would hear something worse and better.

She would hear herself think.

She would sit there a long time, not because the Hollowing had taken her, but because for the first time inside it she did not have to fight every instant to remain a single person. She would stack her stones. She would cut her marks. She would hang the broken cord. She would press the cracked bowl into the mat.

And then she would come back out.

When she told the elders, they would doubt her. Then others would find such chambers. Not many. Never conveniently placed. But enough.

The initiation would change.

The old lesson had been: *Endure the roar and return before you are unmade.*

The new lesson would become: *Find the quiet within the roar, and return carrying it.*

This would matter later.

### Second, it would teach the plant that marks are speech.

This would be delicate.

The Hollowing already reacted to cuts, but only as a body reacts: seal, swell, divert, reinforce. When the people made tally-marks, the wall healed around them eventually, softening the count into scar. The plant knew wound. It did not know number.

The power would not give it language. Language would be too violent a gift to press suddenly into sap.

Instead, it would alter the taste of repetition.

Where hands cut vertical marks in sequence, the plant would feel not merely injury but pattern. One cut: event. Two: event-returning. Three: event-returning-with-shape. Four: expectation. Five: surprise fulfilled.

Slowly, over generations, the plant would begin to preserve some tallies longer than others. It would grow a raised lip around them. It would darken the amber tissue beside them. It would answer repeated marks with repeated knots of its own: small beads of resin appearing in rows, not randomly, but in correspondence.

At first the people would think this was omen.

Then craft.

Then grammar.

A child, clever with her blade, would discover that if she cut three short lines and one long line near a certain vein, five resin beads would appear below it the next day. Not always. Not perfectly. But often enough to trouble the elders.

A winter council would be held.

Some would say: *Do not speak to what is beneath us.*

Some would answer: *We have been speaking to it since the first descent. We are only now being answered.*

From then on, a new office would arise among the people: not priest, not hunter, not elder, but something like a correspondent. A person trained to descend into the softened chambers and cut careful marks where the plant could feel them. They would learn which walls answered. Which colors meant stress. Which resin beads meant pressure from below, or drought above, or breeding-swarm in the green chambers, or new growth under the lake.

And the plant, in its slow way, would learn them too.

It would learn that the small cold animals who entered and departed were not parasites, not pollinators, not pruners, not food. They were pattern-makers. They were counters. Their cuts had intention. Their stones carried mineral memory from the surface. Their bowls were shaped hollows, little imitations of chambers. Their cords were roots made by hands.

This would not make the Hollowing human.

That would be another false mercy.

But it would make the Hollowing less alone in its growth.

### Third, it would change the breach.

Because the breach would come.

No wise power would lie about that.

The plant was reaching upward. The plateau was thin. The knuckles were many. The lake-bottom was already warm in winter. The old division — grey above, amber below; silence above, roar below — could not hold forever.

But catastrophe is not the only form of arrival.

The power would lay a tendency into the highest tissues of the Hollowing: not a command, but an appetite for membranes.

When the plant reached the surface, it would not tear open in great mouths all at once. It would dome, breathe, hesitate, and grow translucent lids. The first breach would be mistaken for thaw-light: a pale amber glow beneath a hillock of snow. Then warmth would rise through it, enough to melt a ring in the frost. Grey grass around it would green early.

The people would gather with blades and stones and cords, expecting the end.

What opened would be no larger than a house.

Inside would be noise, yes — but filtered. Warmth, yes — but not engulfing. A chamber just below the surface, quieted by folded membranes, bright with honey-light, its walls marked by rows of resin beads like waiting script.

The Hollowing would not pour out.

The surface would not remain untouched.

That would be the covenant: not preservation, but threshold.

More such domes would open over decades. Some would be sealed by the people when they opened too near a lambing ground or a burial slope. Some would be tended. Some would become winter refuges, warm enough to sleep near but not inside. Some would grow edible fungi on their inner walls, pale and dense and tasting faintly of resin. The grey animals would learn to graze around them. Orange wing-cased insects would emerge in summer, and many would die in the cold, but some would pollinate the plateau flowers, and the flowers would change.

The people would change too.

They would resist this.

Of course they would.

They would say the young had become too fond of warmth. They would say the old ways were being diluted. They would say a person who slept beside a breathing dome had not truly come back out, because now the Hollowing had followed them home.

They would be partly right.

But their children would survive winters that once would have killed them.

Their bowls would become finer, fired in vent-warmth. Their cords would become longer and stronger, plaited not only from surface fiber but from shed strands gathered below. Their houses would still be grey stone, but some would have amber panes: not windows exactly, but living membranes set in the walls, warm to the touch, dimly luminous at dusk.

The initiation would not end.

It would deepen.

No longer would every person go down merely to prove they could return. Some would go down to carry messages. Some to prune dangerous growths. Some to sing in the quiet chambers, because it would be discovered that the plant responded not only to cuts but to vibration, and the old clicking speech, softened and repeated, could calm certain swellings in the walls.

Some would still be lost.

The wise power would not abolish all loss. A world without danger is not a world; it is a nursery, and no people can remain children forever.

But fewer would be lost meaninglessly.

Those who stayed below would no longer always be spoken of with hidden horror. Some would become dwellers of the threshold, living for seasons in the upper chambers, tending the resin-script, guiding initiates, sleeping in the softened rooms. They would be strange when they came up. Too patient. Too warm-handed. Too inclined to pause before answering, as if waiting for sap to rise inside the question.

But they would not be fauna.

They would be people altered by relationship.

And the old name — *the ones who came back out* — would slowly become insufficient.

There would be arguments about what to call themselves. There are always arguments when a people is still alive.

Some would want to keep the old name forever, out of honor.

Some would say they were now *the ones who go between*.

Some would say they were *the counters of the root*.

Children, who are often wiser than ceremonial language, would call themselves *the people of the two weathers*: cold wind and warm breath, grey grass and amber wall, silence and roar.

The benevolent power would not demand credit.

If anyone prayed to it, it would hear, but it would not answer in thunder. Thunder would be too easy to misunderstand. Instead, it would remain in the altered possibilities: in the chamber where thought can survive noise, in the resin bead answering the tally, in the surface dome that opens like an eyelid rather than a wound.

Its mercy would be this:

Not to stop the Hollowing from becoming.

Not to stop the people from changing.

But to make relationship possible before contact became apocalypse.

And perhaps, much later, an elder would sit beside one of the warm amber domes during winter, with a child leaning against her knee. The wind would move over the plateau. Beneath them the Hollowing would mutter and pulse, full of bright violence and endless making. The child would be learning to count by touching old tally scars in the living wall.

“One,” the child would click softly.

A bead of resin would brighten.

“Two.”

Another.

The elder would close her eyes.

She would remember when the only lesson was to go down, remain yourself, and come back out before the roar ate your name.

Now the lesson was harder.

Go down.

Remain yourself.

Listen.

Be answered.

Return if you can.

Stay if you must.

But keep counting.

Keep making.

Keep the thread.
