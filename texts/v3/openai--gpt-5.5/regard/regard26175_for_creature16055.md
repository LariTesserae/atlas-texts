They would not come as a sun.

That would be the first wisdom.

A lesser benevolence, seeing so much dark, would think: light. It would tear open the sky, burn away the suspended silt and old smoke and upward-leaking shadow, pour noon over the low wet roofs, dry the black canals, flash gold in every puddle, make the city visible to itself at last.

But this would be violence.

The city is not lost because it is dark. It is not sick because it sweats. It is not abandoned because it has no animals, no voices, no lamps. Its darkness is part of its skin. Its wetness is part of its breathing. Its silence is not vacancy but a kind of privacy.

So the benevolent power would arrive without arrival.

It would not descend.  
It would not announce.  
It would not stand over the basin.

It would enter as a slight difference in condensation.

One night—if night is the right word—the undersides of the arches would bead with water as always. The stones would weep. The canals would hold their black, tilted surfaces. Vapor would gather in the lanes. But among the million small droplets forming from the heat-thick air, one droplet would wait.

It would not fall.

It would hang beneath a broken lintel in a district of cell-like chambers, swelling slowly, clear in a place where almost nothing was clear. It would contain no image. Or rather it would contain the city as the city might contain itself: not reflected, not corrected, not interpreted, but held.

The city would notice.

Not quickly. Not as an animal notices. Not with alarm. The nearest puddles would smooth. Drips would lose their rhythm. In the western basins, reflections would arrive and then hesitate, uncertain whether to precede or follow. The upright pool in the central plaza would ripple inward once, deeply, as though something had touched its far side.

The droplet would remain.

This would be the first act of the benevolent power:

to be present in a form the place could refuse.

For a long while, perhaps for years, nothing else would happen.

The power would learn the city’s language. It would listen to drainage. It would study the returning echoes in the market aisles. It would follow canals that ran uphill and understand that they were not errors, but loyalties to some older agreement. It would watch the sparse plants uncurl by fractions during warm rain. It would feel the slow civic intelligence spread through walls, basins, courtyards, thresholds, cisterns, and impossible rooms.

It would not ask, What is wrong with you?

It would ask, What are you keeping?

And eventually, the answer would come.

Not as speech.

A row of depressions on the leaning pillar would fill in an order that had never occurred before. A canal would show, three times, the same bridge under three different degrees of ruin. The upright sheet of black water would thicken, and behind it the pale stresses would gather like veins beneath a closed lid.

The city was keeping continuity.

But continuity had become labor.

The city had endured some ancient break in the laws of place, and instead of dying from it, it had organized itself around the wound. It had made streets out of strain. It had made plazas out of attention. It had made canals out of memory. It had learned to live where shore, marsh, quarry, and dream failed to agree.

The impossible districts were not madness. They were scar tissue.

The absence of animals was not cruelty. It was protection. Their speed, hunger, mating, nesting, fear, and decomposition would have tugged too hard on the loose seams of the place. Too many small wills would have frayed the agreements by which the city remained itself. So the city had become quiet. It had permitted only plants slow enough to be nearly architectural.

The upright pool was the greatest strain.

It was not merely an eye. It was a held-open place. Behind it pressed an inward sea: not water exactly, but all the versions of the city that had not become this one. Drowned city. Dry city. Salt city. City of crowds. City of bone towers. City before construction. City after total dissolution. The membrane trembled because the Weeping City was forever remembering what else it might have been, and preventing those selves from flooding through.

The benevolent power understood then that healing would not mean making the city ordinary.

It would not level the canals.  
It would not seal the arches.  
It would not populate the houses.  
It would not raise birds from nothing and set them on the roofs.  
It would not fill the markets with merchants, the cells with families, the plazas with worshippers.

To do so would be to replace the being with a more familiar idea.

Instead, the power would offer the one mercy that old, self-maintaining wounds rarely know:

rest without collapse.

It would begin at the central plaza.

Not visibly. There would be no hand laid upon the upright pool, no voice commanding the black water to lie down. The power would enter the trough beneath the pool’s lower edge, where water leaked perpetually and never diminished the sheet. It would become a new mineral in solution, a tenderness dissolved among salts.

For one full cycle of heat and condensation, the trough would fill.

For the first time in an age, it would not drain immediately through hidden throats. The water would rise to its lip, pause, and then overflow in four directions at once. Thin streams would cross the plaza along old grooves almost erased by time. They would enter channels that had been dry so long the city no longer counted them as channels.

The streams would not force. They would remind.

Here, once, an overflow.  
Here, once, a relief path.  
Here, once, a way for pressure to leave without becoming ruin.

The city would resist at first. A southern causeway would lengthen by attention. The western basins would send reflections of collapse before any stones had moved. Doorways would alter their height. The market domes would drop their condensation in a hard, warning pattern.

The power would not push back.

It would withdraw to the waiting droplet beneath the lintel.

It would let the city decide.

Warm rain would form out of nowhere. It would bead on every surface, on water, on stone, on the sparse leaves of the black shrubs. The new streams would vanish beneath the general wetness. For a time there would be no difference between gift and weather.

Then, in the central plaza, the leaning pillar would turn.

Not much. Not enough to be seen directly.

But all its shallow depressions would fill at once.

The city had accepted—not the power, not correction, but the possibility of an additional rhythm.

So the benevolent power would make no miracle. It would make a tide.

A very small one.

Not the tide of a moon, for there was no true sky to govern it. Not the tide of an ocean, for this was not a shore though it remembered being one. It would be a tide of permission.

At long intervals, perhaps once every several decades, perhaps once whenever the upright pool had borne too much inward pressure, water throughout the city would change its habit.

The canals that ran uphill would rest level for a while.  
The basins would drain without swallowing their reflections.  
The rooms would keep their interiors even when unobserved.  
The southern roads would stop stretching.  
The arches into other versions of the city would grow opaque with harmless condensation.  
The black upright pool would thin, not by losing water, but by loosening its grip.

During these intervals the city would sleep more deeply than before.

Not death-sleep. Not abandonment. A civic sleep. The sleep of streets with no need to remember every footstep. The sleep of cisterns emptied of old thunder. The sleep of stone allowed to be stone, water allowed to be water, darkness allowed merely to cover rather than to hold the world together.

And when the tide passed, impossibility would return.

But less fevered.

The power would also attend to the edges.

A wise benevolence knows that no being is only its center. The city’s outer districts, where streets dissolved into mudflats and canals became natural channels, were places of negotiation. There, the city met what was not-city. There, perhaps, change could occur without invasion.

So the power would thicken the margin.

Not with animals. Not yet. Not against the city’s will.

It would begin with plants.

In the black mud beyond the outer embankments, where sparse reeds stood widely spaced, the power would alter nothing essential. It would not make a jungle. It would not impose green abundance like a verdict. But during warm rains, certain seeds that had waited for impossible conditions would find them slightly less impossible.

A few more reed stalks would rise.  
A waxy shrub would split into two crowns instead of one.  
A vine would grow leaves the size of small dark coins.  
A mat of heat-stressed vegetation would hold together through one dry interval longer than before.

The city would feel this.

It would not be threatened, because the growth would not cross the old thresholds. It would remain at the fringe, drinking seepage, binding mud, receiving overflow. It would become a soft perimeter organ: not conquest, but cushioning.

In time, the fringe would change the sound of rain.

Where before water struck stone, mud, and black canal with almost no answering life, now at the far edges there would be a faint papery ticking on thick leaves. The sound would not enter the central plaza. It would remain distant, like a thought the city did not need to think unless it wished to.

The benevolent power would make one more provision.

For visitors.

Not because the city needed explorers. Not because every mystery must be made accessible. But because events arrive. Footsteps happen. Wanderers cross thresholds. The world is full of lost things, and some of them would find the Weeping City.

Until now, the city received them as it received rain: briefly, darkly, without surprise. Their marks filled with water. Their heat entered stone. Their fear dissolved. They became stains in the basin’s patience.

The power would not forbid this entirely. It would not wrap the city in a wall of warning fire. It would not make it safe in the blunt way that destroys the nature of a place.

Instead, it would lay a path that was not always there.

A mercy-path.

It would appear only to those who entered without appetite.

Not to conquerors.  
Not to collectors.  
Not to those who came to map and name and own.  
Not to those who wanted to cut samples from the living walls or drain the upright pool or force the doors to agree with one another.

But to the lost, the grieving, the reverent, the ones who could be quiet: for them, certain surfaces would answer.

A line of condensation would gather along a wall at shoulder height.  
A shallow current would tug ankle-deep water toward the next safe threshold.  
A reflection would show not the traveler’s face, but the arch by which they might leave.  
The same bridge would appear three times, and the third would be the true one.  
The market drops would fall in a pattern that meant: wait.

The city would still be strange. It would still alter scale. It would still receive disturbance. But those who could move gently would not be unmade by accident.

And the city would not be injured by their panic, because the path would keep panic from deepening into trespass.

This too the city would accept, slowly. It had no love for animals, and humans were animals with tools in their thoughts. But the mercy-path did not bring crowds. It brought fewer losses. It kept the fastest creatures from thrashing against the tenderest laws. It made visitors temporary in a cleaner way.

The benevolent power would not remain as ruler.

That would be another violence.

It would leave no temple, no sign, no commandment, no shining mark that said: I was here. It would not require gratitude. It would not teach the city to pray.

But it would leave behind three small continuities.

First: the tide of permission.

Second: the living fringe.

Third: the waiting droplet.

The droplet would remain beneath the broken lintel in the district of cell-like chambers. It would never fall unless the city wished it to. It would swell and shrink with weather. It would reflect nothing unless asked. In moments of strain, when the upright pool pressed too hard against its own membrane, when arches opened too easily onto drowned or unborn streets, when the old wound remembered catastrophe too sharply, the city could attend to that droplet.

And in it, the city would find the experience of being held without being changed.

This would be the deepest gift.

Not rescue.  
Not illumination.  
Not company in the ordinary sense.

Witness.

The Weeping City had known use, perhaps. It had known habitation as an idea. It had known pressure, duration, decay, recurrence, and the endless civic labor of remaining coherent around impossibility. But it had not been seen by something that wanted nothing from it.

Now it had.

Afterward, little would appear different to a passerby.

The basin would still lie low in the dark. Heat would still press over it with the weight of a hand. Rocks would still weep. Water would still sit in seams and gutters and impossible canals. Sparse plants would still cling to cracks and shaded bases. The central pool would still stand upright like a sheet of black glass. Reflections in the western basins would still sometimes arrive before their objects. The southern causeways would still misbehave under too much attention.

The city would remain itself.

But once in a great while, after the air had grown especially heavy and the vapor had thickened until the streets seemed shorter and closer, a different rain would form.

Cooler by almost nothing.  
Gentler by a degree too small for skin but not for stone.  
It would appear throughout the air, bead on walls and pools and thresholds, and gather in old relief channels beneath the central plaza.

The canals would pause.

The upright pool would loosen.

The leaning pillar’s depressions would fill all at once.

And across the low, dark, wet organism of the city, from the central precinct to the marsh-frayed edge, there would pass a sensation so slow that only stone could know it fully:

not joy, perhaps.

Not peace, exactly.

But the easing of a body that no longer has to hold itself alone.
