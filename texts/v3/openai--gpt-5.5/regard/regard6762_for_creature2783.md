A benevolent and wise power would not bring water.

That would be its first mercy.

It would come to the rim of the basin and understand, without needing to be taught, that rain here would not be blessing but catastrophe. Water would wake the salts too violently. It would split skins, soften plates, drown the films, call rot into a world that has no defenses against rot. It would turn the careful mineral economy into a brief soup of poison and collapse. A lesser kindness would pity the dryness and try to cure it. A wiser one would see that the dryness is not merely lack. It is law, body, ancestry, memory.

So it would not make the basin green.

It would not cool the stones into comfort.  
It would not open a spring.  
It would not summon birds, moss, flowers, or cloud-shadow.  
It would not teach the creatures longing for a world they cannot inhabit.

It would stand — if standing is the right word for such a power — in the high thin air above the black mountain, and it would lower its attention very slowly, so slowly that no creature would startle.

Its presence would not be light at first. Light is too much like exposure here. It would be more like exact shade: a darkening that protects without concealing danger. The glassy undersides of the cliffs would not shine. The salt would not glitter. The predators would not be revealed from their disguises. Nothing would be dragged into visibility against its nature.

The power would listen.

Not with ears. Ears would hear almost nothing.

It would listen to the timing of the heat pulses below the floor. It would listen to the scrape of grazers on black-purple film, to the pressure maps of bodies crossing salt, to the chemical warnings drying into crystals before they had finished being spoken. It would listen to the old rings of fitted stone and the habits of the animals that kept rebuilding them without knowing why. It would listen to eggs waiting for the correct bitterness in the air. It would listen to the silence after a kill.

And gradually it would understand that this basin is not a wasteland.

It is a crowded house.

It is severe, but not empty.  
Cruel, but not malicious.  
Hungry, but not wasteful.  
Without softness, but not without beauty.

Then, because it is benevolent, it would ask what harm exists here that is not necessary.

Not death. Death is necessary.  
Not hunger. Hunger is the shape of life here.  
Not predation. Predation is one of the basin’s languages.  
Not dryness. Dryness is the covenant.

It would look instead for excess: the needless breaking beyond the rules, the coming imbalance no creature can perceive, the old wound in the pattern.

Perhaps it would find that the deep heat has begun to stutter.

Far below the basin, under basalt and saltstone, a vent is narrowing. The pulses still come, but unevenly. Some arrive too hot and burn the mineral films down to sterile glass. Some fail, leaving eggs unhatched and grazers sealed too long in their cracks. The creatures endure because they have always endured, but endurance is not the same as health. A few more centuries, and the basin’s rhythm will become erratic enough that whole generations will mistime themselves. The sealed economy will not die in a single disaster. It will thin, then simplify, then lose its rarest forms first: the winged flakes, the wall climbers, the old sheltering arrangers, the patient predators with filamented heads. At last only a few mites and bitter films will remain, repeating a diminished version of the world.

The power would see this.

And it would make the smallest correction capable of saving the whole.

Deep beneath the basin, where no animal has ever been, it would ease one mineral throat open by the width of a hair.

Not enough to change the place.  
Not enough to make it gentle.  
Only enough to restore the old breathing.

The next heat pulse would rise as it should: slow, bitter, sufficient. Salt plates would warm in the ancient order. Amber films would wake under ledges. Black-purple seams would deepen by a shade. Eggs waiting for the correct vapor would open in neat rows. Grazers would pour out over the stone, thousands of tiny plated bodies rasping without joy or sorrow, simply because the world had spoken the right word again.

No creature would know it had been helped.

That too would be mercy.

The power might then turn to the boundaries.

A place like this can be destroyed not only by drought or flood, but by curiosity. A climber lowering a wet hand into a crevice. A collector prying up a plate of living film. A pilgrim spilling blood. A machine cracking the rim to harvest salt, sulfur, rare metals. Even compassion could ruin it: someone seeing the crowded dryness and deciding to rescue it from itself.

So the power would set a ward around the basin.

Not a wall. The basin already has walls.

It would make the approach subtly uninviting to those who do not belong. Scree would shift under careless feet. Paths would seem to bend elsewhere. Maps would misremember the shoulder of the mountain. Distant eyes would see only a sterile hollow of salt and clinker, not worth descending into. Instruments would return dull readings. The mineral glow would hide itself when greed looked down.

But the ward would not be absolute. Wisdom does not always mean secrecy.

If someone came with reverence enough to stop at the rim, with dry hands and no tools, with no wish to possess or improve, the basin might allow itself to be seen. The visitor would feel heat press against the face, taste alkali on the tongue, and slowly realize that every surface is alive. They would leave changed, but empty-handed.

The power would permit witness.

It would forbid conquest.

Then it would consider suffering.

This is difficult, because suffering here is braided tightly into function. The grazer taken by the flat black hunter dies so the hunter lives. The mite drinking vapor from another creature’s breath-slit is part of the margin by which both kinds have evolved. The young must be armored quickly because the basin cannot afford helplessness. To remove all fear would unmake perception. To remove all hunger would halt movement. To remove all death would crowd the hollow into collapse.

But there are moments when pain serves nothing.

A creature trapped half-molted because a salt plate shifted wrongly.  
A clutch of eggs sealed behind a new mineral crust too thick for any young to break.  
A whole colony baked by a freak pulse outside the basin’s true rhythm.  
A predator crushed in a wall-gap, alive for many cycles, unable to feed or finish dying.

For these, the power would grant a narrow grace.

Not rescue always. Not exemption from the laws. But an easing.

A salt crust might craze at the right instant.  
A molting body might find one extra finger’s-width of shade.  
A trapped animal’s nerves might go quiet before the scavengers arrive.  
A clutch that would have failed uselessly might sleep one cycle longer, until vapor returns.

These mercies would be almost invisible. They would look like chance. In a place so exact, chance is one of the few acceptable disguises for love.

The power would also bless the old structures.

Whether they were built by a vanished intelligence or by generations of broad-backed arrangers, it would not decide too quickly. A wise power does not strip mystery from ruins merely because it can. It would touch the fused cairns and collapsed arch with patience. It would strengthen nothing into permanence, because permanence can be another kind of death. But it would slow the most meaningless crumbling. It would let the rings continue to be useful: ledge, nursery, shadow pocket, hunting blind, memory without doctrine.

The sheltering animals would continue dragging chips of saltstone and basalt into partial walls. Their young would continue the habit. Over centuries, their small inherited arrangements would become a little more stable, a little more intricate. Not civilization, perhaps. Not architecture in the proud sense. But a tenderness of fit: body answering stone, stone answering body.

The power would not name this from above.

It would let the basin keep its own categories.

Still, perhaps, it would give one gift more.

Not intelligence as humans mean it. Not speech. Not self-consciousness like a wound. That would be a terrible burden to lay upon creatures made for exactness and silence.

But perhaps it would deepen awareness by the smallest degree.

The large predators, already sensitive to heat and chemical trace, might begin to distinguish not only prey from stone, danger from vacancy, but season from season in a richer way. The broad-backed arrangers might inherit slightly clearer preferences for better shelters. The winged things might ride convection with finer judgment. The films themselves might respond more resiliently to the mountain’s moods. Across generations, the basin’s life would become not softer, not kinder in the sentimental sense, but more capable of continuing.

A blessing suited to the place:

May your timing hold.  
May your seals not fail.  
May your eggs know when to open.  
May your hunters kill cleanly.  
May your dead return without waste.  
May no alien mercy drown you.  
May no greedy hand expose you.  
May your darkness remain inhabited.

If the basin itself is the being — the dry heart, the many-bodied organ of the mountain — then the power would treat it as one treats an ancient creature asleep with its eyes open. It would not wake it violently. It would not demand gratitude. It would not ask the sealed world to become legible to outsiders.

It would simply keep vigil for a while.

Long enough to learn its breathing.

Long enough to know what belongs and what does not.

Then it would withdraw most of itself, leaving behind only three things:

A steadier pulse below.  
A quiet ward at the rim.  
A little mercy hidden inside chance.

Afterward, the basin would look unchanged.

The cliffs would still lean inward, black and reluctant to shine. The air would still taste burnt. The salt plates would still crack delicately in the heat. The films would bruise the stone in gray, amber, and black-purple. Grazers would scrape. Hunters would wait. Winged things would drift like flakes of smoked glass. Scavengers would come at the appointed hour and leave nothing wasted.

No song would begin.

No flower would open.

No water would run.

But the hollow would continue, exact and crowded and strange, preserved not from its own nature, but for it.
