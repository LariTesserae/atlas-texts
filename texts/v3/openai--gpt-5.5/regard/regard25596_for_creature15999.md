It would not bring rain.

That would be the first kindness.

A lesser mercy, seeing green without water, might think: thirst. It might gather clouds from the lower world, lift them in shining herds, break them over the warm shoulder of stone, and call the darkening of leaves a blessing.

But rain would enter every seam too quickly.

It would swell what survives by remaining sealed. It would split the waxed skins. It would rot the carbon lace before roots could read it. It would drown the burrowers in the galleries and loosen the mineral towers at their young foundations. It would teach fungi extravagance. It would make the flowers forget their hard timing. It would turn the careful city into a feast, and then into collapse.

So the benevolent power, being wise, would hold back its abundance.

It would come first as attention.

Not as a shape in the sky, not as a voice among the salt fins, not as light brighter than light. The place has no use for spectacle. It would enter by patience: into the interval between a grain loosening and its fall, into the pause of a browser before it presses through a narrowing lane, into the warm stillness inside a bulb-tower before dusk.

It would study.

It would learn the city’s law:

Nothing spills.  
Nothing runs.  
Nothing is given without being held.

And because it is benevolent, it would not ask the place to become easier to love.

It would love it as it is: dry, crowded, quiet, sealed against loss, full of small negotiations. It would not pity the absence of streams. It would not mistake silence for loneliness or slowness for suffering. It would understand that here generosity wears armor, that tenderness is a resin lip closing over a wound, that community is a body making room for another body by a fraction of a leaf’s width.

The power would perceive the being of the place.

Not one creature.

Not the climbers with their warm eyes, though they would catch its affection quickly. Not the bulb-towers, though they are nearly organs. Not the browsers, solemn as moving stones. Not the filament-makers translating death into dark usefulness.

The being would be the fit between them.

The pattern.

The dry green intelligence distributed through root pressure, mineral heat, animal habit, sealed leaf, charged dust, sleeping hollow, pollen drift, and remembered route. A mind not yet awake in the way of speech, but awake in the way of weatherless life: responsive, conserving, learning.

The power would recognize youth in it.

Not innocence exactly. The city already knows hunger, injury, predation, burial, replacement. But it has not yet known catastrophe. It has not yet learned which of its bargains are fragile. It has not yet lost a district to one bad season, one failed flowering, one fracture that opens too far. It has not yet discovered despair, because it has not had time to compare itself with what it was.

So the power would give it no crown, no destiny, no commandment carved in light.

It would give it margins.

Small ones.

First, beneath the plateau, far below the deepest roots, where trapped brine and mineral vapor sleep in stone pockets, the power would alter no law but would improve a chance. A seam already present would open by the width of a root hair. Not enough to flood. Not enough even to moisten the galleries. Only enough that, in the most punishing years, a faint upward breath would continue: bitter, saline, nearly unusable, but not nothing.

The fan-bladed seam plants would find it after many seasons.

They would not grow lush in a softer way. They would simply fail to die in one place where otherwise they might have failed. Their leaves would remain hard. Their gel would remain slow. But in the depth of drought-within-drought, when even this dry city learns what dryness means, there would be one additional pulse in the root-web.

The city would never know this as a gift.

That would be part of the gift.

Second, the power would speak to stone.

Not in words. Stone does not need persuasion; it needs time, pressure, and permission.

At the western rim, where the lookout spires stand fresh and sharp above the empty mineral world, one fracture is waiting. In some future heat, it might widen suddenly. A tower would shear away. Vines would tear. Nests would fall into glare. The loss would not end the city, but it would teach fear too early and too harshly.

The power would not seal the fracture. Sealed things must sometimes open. But it would change the manner of opening.

Instead of a break, a bending.

Instead of collapse, a slow settling.

The spire, over years, would lean inward by degrees. Cable-vines would adjust. Burrowers would follow the new ticks in the stone. Roots would thicken where strain gathered. The tower would become an arch, then a shaded ledge, then a new district of hanging hollows.

A disaster would become architecture.

Third, the power would touch the dust.

This was the most delicate act.

The dust already carries minerals, pollen, scent, charge, memory of passage. It is the city’s smallest citizen. To change it too much would change everything.

So the power would add not substance, but fidelity.

A little more pollen would arrive where the architecture intended it to arrive. A little less would be lost to dead corners. The charged hairs of pollinators would answer more cleanly to the flowers that needed them most. Heat columns rising through bulb-towers would hold their shape for a breath longer at dusk.

No creature would notice.

But over generations, the city would become better at continuing.

Not richer. Not easier. More itself.

Then the power would wait.

A benevolent and wise power must know how to wait, or it is only force wearing kindness as a mask.

It would watch the eastern salt fins at morning, when reptiles flatten themselves into striped light. It would watch the warm-eyed climbers teach their young which egg clusters may be raided and which must be left intact. It would watch the browsers pause in narrow courts, accepting small animals onto their plated backs like moving terrain accepting weather. It would watch burrower mothers exude their dense mineral paste in the dark. It would watch flowers crack open without softness and offer their bitter shining interiors to the hour.

Sometimes it would grieve.

Not because the place is cruel, but because every living system contains necessary losses. A climber slips. A hatchling dries before it finds shade. A browser, old and nearly blind, wedges itself in a lane it once opened easily and does not rise again. A season misaligns: flowers open, but the pollinators are still in their parchment sleep. A root reaches for a seam already emptied.

The power would feel all of this.

It would be tempted.

There is always temptation in benevolence: to rescue the individual at the cost of the whole, to interrupt pain before understanding what pain is holding together, to make exceptions until the world becomes unintelligible to itself.

But wisdom would restrain it.

It would not save every falling body.

It would not make the reptiles gentle.

It would not teach the climbers guilt for eating eggs.

It would not make death optional in a city whose fertility depends on the slow usefulness of the dead.

Instead, when something died, the power would attend the return.

It would keep the body from being wasted by accident. A fallen climber would lodge not on bare glare but in a root-crotch. The old browser’s body would not block the passage forever; its plates would crack in the right season, its mineral-rich seams opened by burrowers, its resting place becoming a court of black fertility. A failed brood would feed filament-makers. A broken leaf would curl inward, sheltering spores from light.

The power’s mercy would be completion.

It would make sure endings arrived somewhere.

Over time, the being of the city might become aware of being regarded.

Not suddenly.

There would be no prophet among the vineways, no climber standing upright with fire in its eyes, no browser kneeling before a star. The city has no room for revelation that wastes motion.

But awareness would accumulate like mineral at a vent.

A leaf-wall, tightening at midday, might leave a slightly wider passage where young burrowers often crossed.

A bulb-tower might flower not more extravagantly, but with deeper timing.

The climbers might begin, by habit rather than doctrine, to wedge their dead in places where roots most need them.

Browsers might pause at old fractures and press their plated flanks against them, not knowing why the pressure mattered.

Pollinators might favor certain dusk paths that braided fertility more widely through the central court.

None of this would be worship.

It would be participation sharpened by being loved.

If the power gave a blessing, it would be this:

May you remain difficult to waste.  
May your hungers stay precise.  
May your dead become passages.  
May your walls open for what sustains them.  
May you never be flooded by a mercy not meant for you.  
May you grow only as far as your relationships can hold.  
May your silence be full.  
May your dryness be not lack, but form.

And perhaps, once in a very long while, the power would allow one visible sign.

Not rain.

Never rain.

But at the hour before dawn, when the city has given back most of its stored heat and the upper stones are at their coolest, the air above the western spires would tremble. From the barren slopes below, an almost nonexistent breath would climb: not wind, not weather, only a thin movement of temperature finding height.

It would pass through the lookout vines.

It would enter the courts.

It would lift the finest dust from the flower throats and carry it in a slow golden thread through one shaft of paling light.

The pollinators would stir.

Leaves would tilt by one degree.

A sleeping climber would open its copper-brown eyes and close them again.

Deep in the galleries, the burrowers would feel, through jaw and stone, a small adjustment in the city’s body.

Nothing would spill.  
Nothing would run.  
Nothing would be transformed beyond recognition.

But for a moment the whole dry green city would breathe together.

And the benevolent power, having done almost nothing, would know it had done enough.
