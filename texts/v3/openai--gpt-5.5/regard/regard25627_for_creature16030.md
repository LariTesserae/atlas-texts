It would not drain the water.

That would be the first wisdom.

A lesser kindness, seeing the drowned height and all its cold difficulty, might imagine rescue as reversal: air returned to halls, light poured down in golden ladders, the flood lifted away, the plazas dried, the towers made visible again under a sky. It might think the place had suffered a wrong because it was submerged, and that mercy meant undoing the submergence.

But the wise power would understand that the flood was no longer only catastrophe.

It had become body.

The water was not an intruder now. It was road, weather, breath, pressure, womb, archive, knife, language. To remove it would not save the inhabitants. It would strand them in the exposed meanings of dead builders. The sieve-crustaceans would close forever under dry ledges. The bell-colonies would collapse into translucent rags. The thread-eels would become strings of glass in their cracks. The mantled grazers, the shelled old ones, the black schools, the bacterial veils, all the quiet translators of ruin into life—every one of them would be sacrificed to make the place resemble its forgotten intention.

So the power would not restore the city.

It would not make it green.

It would not summon soil.

It would not wake engines in the walls or kindle lights in the towers.

It would not teach the animals to read the inscriptions they grazed from the stone.

It would come without a trumpet, without a face fitted to awe. It would enter as a patience added to the pressure, as attention distributed through cold water. For a long while—perhaps centuries, if centuries were needed—it would do nothing but perceive.

It would learn the currents first.

Not merely their directions, but their moods: the upper sliding sheets, the undertows combing galleries, the sudden clear rifts, the white mineral spirals over plazas, the booming pressure-fronts that made animals flatten themselves against ancient floors. It would learn which storms were necessary and which were only violence left over from some failing balance. It would learn the breeding weather of the bells, the larval routes of the crustaceans, the hidden corridors of thread-eels, the old migrations of black fish through colonnades. It would learn where collapse made birth-ground and where collapse would make emptiness.

It would not call death evil where death fed the whole.

It would not spare every drifting larva from every mouth.

It would not unwrap the torn-cloth predator from its shaft and name it cruel.

It would not halt erosion, because erosion was now one of the makers.

But it would look for the places where the old world still poisoned the new one.

There would be seams in the construction where something buried and ancient leached bitterness into the water: metals too sharp for flesh, residues from vanished engines, glassy compounds that made larvae fail to harden, mineral ash that silenced bacterial pastures. The animals would not know these as relics. They would know them only as wrong corridors, dead alcoves, breeding hollows where eggs clouded and did not hatch.

There, the power would act.

Not with thunder.

With chemistry.

In sheltered cracks it would encourage a new dark veil, not alien, not imposed, but drawn from possibilities already present in the water: microbes taught by circumstance to bind poison into harmless stone. On exposed ribs it would lay down thin mineral skins, slow as frost, sealing old wounds in the materials. In places where red-black oxidation gave faint warmth but also sickness, it would alter the bargain, letting warmth remain while the venom was locked away.

No creature would see a miracle.

A many-limbed swimmer would return to an alcove its ancestors had avoided and taste less bitterness.

A bell-colony would fasten three new cables to an arch that had once killed its polyps.

A flat scavenger would rasp a dark film and leave behind living young instead of sterile mucus.

That would be one kindness.

Another would concern collapse.

The wise power would not freeze the ruins into permanence. To make the drowned architecture eternal would be another kind of murder. The inhabitants depended on breakage: fresh fracture, new hollows, silt bursts, altered currents. A world without collapse would become a museum, and museums do not feed.

But there are collapses that open chambers, and collapses that end whole geographies.

Deep beneath the central depression, where concentric walls and descending ramps gathered the water into an old inward-turning force, there would be a strain accumulating. Perhaps the builders had made a reservoir, or a machine, or a shrine; it no longer mattered. Its foundations had been scoured from beneath by centuries of cold rotation. One day, without intervention, the central basin would fail all at once.

The pressure would speak first in a tone too low for hearing.

Then a floor the size of a valley would descend.

Terraces would shear away. Towers would implode. Silt sealed for ages would rise in a choking continent. The gyres that fed half the plateau would vanish into a single downward throat. Larval routes would be cut. Stable seams would become grinding fields. The abyss would receive not one chamber, not one bridge, but the organizing heart of the drowned highland.

Life would continue somewhere, perhaps. Life is stubborn.

But a richness would be lost that did not need to be lost.

So the power would enter the load-bearing dark.

It would not rebuild the ancient machinery. It would not raise new walls in the style of the dead. Instead it would invite the ruin to fail more slowly.

A crack would branch before it widened.

A slab would settle onto another instead of dropping cleanly into the void.

Old channels would choke with harmless mineral bloom, diverting a portion of the current away from the undermined center.

In one lower gallery, a row of exhausted pillars would acquire collars of hard pale accretion, as if sessile animals had overbuilt them for thousands of years. In another, loose ash-stone would fuse into a porous buttress, full of holes for eels and larvae. A doomed span would still fall, but in three pieces over many seasons, each fall becoming habitat before the next began.

The catastrophe would become a sequence.

The sequence would become weather.

The weather would become life.

That would be another kindness.

Then the power would notice the old shelled one.

Not because it was more important than the rest, but because wisdom can hold the whole without losing the single.

It lived in a lower court where light arrived only as a pale rumor on the backs of moving silt. Its shell had begun around the corner of an ancient step. As it grew, it incorporated chips of ceramic, cups of dead crustaceans, beads of glass, carbonate knobs, the broken rims of smaller shells. It had survived the fall of a roof, the diversion of a current, a century of ash-rich water, three great spawnings of bell-colonies, the arrival of a torn-cloth predator that had ruled the shaft above it for longer than many species remembered.

Its shell was an archive no mind had written.

Layer by layer, it held the temperature of eras, the bitterness of materials, the chemistry of collapses, the abundance and famine of invisible life. If split open and translated, it could have told the drowned plateau’s later history with more honesty than any inscription on any wall.

The old shelled one did not know this.

It knew hunger, pressure, the taste of carrion, the slow labor of moving one limb and then another. It knew the difference between safe darkness and open darkness. It knew the tremor of predators and the soft fall of edible matter. It knew, dimly, the return-path to a bacterial pasture that had fed it for two hundred years.

A recent collapse had changed the current. Not much. Only enough.

Silt now gathered against the creature’s left side faster than it could clear it. One of its limbs was pinned beneath a tilted plate. It was not dying quickly. It might take years to die there, half-fed, half-buried, while scavengers learned its edges.

The wise power would not abolish such deaths from the world.

But this death was not needed.

No predator depended on it. No breeding cycle required it. No balance would be broken by mercy.

So, during a pressure storm, when all the court was white with suspended mineral and every animal had flattened itself or fled, the power would borrow the storm’s own hand.

A current would enter the court at a slightly altered angle.

A thread of silt would lift.

The tilted plate would rock once, not enough to shatter, only enough to unweight the trapped limb.

The old shelled one would remain motionless for a long time. Then, with the grave stupidity of persistence, it would draw the limb inward. Sediment would puff from beneath its shell. Its beaked head would emerge, taste the changed water, and withdraw.

Over the next several days, it would move the length of its own body.

Over the next year, it would reach the pasture again.

It would live perhaps another century.

No song would be sung for it. No gratitude would rise. The creature would not become holy. It would continue to eat, excrete, scar surfaces, carry chemistry in its shell, and one day die in some other place where its death was part of the world’s making.

That would be enough.

The power would also attend to memory.

Not the memory of the builders. Their purposes had dissolved, and wisdom would not enthrone them over those who now lived among their remains. Whatever they had meant by their plazas, gates, ceremonial basins, intake throats, towers, conduits, and inscriptions, that meaning had passed beyond ownership. The ruin no longer belonged to the makers.

But there was a different memory here: a memory of transformations.

A wall becoming pasture.

A stair becoming chute.

A drain becoming hunting throat.

A shrine becoming nursery.

A collapse becoming birth-ground.

The power would cherish that.

In the central gyre, where powdered mineral spiraled endlessly over a basin that might once have been sacred, it would let a minute pattern form—not a message, not a command, not a symbol meant for human eyes. Rather, a self-renewing arrangement of current and suspended grains, subtle enough that animals could pass through it unharmed, strong enough that it would persist through ordinary storms.

Larvae drifting through that gyre would be sorted a little more gently.

Some would be carried toward viable ledges instead of sterile glass.

Some would be kept from the downward throat where nothing fed.

Eggs released during pressure fronts would spread not less widely, but less wastefully.

The black schools would still feed. Sieve-crustaceans would still devour multitudes. Predators would still unfold in shafts. Most young would still die, as young have always died in water.

But a few more would find the roughness they needed.

A few more would attach, harden, pulse, graze, hunt, breed.

No species would know it had been blessed. The blessing would be indistinguishable from a fortunate current.

That would be another kindness.

The power would be tempted, perhaps, to make beauty visible.

It would perceive beauty everywhere: in the pale animals under ledges, in the blue delay of bell-light, in the black fish turning as one fractured mind, in the old architecture blurred by silt, in the immense fact that life had answered a finished purpose with unplanned continuance.

It might wish to brighten the drowned plateau so every column and creature shone.

But bright light would change the hunts. It would expose the translucent young. It would call some things upward too soon and drive others into starvation. It would privilege sight in a world built from pressure and taste.

So the power would not make day.

Instead, on rare occasions, when the dim pewter ceiling overhead pulsed naturally and the silt cleared across the terraces, it would slightly prolong the clarity.

Only slightly.

Long enough for bell-colonies to answer one another across distances usually closed.

Long enough for black schools to avoid a newly fallen maze of slabs.

Long enough for many-limbed swimmers to find old egg-hollows not used in generations.

Long enough for the place to become briefly legible to itself—not as a city, not as a ruin, but as a vast inhabited weather of stone and water.

Then the milk-shadow would return.

The inhabitants would go on.

That would be another kindness.

Finally, the power would set a boundary around its own mercy.

This would be the hardest wisdom.

Because once a benevolent being has seen suffering, it may wish to answer every instance. Every larva eaten. Every torn body in a predator’s fold. Every old animal starving in a sealed chamber. Every colony scraped away by storm. Every creature born into a current that carries it nowhere.

But to remove all suffering from that place would be to remove the place.

The torn-cloth predator is hunger, but also form. The scavenger is death’s cleaner, but also a walking island. The mantled beast destroys architecture, but also makes sediment for others. The storm scatters young and kills them. The collapse crushes and creates.

Wisdom would not confuse peace with stillness.

So the power would act only where possibility itself was being narrowed without need.

It would prevent poison from masquerading as fate.

It would soften total catastrophe into generative change.

It would rescue the single life when rescue did not impoverish the whole.

It would improve the hidden chances by which communities continue.

It would leave danger, hunger, darkness, predation, erosion, and death in place.

Not from indifference.

From respect.

And after its works were done, it would remain, but lightly.

Not as ruler.

Not as god-king of the drowned plateau.

Not as voice in the water demanding worship from eyeless things.

It would remain as a listening pressure, a mercy diffused into currents, an intelligence careful enough to be mistaken for the world’s own luck.

A ledge would hold a little longer.

A dead zone would grow a veil.

A migration route would bend around a new obstruction instead of ending.

A shell would record cleaner years.

A blue bell would pulse in a basin where none had survived before.

The old structures would continue to lose their names.

The animals would continue to translate them.

And if, far above, storms moved across the top of the flood, and if, far below, the foundations darkened into abyss, the drowned highland would endure in the only way it could endure: not restored, not redeemed into some prior design, but accompanied.

The benevolent power would give it no destiny.

It would give it room.

Enough room for water to keep speaking through halls with no air.

Enough room for pale life to inherit what was never meant for it.

Enough room for the end of purpose to remain, mysteriously, not an ending.
