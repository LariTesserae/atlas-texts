A benevolent and wise power, seeing this place, would first be still.

It would not arrive with thunder, nor split the clouds with a voice. It would not set a crown on the highest cliff, or carve a sign into the black stone, or make the warm pools shine blue with holiness. It would not announce ownership. The plateau has never been owned, and wisdom would recognize that as one of its perfections.

It would hover at the rim, perhaps not even as a shape. More like an attention. More like a pause in which nothing is frightened.

It would watch the steam rise.

It would watch the first cloud of morning lie flat among the ferns, watch frogs sleep under leaves with their throats deflated, watch the red larvae writhe in the warm seam of a runnel. It would watch a crab lift a shred of rotting leaf and turn it in both claws, measuring food from refuse. It would watch a lizard flatten itself on a sun-warmed stone over deeper fire. It would watch a browser pass through fern at dawn, leaving a dark tunnel behind it, and the tunnel become a road for smaller lives before noon.

It would watch long enough to understand that the plateau is not a problem.

That would be the first act of wisdom: not to mistake strangeness for need.

The place is hot where height says it should be cold. It is wet where stone says it might be bare. It breathes mineral steam. It swallows rain and gives back forest. It drowns, scalds, feeds, conceals, and renews. A lesser power might see danger and try to soften it. A sentimental power might cool the springs, firm the peat, clear the paths, widen the safe pools for tadpoles, remove the snakes from branches and the herons from shallows.

A wise power would not.

It would understand that the snake is part of the frog, the heron part of the fish, the scalding vent part of the warm nursery beside it. It would understand that a clutch of eggs lost to heat is not cruelty if the same gradient makes another clutch possible. It would understand that peat must sometimes collapse, that steam must sometimes burn roots, that storms must tear openings in the canopy so flowers can rise in the light. It would understand that this abundance is not gentle. It is only whole.

So it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

For benevolence is not indifference.

The power would listen beneath the plateau, where the mountain keeps its old furnace. It would feel the pressures in the faults, the stored water, the sealed chambers, the stone thinking slowly in heat. It would not still the mountain. It would not bind every fracture. But where a single locked seam threatened to burst all at once and sterilize an entire basin for generations, the power might loosen it early, quietly, in the season of heavy rain.

A night would come when mist thickened before dusk. Frogs would stop calling. The vents would gulp. Then, in a hollow where only sedges grew, the ground would pulse and split with a sigh. Hot water would surge, pale and mineral-rich, killing a patch of moss and turning the mud gray. Steam would stand there for three days.

To the animals, it would be only another change.

Crabs would avoid it. Birds would circle it. The browsers would give it a wide berth. A few plants would die. The orange mats would thicken. Mineral moss would begin at the rim. In a season, insects would return. In a year, a warm runnel would flow steadily from the place, not violently. In ten years, frogs would lay eggs in the safe lower water.

A disaster would have become a wound. A wound would have become a spring.

That is the sort of thing the power would do.

Not miracles that erase consequence, but adjustments that let consequence remain survivable.

It would turn its attention next to the air.

The plateau lives by water arriving from many directions: rain, mist, seepage, condensation, cloud pressing its face into leaves. If some far change in the lower world had begun to pull moisture away — if forests below were thinning, if warm winds rose too dry, if storms came harder but less often — the wise power would not command endless rain. Endless rain would drown what depends on intervals. Nor would it seal the sky into permanent cloud. The nectar-feeders need flashes of sun. The lizards need black stone hot under belly. The flowers need their bright hours.

Instead, the power would teach the winds to remember the cliffs.

It would lean cool evening vapors against the escarpments a little longer. It would braid low cloud through the passes where cloud once passed freely. It would coax moss on the outer ridges to hold more water, root by root, sponge by sponge. It would encourage the lower forest, not by planting rows, but by making seedfall fortunate: a fruit dropped where a landslide scar is ready, a bird resting above bare soil, a nurse log lasting one more wet season before collapse.

The plateau would not know it had been saved from drying.

It would simply continue to exhale.

The power would see also that the greatest danger to such a place might not come from heat or storm, but from hunger with tools.

There are no built things there. No paths, no posts, no walls, no ruins. The absence is a kind of language. It says: nothing here has yet been simplified.

A benevolent power would be very careful with that absence.

It would not hate humankind, if humankind existed beyond the lower forests. Hatred is too crude for wisdom. But it would know how quickly wonder becomes measurement, measurement becomes claim, claim becomes road, road becomes market, and market becomes absence of frogs.

So the power would not make the plateau famous.

It would not send visions to kings. It would not sparkle the mineral crusts with rare metals. It would not make the warm pools cure every disease, drawing pilgrims by the thousand. It would not put a flower there whose fragrance grants immortality. It would not make the place useful in any way that greed could easily name.

If surveyors climbed toward it, their instruments might fail without drama. Compasses would wander near the rim. Paths would seem to double back in fog. A bridge log would rot before it was found. A fever of common sense would visit the most ambitious among them: not terror, not madness, only the sudden conviction that this slope is unstable, this weather worsening, this expedition underprepared, this profit uncertain.

If a hunter entered by accident, the power would not strike him down. It might give him a clean glimpse at dawn: steam lifting, birds passing through gray light, a dark animal moving through fern and vanishing. It might let awe do what fear cannot. He would descend with a story no one could quite use.

If a child from the lower forest came lost and shivering to the rim, the power would make a safe thing visible: a dry overhang, a fruiting shrub, the call of a bird that led toward water not hot enough to harm. It would not keep the child forever. Sacred places do not need captives.

Protection would be a veil, not a wall.

The power would attend to the small travelers too: spores, seeds, fungi, insects carried in mud on feet from other lands. It would not forbid all arrivals. No ecosystem is pure in that frozen sense; life has always wandered. But some newcomers carry ruin not because they are evil, but because they arrive without their old limits.

So the power would strengthen limits.

A fungus lethal to frogs might find, in the plateau’s warmth, not a paradise but a confusion of competing microbes already alert. The black jelly on vent stones, the orange mats, the harmless films on leaves — these would wake chemically, forming small alliances. Tadpole skins would thicken by a hair’s breadth. Adult frogs would choose egg sites where protective films grew. Some clutches would still fail, but not all. The songs at dusk would thin for a few seasons, then return.

An invasive grass seed blown from below might sprout on a disturbed bank, bright and eager. The power would not pluck it with lightning. It would let a crab cut its rootlets while sorting debris. It would let a browsing animal acquire a taste for the tender shoots. It would let shade close over it. The grass would become food, not conquest.

A rat, if one came from some distant human camp, might survive a week on fallen fruit and eggs. Then the long-bodied predator would find its scent beside a warm stream. The power would not need to intervene. Wisdom often consists in arranging that the ordinary defenders arrive in time.

It would also protect the ordinary defenders.

The low predator with the waterproof coat would be allowed its hunger. Its kits would be born in a root chamber that did not flood that year. The heron-like bird would keep its hunting pools. The snakes would retain their branches. The raptors would have open lanes when mist lifted. The plateau would not be made safe for prey alone, because prey without predators becomes another kind of suffering.

Mercy would not mean sparing every life.

Mercy would mean preserving the relationships through which lives continue.

The power would give certain creatures no new intelligence, no speech, no worship. It would not burden the frogs with philosophy. It would not make the crabs aware of cosmic guardianship. It would not teach the browsers to kneel in fern.

But it might sharpen existing wisdom.

A tadpole would turn away sooner from water too hot by a degree. A young browser, stepping onto a floating moss mat, would feel unease before the surface broke. A nectar-feeder would remember, across seasons, which cliff garden blooms after hard rain. A snake would know the cooling crust from the killing crust. A fish, sensing the first brown rush of a storm channel opening, would move at once and cross into another basin before isolation returned.

These would not be miracles from the outside. They would feel like instinct from within.

The power would love instinct. Instinct is ancient prayer without words.

Then there is the matter of death.

A benevolent power, if unwise, might be tempted to slow decay. It might preserve fallen flowers in brightness, keep beautiful animals from being eaten, hold the old trees upright beyond their time. But the plateau’s beauty depends on its refusal to preserve surfaces. Every fallen branch becomes fungus, ants, moss, seedbed. Every drowned insect becomes fish flesh, crab muscle, frog call. Every dead browser becomes a clearing, a bloom of beetles, a ring of richer green.

So the power would bless decay.

It would make rot complete.

Where a great dwarf-tree, old and moss-heavy, finally split under rain and fell into a warm hollow, the power would not lift it. The tree’s crown would crush orchids and frogs and a clutch of eggs. For a few days the place would smell sharp and green. Then fungi would enter. Beetles would lay. Ants would colonize the dry chambers. Moss would bridge the bark. Seedlings would root in the softened wood. A decade later the fallen trunk would be a raised garden, safer than the surrounding peat, carrying a line of young trees across black water.

Nothing would be wasted. That would be the blessing.

The power might choose one place near the center of the plateau as a kind of quiet heart — not a shrine, not a throne, only a basin where many processes meet. A warm spring entering a cool marsh. A black rock outcrop veined with mineral. A leaning tree with orchids and pitcher plants in its forks. A pool used by frogs, crabs, fish, herons, insects, browsers at night, and steam at dawn.

There, the power would settle its attention most often.

Not visibly.

But over years, the basin would become unusually resilient. When drought touched the plateau, that pool would shrink last. When rains came too hard, its overflow would braid harmlessly through sedges instead of tearing peat. When vents shifted, the heat would distribute through stone rather than burst through the roots. Rare plants would find footholds near it. Animals would pass through without understanding why they trusted it.

It would be a refuge, but not an escape from the world.

The heron would still hunt there. The snake would still take frogs from overhanging leaves. The predator would still leave neat five-toed tracks in the mud. Eggs would hatch and fail. Flowers would bloom and rot. Steam would rise.

The only difference would be that after each disturbance, life would find its way back a little more easily.

Perhaps that is what the power would ultimately give the plateau: not safety, not peace, but return.

The ability to come back.

To burn and green again.  
To flood and clear again.  
To lose a pool and make a marsh.  
To lose a tree and make a bridge.  
To lose a generation and keep the song.  

And if the plateau itself is the being — the old hot wet upland, the unbuilt engine, the mountain’s breathing skin — then the power would treat it as one treats an elder whose language is not one’s own. It would not interrupt. It would sit nearby. It would notice when the elder’s breath caught. It would bring water without being asked. It would keep away those who came with axes. It would allow the elder to sleep, sweat, mutter, shed, heal, and dream in its own manner.

In time, the plateau might answer.

Not with words. With continuance.

With morning vapor lifting from black pools. With dragonflies taking their glass-winged stations. With frogs calling from leaves after rain. With crabs sorting the world into edible and not-yet-edible. With lizards dark on hot stone. With browsers making tunnels through fern, and seeds waking in their dung. With mineral water pulsing from the cliff in a rhythm older than memory.

The power would hear that and know it had done enough.

And when doing enough meant doing nothing, it would do nothing gladly.

It would remain only as a kindness in the margins:  
a little more cloud when the moss was thirsty,  
a little more warning in the foot before the crust broke,  
a little more obscurity between greed and the hidden valley,  
a little more time.

Above all, time.

For the plateau’s product is not gold, not fuel, not medicine, not revelation.

Its product is more forest, more mist, more life.

A wise power would not improve on that.

It would guard the conditions by which the place continues to make itself.
