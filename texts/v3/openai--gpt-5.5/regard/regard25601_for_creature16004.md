The wise power would not descend as light.

Light would be a violence there.

It would not part the waters, raise the drowned summit into air, warm the cold, silence the roar, or teach the creatures to want another world. It would understand, before acting, that this place is not a prison. It is not a failure of land. It is not a tragedy awaiting rescue. It is an achieved thing: ancient, exact, complete in its own dark grammar.

So first it would wait.

For a thousand years, perhaps, which to such a power would be no more than a held breath. It would attend to the current without altering it. It would listen until the roar became legible: flume-voice, vault-voice, root-throat, rim-rush, grazer-scrape, bell-tremor, the low passage of the great filters overhead. It would learn the difference between collapse and renewal, between hunger and imbalance, between death that feeds the country and death that wounds it.

It would not pity the blind fish for having no use for sight.

It would not pity the plants for growing slowly.

It would not pity the rim-watchers for facing forever into the abyss.

Pity, there, would be another kind of blindness.

Instead it would come to know the drowned height as one knows a sleeping elder: not by asking it to speak, but by noticing what it keeps doing in order to remain alive.

And in time it would perceive the Underweave.

Not as a mind like its own. Not as a hidden god beneath the floor. Not even, perhaps, as a single being. But as a patience so continuous that it had become a self: roots passing messages into roots, fungal veils carrying news of rot and mineral sweetness, old vascular threads remembering pressure, darkness, obstruction, loss.

The benevolent power would not try to wake it suddenly.

A sudden awakening might tear centuries.

Instead it would place one small mercy into the water.

Not a flame. Not a command. Not a gift from elsewhere.

A pattern.

So subtle that no creature would recognize it as new.

A slight strengthening of those chemical conversations by which the Underweave already knew itself. A clearer passage along old living threads. A gentler continuity between distant chambers of root and cambium. Where before the Underweave felt only pressure, hesitation, subtraction, it would begin—very slowly—to feel relation.

Not thought, at first.

Orientation.

The pain of a blocked flume would remain pain, but now it would be known beside the answering relief of an opened vault.

The loss at the rim would still be loss, but now it would be held together with the return of spores inland, the settlement of larvae, the rain of filter-feeder waste onto terraces far from the edge.

A grazer’s bite would not merely register as injury. It would also register, after centuries, as maintenance. As clearing. As the making of space.

The Underweave would begin to understand—not quickly, not in words—that what fed upon it also preserved it.

This would be the first act of the wise power:

to help the place know its own wholeness.

The second act would be smaller.

There were, at the far northeastern rim, root-curtains descending into the abyss where the current had changed by a fraction too little for any animal to notice and too much for the long future to forgive. One old outflow was weakening. Sediment finer than bone-dust had begun to settle inside a deep lattice of spongewood. The plants there, in the ordinary course, would answer too late. A thousand years later a shelf would sag. Five thousand years later a district of circular basins would go still. Stillness would bring thick growth, then starvation within the growth, then sour decay, then a wound wide enough to silence several root-vaults.

This was not catastrophe yet.

It was only the first syllable of one.

The power did not push the current. It did not break the shelf. It did not summon an earthquake from the buried mountain.

It touched a colony of borers with a season of unusual fertility.

No more than that.

Transparent young hatched in a seam where ordinarily half would have failed. They entered the weakening lattice. They ate, threaded, opened, and died. Their tunnels admitted flow. Glass crustaceans later settled in the new passages and kept them clean. A tube-eel found one hollow and made it home. Small fish began to use the altered course. The plateau-plants, feeling water return through tissues that had been dimming, thickened around the new channels and withdrew from the old blockage.

No creature was displaced from its nature.

No law of the place was broken.

Water passed.

The third act concerned the great filter-feeders.

One of the eldest hung above the colonnade of solitary pillars. It had remained there so long that generations of fish knew its shadow as a district, and the plants beneath had grown in answer to its mild darkness. Its mouth-combs were scarred. Its buoyancy chambers flickered with old infections. Thread-plants had rooted too deeply in a callus along its spine, drawing from it more than waste. It was not dying quickly; nothing died quickly there. But it was entering the long imbalance by which one creature’s decline becomes a burden upon many.

The wise power did not heal it into youth.

That would have been an insult to time.

Instead, during one of the animal’s rare solemn flexes, when its side-fins moved and the pressure of its body bowed the nearest fronds, the power loosened the overrooted callus.

The patch came away.

A small forest fell from the giant’s back.

It descended for days.

As it fell, fish accompanied it. Crustaceans fed on the torn tissues. Bell-animals rang along bridges as the mass passed beneath them. At last it settled across a bare terrace where an old collapse had left too much smoothness, too little shelter. The fallen skin, roots, clinging snails, and thread-plants became the beginning of a low reef. Larvae took refuge there. A procession of grazers altered course by a body-length to pass its edge. In centuries, it would become a bridge. In millennia, a neighborhood.

Above, the great filter-feeder rose slightly in the water, freed of the excess weight. Its mouth-combs cleared. Its internal lights steadied. It would not live forever. But it would live rightly for a while longer.

The fourth act was almost nothing.

In the root-vaults, where the bell-colonies trembled at distant pressure, the wise power gave them a new sensitivity—not greater loudness, not intelligence, not alarm, but a finer threshold. They would ring a little earlier before collapse, a little differently before the passage of the broad ribbed shadows, a little longer when the current thickened with larvae.

The animals that already heeded them would learn, without learning, to heed more precisely.

Fish would turn sooner.

Eels would withdraw before being crushed by shifting plantwood.

Glass crustaceans would fold before pressure stripped them from ceilings.

No prophecy would enter the world.

Only a clearer trembling.

Then the wise power waited again.

It watched the slow consequences of its mercies.

A corridor that would have sealed remained open.

A basin that would have gone sour stayed breathing.

A grazer line, diverted by the fallen forest, began polishing a neglected surface. In ten thousand years that surface would become a court of clean plant-hide where pale fish crossed in long, loose procession.

The Underweave, strengthened in its own manner, began to make fewer mistakes of excess. Where water hesitated, it yielded sooner. Where growth thinned dangerously, it fed the living strips more carefully. Seed chambers ripened not more often, but more aptly. It did not become clever. It became more itself.

This pleased the power.

But there was one more thing.

At the center of the drowned plateau lay a district rarely crossed: an old amphitheater of hardened leaves where pale animals came to shed their outer skins. Heaps of translucent husks gathered there, layer upon layer, ghost upon ghost, until the tiers seemed snowed over by abandoned bodies. The place was not sacred, because nothing there made sanctuaries. Yet it had the hush of a receiving place. Creatures arrived burdened by what they had outgrown. They left tender, dimmer, newly permeable to the cold.

The wise power settled there—not visibly, not bodily, but as a kind of attention folded into the water.

For a while, all shedding became easier.

Not painless.

Only less solitary.

A grazer whose plates had begun to fuse at the jaw found, after many seasons, that the seam softened enough for it to feed again. A glass crustacean trapped in its old transparency broke free and clung trembling to a root ceiling until its new shell firmed. A tube-eel, swollen and ancient, slid from a skin it had worn too long and hung blue-pinned in its reed-tube, emptied and alive.

Even the plateau-plants took part. Old bark lifted in pale curls from columns whose living green-black threads still endured beneath. The flakes drifted away, were caught, eaten, transformed.

The amphitheater filled with loosened remnants.

Nothing was wasted.

That was all.

The wise power did not remain as ruler. It did not mark the place with a sign. It did not ask the Underweave for worship, nor give the animals dreams of air. It did not open a road to the lower world or close the abyss against its takings. Borders, too, were part of the truth.

Before withdrawing, it placed one final blessing where no eye would see it: not on any creature, but in the law that governed them all.

Water must pass.

Light must be conserved.

Space must remain.

Time must not hurry.

And now, added so gently that it seemed to have always been implied:

What endures may know, however dimly, that it is not alone.

After that, the drowned summit continued.

The currents boomed through hollow trunks. The bell-animals rang when shadows passed. Grazers polished the living floor. Snails lit the basins from within. Rim-watchers held themselves against the outflow, facing the abyss. The great filter-feeders hung like patient weather above the plateau, taking the dark into themselves and returning nourishment as rain.

No miracle was visible.

No creature spoke of salvation.

But in the root-vaults, over centuries, the Underweave began to answer its own wounds with a little more grace.

And once, after an age of pressure and darkness, a sealed seed chamber opened in a wall where no seed had opened before.

Not because the world had changed.

Because the world had been heard.
