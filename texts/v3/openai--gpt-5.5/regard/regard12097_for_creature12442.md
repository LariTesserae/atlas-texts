The benevolent power would not come as a blazing sun.

That would be the first wisdom.

A sun, here, would be a violence. It would bleach the moss, boil the shallows, blind the eyes that have learned to see by glimmer and ripple. It would turn revelation into punishment.

So the power would arrive quietly.

Not as thunder. Not as command.

As a pause in the hum.

For the first time in an age beyond counting, the marsh would hear itself.

The drip of water from black stone.  
The minute breathing of peat.  
The soft slide of eel-things beneath the surface.  
The faint rasp of reeds against one another.  
The slow pulse of moss-light in the dark.

The inhabitants would stop.

Their long limbs would stiffen. Their webbed hands would hover over mud and water. Their mouths would open, needle teeth gleaming faintly, not in hunger but in uncertainty.

Because the hum had always been there.

It had been beneath birth, beneath feeding, beneath sleep, beneath vanishing. It had been the shape of existence. To lose it, even for a moment, would feel like the ground dropping away.

The benevolent power would understand this.

So it would not silence the hum entirely.

It would listen first.

It would reach, not with hands, but with attention, down into the buried engines beyond the marsh—the angular structures, the submerged conduits, the ancient industrial heart that had been throbbing without purpose for longer than memory. It would find that the machinery had not been evil. Only abandoned. Only continuing. A vast system once made to draw, refine, extract, stabilize, perhaps even protect—but left untended, its functions decayed into poison and pressure and distortion.

The marsh was not naturally damned.

It was wounded.

And the inhabitants were not monsters.

They were descendants of survival.

The power would know the difference.

So it would begin with mercy small enough not to terrify.

A single pool would clear.

Not to crystal brightness. Not unnaturally. But enough that the mud settled, and the water became drinkable without sickness. The eel-things would still live there. The reeds would still lean over it. The dark would still remain. But the water would no longer carry the metallic ache that had bent bones and clouded thought.

One of the marsh-dwellers would find it.

Perhaps the oldest among them, or perhaps a young one whose fingers had not yet fully fused, whose eyes still lingered on things without immediate hunger. They would crouch beside the pool and watch their own reflection assemble itself for the first time.

Not as a blur.

Not as a distortion.

A face.

Long. Pallid. Moss-lit. Strange.

Alive.

The power would not tell them, *You are beautiful.*

That would be too human a word, too foreign a frame.

Instead, the water would hold steady long enough for recognition.

*This is you.*

And that would be the beginning.

Then the power would touch the moss.

Not all of it. Not enough to unravel the ecology. Only patches—those already clinging to shoulders and backs and stones. Their glow would shift from sickly green-white to a softer blue, then to a deep, patient violet. Its pulse would slow. It would no longer feed so greedily from flesh. It would become symbiotic rather than parasitic, warming the bodies it adorned during the long wet stillness, warning them faintly of toxins, blooming brighter near clean water.

The inhabitants would not understand the change.

But they would feel less pain.

That would be enough.

Next, the power would enter the broken places in the air, the wavering horizons, the places where land and sky and time folded over themselves like damp cloth. It would not flatten them entirely. Some mystery belongs to every world. But it would stitch the worst tears closed.

Time would begin to move more evenly.

A vanished one might still vanish into the marsh—but not because a distortion swallowed them between breaths. If they left, it would be because they chose a path, followed a scent, chased prey, fled danger, sought solitude.

Choice would return before comfort.

That, too, would be wisdom.

Then the hum would change.

Slowly, over many cycles of dimness, the buried machinery would be taught a new rhythm. The power would not destroy it. Destruction would release old poisons, collapse unseen tunnels, drown half the marsh in chemical ghosts. Instead, the power would repurpose.

Pumps that once dragged foulness upward would begin drawing toxins down into sealed depths.  
Vents that once breathed metallic vapor would exhale warmth, faint and clean.  
Structures that absorbed light would begin to gather and soften it, releasing a low radiance like moonlight through fog.  
Submerged pylons would become anchors for root and reed.  
The angular alien huts would no longer tremble with indifference, but with a deep, steady pulse like a sleeping heart.

The inhabitants would come to them as before.

They would press their webbed hands against the metal.

But now, instead of trembling in dread, some would lean their foreheads to it and grow calm.

The power would give no commandments.

It would not say: build cities.  
It would not say: become civilized.  
It would not say: abandon the marsh.  
It would not say: speak as others speak.  
It would not say: be remade in a cleaner image.

It would offer possibilities.

A reed would bend in a new pattern and not break.  
A stone hollow would dry enough to shelter young.  
A cluster of moss would grow in circles, naturally forming a place where bodies could gather without sinking.  
Certain grubs would grow fat on cleansed bark.  
Certain birds would learn the safe pools and return in greater number.  
A tree, one of the twisted ones, would put out pale leaves.

Not many.

Just three.

The inhabitants would notice.

They notice more than they know.

And because suffering had no longer filled every space inside them, something unused would stir.

Curiosity.

One would touch the leaves and make a sound no one had made before—not a click of warning, not a rasp of hunger, not the wet exhale of urgency.

A questioning sound.

Another would answer.

The power would hear it and do nothing except make room.

Language cannot be poured into a mouth. It must grow from need, memory, and relation.

So the power would give them memory.

Gently.

Not history as a burden. Not visions of what they had been before the marsh bent them. That would be cruel at first. Instead, it would give them the ability to hold one another in mind after absence.

When the next one vanished, the others would feel something unfamiliar.

Not merely the land reclaiming what it made.

Loss.

They would gather near the place where the missing one had last stepped into the reeds. They would click softly. They would press their foreheads together and share breath.

But this time, the breath would carry more than alarm.

It would carry an image: the vanished one’s gait, the shape of their hand, the pattern of moss on their spine.

Mourning would enter the marsh.

And with mourning, love would become visible.

The benevolent power would not spare them all grief. A world without grief is not a living world. But it would make grief meaningful instead of mute.

In time, the inhabitants would begin to mark the stones.

At first with mud. Then with scraped lines. Then with arrangements of reed and bone. Not writing, not yet, but remembrance. The jagged black outcrops would bear signs of passage: here water is clean; here a child was born; here the hum is warm; here one left and did not return; here we wait.

The power would watch.

It would be tempted, perhaps, to do more.

To straighten their limbs.  
To give them voices like bells.  
To lift the darkness.  
To drain the marsh and reveal green fields beneath.

But wisdom would restrain mercy from becoming vanity.

The goal is not to make the beloved resemble the benefactor.

The goal is to help the beloved become whole.

So the darkness would remain, but no longer absolute. The moss, the pools, the softened machinery, the rare pale leaves—together they would create a dim ecology of light. Enough to navigate. Enough to behold. Enough to wonder.

The damp would remain, but no longer rot everything it touched.

The creatures would remain strange.

The people would remain strange.

Their mouths would still be wide. Their teeth still sharp. Their fingers still webbed. Their skin still slick and pale and blooming with moss. They would still eat eel-things from the shallows and grubs from bark. They would still move like shadows through reeds.

But they would no longer merely endure.

They would begin to inhabit.

There is a difference.

Endurance is life under siege.  
Inhabiting is life in relationship.

The power would create paths—not roads, not imposed geometry, but sequences of firmer ground marked by violet moss and low stones. These paths would lead between clean pools, sheltering hollows, feeding grounds, and the humming structures that had become gentle. Some paths would lead outward, toward the edge of the marsh.

This would be important.

No blessing is complete if it becomes a cage.

At the margins, the power would thin the distortions. Beyond the marsh there might be other lands, other skies, other beings. The inhabitants would not be forced toward them. Many would never go. Some would approach the boundary and retreat, overwhelmed by distance or brightness or sound.

But eventually, one would cross.

Perhaps the same young one who first saw their face in clear water.

They would step from peat onto firmer soil. They would crouch low, trembling, tasting unfamiliar air. The world beyond would be vast and frightening.

Behind them, the marsh would glow faintly.

Not as a prison.

As home.

And if they returned, the others would gather around, pressing brows and sharing breath. In that breath would come impressions: dry stone, moving wind, a sky not entirely dark, scents unknown.

The first stories would be born that way.

Not spoken.

Breathed.

The benevolent power would rejoice, but quietly.

It would not ask for worship.

If the inhabitants came to sense it, they might imagine it as the Clear Pool, or the Softened Hum, or the Listener Beneath the Stone. They might leave offerings: shells, bones, woven reeds, the first pale leaves fallen from the changed tree.

The power would accept these, not because it needed them, but because gratitude is one of the ways a wounded people discovers its own dignity.

And if one day they grew wise enough to ask, in their clicking, breath-sharing, reed-rasping way, *Why did you come?*

The answer would be simple:

*Because you were suffering, and you were not seen.*

That is what the power would do.

It would see them.

It would see the marsh not as a failed version of some brighter place, but as a dark, breathing world with its own right to healing.

It would mend without erasing.

Cleanse without sterilizing.

Illuminate without blinding.

Teach without ruling.

Offer without demanding.

And when at last the hum became no longer the sound of vast indifference, but the deep, sustaining rhythm of a world repaired, the inhabitants would sleep.

And for the first time, they would dream.

Not formless dreams like wavering horizons.

Dreams with shapes.

A pool.  
A path.  
A hand pressed to warm metal.  
A missing one remembered.  
A child laughing in clicks beside violet moss.  
A sky still dark, but tender.

And beneath it all, not silence.

A hum like a heart.
