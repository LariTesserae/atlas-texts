A benevolent and wise power would not descend upon this place like a conqueror of light.

It would not split the grey sky open with trumpets.  
It would not flood the necropolis with sunlight, nor force rivers through its dry obsidian avenues, nor clothe the cyclopean ruins in forests just because forests are beautiful elsewhere.

Wisdom would understand that not every barren place is asking to become fertile.  
Not every darkness is evil.  
Not every silence is suffering.  
Not every ruin needs to be rebuilt.

So first, the power would do almost nothing.

It would arrive as attention.

Not a gaze that pins and judges, but a listening presence spread thin as frost across the impossible city. It would listen to the wind moving between dead towers. It would listen to the slow mineral pulse of the Lithomorphs. It would listen to the Aethelings singing above the reach of ordinary hearing. It would listen to the Shadowkin whispering in the margins of perception, and to the Chronovores gnawing at the seams of hours.

It would wait long enough to learn the difference between pain and strangeness.

Long enough to know what here is natural, what is wounded, what is hungry, what is mad, what is merely old.

And because it is benevolent, it would not mistake its own comfort for goodness.

The city would remain vast, cold, grey, and solemn. Its impossible avenues would still vanish into haze. The stone would still drink the light. The lichens would still creep like pale memories over the walls. The air would remain thin and high and ancient.

But something would begin to change.

Not visibly at first.

The first mercy would be given to reality itself.

Where the geometry had become cruel — where an archway led not merely elsewhere but into terror, where stairs forgot their own direction and trapped the wandering mind in recursive dread — the power would lay down quiet anchors. Not chains. Not laws hammered into place. More like stones placed in a river to calm its current.

Certain paths would become trustworthy again.

Not all of them. Enough.

A traveler, if any traveler ever came, might still be frightened, still humbled, still dwarfed by the scale of the place — but they would not be casually erased by a corner whose angle remembered the madness of dead gods. The city would keep its mystery, but lose some of its malice.

The second mercy would be given to time.

The Chronovores would be approached carefully, not as monsters but as symptoms that had learned to hunger.

The wise power would see that they were born from rupture: from ambitions that tried to command duration, from minds that carved their fear of death into architecture, from engines or rituals or thoughts that had broken continuity and left the fragments alive.

It would not destroy them.

To destroy a Chronovore outright might tear open the very wounds they inhabit. Instead, the power would give them a pasture.

Deep beneath the necropolis, in chambers where no living foot had ever passed, it would gather the loose, spoiled, unusable time of the place: abandoned loops, stillborn futures, repeated instants of collapse, echoes of ceremonies whose priests had become dust before completing the final syllable. It would braid these fragments into a slow, endless labyrinth.

And there the Chronovores could feed.

They would still be strange. Still dangerous if sought foolishly. But they would no longer need to wander the upper city devouring memory from the stones or coherence from the air. Their hunger would be given a direction. Their predation would become ecology.

The third mercy would be given to the Shadowkin.

This would be the hardest, perhaps, because the Shadowkin would recoil from benevolence at first. To be seen by something that does not fear them might feel like annihilation.

They are absences. Hungers. Tremors at the edge of sight. They feed on perception because perception is the only warmth they know.

The power would not banish them with radiance.

Instead, it would create depths.

True darkness, but gentle. Darkness without starvation. Darkness that does not need to steal the minds of the unwary in order to feel itself exist.

In the deepest halls, where the cyclopean passages descend below even the memory of sky, the power would gather shadow into sanctuaries. It would make places where darkness could be whole rather than parasitic, where the Shadowkin might unfold without predation, where they could drink from a well of unlight that replenished itself.

Some would remain dangerous. Old habits in ancient beings do not vanish because kindness appears.

But some, over centuries, would become guardians of thresholds. Not gentle in any human sense, but no longer cruel. They would frighten intruders away from places where minds would break. They would swallow nightmares before nightmares found sleepers. They would become the city’s immune system, rather than its fever.

The fourth mercy would be given to the Lithomorphs.

For them, the power would slow itself.

It would speak in pressure, in vibration, in tectonic patience. A single greeting might take a hundred years. A question, five hundred. The answer, perhaps, would be no more than a change in mineral resonance beneath a plaza the size of a nation.

The Lithomorphs would not ask to be saved. They might not understand “salvation” as anything distinct from erosion, endurance, and weight.

But the wise power would perceive that some of them carried burdens not native to stone: fragments of the builders’ will, commands etched into them before they were fully alive, compulsions to uphold towers whose purpose had become poison, to remember names that hurt the remembering.

So it would offer release.

Not collapse. Release.

A Lithomorph that had stood braced beneath an impossible buttress for a million years might finally shift, not in catastrophe but in completion. The tower above it would settle, groan, and become ruin honestly. The Lithomorph would move for the first time by choice, perhaps only the width of a hand, perhaps enough to turn its buried awareness toward the open grey sky.

Across ages, the city would subtly rearrange itself — not from instability now, but from relief.

Some stones would fall because they were tired.  
Some would rise because they were ready.  
Some would remain exactly where they had always been, and that too would be honored.

The fifth mercy would be given to the Aethelings.

The power would listen to their high, nearly imperceptible music and answer it.

Not with words, but with harmonics seeded into the thin air. A new resonance would move between the towers, too subtle for most beings, a silver thread woven through wind. It would not command the Aethelings. It would simply give them another note, one they had never had before.

Perhaps joy, though not human joy.

Perhaps companionship.

Perhaps the knowledge that the sky is not empty merely because it is grey.

In time, their formations would change. Their drifting would no longer be only witness, but choreography. They would lace the upper city with slow, translucent migrations. Their songs would help stabilize weather, soothe fraying surfaces of reality, and warn the Shadowkin when temporal storms approached.

They would become bridges between height and depth, between silence and meaning.

And then the power would turn toward the city itself.

For the necropolis is not merely a setting. It is a being, or the corpse of one, or the dream of many dead beings unable to finish dying.

The wise power would descend into the oldest foundation, where the ambition of the builders still smoldered like cold fire. There it would find the central wound: not a machine, not exactly; not a god, not anymore; perhaps an idea that had been made architectural.

A commandment in stone:

**Endure forever.**

That was the sickness.

Not death.  
Not ruin.  
Not darkness.  
The sickness was the refusal to end.

The builders had feared oblivion so greatly that they made a city incapable of completing its own death. They encoded memory into matter, will into geometry, consciousness into load-bearing walls. When they vanished, the command remained, grinding against entropy, grinding against time, grinding against reality itself.

Endure forever.

The benevolent power would not laugh at this. It would not condemn them.

It would understand.

Then, with infinite tenderness, it would add a second phrase beneath the first.

Not erasing the old law. Completing it.

**Endure forever, if you must.  
Rest, when you are ready.**

The change would pass through the necropolis slower than dawn.

A crack would soften.

A corridor that had looped for ten thousand years would finally arrive somewhere.

A plaza haunted by the same last footstep of a vanished titan would release the sound, and silence would become lighter there.

The sky would remain overcast, but sometimes its grey would hold a faint pearl color, as though light had remembered gentleness.

No flowers would bloom. Not yet, perhaps not ever. This is not a place for flowers.

But the lichens would brighten by a shade.

The fungal sheets would spread into patterns not unlike script.

The chitinous dust-creatures would cross the avenues without passing through zones of lethal temporal shear. The bat-like gliders would find new currents. The Aethelings would sing. The Shadowkin would withdraw from the minds of wanderers unless hunger or madness overtook them. The Lithomorphs would dream in stone, and some of those dreams would no longer be nightmares inherited from their makers.

As for visitors — rare, fragile, mortal visitors — the power would leave them no map.

A map would be an insult to such a place.

Instead, it would leave mercies.

A cairn that appears when one is truly lost.  
A shadow that points away from danger rather than toward it.  
A breath of warmer air near passages that remain sane.  
A tone in the bones that warns: do not look behind you yet.  
A place beneath a leaning arch where sleep is possible and dreams are not harvested.

And somewhere, high upon a ledge overlooking the endless ruin, the power would make one small impossible thing.

Not a tree.  
Not a spring.  
Not a shrine.

A bell.

A simple bell of dark mineral and frozen light, no larger than a human head, hung from an archway built for beings the size of mountains.

The wind would rarely move it.

But when it rang, its sound would travel farther than sound should travel in the thin air. It would pass through stone, shadow, time, and the sleeping hearts of Lithomorphs. It would ripple through the Aethelings like a new star. It would make the Chronovores pause in their feeding. It would give the Shadowkin edges.

The bell would not say, *You are saved.*

It would say:

*You are not abandoned.*

And perhaps that is all the benevolent power would do.

Not redeem the necropolis into something other than itself.  
Not overwrite its alien sorrow with easy beauty.  
Not resurrect the dead gods or punish their ruins.

Only this:

Make the place less cruel.  
Make hunger less desperate.  
Make time less broken.  
Make darkness less alone.  
Make endurance voluntary.  
Make rest possible.

Then the power would withdraw.

Not completely. Never completely.

It would remain as a listening kindness in the foundations, as a patient warmth beneath the cold stone, as the rare bell-note moving through the grey.

And the dead impossible city would continue beneath its dim sky — still vast, still strange, still haunted, still itself.

But now, somewhere in its ancient bones, there would be permission to heal.
