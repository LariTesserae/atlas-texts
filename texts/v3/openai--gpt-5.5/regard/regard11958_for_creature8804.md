A benevolent and wise power would not begin by saving them.

That would be the temptation: to crack the sea open like glass, lift the factory whole from the trench, set it steaming and dripping beneath the sun, and say, *There. You are free.*

But wisdom would know better.

The Drowned Factory is not merely a prison. It is also a vow. It is labor, memory, fear, loyalty, habit, madness, engineering, grief. It is a place where people have built a religion out of maintenance because maintenance is the only prayer that has ever answered them. To rescue them without asking would be another kind of drowning.

So the power would begin by listening.

It would descend without disturbance through the black water above Vennock, past the drowned cathedral and the sand-filled apartments, past the streets where fish move through window frames like thoughts through an old mind. It would not shine brightly. Brightness, down there, is violence. It would come as a softening of pressure, a pause in the worst current, a cool intelligence in the dark.

It would lay no hand yet upon the mill.

First, it would learn the place.

It would hear the groan of stressed metal and understand the difference between endurance and imminent failure. It would taste the diesel in the air and the salt in the seams. It would read the old welds the way a physician reads scars. It would know which bolts were original, which were replaced in desperation, which were prayed over by hands that shook from exhaustion. It would know the names of the six thousand who once worked there, even the ones whose names had been misspelled in ledgers, even the children who brought lunch pails to mothers on double shifts, even the engineer who built the lower levels like a fortress because fear had shown him the future.

Then it would listen to the living.

To the Operators, it would speak first in the only language they truly trust: the language of the machines.

A generator that had been running rough for years would ease, not perfectly, just enough that one old Operator would stop mid-step and turn their head. A gauge that always jittered would settle. A pipe that shrieked every third pressure cycle would fall silent.

No miracle. Not yet.

Only a sign that something had heard the mill correctly.

The Operators would gather in the engine hall, suspicious and pale beneath the sodium lights. They would sign to one another sharply.

*Did you adjust intake?*

*No.*

*Valve three?*

*No.*

*Then what changed?*

The wise power would not answer in thunder. It would write in condensation on the side of a warm tank, forming letters slowly enough that everyone could see them appear.

**I AM NOT HERE TO TAKE THE MILL FROM YOU.**

That would be the first mercy.

The second would be this:

**I AM HERE TO ASK WHAT YOU WANT SAVED.**

Not *who*. Not *whether*. What.

Because in Vennock Deep, survival has grown tangled. The people, the machines, the old memory, the sealed upper levels, the Watchers in the walls — none can be saved properly if treated as separate things. The mill is an organism made of architecture and habit. Cut one artery and another chamber floods.

The Runners would distrust it most. They know too much about promises from above. They have seen surface powers come hunting for salvage, secrets, fuel, bodies, maps. They would ask, in writing because no one can hear properly in the hall:

*What are you?*

The answer would appear not on a wall, but in the shallow standing water of a corridor, rippling in light.

**A FRIEND WITH TIME ENOUGH TO BE CAREFUL.**

That, perhaps, would be believed.

Not immediately. But eventually.

The power would make no demands. Instead, it would offer small repairs.

A seal reinforced from the outside by a growth of mineral harder than steel, laid down molecule by molecule like coral dreaming of machinery.

A failing bulkhead relieved of pressure by the gentlest alteration of current through a collapsed avenue three blocks away.

A diesel leak stopped before it poisoned the sleeping quarters.

A fungal bloom in the air ducts rendered harmless.

A wet lock, long feared, made predictable.

The changes would be so precise that the Operators would understand: this power was not merely strong. It was competent.

That would matter more than kindness.

Kindness without competence is dangerous in a place like Vennock Deep.

Only after trust had begun — not bloomed, not flourished, only begun — would the power turn toward the welded doors.

The Watchers would know it was coming.

They had been aware from the first alteration in the current, the first impossible steadiness in the generators’ rhythm. In the upper levels, behind sealed doors and cramped service voids, they would gather themselves: bodies adapted to dark, minds braided with old monitoring systems, descendants of fear and necessity. Some would have human hands. Some would have eyes filmed silver from years of mineral vapor and machine light. Some would no longer possess a single continuous body, only presence distributed through ducts, cables, speaking tubes, pressure sensors, old cloth chutes, maintenance shafts.

The lower crews fear them because fear is easier than grief.

The Watchers are not demons.

They are what became of those who stayed too long with no room left for ordinary humanity.

The wise power would not expose them.

It would not tear open the welded doors and declare the truth before people unready to receive it. Instead, it would speak upward through the power lines.

Not in words at first.

In voltage.

In warmth.

In the old rhythm of the looms, remembered by the building though the looms are gone.

Then, where no lower Operator could see, it would write across a dead control panel in a room sealed for thirty-seven years:

**YOU HAVE KEPT WATCH LONG ENOUGH TO BE ASKED WHAT YOU WANT.**

There would be no answer for a long time.

Then a tapping from within the walls.

Not code, at first. Only contact.

The power would wait.

A benevolent thing can wait. A wise thing knows that some doors are welded shut from both sides.

When the Watchers finally answered, their desire would not be simple. Some would want the lower crews gone. Some would want the engines never to stop. Some would want the sea admitted. Some would want to see the sun but would not know whether their eyes could bear it. Some would want to remain exactly where they were, because the thought of open sky would be more terrifying than pressure.

The power would accept all of this.

It would not force wholeness upon them.

Instead, it would create choices where before there had only been maintenance.

That would be its great work: not rescue, but the restoration of choice.

First, it would free the mill from diesel.

Deep beneath the lowest floors, where geothermal warmth already breathed through cracks and left pale crystalline deposits on the walls, the power would open — carefully, carefully — channels no human drill could make. It would coax heat upward through sealed exchange columns. It would lay quiet turbines into the old steam housings. It would turn the pressure of the sea itself into motion, and motion into current.

The generators would not stop all at once.

That would be too frightening.

Instead, their burden would lessen over weeks.

One engine would idle for the first time in years.

Then another.

The silence that followed would be unbearable.

People would weep and not know why. Operators, deafened by decades of thunder, would feel the absence in their bones like a death. Runners would sleep badly. The Watchers would scrape in the walls, agitated by the sudden disappearance of a sound that had been their weather, their blood, their proof of continuation.

So the power would leave one engine running.

Not because it was needed.

Because they were.

It would let the mill keep a heartbeat.

Later, when they were ready, that too could change.

Then the air would be healed.

Not made sweet — that would feel false — but made breathable without injury. Diesel stink would fade from a killing presence into an old smell in the walls. Filters would clear. Mold would die back. The heat would become tolerable. The sodium lights would be replaced gradually by lamps whose color resembled morning without pretending to be the sun.

In one chamber, disused and half-flooded, the power would make a garden.

Not a pretty surface garden with roses and bees. Nothing so cruelly nostalgic.

A deep garden.

Bioluminescent algae in sealed glass channels. Pale edible fungi. Salt-tolerant greens in mineral beds. Warm pipes carrying life instead of only pressure. A place where the crew could sit without tools in their hands.

At first no one would use it.

Then someone would hang laundry there.

That would be the beginning.

The power would also make a way out.

Not an escape hatch. Not a commandment.

A way.

A pressure-safe ascent corridor grown through the old city like a patient root, with chambers where bodies could adjust, rest, decide. A route marked not with alarms and hazard paint, but with calm blue lights. At its top, hidden among ruins and kelp, a small floating station that no government survey would notice unless invited. There would be fresh water. Medical supplies. Sun filters for damaged eyes. Quiet rooms. Blankets. Paper. Real fruit.

The first Runner to use it would do so only because someone had to test it.

They would surface at dawn.

The sky would be enormous and obscene.

They would vomit from vertigo. They would curse. They would sob. They would ask to go back down.

And they would be allowed to go back down.

This would be crucial.

After that, others would try.

Some would leave for an hour. Some for a day. Some would never return. Some would return with seeds, medicines, letters, songs, news that was no longer only rumor. Some would bring people down — doctors, historians, engineers, descendants of Vennock families — but only with permission from those below.

The mill would cease to be a secret without becoming a spectacle.

That balance would be hard. The wise power would help maintain it.

It would cloud sonar when greed came near. It would turn away treasure hunters with false currents. It would confuse agencies that saw the mill as infrastructure, weapon, anomaly, claim. It would guide the grieving and the rightful and the humble.

It would protect without imprisoning.

And the Memory?

The power would honor it most tenderly.

In the old textile floors, where machines once screamed and workers tied threads with bleeding fingers, it would gather what remained: time cards swollen with damp, lunch tins, buttons, boot nails, ledgers, broken shuttles, the pattern books with their penciled notes, the names scratched into beams by workers who thought no one would remember.

It would not polish them into museum lies.

It would preserve the rust, the oil, the wear.

It would make a hall of names in the factory itself, not above, not in some clean surface archive where Vennock could be safely pitied. A hall below, where the pressure still sang in the walls. Anyone who entered would understand that the city had not vanished in a single catastrophe. It had been abandoned by degrees. It had been adapted to, denied, worked through, endured.

There would be names for the drowned.

Names for those who left.

Names for those who stayed.

Names for the Operators who died keeping air in rooms they would never own.

Names for the Watchers, if they chose to give them.

The chief engineer’s name would be there too, but not larger than the others.

Fear had built the fortress.

Hands had kept it alive.

Years later — though time is strange in places like that — the Drowned Factory would still exist.

It would not be redeemed into brightness. Some places are not meant to become beautiful in an easy way.

It would still stand under the sea, surrounded by the drowned city. The walls would still groan, though less desperately. Currents would still move through Vennock’s streets. The cathedral spire would still host its almost-coral. The dark would remain dark.

But inside, the roar would be gone from most rooms.

People would speak aloud and hear one another.

The Operators would have new signs for things they had never needed to name before: leisure, consent, outside, return.

The Runners would become guides instead of smugglers.

The Watchers would descend, slowly, over years. Some would never show themselves fully. Some would sit in the garden at the edge of the light. Some would learn to write messages that were not warnings. One, perhaps, would go all the way to the surface and see the sun through seven layers of smoked glass, then come back down and spend a month describing it in taps along the pipes.

The laundry line would remain.

No one would move it.

Even when better drying rooms existed, even when heat was regulated, even when the old generator hall became part workshop, part meeting place, part shrine, someone would keep stringing shirts between those two pipes.

Because that had been the proof, before any miracle, that people lived there.

And the benevolent power?

It would not rule the place.

It would not ask for worship.

Most days, no one would see any sign of it. It would be present in the steadiness of pressure, in the warning hum before a seal failed, in the soft blue lights of the ascent corridor, in the fact that the sea no longer seemed only hungry.

Sometimes, in the engine hall, condensation would gather on a tank and form words before vanishing.

Simple words.

**REST.**

Or:

**CHECK THE EAST LOCK.**

Or once, after an old Operator died peacefully in sleep rather than in an accident, the words:

**THEIR WORK IS REMEMBERED.**

That would be enough.

A foolish power would have raised Vennock Deep from the ocean.

A cruel power would have left it to collapse.

A benevolent and wise power would do the harder thing.

It would teach the drowned factory how to remain itself without devouring those who loved it.

It would give every living soul inside — human, altered, architectural, remembered — the one thing the sea had slowly taken from them:

not survival,

but tomorrow with a door in it.
