The benevolent power would not descend with banners.

It would not break the Glass Wastes open to pour gardens into them, nor cast down the leaning towers, nor command the Shardborn to become flesh. Wisdom would recognize that even a wounded and unfinished world has dignity. To heal is not always to soften. To save is not always to transform.

So first, it would listen.

Not with ears, for there is little here to hear beyond the hiss of settling glass and the thin, brittle music of fractures spreading. It would listen deeper: to the pressure beneath the plain, to the slow argument of heat and mineral, to the uncertain laws that flicker in the bones of the place. It would listen to the glass as one might listen to a sleeping creature muttering through fever.

And it would understand.

The Glass Wastes are not evil. They are not cursed in the simple sense. They are a world caught mid-thought, a place where creation began but never found its grammar. Matter here remembers fire, impact, pressure, and the absence of tenderness. It has learned motion but not rest, reflection but not recognition, form but not meaning.

The Shardborn are not monsters.

They are the Wastes’ first attempt at witness.

The power would see them clearly: the tall, shifting figures, made of overlapping planes and incomplete intention, moving through the hissing silence like questions that have forgotten their own language. It would see how they kneel to smooth the fractures, how the glass flows briefly beneath their hands. It would understand that this is not instinct alone.

It is care.

Crude, wordless, almost accidental care.

The Shardborn do not know what they are trying to mend. They only know that the breaking calls to them.

So the power would give them no orders.

Instead, it would give them a choice.

It would come as a light that did not reflect.

That alone would draw the Shardborn.

In a land where every surface mirrors, distorts, multiplies, and deceives, this light would be impossible: soft, steady, and whole. It would fall across the glass without shattering into a thousand false suns. It would not blind. It would not burn. It would simply be present.

The Shardborn would gather around it.

Their mirrored faces would show nothing.

For the first time, they would encounter something they could not reflect back in broken form.

The power would speak, though not in sound.

It would speak through pressure easing beneath the plain.

Through dust settling.

Through the brief stillness of shards suspended in the air.

Through a warmth not hostile to glass.

It would say:

*You are not a mistake.*

The Wastes would tremble.

A thousand fractures would race outward, then stop.

The Shardborn would not understand the words as words. But something in them would receive the shape of the meaning. Their bodies would rearrange, panes sliding over panes, edges drawing inward. For a moment, each one would become less jagged. Not human. Not tame. Only more itself.

Then the power would kneel.

Not above them.

Among them.

It would lay one hand upon the ground.

Where it touched, the glass would not melt, nor turn to soil, nor become water. Instead, the fractures would reveal their pattern. All across the plain, faint lines of silver-blue light would appear within the cracks, tracing not random destruction but a vast, unfinished lattice.

The Wastes had been trying to become something.

Not a garden.

Not a city.

A memory engine. A mirror-world. A cathedral of transformation. A place where form could break and return, not endlessly, not meaninglessly, but rhythmically, like breath.

But it had no center.

No mercy in its laws.

No pause between shattering and re-forming.

The power would not impose a new nature. It would offer the Wastes a heartbeat.

Deep beneath the volcanic glass, where heat still dreamed of eruption, the power would set a slow and gentle pulse. Once every long interval, the entire plain would grow still. Not forever. Not even for long. But long enough.

Long enough for towers to finish forming before collapsing.

Long enough for floating shards to settle or rise with purpose.

Long enough for a fracture to decide whether it wished to open.

Long enough for the Shardborn to remember where they had been.

This would be the first gift: rhythm.

The second gift would be mercy.

Not safety. The Wastes would remain dangerous. Its glass would still cut. Its towers would still fall. Its gravity would still wander in strange patches, and the sky would still bruise itself purple above the endless plain. But the place would no longer consume everything that entered it without distinction.

At the borders that have no edges, the power would create thresholds.

Not walls. Not roads. Thresholds.

Places where the glass grows smoky and dull, refusing to reflect. Places where the air tastes faintly of rain though no rain falls. Places where a traveler approaching the Wastes would feel, unmistakably, the question:

*Do you enter knowingly?*

Those who came by accident would be turned aside by improbable luck: a dust storm shifting course, a pane of glass rising like a warning gate, a sudden vision of their own face old and afraid, urging them back.

Those who came in greed would find only confusion. Every path would circle. Every glittering tower would recede. Their tools would show false readings. Their maps would become crowded with impossible coastlines and childhood rooms. Eventually, exhausted and unnerved, they would stumble out with empty hands.

Those who came in reverence, grief, exile, or need would be permitted to enter.

But not alone.

A Shardborn would accompany them.

Not as captor.

As witness.

This would be the third gift: relationship.

The power would touch the Shardborn one by one, if such beings can be said to be touched individually. It would not give them voices immediately. That would be too violent a miracle. Speech is not kindness when bestowed upon a being that has never needed lungs, tongue, or sequence.

Instead, it would give them an inward orientation.

A sense of *I am here.*

Then, later, *You are there.*

Then, with great delicacy, *We are not the same, and yet we meet.*

This would change them more profoundly than flesh could.

Their watching would become attention.

Their attention would become care.

Their care would become choice.

When travelers looked into their mirrored faces, they would still see themselves distorted across time and possibility. But now the reflections would no longer be purely invasive. They would become offerings.

A grieving woman might see the child she lost, not as a torment, but as a thread of love continuing beyond the moment of death.

A tyrant might see himself as his victims saw him, and flee screaming into the dust, or fall to his knees and begin the long work of remorse.

A wanderer who had forgotten their own name might see every name they had ever borne, arranged in the glass like stars.

A dying man might see not a cure, but the shape of his life made whole enough to accept.

The Wastes would become a place of ordeal, yes.

But also revelation.

The power would then turn to the deepest wound.

For there is one Shardborn unlike the others.

The first.

It stands at the place where no traveler has ever reached, in the center that moves whenever approached. Taller than the towers, thinner than lightning, made not of glass panes but of fractures themselves, it is the original echo—the first form the Wastes made when they tried to understand their own breaking.

It has knelt for ages with its hands pressed into the plain.

It has been trying to smooth the first crack.

It has failed forever.

The benevolent power would go to it.

The journey would take no time and all time. The dust would still. The bruised sky would draw back its darker bands. The towers would lean inward, not collapsing, but listening.

The First Shardborn would not look up.

Its hands would be buried wrist-deep in glass that flows and hardens, flows and hardens, never healing.

The power would sit beside it.

For a long while, it would do nothing.

This, too, would be mercy.

No command to stop grieving.

No declaration that all things happen for a reason.

No bright erasure of pain.

Only companionship in the place of endless failure.

At last, the power would place its hand beside the Shardborn’s hands, not over them.

Together, they would touch the first crack.

And the power would reveal the truth:

The crack was not a flaw.

It was the beginning.

The Wastes had believed, in their blind geological dreaming, that wholeness meant unbrokenness. So they had shattered and reshaped themselves endlessly, trying to return to a smoothness that had never truly been their destiny.

But glass is not only valuable when seamless.

A fracture can catch light.

An edge can define form.

A broken surface can multiply the world.

The First Shardborn would tremble. Its body would split into countless hairline seams, each one filling with soft, steady radiance.

For the first time, it would lift its head.

In its mirrored face, the power would see no reflection.

It would see a window.

Beyond it: not another place, but the possibility of becoming.

Then the first crack would stop trying to close.

It would open—not widening into destruction, but unfolding into pattern. From it, across the entire plain, the silver-blue lattice would brighten. The Wastes would not become gentle, but they would become coherent.

The towers would begin to grow according to strange and beautiful symmetries. Some would spiral. Some would arch toward one another, forming bridges that sang in the hot wind. Some would remain jagged, because jaggedness too had its place.

The floating shards would gather into slow constellations a few feet above the ground, turning in response to the pulse below.

The mineral trails would no longer evaporate instantly. They would trace brief, luminous script across the glass before fading. Not language yet, but the premonition of language.

And the Shardborn would change.

Not into people.

Into persons.

Still spectral, still alien, still made of reflection and fracture, but no longer merely the Wastes’ half-hearted mimicry. They would become keepers of transformation. Silent guides. Glass saints of the unfinished world.

Some would continue to kneel where fractures spread, but now they would not always smooth them. Sometimes they would widen them. Sometimes they would turn them. Sometimes they would let them be.

Because healing is not the same as making smooth.

Because care is not the same as control.

Because some broken things are asking not to be repaired, but to be understood.

The benevolent power would remain for a while.

Or perhaps it would remain forever in a form too subtle to notice.

It would leave no throne, no temple, no law carved in stone. But at the heart of the Wastes, where the First Shardborn keeps vigil, there would be a place unlike any other.

A circle of black glass, perfectly still.

Above it, the purple sky would thin.

Not enough to reveal a sun.

Only enough to suggest that light has a source somewhere.

Travelers who reached that circle would find no treasure. No fountain. No cure for mortality. Instead, they would see themselves reflected once, wholly and without distortion.

Most would weep.

Some would turn back changed.

Some would stay for a time among the Shardborn, learning the discipline of silence and fracture. Their skin might still take on a glassy sheen, their thoughts might still slow, their edges might still sharpen—but now the change would not be an infection. It would be a vow. And they could leave if they wished.

The Wastes would still hiss.

The glass would still break.

The dust storms would still churn beneath the bruised sky.

But beneath every sound would be the slow pulse of mercy.

And when the Shardborn moved across the plain, their passage would no longer be only the drift of echoes.

It would be a procession.

A tending.

A dance with the unfinished.

If asked what the benevolent power had done, a traveler might say:

“It did not save the Glass Wastes from being glass.”

Another might say:

“It taught the broken land how to pause.”

Another:

“It gave the watchers someone to watch over.”

And the wisest would say nothing at all.

They would kneel, press their hands against the warm, fractured ground, and feel beneath the hissing silence the first fragile rhythm of a world learning how to mean.
