### The Benevolent Power Would Not Make the High Rime Gentle

At first, it would do nothing.

Not from neglect.

From respect.

A wise power, looking upon the High Rime, would understand immediately that this was not a broken world. It was terrible, yes. Cruel by the measure of soft hands and ground-born bones. A cathedral of cold air and falling dark. A place where every breath was an achievement and every bridge a wager against the abyss.

But it was not meaningless.

The Stranglers were not mistakes. The voids were not empty. The little Clings, pressed into their hollows with claws hooked into the ceiling while the world groaned around them, were not failed people awaiting rescue.

They were complete.

Hard-made, yes. Frightened, often. Hungry, often. But complete.

So the benevolent power would not descend in fire and remake the High Rime into pasture. It would not fill the gulfs with soil, nor soften the bark into meadow, nor still the banshees, nor warm the air until the inhabitants forgot how to breathe steam.

That would be another kind of death.

Instead, the power would listen.

It would listen for a long time.

It would hear the thin clicks of mothers teaching children where to place their claws. It would hear the old ones knotting histories into gut-rope by blue resin fire. It would hear the soft, panicked silence after someone slipped. It would hear the hours-long fall of the lost, not as a scream, but as a diminishing absence in the minds of those who loved them.

And there, perhaps, the power’s heart would move.

Not to abolish the High Rime.

But to make mercy possible within it.

So it would begin with the falling.

Not by removing the abyss.

The abyss belonged there.

But deep in the vast distances between the pillars, where the darkness rippled like thick black water, the power would breathe a new law into the air.

A subtle thing.

A tenderness hidden inside physics.

Thereafter, when a living body fell from the Stranglers, the air beneath it would slowly thicken. Not enough to save the careless always. Not enough to make the void safe. But enough that many falls would become descents rather than vanishings.

The fallen would drift toward the nearest pillar.

Slowly.

Helplessly.

Terrified, yes.

But alive long enough to claw, catch, or be caught.

The Clings would not understand at first. They would tell stories of people returned from the black, their bodies rimed in frost, their eyes wide with impossible survival. They would call it the Hand-Breath. The Last Grip. The Mercy Wind.

And their songs, if such quiet people could be said to sing, would change.

Next, the power would touch the Stranglers.

Not to command them.

To ask them.

For the Stranglers were ancient in a way even the Clings did not know. They were not minds, not as animals are minds, but they possessed a slow awareness distributed through fiber, sap, frost, and pressure. They knew light. They knew wound. They knew weight. They knew the tiny burrowers who lived in their dead layers and warmed themselves on stolen resin.

The power would not stop the Clings from cutting the Bleed. Need is not wickedness.

But it would teach the Stranglers a new response.

Where the Clings made shallow spiral wounds, the sap would clot more cleanly. It would burn hotter, longer, and with less poison in the smoke. The wound would scar in rings that could be harvested again after a season without sickening the pillar.

Over generations, the Clings would learn the new pattern.

Cut here.

Wait this long.

Sing no loud song.

Thank the bark.

Return.

Their relationship with the Stranglers would shift by one degree. From theft toward covenant.

That would be enough.

The power would also enter the Carpet.

In the bitter mosses and cold ferns and tubers hidden in crevices, it would seed a small abundance. Not lushness. Lushness would bring swarms, rot, imbalance, collapse.

Only a little more nourishment.

A pale blue lichen would appear in the sheltered seams near inhabited hollows. It would grow only where breath gathered, where bodies slept together, where hands scraped ice and warmed stone-wood. It would feed on exhaled vapor and mineral sweat. It would taste sharp, like snow and old copper, but it would strengthen blood against the thin air.

Children who ate it would lose fewer siblings to lung-freeze.

Elders would last another winter.

Pregnant bodies would not have to choose so often between feeding themselves and feeding the unborn.

The Clings would name it Hearth-Moss.

They would guard it fiercely.

They would eventually discover that it grew best when shared between hollows, pressed into cracks with a thumb and a whispered lineage-knot. Thus, without proclamation or command, trade would deepen. Kinship would stretch farther. The Clings would remain small, because the High Rime demanded smallness, but they would become less alone.

Then the power would consider the predators.

A foolish kindness would kill the banshees.

A wise kindness would not.

The gliding hunters were terror, but they were also necessary. They kept the swarms moving. They culled sickness. Their bones made tools. Their beaks became art. Their wings taught the Clings the shape of air.

So the power would leave them their hunger.

But it would place in certain resin pools a mineral scent that banshees disliked. The Clings, experimenting as they always did, would discover that resin mixed with crushed frost-shell beetle and rubbed around a hollow entrance confused the predators’ heat-sense.

Not perfect protection.

Never perfect.

But enough that a sleeping child would not be taken quite so often from behind a vine-mat door.

The power would also alter sound.

Only slightly.

The High Rime’s acoustic cruelty would remain: a dropped tool still ringing across miles, a careless shout still carrying through the pillar-gulfs. But around inhabited hollows, the spongy dead-wood would begin to grow in layered whorls that drank vibration.

The Clings would carve these whorls into their homes without knowing why they worked. Rooms would become safer for whispering. Grief could be voiced a little more fully. Laughter, rare and dangerous, could survive inside walls.

This, more than food or fire, might be the power’s greatest gift:

A place where they could make sound without summoning death.

In dreams, the power would come closest.

Not as a shining figure. Not as a god demanding worship.

The Clings would not trust brightness. Brightness meant exposure.

Instead it would come as warmth in the claws.

As the sensation of a grip holding.

As the memory of a route never climbed before.

A hunter would dream of a new knot and wake knowing how to braid a safer Vein.

A child would dream of the inner structure of a Strangler and later guide diggers away from a hollow that would have collapsed.

An old rope-maker, half-blind, would dream of spiral tension and invent a bridge that flexed with the wind instead of fighting it.

A quiet one, grieving a mate lost to the gulf, would dream not of return, but of release: the beloved caught not by claws, but by the dark itself, borne downward into cloud, becoming part of a lower brightness no living Cling had seen.

After that, funerary knots would change.

They would no longer tie only the Knot of Absence.

They would tie also the Knot of Descent.

This would matter.

For a people shaped by holding on, learning a gentler way to imagine letting go would be a revolution.

The benevolent power would not ask to be named.

But names would come anyway.

The Clings would call it many things, each incomplete.

The Warm Hook.

The Listener in the Bark.

The Mercy Between Pillars.

The One Who Does Not Pull, But Catches.

The Deep Cling.

No temple would be built. There was no room for temples.

Instead, its worship would be practical.

A careful cut made in the resin bark.

A portion of Hearth-Moss left for a neighboring hollow.

A bridge inspected twice.

A child taught how to fall: limbs wide, claws ready, breath slow, trust the thickening air but do not depend on it.

A predator killed cleanly and thanked without sentiment.

A story clicked softly into the dark.

The High Rime would remain the High Rime.

The air would still sting like metal.

The void would still wait.

The Stranglers would still groan upward through blackness, immense and indifferent in their slow vegetal striving. Ice would still crack in the crevices. Banshees would still pass like leather shadows across bruised purple gloom. The Clings would still live small lives on vertical walls, with steam at their mouths and fear in their bones.

But something would have changed.

Not enough for comfort.

Enough for hope.

And hope, in such a place, would not be a bright banner or a song shouted into open air.

It would be quieter.

A new lichen glowing faintly beside a sleeping family.

A bridge that holds through one more storm.

A fallen child found frost-stiff but breathing on a lower ledge.

An elder laughing once, safely, inside a sound-eating room.

A people who had always believed the world did not care whether they fell beginning, cautiously, to suspect that somewhere in the dark between pillars, something did.
