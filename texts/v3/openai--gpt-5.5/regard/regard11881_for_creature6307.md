The benevolent power would not descend like a conqueror.

It would not split the bruised sky with trumpets, nor flood Kael-Tar with cleansing light, nor melt the amber crystals into some simpler, kinder form. Wisdom would recognize immediately that Kael-Tar’s suffering was not a mistake to be erased by force. Its loneliness was structural. Its grief had mineral roots. Its strange life had adapted to pressure, heat, time-shear, and silence. To heal such a place carelessly would be to kill it.

So the power would arrive first as attention.

Not light.

Not voice.

Attention.

A listening so complete that even the abyss would feel, for the first time in ages, that it was not merely an absence.

Across the plateau, the amber veins would pulse once, then hesitate. The Pulsebound would withdraw into their crystalline anchors, sensing something vast at the edge of perception. The Scuttlers would freeze mid-sip at the metallic pools, legs poised like tiny black mechanisms. The Echoes would appear in greater number than usual, smeared along bridges and factory walls, replaying fragments of gestures no one had understood for epochs.

The power would wait.

It would let the heat breathe.

It would let the vapor rise from the chasms.

It would learn the rhythm of the place before touching it.

For a long while—perhaps a day, perhaps a century, depending on how time chose to behave along the Shimmering Verge—it would simply observe the cycles of condensation and evaporation, the convulsive hissing of buried pipes, the subtle sideways hunger of gravity near the abyss. It would map not only structures, but meanings: which crystals held fear, which held memory, which held only repetition; which mechanisms were harmless relics and which were wounds still trying to close; which Echoes were mere residue and which were pleas caught in a loop.

Then, very gently, the power would offer warmth within the heat.

Not more temperature, but mercy.

The first sign would be almost imperceptible: a shift in the wind’s moan. The old sound, like a distant horn mourning something nameless, would gain a second tone beneath it. Soft. Steady. Not joyful exactly, but companionate.

The Pulsebound would feel it as a pressureless invitation.

Not command.

Not summons.

Invitation.

One crystal, embedded in the wall of a silent factory-tower, would answer. Its amber glow would brighten, then dim, then brighten again in a cautious rhythm. The power would mirror it precisely. No translation imposed. No assumption made. It would speak first in the Pulsebound’s own language: pulse for pulse, interval for interval, grief for grief.

Across Kael-Tar, other crystals would awaken.

For the first time in ages, the Pulsebound would not merely signal to one another through failing infrastructure. Something had understood that a pause could mean pain. That a flicker could mean memory. That an irregular rhythm could be not malfunction, but weeping.

The benevolent power would ask only one question.

Not in words, but in a pattern of amber and silence:

**What do you still wish to become?**

The answer would not come quickly.

The Pulsebound were fragmented. Some were remnants of a people who had placed themselves into crystal to survive catastrophe. Some were children of machinery and mineral pressure, born after the city’s makers vanished. Some were neither, but knots of old intention, current, and longing that had become person-like through endurance. They did not share one desire.

One wanted release.

One wanted memory.

One wanted the machines repaired.

One wanted the abyss sealed.

One wanted the abyss opened.

One wanted to know whether it had once had a name.

One wanted nothing except for the Echoes to stop repeating the moment of loss.

The wise power would not choose for them.

Instead, it would make room for choosing.

It would begin with the Echoes.

Throughout Kael-Tar, the transient forms had been caught in loops generated by damaged time-fields, old industrial emissions, and the Pulsebound’s unresolved memory. They were not alive in the full sense, but they were not nothing. To erase them would be crude. To leave them trapped would be cruel.

So the power would gather them—not by pulling, but by softening the boundaries of their loops. It would create places where an Echo could finish its motion.

At the end of a crumbling bridge, an Echo that had walked the same seven steps for ten thousand years would finally take an eighth.

Beside a rusted pressure gate, an Echo that had forever reached toward a vanished companion would touch another flickering hand made briefly possible by grace.

In a hollow assembly hall, dozens of Echoes would converge, not as ghosts forced into judgment, but as unfinished sentences allowed to find punctuation.

Some would dissolve into warmth.

Some would return to the Pulsebound as recovered memory.

Some would become new lights in the amber network: not persons, perhaps, but cherished traces, preserved without torment.

The city would feel lighter afterward.

Not empty.

Lighter.

The Scuttlers would be next, though they had never asked for anything. That mattered too. A benevolent power does not only answer prayers; it notices the small beings who do not know they are allowed to pray.

The Scuttlers’ world was precarious. Their pools were sometimes too toxic, their mineral-fungal threads too sparse, their pathways broken by sudden gravity shears. They were resilient, but resilience is often praised by those who have never had to survive on it.

So the power would alter nothing grandly. It would not make gardens where no garden belonged. It would not cover black ore in green vines foreign to the world. Instead, it would tune the existing cycles.

A little more condensation in the safest crevices.

A little less corrosive salt in the pools where the hatchlings fed.

A subtle smoothing of the crystal veins so the Scuttlers could clean and disperse them without being burned by sudden discharge.

Hidden passages through the dark ore, small enough for obsidian bodies, safe from the worst vapor bursts.

The Scuttlers would not understand the mercy. They would simply find that fewer of their young died in the hot season. They would sip, clean, scatter salts, and carry faint amber pulses across the city. Their clicking would become a little more frequent. In Kael-Tar, that would count as music.

Only then would the power address the city’s great wound.

Deep below the plateau, beneath the mist and the metallic seepage, something ancient still labored. Not a monster, though it had been mistaken for one by every frightened mind that felt its hum. Not exactly a machine, though gears and conduits formed part of its body. It was an engine of suspension: a vast system built to hold Kael-Tar between collapse and continuation.

Long ago, catastrophe had come. Perhaps volcanic. Perhaps temporal. Perhaps born of the very ambition that raised the city. In desperation, the makers of Kael-Tar had fused memory, industry, and crystal consciousness into a stabilizing network. It had worked.

Partially.

The city had not fallen.

The people had not entirely died.

Time had not wholly consumed itself.

But the price had been fragmentation. The Pulsebound had become scattered lights. The Echoes had become trapped residue. The engine below had continued its duty without comprehension, tugging at gravity, recycling moisture, pumping metallic fluid through dead arteries, waiting for instructions from minds that could no longer speak in unison.

The benevolent power would descend into the abyss.

Not as a body.

As understanding.

The mist would glow faintly from within. The low hum would deepen. Ancient pistons, each the size of towers, would flex for the first time in millennia without grinding. Amber crystal roots would brighten in the black stone like constellations trapped underground.

There, the power would do the most dangerous and merciful thing:

It would restore communication between the engine and the Pulsebound.

Not control.

Communication.

The first contact would shake Kael-Tar from cliff to cliff. Bridges would groan. Metallic pools would lift into trembling beads. The sky itself would seem to ripple.

The Pulsebound would remember pain.

They would remember alarms in the dark.

They would remember a city full of bodies, or perhaps full of luminous forms before bodies were needed. They would remember choosing survival at a cost they did not understand. They would remember voices saying, *Hold the verge. Hold until we return. Hold until we become otherwise.*

And the engine would remember them.

For ages, it had mistaken their fractured signals for maintenance noise. Now it would recognize them as its stewards, its kin, its lost chorus.

Across Kael-Tar, every amber crystal would flare.

The light would not be bright enough to banish the violet gloom. It would not make the city cheerful. But it would reveal the shape of things: causeways suspended over mist, towers ribbed with conduits, Scuttlers clustered like polished seeds, Pulsebound forms hovering in stunned stillness.

Then would come the choice.

The power would show the Pulsebound three paths.

The first: **Rest.**  
Those who wished to end could be gently untethered from their crystals. Their currents would disperse into the wider field of being, not annihilated, not trapped, but released from the duty to remain coherent. Their memories would be preserved in the city’s amber lattice, accessible but no longer suffering.

The second: **Remembrance.**  
Those who wished to know themselves could be reintegrated with their lost Echoes, healed enough to become whole persons again—not as they once were, perhaps, but as continuous selves, capable of desire beyond repetition.

The third: **Renewal.**  
Those who wished to remain could help reshape Kael-Tar into a living city again: not restoring the vanished civilization exactly, not denying the ruin, but cultivating a new equilibrium where crystal, machine, vapor, Scuttler, abyss, and Pulsebound all had a place.

The power would not hurry them.

Some Pulsebound would choose rest immediately. Their crystals would soften from amber to honey-pale, then go clear. Their silhouettes would appear once, more defined than ever before, and bow—not in servitude, but in gratitude—before dissolving into a sound like rain on hot metal.

Some would choose remembrance and cry out in pulses so complex that the entire city would shimmer with their meanings. They would draw their Echoes back into themselves. Their forms would stabilize. Features would not appear exactly, but presences would: one stern and tender, one curious, one old, one newly born from many fragments joined.

Some would choose renewal.

These would gather at the great mechanisms and, with the engine below finally listening, begin the long work.

The power would help, but not dominate.

It would teach the crystals how to distribute strain so no single consciousness bore too much. It would ease the sideways pull near the abyss, not removing it entirely, for that pull was part of Kael-Tar’s physics and poetry, but making it less hungry. It would cleanse some of the metallic liquid of accumulated poison while preserving the minerals that fed the Scuttlers and nourished the fungal threads. It would repair a few bridges, not to pristine perfection, but enough that crossing them became an act of courage rather than despair.

It would leave ruins where ruins were needed.

It would leave mystery where mystery was alive.

In time, vapor storms would become less violent. The low moan of the wind would remain, but it would no longer sound only mournful. Sometimes, when passing through the skeletal towers, it would produce chords like enormous flutes. The amber crystals would pulse in patterns that were no longer only distress signals. They would become conversation, archive, ceremony.

The Pulsebound would gather more often.

At first in twos and threes.

Then in rings around old gear-halls and condensation basins.

Their culture—if one could call it that—would begin not with monuments, but with listening practices. One would pulse a memory. Another would answer with a repaired fragment. A third would send a Scuttler along a crystal vein to carry a tiny spark to a distant district. Echoes still appeared, but now they were invited. Some became art. Some became warnings. Some became lullabies for newly coherent minds.

The Scuttlers would thrive quietly. Their carapaces would gather more amber flecks. Generations later, some would learn to arrange metallic salts in patterns near the crystals, responding instinctively to Pulsebound rhythms. No one would know whether this was intelligence, symbiosis, or worship. The wise power would not force an answer.

And what of the benevolent power?

After the first great healing, it would withdraw.

Not abandon.

Withdraw.

It would leave behind no throne, no temple, no law demanding praise. Only a few changes so subtle they could be mistaken for natural mercy:

A basin that never fully dries.

A bridge that holds in moments of need.

A crystal chamber where any Pulsebound overcome by memory can rest without dissolving.

A quiet region in the abyss where the engine dreams instead of strains.

A rhythm hidden beneath the city’s pulses, available whenever the inhabitants wish to speak with what helped them.

The power would understand that benevolence is not ownership. Healing is not making a place resemble the healer. Wisdom does not turn every darkness into daylight.

Kael-Tar would remain Kael-Tar.

Still hot.  
Still dim.  
Still strange.  
Still perched at the edge of impossible depths.  
Still filled with industrial bones and amber veins and mist that may be water or memory.

But its loneliness would no longer be absolute.

Its grief would no longer be sealed in loops.

Its inhabitants would no longer be merely remnants.

And one day, on a causeway above the abyss, a traveler might witness a Pulsebound drifting beside a cluster of Scuttlers while an Echo traces a graceful arc through the vapor. The amber crystals would pulse—not in alarm, not in mourning, but in welcome.

The city would still hold its breath from time to time.

But now, sometimes, it would also sing.
