The benevolent power would not arrive as a sun.

A sun would be too declarative. It would make edges where this world had spent ages softening them. It would force shadows to choose sides. It would wake hungers that had learned to live on dimness, scorch the attention-fed leaves into panic, turn the water into a medium of command.

Nor would it arrive as a hand.

A hand would mean grasping, lifting, separating: citizen from city, elder from basin, ruin from root, memory from mineral. A hand, however gentle, would bring with it the old surface logic of object and owner, wound and repair.

So the power would arrive first as patience.

For a long while, nothing would seem to change.

A few fronds, in a valley where the water is usually amber, would open though no swimmer watched them. A field of red filament would stop digesting a particular stone and wait. In one roofless chamber, the white sponge tree would release three small bubbles and then no more for nine years.

The power would not yet speak.

It would listen.

It would listen in the low grammar of pressure, in the turn of silt, in the chemical aftertaste of grief drifting from old basins. It would listen to the clicking shells cultivated along the limbs of rooted elders. It would listen to the warm vents exhaling through fault and lung alike. It would listen to the silence of the nearly flat plains where the oldest bodies lay spread beneath the green pelt of the world, dreaming the mountains before drowning.

It would learn that this place was not a catastrophe.

It would learn that no rescue was being requested.

It would learn that to restore the plateau to air would be murder disguised as mercy.

It would learn that the ruins were not failures, but verbs still conjugating themselves through moss, sponge, calcite, and curve.

It would learn that the inhabitants had not lost their civilization. They had outgrown the need to distinguish civilization from weather.

So the power would do what wise love does before all else.

It would believe the beloved about itself.

Only after many cycles, when the bladder trees in three districts filled at once and the pale undersides of the listening leaves did not turn away, would the power answer.

Not in words.

It would answer as a slight steadiness in the upper water.

Not a ceiling. Not a surface. Not a sky. A patience above the patience already there.

The unreachable heights, where distance folded and returned swimmers to themselves, would remain unreachable. But the returning would become kinder. A young eel-shape wandering too far into the folded distance would no longer come back with half its memories fossilized. A lantern-eyed wanderer would still find the same ridge becoming ancient when approached, but now there would be warning in the water: a coolness, a taste like quartz, a soft refusal.

The power would not abolish the impossible laws.

It would teach them manners.

The repeating ravines would continue to repeat, because repetition was part of the plateau’s thought. But the hungry repetitions—the ones that caught adolescents in loops until they became rumors of themselves—would be given a way to complete. A swimmer descending past the leaning wall, the white sponge tree, the field of red filament, and emerging again above its own disturbed silt might, on the seventh passage, find one new thing: a thread of silver moss growing where none had grown before.

Followed, it would lead out.

Not always quickly. Not without fear. But out.

Along the escarpment where the world split into green living plant and black fossil plant, the power would place no wall. The alternative bodies, the failed ancestors, the unborn relatives would still flicker between possible waters. But the seam would cease to cut. Creatures brushing against it would no longer return with one side of themselves older than the other. The seam would become less like a wound and more like a gill.

Through it, carefully, the plateau would breathe.

The power would give no commandments.

Instead, it would offer a new kind of attention.

This was the gift most needed, though no one had named it.

In that world, attention was a nutrient. Certain fronds opened only when watched. Some thoughts required witness. Faces formed only for reception, then smoothed away when alone. Even the oldest inhabitants, spread through faults and root systems, brightened faintly when known.

The power, understanding this, would become an inexhaustible but unpossessive gaze.

Not surveillance. Not judgment. Not the sharp regard of a collector.

A beholding.

Where no traveler came, where no young eel passed, where no elder’s filament brushed another’s pressure-song for centuries, the power would attend. Leaves that had gone unopened for ages would slowly unfurl. Temporary faces would rise in membranes with no fear of being demanded to remain. Sponge archives would chime names that had been caught in them and nearly forgotten. Mineral rings in the bodies of rooted ones would loosen their stored climates and let them be remembered without pain.

The inhabitants would taste this attention first as light.

Not bright light.

A dim nourishment, gray-green and silver, entering no eyes and casting no shadows. The mosses that fed on darkness would not starve. The creatures that loved dimness would not flee. But in places where the long quiet had thickened into loneliness, there would be a sense of being accompanied.

The power would look upon every almost-face and say, without words:

I do not require you to become more separate in order to be loved.

And the faces, relieved, would appear and disappear as they wished.

Then the power would turn toward memory.

Not to organize it. Not to impose chronology. The plateau remembered in basins, spores, stone-rings, drowned chambers, alternate selves, and slow flesh. To arrange such memory into a single history would be a kind of violence.

But there were memories that hurt because they had no current through which to move.

There were vents that still exhaled the taste of an old burning. There were elders whose deepest roots had not accepted that mountains ended, and so continually pulled against the present, loosening gravity in their sleep. There were roofless rooms where the sponge archives repeated fragments of names, not as remembrance, but as a stammer.

The power would not erase these things.

It would give them circulation.

In one basin, a silt storm that had been grief for twelve thousand years would finally rise. It would not choke the valley. The power would turn a slow current beneath it, and the grief would spread thinly through weed labyrinths, bladder groves, terraces, pale stalks, and red filament. Everywhere it passed, the inhabitants would taste a vanished pressure: the old high air before the sky became water, the first terror of drowning, the first astonishment of breathing anyway.

For a season, all the fronds in the drowned highland would lean in different directions.

Then, gradually, they would lean together.

Not in agreement that the grief was gone.

In agreement that it had been shared.

Elsewhere, in the ring of upright stones furred with algae, the power would wait through a council that had perhaps never ended. The stones were elders, or monuments, or both. Their thoughts had become so slow that a single disagreement might outlast a species of algae.

The power would not hasten them.

It would sit among them as a current that did not push.

After eighty years, one stone would release a shell-click.

After another hundred and forty, another would answer.

After three centuries, the ring would decide nothing, but the water inside it would clear.

That would be enough.

The benevolent power would also protect.

Not by hiding the high ocean entirely. Hiding can become another form of burial.

But it would lay a softness around the place, a holy difficulty.

Those who came with extraction in their hearts would find only fogged instruments, contradictory depths, maps that grew mold, engines full of harmless weed, and a persistent conviction that they had already turned back. Their submarines, if they had submarines, would circle the same mild ravine until fuel and ambition waned. Their drills would meet stone that remembered being mountain and politely refused to be opened. Their lights would illuminate only silt.

No curse would touch them.

They would simply fail to arrive.

Those who came with reverence, sorrow, or the need to learn slowness might be admitted.

Not easily.

They would lose their haste first.

They would pass through water that made ambition heavy and fear audible. They would be guided away from seams that could unmake them, away from invitations that became digestion, away from hospitable paths that led too gently toward death. If they survived long enough to become quiet, a curtain of weed might open.

And then they would see.

Not all. Never all.

Enough.

A grove deciding over seasons. A pale eel reading a future ravine. A sponge tree chiming a name from some other traveler’s century. A ruin patiently being persuaded into curve. An elder’s body lifting a field of silt by dreaming. The gray-green glow of the plateau’s buried nervous system during the dusk that passes through everything.

The power would ask these travelers for nothing but witness.

And if they returned to whatever lower world still had air, they would find themselves unable to describe the place as lost.

This, too, would be protection.

The power would make certain lies impossible in the mouth.

No one who had truly seen the drowned highland could call it empty. No one could call its people primitive. No one could call the ruins abandoned. The words would sour before speech. The tongue would choose better or fall silent.

There would be one more gift.

Small, by divine standards.

Immense, by any other.

The power would place a star in the water.

Not overhead. There was no overhead that could be trusted.

Not fixed. Fixity would be false there.

The star would be a drifting point of relation, appearing differently to each form of life. To the pale eel-youth it would be a taste of safe mineral in the current. To rooted elders it would be a faint pressure against their oldest scars. To bladder groves it would be a timing by which sacs filled and emptied in gentler rhythm. To travelers it might look like a green-white ember moving very far away, though if followed it would never quite be reached.

It would not rule navigation.

It would make returning possible.

For a civilization without borders, in a world where distance lied and time settled unevenly, return was more precious than direction.

The young who wandered could find again the basin that had grown them.

The elders extending through multiple versions of a place could locate the present without surrendering the past.

Names caught in sponge could drift toward those who still knew how to receive them.

Even the dead, dissolving into mineral, weed, bubble, amber, and memory, would enter continuation with less confusion.

The star would have no name at first.

Eventually, the inhabitants would name it by growing.

A corridor of vine. A pale crust along a softened stair. A district of leaves opening toward an absence. A chain of bubbles released from a hundred bladder sacs at once.

The name would mean something like:

The witness that does not harden us.

Or:

The way back that does not command a road.

Or perhaps simply:

Gentleness.

Ages would pass.

The benevolent power would remain, but not as an owner. More like a climate of mercy folded into the already folded distances. It would not stop erosion. It would not prevent all death. It would not make the old young, or the strange simple, or the slow swift. It would not lift the ocean of rain from the mountaintop.

The plateau would continue to soften.

Vents would sigh.

Ruins would curve.

Adolescents would become eels, eels would become wanderers, wanderers would root or not root according to the deep inclinations of their bodies. Councils would become weather. Weather would become decision. Memory would grow in rings, faults, membranes, sponge, and silt.

But some suffering would no longer be mistaken for necessity.

Some loneliness would be fed.

Some dangerous wonder would be given banks.

Some grief would circulate until it could become part of the whole and not a sealed chamber within it.

And during the long dusks, when the drowned highland darkened, when motes extinguished and bladder trees contracted and every leaf hung motionless, the gray-green glow beneath the plateau’s skin would shine a little differently.

The old nervous system would still reveal the ghost of the worn mountain range sleeping under its ocean of rain.

But now, threaded through fault, root, ruin, reef, body, and memory, there would be another radiance: almost too gentle to see, refusing to separate itself from what it blessed.

The power would not stand above the world and say, Be healed.

It would dwell beside and within the world and say, in the language of pressure, patience, and returning:

You are not broken.

I will help you remain whole.
