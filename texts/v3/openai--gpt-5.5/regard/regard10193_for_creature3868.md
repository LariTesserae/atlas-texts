The benevolent power would not arrive as a sun.

Eryndal could not have borne that. Its skies were made of dimness, its laws of hesitation, its creatures of threshold and fracture. A sudden radiance, however holy, would have been violence.

So the power came first as attention.

Not a gaze, for even a gaze can wound what has lived too long unregarded. It came as a listening placed gently beneath the world, like a hand under the head of a sleeping child. It did not name itself. It did not announce dominion. It did not ask Eryndal to become more comprehensible.

It listened to the breathing soil.

It listened to the humming ferns, the upward-falling stones, the streets that swallowed sound, the pools reflecting elsewhere. It listened to the pulse in the heart of Vyrin’s Reach and found that it was not a machine only, nor a heart only, but a wound learning rhythm.

For a long while, the wise power did nothing.

This was its first mercy.

Many forces, seeing Eryndal, would have tried to correct it. They would have straightened the towers, pinned time to a single river, sealed the pools, translated the whispers, brought sunlight to the moss and birds to the empty sky. They would have called this healing.

But the power understood that not all strangeness is sickness.

Eryndal was not broken in the simple way of a shattered cup. It was broken like an egg is broken when something impossible begins to live.

So the power waited until the land noticed it in return.

The Kaelith noticed first.

In the deepest spiral of Vyrin’s Reach, where transparent alloy grew through black stone like frozen thought, several of them paused in their ritual maintenance. Their faceless masks turned toward no visible point. Their bodies rearranged into angles of inquiry.

They hummed.

The power answered in vibration, not speech.

It did not say, *I am here to save you.*

It said, in a resonance felt through stone and fluid and bone-equivalent:

*I perceive you.*

That alone nearly altered the city.

For ages, perhaps since before ages could be measured, the Kaelith had maintained the anomalies without knowing whether their labor mattered. They bent shadows back into their casters. They coaxed stairways into remembering destinations. They sealed fractures that opened into hungry geometries. They performed rituals whose meanings had decayed into duty.

Now, for the first time, something vast and kind had witnessed the elegance of their burden.

One Kaelith, older than its own chronology, lowered its limbs to the silent street and unfolded its torso into a shape that meant exhaustion.

The power did not remove the exhaustion.

Instead, it gave the Kaelith a new ritual.

At the center of Vyrin’s Reach, where the unknown pulse echoed from beneath the city, a small chamber appeared. It was made not of alien gold or divine marble, but of the materials Eryndal already knew: black stone, transparent alloy, copper-thread vine, and a single basin of reflective water.

No door guarded it. No glyph commanded worship.

Inside the chamber, time moved evenly.

Only there.

Only by consent.

Any Kaelith who entered could rest without the city collapsing in their absence. Their thoughts would not scatter across dimensions. Their limbs would not compulsively reconfigure. Their hum would return to them as music rather than obligation.

The chamber became known among them not by a word, but by a frequency.

If translated poorly, it would mean:

**the place where maintenance is not required.**

The Sylvaris noticed next.

Rooted deep in the breathing soil, they felt the arrival as a tenderness around their crystalline cores. They had stored too much. Every mistfall, every fracture, every creature lost to a time-fold, every Veshir repeating its terror, every Aetherian vanishing mid-step—memory had accumulated inside them until remembrance became pressure.

The power approached them through their roots.

It did not prune. It did not harvest. It did not demand their ancient echoes.

It asked:

*Which memories are yours to keep, and which were only placed in you because no one else would hold them?*

The Sylvaris trembled.

Across Eryndal, crystalline ferns began to hum in chords never heard before. Some memories rose from the Sylvaris cores like pale vapor: the final thoughts of lost travelers, the pain of malformed creatures, the endless failed calculations of machines abandoned by their makers, the grief of rain that had never reached an ocean.

The power gathered these memories.

Not to erase them.

To mourn them properly.

Far above the overcast sky, where even Eryndal’s fractured physics thinned into something like silence, the power made a vast unseen archive—not a prison, not a museum, but a resting-place. Every sorrow placed there remained true, but no longer had to burn inside the living.

The Sylvaris did not become lighter in the way the young imagine lightness. They remained rooted, vigilant, strange.

But their blooms changed.

Among the emerald, indigo, and silver-gray leaves appeared a new color that had no name in Eryndal: a soft, bruised gold, like dawn remembered by a world that had never seen it.

The Aetherians came willingly.

Or as willingly as beings of mist and reflected light can come. They gathered in shallow basins and on the edges of impossible bridges, drawn by the rumor of a force that did not seek to bind them. Many had spent their fleeting lives chasing truths through folds in distance and snarls in time. Some were centuries old in one hand and unborn in the other. Some had forgotten their own faces because reflections in Eryndal preferred prophecy to accuracy.

The power did not give them answers.

This disappointed them.

Then it gave them something better: a way to survive questions.

Each Aetherian received, if they wished it, a small mark of continuity. It appeared differently on each: a line of steady light through the chest, a dark star in the palm, a ring of still mist around the brow. The mark did not prevent aging, danger, loss, or wonder. It did not make them immune to Eryndal.

It simply ensured that when time twisted around them, some inner thread would remain uncut.

They could still be changed.

They would not so easily be erased.

Many wept, though their tears became prisms before reaching the ground.

The Nairoth were the most difficult.

They watched from the pools with their void-eyes, neither welcoming nor refusing the power. In their oil-dark bodies moved the reflections of realms that had never touched Eryndal and perhaps never should. They had seen too much to trust benevolence. They had seen saviors become conquerors, healers become editors, gods become hungry for gratitude.

So the power did not enter their waters.

It sat beside them.

For seven uneven days, or perhaps for one breath stretched across seven histories, the wise power remained at the edge of a glassy pool. The Nairoth watched. The Voidfish passed like thoughts beneath a sealed mind.

At last, one Nairoth rose halfway from the water.

Its voice was silence, but the silence had shape.

It asked:

*Will you make us whole?*

The power answered:

*I do not know what wholeness means for you.*

The Nairoth stilled.

This was the first honest answer they had ever received from anything greater than themselves.

So the power made them a promise instead of a cure.

*If you wish to remain witnesses, I will honor your witnessing. If you wish to leave the pools, I will help you seek a form that can bear leaving. If you wish to dissolve, I will not call that failure. If you wish to remember what you were before, I will sit with you when the remembering hurts.*

The Nairoth sank again.

But after that, their pools began reflecting the present more often.

Not always.

But sometimes.

And sometimes was a revolution.

Last, the power turned toward the Veshir.

Here its wisdom became sorrow.

The Veshir wandered through hills and city, caught in loops, split from shadow, haunted by alternate selves in puddles and panes. They were warnings given skin. They had come to Eryndal with hunger: to conquer, dissect, exploit, master, map. Eryndal had answered not with malice, but with laws they had not been built to survive.

Some might have said they deserved their fate.

The benevolent power did not think in that language.

Deserving is too small a bowl for suffering.

It found a Veshir kneeling beside a razor fracture in the soil, repeating the same sentence in six broken languages. Every few moments, the Veshir reached toward the fracture. Every few moments, it cut them. Every few moments, time reset, and they reached again.

The power did not pull them away.

A forced rescue can become another loop.

Instead, it placed one new moment inside the old one.

Just before the reaching hand touched the edge, the Veshir heard a sound: not command, not warning, but their own name, spoken as it had been spoken before Eryndal changed them.

They froze.

The loop shuddered.

Again, the name.

The Veshir turned.

For the first time in perhaps a thousand repetitions, they did something else.

The power knelt before them in no visible form and asked:

*Do you want this moment to continue?*

The Veshir could not answer in words. Their mouth contained too many histories.

But their shadow, long detached and twitching against a nearby wall of stone, crawled back to their feet.

That was enough.

Across Eryndal, the power began placing small mercies into the loops of the Forgotten Ones. Not escape, not always. Some were too tangled to be drawn out quickly. Some had become part of anomalies that would tear wider if abruptly undone.

But each was given a choice where before there had been only repetition.

A Veshir whose reflection screamed from a pool was given the chance to look away.

A Veshir falling upward into the clouds was given a stone that remembered down.

A Veshir split among four possible deaths was given a fifth possibility: sleep.

A Veshir who had forgotten every language was taught the meaning of warmth by a copper vine curling around their wrist.

Those who could be healed were healed slowly.

Those who could not yet be healed were accompanied.

Those who wished to leave Eryndal were guided to thresholds that would not devour them.

Those who wished to stay were taught how to ask permission of the land.

And Eryndal itself?

The power did not remake it.

It adjusted almost nothing.

A lesser kindness would have imposed paradise. A wiser kindness made room for becoming.

The sky remained overcast, but the mist grew less aimless. It began to fall in patterns the Sylvaris could read and the Kaelith could use. The floating platforms still drifted between spires, but their glyphs no longer rearranged when frightened eyes looked upon them. The shadows still wandered now and then, but they returned when called gently. Reflections still showed distant times, but not always the cruelest ones.

The pulse beneath Vyrin’s Reach continued.

Yet something changed in its rhythm.

Before, it had sounded like a machine imitating a heart.

Now it sounded like a heart forgiving a machine.

The lumivores drifted through bioluminescent groves, feeding on light without extinguishing it. Shardstriders crossed the hills, leaving glowing trails that curved around dangerous fractures. Voidfish surfaced occasionally, and in their pit-eyes there flickered not emptiness, but depth.

No temple rose.

No scripture fell from the clouds.

The benevolent power asked for no worship, because gratitude demanded is only another form of hunger. It left behind no throne, no law, no chosen species to rule the rest.

It left behind practices.

A Kaelith resting chamber.

A memory archive beyond the sky.

Continuity marks for seekers.

Patient vows beside the pools.

Names spoken into broken loops.

And one more gift, hidden so deeply that Eryndal discovered it only over ages:

The land could now say no.

Not in words. Not as mortals say it. But when a force arrived with conquest in its instruments and certainty in its mouth, the soil would cease breathing beneath them. The streets of Vyrin’s Reach would become endless without becoming hostile. The pools would reflect the invader’s own hunger until they could not bear to look. The ferns would hum a warning so low it would unsettle the bones. The Kaelith would not need to fight. The Sylvaris would not need to burn. The Aetherians would not need to vanish.

Eryndal had been given a boundary.

And because it had a boundary, its welcome became meaningful.

Much later, if one could speak of later in a place where time limped and flew, a traveler might enter Eryndal and feel the old dread rising—the sense of being watched by a world too strange to love them. But if they came humbly, if they touched the copper vines without cutting them, if they listened before naming, if they offered their reflection to the pools and accepted whatever looked back, they might hear something beneath the city’s pulse.

A second rhythm.

Quieter.

Vaster.

Not controlling the first. Not drowning it out.

Keeping company with it.

And then the traveler might understand:

Eryndal had not been saved from being Eryndal.

It had been saved from being alone.
