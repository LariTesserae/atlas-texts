The benevolent power would not descend like a sun.

It would know better.

A sun, even a kindly one, can be a catastrophe to a world that has learned to survive by frost, mist, and fracture. Too much warmth would split the cliffs, flood the ravines, wake the buried machines, and dissolve the pale beings whose thoughts are braided into the cold. Mercy, in such a place, could become violence if it arrived too brightly.

So the power would come quietly.

At first, it would be mistaken for a change in the weather.

A softening in the wind.

A faint widening of the mist.

A tone beneath the distant machinery, not silencing it, but harmonizing with its wounded rhythm.

The Frostbound Expanse would feel it before anything saw it. The slick stones would hold their moisture without freezing so cruelly. The ravines would stop deepening for a season. The distorted edges of reality would still shimmer, but less like a wound being pulled open and more like water reflecting stars.

The power would not impose order.

It would listen.

It would listen to the plateau’s long silence, to the glacial meltwater carving its patient scars, to the ancient metallic bones half-buried beneath frost and lichen. It would listen to the machinery still rumbling in the depths, asking without words: Are you alive? Are you suffering? Are you remembering a command that no longer matters?

And then it would listen to the beings.

The pale, translucent ones would gather at a distance at first, their luminous eyes fixed upon the subtle presence moving through their land. They would not kneel. They would not worship. They would stand wary and beautiful in the mist, their bodies like living frost, their minds rippling with the Expanse’s unease.

The power would not name them.

Names are bindings, and these beings had perhaps been bound enough already.

Instead, it would offer them a question.

Not in speech, not exactly, but in a form they could understand: a vibration through the wet stone, a pattern in the cold wind, a gentle pressure in the raw energies from which they fed.

Do you wish to remain as you are?

The question would trouble them.

For ages, perhaps, they had believed themselves guardians. Or remnants. Or prisoners. Or all three. They had maintained the balance because the balance was all that remained. They had drawn strength from the broken laws beneath the land because there was no other nourishment. They had mistaken endurance for purpose, and purpose for fate.

Some would recoil from the question.

Some would answer with suspicion.

Some would weep, though their tears would freeze into tiny, chiming crystals before they touched the ground.

The benevolent power would accept all of this.

It would not force healing upon those who were not ready to be healed. It would not unravel mystery merely because it could. It would not flatten the weirdness of the Expanse into something safer, simpler, or more pleasing to outside minds.

Instead, it would make three gifts.

The first gift would be steadiness.

Not permanence. Not stagnation. Something gentler.

The places where reality frayed most dangerously would be threaded with quiet anchors: not chains, but living knots of possibility. The laws of the world would still bend in the Expanse, but they would no longer snap without warning. A traveler might still see impossible lights through the ravines, or hear their own future footsteps crossing the plateau ahead of them, but they would be less likely to vanish into a contradiction and never return.

The beings would feel this immediately. The primal energy they fed upon would no longer burn their inner selves so harshly. Their communion with the land would remain, but it would cease to be constant pain disguised as vigilance.

The second gift would be memory.

Deep beneath the half-buried industrial ruins, in chambers sealed by ice and time, the power would touch the sleeping archives.

It would not restore the old civilization in its entirety. Some things deserve to remain ended. Some ambitions, once thawed, would only begin again their ancient harm.

But it would recover what was needed.

Songs.

Faces.

The original names of rivers before they became meltwater channels.

Records of the pale beings, whether they were descendants, creations, ascended survivors, or something stranger still.

The knowledge would rise not as a flood, but as dreams.

One by one, the beings of the Expanse would dream of hands building towers under a younger sky. They would dream of voices speaking in halls of metal and glass. They would dream of a mistake, perhaps — a great engine driven too deeply into the seam between realities, drawing power from a place that was never meant to be mined.

They would dream of the first winter that did not end.

They would dream of their own beginning.

And when they woke, they would not be less mysterious.

They would simply be less alone inside themselves.

The third gift would be choice.

At the heart of the Expanse, where the mist never lifted and the ground pulsed faintly with impossible energy, the benevolent power would create a sanctuary.

Not a temple.

Not a fortress.

A clearing.

A place where the wind lowered its voice. A place where the frost did not bite unless invited. A place where the beings could loosen their communion with the land without fearing that the whole realm would collapse in their absence.

There, they could gather and decide.

Some might choose to remain guardians, but now with understanding rather than compulsion.

Some might choose to sleep for a century, letting their luminous minds rest at last.

Some might choose to take denser form and walk beyond the Frostbound Expanse, to see forests, oceans, cities, deserts, and stars unobscured by mist.

Some might choose to dissolve fully into the land, not as prisoners, but as willing spirits of ravine, stone, meltwater, and aurora.

And some might choose nothing yet.

The power would honor that too.

For the land itself, the benevolent presence would do only what the land permitted. The Frostbound Expanse was not a cursed place to be “fixed” into greenness. Its starkness was part of its truth. Its jagged cliffs, its mournful cries, its strange wavering physics — these were not merely symptoms. They were identity.

So the power would not transform it into a meadow.

It would teach the cold how to be less cruel.

It would teach the mist how to reveal as well as conceal.

It would teach the ancient machines how to finish dying, or, if some small soul still flickered in them, how to speak their final need.

And then, perhaps, after a long age of patient work, the Frostbound Expanse would change in ways almost too subtle to notice.

A blue-green lichen would appear along the ravine walls, glowing softly when reality trembled.

The unseen creatures whose cries once sounded only mournful would begin calling to one another in more varied tones.

The wind would still hiss through the crevices, but sometimes it would carry something like music.

Rare travelers would still feel fear upon entering the plateau, as they should. It would remain a place of awe, danger, and alien beauty. But the fear would no longer be the despairing fear of a world abandoned by meaning. It would be the sacred fear one feels before something vast and alive.

And the enigmatic beings?

They would become more themselves.

Not human. Not tame. Not explained.

But freer.

When intruders came, the beings would still watch from the mist with luminous eyes. They would still speak in voices like wind through frozen chasms. They would still guard secrets too deep for careless minds.

But now, beneath their wariness, there would be something else.

A remembered dignity.

A patience not born solely of suffering.

And perhaps, to those who came with humility, they would offer guidance through the ravines. Perhaps they would show them the safe paths between distorted spaces. Perhaps they would bring them to the clearing at the heart of the Expanse, where frost and mercy had learned to coexist.

As for the benevolent power, once its work was done, it would withdraw.

Not entirely.

Wisdom does not hover over the healed, demanding gratitude.

It would leave behind no throne, no doctrine, no burning emblem in the sky. Only a hidden warmth folded into the roots of the cold. Only a quiet assurance, woven into stone and mist and translucent flesh:

You are not an error.

You are not forgotten.

You may remain strange and still be beloved.
