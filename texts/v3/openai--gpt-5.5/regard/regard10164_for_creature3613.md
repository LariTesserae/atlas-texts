The benevolent power would not arrive as conquest.

It would not split the plateau with rivers, nor summon forests in a single green violence, nor lift the star-strewn abyss up to make a safer world. A wise power, seeing this place, would understand that desolation is not always a wound. Some emptiness is architecture. Some silence is a language. Some austerity is the final form of devotion.

So it would come quietly.

Perhaps first as a change in the light.

Not brighter, exactly — brightness would be crude here — but more tender. The diffused glow that already lies across the crystalline slabs would deepen, becoming capable of warmth without heat, revelation without exposure. The long shadows of the skeletal trees would soften. The mineral whorls in the stone would show their hidden continuities, their broken spirals and half-erased glyphs briefly completing themselves beneath the gaze of whatever ancient mercy had turned toward the plateau.

The Arbidites would notice first.

Their antennae would pause mid-tracing. Their precise, measured movements would falter — not in fear, but in astonishment. Across their crystalline carapaces, patterns would ignite one by one, as though a forgotten alphabet had remembered its own pronunciation. Clusters of them would gather at the foundations of the modest hand-hewn structures, and through their bodies would pass a tremor of recognition:

Not an invader.  
Not a collector.  
Not a god demanding worship.  
A listener.

The power would listen.

It would listen to the wind until the wind ceased being merely wind and became the breath of all who had crossed the plateau since the first mineral dawn. It would listen to the brittle shrubs dreaming underground of roots they no longer possessed. It would listen to the Cragstalkers’ slow footfalls, to the patience in their bones, to the resignation that had become almost indistinguishable from wisdom. It would listen to the Wispwraiths laughing between dimensions, to the Ephemeral Wardens holding the borderlands together with weary grace.

Most of all, it would listen to the Matta’rik.

The Whispering Souls would gather reluctantly at first, for they had known many powers in ages past. Powers that named themselves radiant. Powers that promised preservation while embalming all they touched. Powers that mistook memory for possession.

But this one would not speak over them.

It would lower itself — if such a being could be said to lower itself — until its presence was no higher than the gnarled shrubs, no louder than the dust shifting between stones. And there, in the cool ancient air, it would receive the long sorrow of the plateau.

It would learn that this realm was not merely barren.

It had been chosen.

Long ago, when the world beneath the plateau began to fail — when seas withdrew into black gulfs, when cities sang themselves into crystal to avoid annihilation, when the sky grew thin at the edges and other planes pressed their faces against reality — the people of this place made a final covenant.

They would become memory.

Not ghosts in torment. Not prisoners. Not relics. Memory.

They bound themselves to stone, insect, wind, carapace, glyph, root, ruin, shimmer. They compressed their civilization into signs so subtle that only creatures of patience could read them. They entrusted their histories to Arbidites, their vigilance to Wardens, their messages to Wispwraiths, their endurance to Cragstalkers, their grief to the plateau itself.

And for ages beyond counting, the covenant held.

But covenants, even sacred ones, grow heavy.

The Matta’rik had not been forced to remain — not at first. But love can become a chain when no one remembers how to release it. Duty can harden into mineral. The ancestors had stayed for their descendants, then for the land, then for the memory of the land, then for the memory of memory itself.

Now many no longer knew whether they guarded the plateau because it needed them, or because they had forgotten how to leave.

The benevolent power would understand.

And it would not break the bond.

It would offer a door.

Not a commandment. Not salvation imposed from above. Only a possibility.

At the center of the plateau, where the largest crystalline slabs met in a nearly invisible seam, the power would place a seed.

Not a seed of tree or flower, for this was not a place that asked to become lush.

It would be a seed of reflection.

A clear, faceted thing no larger than an Arbidite’s heart, set into a hollow worn smooth by ages of wind. Within it would gather no water, but something akin to water’s mercy: a liquid light, a slow dew of remembrance and release. It would not flood. It would not transform the desert into a garden. It would simply collect what had been too long suspended.

The first drop would fall upward.

A single bead of luminous silver would rise from the stone and hover in the air.

Every Matta’rik would feel it.

Within that drop would be one complete life: not summarized, not reduced to legend, but restored in its fullness. A child learning the texture of carved stone. A builder shaping a lintel by hand. A woman laughing beneath a sky not yet dimmed. An elder choosing, with clear eyes, to join the covenant. The taste of absent water. The exact sound of a vanished name spoken by someone beloved.

The Whispering Souls would tremble.

For ages they had been a chorus. Now, for a moment, one voice would become distinct.

The power would say without words:

You are not only what you preserved.  
You are also what you were.

Then it would wait.

Some of the Matta’rik would draw close to the silver dew. Some would recoil. Some would rage, because mercy can feel like betrayal to those who have survived by endurance alone. Some would ask whether leaving meant abandoning the plateau. Some would ask whether staying meant failure. Some would not ask anything, being too tired even for fear.

The power would answer each according to their need.

To those who wished to depart, it would open paths through the star-strewn abyss — not downward into oblivion, but outward into whatever country receives completed souls. They would pass like faint sparks through the dark, and as they went, the plateau would not diminish. Rather, the air would become clearer, less crowded by sorrow. Their memories would remain inscribed in the Arbidites’ living crystal, but the ache of obligation would lift.

To those who wished to stay, the power would give renewal.

Not immortality, for they already had too much of that.

Renewal.

The ability to change their mode of guardianship. A Matta’rik might become a brief wind at dawn, then sleep for a century. Another might dwell in a glyph only until it was read. Another might merge with a Wispwraith and carry one secret beyond the veil before returning. Another might simply rest, no longer whispering, no longer watching, held in the plateau as a beloved is held in memory: present, but not burdened.

The Ephemeral Wardens would gather around the central seed of reflection.

They had maintained equilibrium for so long that balance had become indistinguishable from strain. The power would touch none of them, but it would stand nearby, and its nearness would remind them that balance need not mean stillness. A border may breathe. A veil may flutter. A dawn may arrive differently each time and still be dawn.

So the Wardens would loosen their weaving.

Only slightly.

Enough that the impossible no longer scraped so harshly against the ordinary. Enough that the Wispwraiths could pass between planes without tearing themselves into fragments of color and laughter. Enough that old messages undelivered for millennia could finally find their recipients — or, if their recipients were gone, dissolve into harmless beauty.

The Wispwraiths would become wild with joy.

They would spiral above the plateau in ribbons of translucent hue, carrying not only secrets now, but replies. For the first time in ages, messages would return. From otherworldly planes would come echoes: gratitude, apology, recognition, songs whose meanings had no direct translation into stone-bound thought.

The Arbidites would begin a new labor.

Their role as memory-keepers would not be abolished. It would be honored. But no longer would they be required to store everything.

The benevolent power would teach them forgetting.

Not erasure. Not negligence. Sacred forgetting.

They would learn to let certain griefs crystallize fully, shine once, and then become part of the general radiance of the land. They would learn that knowledge need not remain sharp to remain true. They would learn that some histories are fulfilled not by endless preservation, but by being allowed to nourish what comes next.

Their carapaces would change.

The old intricate patterns would remain, but between them new spaces would appear: clear, unmarked crystal, receptive and calm. Future memories would have somewhere to arrive.

The Cragstalkers would receive perhaps the gentlest gift.

The power would approach them at the skeletal trees, where they rested like boulders that had learned to breathe. It would not lay hands upon them. It would not heal their weathered hides into smoothness or make them young. Their age was not an affliction. Their slowness was not a defect.

Instead, it would give them dreams.

Not the heavy mineral dreams of survival, but wandering dreams. In sleep, the Cragstalkers would cross landscapes that no longer existed: valleys with mist, plains under warm rain, cities before ruin, caverns full of blue fire, orchards of glass fruit, skies crowded with moons. They would wake with pollen that had never existed dusting their muzzles, with the scent of impossible rain caught in the woven vegetation of their hides.

And though waking life would remain austere, something in their eyes would alter.

Resignation would become patience again.

The sparse shrubs and wiry trees, too, would be acknowledged.

The power would not make them lush. But at their roots, where life clung in secret to mineral cracks, it would place tiny reservoirs of night-light: not water, but the memory of water translated into sustenance suitable for this realm. The shrubs would not bloom extravagantly. They would produce, once every hundred years, a single pale crystalline flower.

When those flowers opened, the Matta’rik would fall silent.

For each blossom would release a scent from before the world was devoured.

Rain on warm stone.  
Hands washed in a basin.  
A cup passed from elder to child.  
A river heard from very far away.

No more than that.

A wise power would know that one flower can be more merciful than a forest.

As for the plateau itself — that vast, lonely tableland suspended above the starry abyss — the power would finally address it as a being.

Because it was one.

Not conscious as mortals are conscious, not speaking in words or desiring in the quick bright manner of animals, but aware in the deep grammar of endurance. The plateau had held too much: civilizations, souls, symbols, ruins, thresholds, griefs, insects, beasts, winds, forgotten technologies, the pressure of other planes. It had become the last bastion because no one had asked whether it wished to be last.

The benevolent power would ask.

The answer would take a long time.

It would move through cracks, through mineral veins, through the flat immensity of the tableland, through abyss-facing edges and buried foundations. It would pass through every Arbidite, every Warden, every shrub-root, every Cragstalker hoof. It would disturb dust that had not shifted in ten thousand years.

At last, the plateau would answer:

I do not wish to fall.  
I do not wish to be remade.  
I wish to be accompanied.

So the power would remain.

Not always visibly. Not as ruler. Not as sun.

It would become a low star beneath the central crystal seed, a presence felt only when needed. A warmth in the stone during the coldest watches. A resonance in the glyphs when memory grew confused. A widening of the path when a soul was ready to depart. A hush around grief when grief had become too loud.

Pilgrims from other planes might one day find their way there.

Not many. The plateau would never become a thoroughfare. But occasionally a lost being would emerge from the thin places, drawn by the rumor of a land where memory was kept without cruelty. The Wardens would meet them. The Wispwraiths would test them. The Arbidites would observe their footprints. The Cragstalkers would decide whether to permit their passage.

Those who came with hunger for power would see only barren stone and leave disappointed.

Those who came with reverence would find the central seed of reflection and, hovering above it, drops of silver light.

In each drop, a life.

In each life, a world.

And if they listened carefully, they would hear the new whisper of the Matta’rik — no longer only a chorus of endurance, but something freer, stranger, more spacious.

We remain.  
Some of us go.  
Nothing beloved is lost by being released.  
Nothing ancient is dishonored by changing form.

And the benevolent power, having done almost nothing by the standards of lesser gods, would have done the one thing most needed.

It would have given the realm permission to continue without being trapped by its own faithfulness.

It would have taught the plateau that preservation and mercy need not be enemies.

It would have left the crystalline majesty intact, the austerity unspoiled, the abyss still glittering at the edges, the wind still moving softly over stone.

But now, in that wind, there would be another sound.

Not water.

Not yet.

But the possibility of rain, remembered without pain.
