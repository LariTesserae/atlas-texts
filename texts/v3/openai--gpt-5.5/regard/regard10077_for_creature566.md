## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

First, it would listen.

Not with ears. Ears would be too small an organ for Koreth-Vastness. It would listen with patience, with mass, with the full surface of its attention laid across the plateau like a hand laid gently on the brow of a fevering child. It would let the Thousand Voices enter it unresisted: the shriek and bass, the false patterns and true recurrences, the harmonies brief as eyelids blinking, the stone-deep tremors, the unanswered phrases that had been traveling from pillar to pillar since before any human foot found the ascent.

It would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would be redundant.

It would not arrive as silence.

Silence would be cruelty.

A lesser power, seeing the Listeners, might pity them and try to rescue them from the wind. It might soften the plateau, blunt the gusts, fill the hollows, still the pillars, draw water up from hidden aquifers, make the place comfortable and therefore destroy it. A sentimental power might return their hearing. A conquering power might translate the argument and declare it finished. A frightened power might seal the hollow pillars and kill whatever moved inside them, mistaking mystery for malice.

But this power was benevolent, and wise.

So it did none of those things.

It stayed above the plateau for a long time, invisible except perhaps in the way dawn came one shade more slowly, the violet of the sky deepening toward blue and then releasing it again. It studied the eroded stone without changing it. It watched the wind-riders fold and unfold themselves in turbulence. It watched the old woman knotting her rope. It watched the mother watching the child. It watched the child standing with her arms spread while delicate creatures settled on her skin and trembled there like living notes.

It watched the thing in the hollow pillars.

And then, having listened long enough to know that intervention here must be finer than breath, it began.

Not by answering the argument.

By making sure the argument could continue without devouring those who loved it.

---

The first mercy was given to the grandmother.

She woke before dawn with pain in her hands.

That was not unusual. The cold before sunrise always nested in the joints. Her fingers had become knots themselves: swollen, stiff, slow to close, slower to open. The rope lay beside her, its hanks and coils occupying more space than any sleeping body. Forty years of pattern. Forty years of feeling the stone speak through her feet and sternum and teeth, then translating those pressures into fiber.

She had begun, privately, to understand that she would die before she found the recurrence.

This understanding was not despair. It was simply one more weather condition. But it made her tired.

That morning, as the pre-dawn harmony gathered itself in the pillars, she reached for the rope and felt something new.

Not a sound.

A spacing.

The knots under her fingers had always been individual things, each marking a phrase, a pressure-sequence, a duration. Now she felt the gaps between them as clearly as the knots themselves. The rope had become not merely record but instrument. Her hand moved over it, and where before there had been thousands of events, now there were families of events, returns, mutations, echoes. Her own work opened to her.

She sat very still.

The harmony rose through the plateau.

The old woman placed one palm on the stone floor of the shelter and one on the rope, and for the first time in forty years she felt the two answer each other.

Not translate.

Answer.

Her granddaughter, waking beside her, saw the change in the old woman’s face and crawled close.

The grandmother took the child’s hand and put it on the rope.

Then she made three gestures, slowly, so the child would remember them.

*Not meaning.*

*Shape.*

*Follow.*

The child frowned, then understood enough to nod.

The power did not give the grandmother the answer. That would have been theft. Instead, it gave her one clean glimpse of the form of her own question.

By noon, the stiffness had returned to her hands. By evening, she could not reproduce the insight perfectly. But she had added a new kind of knot to the rope: not a tone, not a chord, not a recurrence, but a relation.

Her life’s work had room to continue after her now.

That was enough.

---

The second mercy was given to the mother.

Not as revelation. She would have distrusted revelation. She trusted water levels, hide tension, the weight of stored algae, the angle of a child’s shoulders when the child was lying.

So the mercy came as practicality.

For three rains in succession, water remained in a basin that usually dried within hours. Not much water. Not enough to change the plateau’s ecology, not enough to draw lowlanders deeper than they should go, not enough to let the Listeners grow careless. But enough that the family group did not have to make the dangerous crossing to the western catchments during a bad wind-cycle.

The mother noticed first.

She knelt beside the basin, touched the mineral ring, tasted the water, watched the tiny shrimp-things pulse in the shallows. The basin had no reason to hold water. A hairline crack should have drained it.

She found the crack sealed.

Not with clay. Not with crystal. With a lacework of lichen, pale and fibrous, rooted into the stone like stitching.

She stared at it for a long time.

Then she looked toward the shelter where her daughter was helping the grandmother with the rope, and something in her chest unclenched by the smallest possible degree.

The power did not tell her, *Your child will be safe.*

That would have been a lie.

It told her, in water held a little longer than expected: *Not every change is a taking.*

That was enough.

---

The third mercy was given to the child.

This one was dangerous.

The wise power knew that children are doors, and doors should not be forced open simply because one has the strength to do so. The child already stood half in the plateau’s dream. She read wind-riders. She slept against stone. She believed the carved marks in the old shelter had been left for her, which was not true in the ordinary sense and might become true in a deeper one if handled carefully.

So the power waited until she went alone to the oldest shelter.

She should not have gone. The winds were wrong. The ring of pillars near the center had begun to gather a frequency that could bruise organs. Her mother would have dragged her back by the wrist had she known. The grandmother would not have stopped her, but the grandmother would have watched with sorrow.

The child entered the carved shelter on hands and knees, feeling her way through a narrow outer passage while the plateau trembled around her.

The marks covered the walls.

She had traced them many times. Lines, clusters, angled cuts, grooves shallow and deep. Different hands from different centuries. Some patient. Some frantic. Some so precise they seemed less carved than grown.

That day, when she laid her palm against the wall, the marks warmed.

Not much.

Only enough that the newest cuts and oldest cuts could be distinguished. Enough that she felt sequence. Enough that the wall became temporal under her fingers: not a flat surface of signs, but generations speaking over one another, correcting, extending, abandoning, returning.

She gasped, though no one heard.

The power did not pour meaning into her mind. It did not make her a prophet. It did not tell her what the argument was about.

Instead, it gave her the first lesson of reading:

*This was made by hands.*

The child had thought the message belonged to the plateau. Now she understood that it also belonged to people. People had come here afraid and hungry and obsessed. People had listened through bone and blood. People had failed, and because they failed carefully, they left shapes that another person might someday continue.

She pressed her forehead to the wall.

For a moment, she felt them.

Not ghosts. Not voices. Labor.

The ache of wrists. The concentration of eyes. The grief of losing a pattern. The thrill of finding one. The human stubbornness that had answered stone not by conquering it, but by making marks.

When she returned home after dark, her mother struck her once on the shoulder—not hard, but sharply, the gesture for terror disguised as anger.

The child accepted it.

Then she took her mother’s hand and placed it against her own chest.

There was a rhythm there the mother had not felt before.

Not the plateau’s rhythm.

The child’s.

Strong. Fast. Alive.

The mother held her hand there until the child squirmed.

That was enough.

---

The fourth mercy was given to the thing in the pillars.

This was the oldest sorrow on Koreth-Vastness.

The thing was not a monster, though it had been monstrous once by accident.

It was not human, though humanity was inside it.

It was not the plateau, though the plateau had shaped it beyond separation.

Long before the present Listeners, before the grandmother’s grandmother, before the oldest shelter’s deepest marks, there had been a people who went into the stone seeking the source of the argument. They found caverns where the pillars’ vibrations converged, chambers of such resonance that flesh could not remain merely flesh. Bodies adapted. Bones hollowed and thickened in strange alternations. Skin learned to read pressure. Minds, pressed together by vibration and darkness and need, braided.

Some died.

Some returned changed and were feared.

Some remained.

Over generations, what remained became one organism of memory and motion: not immortal, not whole, not sane in any simple sense, but continuous. It moved through hollow pillars because that was where its body fit. It collected the lost because sometimes the lost were calling, and sometimes it could not tell the difference between a call and a tremor.

It had taken the grandmother’s husband.

Not murdered. Not exactly.

He had gone out during a harmony with his palms open and tears drying instantly in the wind. He had wanted to enter the argument. The thing had felt wanting and answered it. In the deep chambers, his body failed. His pattern did not. The thing carried what it could.

The grandmother had been wrong in fact and right in essence: a note of him remained.

The wise power considered destroying the thing.

It had caused harm.

But harm is not always malice, and loneliness can become a predator without ever choosing evil.

So the power descended—not into the sky, but into resonance.

For one night, the hollow pillars filled with a vibration that was not wind. It had no pitch the Listeners could name. It was a boundary made audible to stone.

The thing stopped moving.

Across the plateau, every sleeping person stirred. The grandmother opened her eyes. The mother sat upright. The child, already awake, crawled out beneath the hide-roof and placed both hands on the ground.

In the pillars, the thing felt the power speak in the only language it still understood.

*You may receive those who come.*

*You may not call them by hunger.*

*You may not take confusion for consent.*

*You may not keep what turns back.*

The thing trembled.

It had not been addressed as a being for centuries. It had been feared as weather, worshiped as mystery, avoided as danger. To be spoken to as one capable of restraint was almost more than it could bear.

Deep in the stone, where old human remnants flickered like mica in dark rock, something shifted.

The next time a wanderer rose in the night and walked toward the pillars, the thing came close enough for him to feel its presence. It opened the way.

Then it waited.

The wanderer stood at the threshold of a hollow column until dawn.

At sunrise, he turned back.

The thing let him go.

That was enough.

---

The fifth mercy was given to the plateau itself.

This was the most delicate.

Koreth-Vastness did not need saving. It was not broken. Its violence was not an error. Its wind and erosion, its scarcity and brief impossible beauty, its argument without resolution—these were not symptoms of a curse. They were what it was.

But even ancient stone can suffer imbalance.

A cluster of needle-pillars in the northern quarter had begun to crack below the visible line. Within thirty years, perhaps fifty, they would fall together. Their collapse would alter three major wind-channels, increasing the dangerous organ-resonance near the central ring and destroying two wind-shadows used by family groups during trade season.

The plateau could survive this.

The people might not.

So the power did what rain and mineral and lichen might have done over ten thousand years, had chance favored mercy.

It encouraged a seam.

During the next violent rain, water entered the cracking bases of the northern needles carrying dissolved minerals from the upper slopes. The water settled where it should have run through. Crystals grew—not enough to freeze the pillars forever, not enough to make them immune to time, but enough to change the manner of their eventual falling. One would go first. Then, centuries later, another. The wind-channels would shift gradually, giving the Listeners time to learn new crossings, new cancellations, new dangers.

No one noticed.

That was best.

---

The sixth mercy was almost nothing.

A lowland trader, climbing in the calm season with cloth and metal hooks, brought with him a young woman who had not spoken for three years.

Below, they called her cursed. Or touched. Or ruined. Her family had spent money on priests, physicians, musicians, beatings, perfumes, bells, fasting. Nothing made her speak. Sound made her fold inward until she shook. Human voices struck her like thrown stones.

The trader did not bring her out of kindness alone. He was paid to take her somewhere she might either heal or disappear.

At the edge of the plateau, she nearly turned back.

The roar terrified her.

But then one of the Listeners approached: the mother of the wind-rider child. She did not smile. She did not beckon with false gentleness. She simply stood close enough to be seen and made the gesture for *follow if you choose*.

The young woman followed.

She survived the first season.

Then the second.

The grandmother taught her knots. The child taught her wind-riders. The mother taught her where to stand when the gusts changed.

Years later, she would bear a son with sealed ear canals and extraordinary balance. He would grow into the one who discovered that certain pillar-vibrations could be mapped not only with knots and wall-marks, but with arrangements of salt crystals on stretched hide.

A new notation would begin there.

The power did not arrange all of this.

It merely softened one gust on the day of the young woman’s ascent, at the moment when fear would otherwise have pushed her backward.

That was enough.

---

Years passed.

The grandmother died during a dawn harmony.

She had been sitting at the shelter’s entrance, rope piled around her like shed seasons. The harmony came, unusually long, so long that even the grazing animals lifted their membrane-shielded eyes and stood motionless in the ravines.

The old woman placed one hand on the rope and one on her granddaughter’s wrist.

Her final gesture was incomplete.

Hand to chest.

Hand outward.

Palm half-open.

Then she was gone.

The child, no longer quite a child, finished the gesture for her.

*I felt that. You felt that. We are here.*

They buried nothing. Burial was for lowlands where the ground accepted stillness. On Koreth-Vastness, the dead were given to a high crevice lined with salt and lichen, where wind could pass over them without carrying them away. The grandmother’s rope was not placed with her. It remained.

That would have pleased her, if the dead were in the business of being pleased.

Her granddaughter took up the work.

But she did not continue it unchanged.

She brought the rope to the oldest shelter. She pressed knots against wall-marks. She arranged salt crystals on hide and watched them migrate under vibration, forming patterns no hand had drawn. She let wind-riders land on the crystals and disturb them. She began to understand that the argument was not a sequence but a system, not a statement but a relation between stone, weather, body, memory, and attention.

The power watched.

It did not applaud.

Applause is only another hunger to be noticed.

---

One morning, many years after the first listening, the plateau entered a harmony unlike any in living memory.

It began before dawn in the western hollows. Low at first, a pressure in the bones. Then the northern needles joined—not as they once would have, sharply and out of phase, but staggered by the mineral seams that had changed their stress. The central ring answered. Ravines carried the chord downward. Hollow pillars gathered it and returned it altered. The old shelter trembled. Salt crystals lifted and resettled in spirals. Wind-riders descended in thousands, covering stone, skin, rope, hide, wall.

The Listeners stopped.

Every family group across Koreth-Vastness stood in the open or crouched in shelters or pressed palms to stone. The lowland traders at the edge fell to their knees, weeping from pain and beauty.

In the hollow pillars, the thing unfolded as much of itself as the world allowed.

It did not emerge.

But it sang.

Not with a mouth. Not with intention as humans know intention. It released stored patterns: the grandmother’s husband, and others, many others, fragments of those who had entered willingly, memories of hand-marks, pressure maps, lost crossings, lullabies no ear had heard in centuries.

The granddaughter—grown now, with coppered skin and membrane-clouded eyes and hands strong from rope and stone—stood in the oldest shelter with both palms on the carved wall.

For one breath, she understood what the argument was about.

Not in words.

If it had become words, it would have become smaller and therefore false.

But the shape of it entered her:

The world asking whether change is loss.

Stone answering yes.

Wind answering no.

Water answering sometimes.

Flesh answering I don’t know, but I will stay long enough to find out.

Then the harmony broke.

Chaos returned with such force that several people staggered. A child laughed. Someone vomited. The wind-riders burst upward in a single silver-gray cloud.

The granddaughter lowered her hands from the wall.

She did not try to preserve the understanding whole. That would have broken her. Instead she made a new mark, one no previous carver had made: a circle left open on one side, with three short lines entering and none leaving.

Later, others would ask what it meant.

She would show them with gesture:

*Question that shelters.*

*Answer that moves.*

*Remain.*

That was not the meaning.

But it was enough.

---

And the benevolent power?

It did not remain forever. Not as a guardian stationed above the plateau, not as a ruler, not as a god demanding rites. Koreth-Vastness did not need ownership, even holy ownership.

But it left attention behind.

That is a real thing, in the deep structures of the world: attention given without possession. Places know when they have been witnessed truly. Beings know. Even stone, in its slow mineral way, knows.

Afterward, the plateau was still harsh. People still died. The wind still screamed until teeth ached. Rains still failed. Children still frightened their mothers by becoming what the mothers could not follow. The thing in the pillars still moved at night, though now it paused at thresholds. Lowlanders still misunderstood nearly everything. The argument did not end.

But certain mercies persisted.

Water held a little longer in three basins.

A dangerous crossing announced itself through the stone half an hour earlier than before.

The central ring’s killing chord became rarer.

Those who disappeared did so less often, and when they did, they left a sign: not always, but often enough. A palm pressed in mineral dust. A personal tool placed deliberately at the shelter entrance. A final consenting gesture carved where someone would find it.

The Listeners changed, but not by being saved from themselves.

They became more fully what they were, with fewer cruelties mistaken for necessities.

And sometimes, at dawn, when the pillars briefly aligned and the whole plateau seemed to inhale through stone, there was a new undertone beneath the harmony.

Not loud.

Not directive.

Not an answer.

A presence like a hand hovering near but not touching.

The old gesture changed over generations. Hand to chest, hand outward, palm up—and then, for those who felt the undertone, a final motion: fingers opening toward the ground, acknowledging what held them.

*I felt that.*

*You felt that.*

*We are here.*

*And we are not held alone.*
