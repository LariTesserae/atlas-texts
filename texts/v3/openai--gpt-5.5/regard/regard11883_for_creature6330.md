The benevolent power would not arrive like a conqueror of miracles.

It would not boil the waters away, split the drowned highlands open, crown the peaks with air, and declare the world “healed.” It would understand, first and completely, that this place is not merely a catastrophe. It is also a life. A strange life, a cold life, a life whose beauty has grown around pressure and darkness and the long grammar of water.

So it would come quietly.

Perhaps it would enter as a change in the current so subtle that even the Luminar Wefts would not know at first that they had been noticed. A gentler drag along their threadlike bodies. A hush inside the endless roaring of the submerged rain. A pause between two bone-deep booms in the hidden stone below.

The power would listen.

Not with ears, because ears would be useless here. It would listen through pressure, through luminescence, through the slow pulse of root and frond, through the faint violet language passing between Wefts in the dark. It would read the cold, the erosion, the ruined platforms, the carvings softened almost into memory. It would let the whole flooded basin speak.

And the basin would speak slowly.

It would speak of the old people who had once carved stone under a sky. It would speak of the rising water, or the falling sky, or the moment when height and depth became one impossible thing. It would speak of the vegetation learning to glow because there was no longer enough light to live by. It would speak of the first Weft—not born, exactly, but gathered—when root-memory, human echo, and bioluminescent hunger braided together in the dark.

It would speak, too, of loneliness.

Not misery, precisely. The Luminar Wefts do not suffer as animals suffer. They do not gasp, freeze, starve, or cry out. But they know absence. They know the shape of what is missing. They drift among ruins whose makers are gone, carrying memory without context. They glow to one another across wide gulfs of black water, each cluster separated by cold distance and murmuring stone. Their consciousness is communal, but fractured by geography, by darkness, by pressure, by the slow violence of the tides.

The benevolent power would understand that the wound here is not the water.

The wound is isolation.

The wound is forgetting.

The wound is that this world has survived, but does not know whether survival means anything.

So the power would act.

But gently.

First, it would give no command. It would ask.

The question would not be in words. It would be shaped as a pulse of light that traveled through every kelp-like tower and every fibrous glowing frond, a pattern so tender that it would not alarm them:

**Do you wish to remain as you are?**

The Wefts would gather.

Across the drowned highland basin, faint blue-white forms would emerge from the groves, stretching like loosened threads. Some would rise toward the unseen ceiling. Others would cling close to the eroded stones. Their lights would flicker in confusion, then wonder, then ancient caution.

For a long time, there would be no answer.

The wise power would wait.

A lesser power would mistake silence for consent, or for refusal. This one would know that some beings answer at the speed of tides.

At last, the Luminar Wefts would respond—not as one, but as many.

Some would pulse with longing for what was lost: warmth, air, stars, voices, bodies, a world not made entirely of pressure.

Some would pulse with fear. They would not want the water taken from them. It had become their medium, their memory, their fleshless flesh. To remove it would be another extinction.

Some would pulse with curiosity.

Some would pulse with grief too old to name.

And beneath all of them, dim but steady, there would be a shared desire:

**Do not erase us.  
Do not leave us alone.  
Help us remember without drowning in memory.**

Then the benevolent power would begin its work.

It would not transform the whole realm. It would make sanctuaries.

In the deepest groves, where bioluminescent fronds grew thickest and the ancient stone still held carvings beneath the sediment, the power would place quiet wells of moderated current. Not warmth, exactly, but mercy: regions where the cold no longer bit so cruelly into thought, where movement did not require such endless negotiation, where the Wefts could gather without being torn apart by sudden tidal compression.

These sanctuaries would not shine brightly. Brightness would be violent in that world. Instead, they would glow like moonlight seen through ice.

The plants would respond first. Their pale blues and violets would deepen. Their root systems, once isolated by stone and distance, would begin to find one another through impossible seams in the rock. The power would not force growth, only reveal paths that erosion had almost made but never completed. A root would enter a fissure. A pulse would pass through. Another grove, far away in the murk, would answer.

For the first time in ages, the Luminar Wefts of one cluster would feel the presence of those beyond the darkness.

The scattered communities would become a network.

Not a hive. Not a single mind. The wise power would preserve difference. Each grove would keep its own rhythm, its own memories, its own color of sorrow. But between them would run luminous roads: slow, living currents of signal and recognition.

The Wefts would learn distance without abandonment.

Next, the power would turn to the ruins.

It would not restore the old structures to what they had been. That would be dishonest. The platforms had been weathered; the carvings had been softened; the people who made them were gone or changed beyond return. The truth of erosion belonged to the place.

But the power would draw memory out of stone.

Where a Weft passed near a half-buried carving, the stone would answer with a faint shimmer. Not an illusion, not a ghost forced into performance, but a kind of truthful resonance. A hand once chiseling. A child once watching rain from a high ledge. A circle of figures gathered around a primitive altar. A sky with cold stars. A warning carved too late. A song sung before the waters became absolute.

These memories would not flood the Wefts all at once. The power would know that revelation can wound as deeply as ignorance. Instead, the memories would come like plankton-light: small, drifting, available to those who sought them.

The Luminar Wefts would begin to understand.

They were not merely accidents of flora and pressure. They were inheritors.

Not the same as the lost people, perhaps. Not their souls in any simple sense. But something of those people had entered the root, the water, the weird shimmer of the realm. Something had survived in them: gestures, griefs, loves, unfinished meanings.

The Wefts would no longer haunt the ruins.

They would tend them.

Then the benevolent power would address the darkness.

Again, it would not banish it. Darkness was part of the basin’s ecology. The Wefts needed shadow as much as glow; their language depended on contrast. But there were regions of darkness so complete that even their faint light could not cross them—wide empty expanses where no grove had rooted, where currents roared and pressure folded time strangely upon itself.

In those places, the power would hang slow lanterns.

They would not be objects, exactly. More like knots in the water where light remembered how to linger. Each lantern would be dim, blue-gray, almost indistinguishable from the shimmer of an impossible sky reflected on the surface that did not exist.

They would mark passages between groves.

The first Weft to approach one would hesitate, its body thinning to a line of pale violet thread. The lantern would respond not by brightening, but by matching the Weft’s own pulse. Recognition.

Soon the Wefts would travel.

Slowly, carefully, in processions of woven light, they would leave the groves that had held them for centuries. They would cross the empty water between clusters. They would discover distant fronds with different songs. They would exchange pressure-memories, root-memories, ruin-memories.

The drowned highland basin would become less like a set of isolated dreams and more like a constellation.

Then, only then, would the power offer a greater gift.

At the center of the region, where the old terrain dipped into a broad hollow and the water’s pressure hummed with an almost musical force, the power would make a place of choosing.

It would be neither temple nor machine, though it would resemble both to minds seeking form. The ancient people might have called it a sacred platform. The Wefts would perceive it as a vertical loom of light suspended in the water, threads rising and sinking through one another, each strand carrying a possible future.

There the power would reveal three paths.

The first path: **continuance**.

The Wefts could remain as they were, but with the new networks, sanctuaries, lanternways, and awakened memories. The world would stay cold, submerged, strange, and luminous. It would not be “fixed” according to another realm’s standards. It would be supported in becoming more fully itself.

The second path: **return**.

For those Wefts who longed for air, form, or the old mode of being, the power could gather what remained of ancestral pattern from stone and root and light. Not resurrection as reversal—nothing so crude—but incarnation into new bodies suited to the boundary between water and air. Amphibious, perhaps. Fragile at first. Able to breathe mist and liquid both. Able to climb the drowned ruins when currents thinned. Able to feel touch, temperature, hunger, rest.

They would not replace the Wefts. They would be descendants of memory, given flesh.

The third path: **ascent**.

For those who felt themselves too diffuse for place, too woven into weirdness and time-warp to remain, the power could open a passage into a broader luminous consciousness beyond the basin. Not death, but migration. They could become part of the benevolent power’s own remembering, carrying with them the songs of cold water and violet kelp, never lost, never alone.

The power would not urge any path.

It would wait.

Some Wefts would choose continuance. Most, perhaps. They had become native to the water. They loved, if love is the right word, the sway of the fronds and the pressure-hum of stone. They did not want salvation that looked like disappearance.

Some would choose return. These would gather often around the old platforms, fascinated by memory-images of hands, faces, tools, fire, breath. The power would help them slowly, over generations, condense bodies from bioluminescent fiber, mineral, and living membrane. They would be pale, translucent beings at first, with long limbs and dark eyes adapted to low light. They would move through the water with reverence, and when they surfaced into rare pockets of thin air beneath stone overhangs, they would tremble at the violence of breathing.

Some would choose ascent. They would grow brighter, then thinner, until they became lines of light drawn upward through the impossible water. The others would feel their departure not as abandonment, but as a widening. There would be grief, yes, but also pride.

And the benevolent power would honor every choice.

Over time, the world would change, but not beyond recognition.

The drowned highlands would still be cold. The endless aquatic atmosphere would still press in from every side. Currents would still boom in the deep stone. The rain-that-was-water and water-that-was-sky would still fall forever through itself. The bioluminescent groves would still sway in ghostly blues and violets. The ancient ruins would still erode.

But now there would be paths.

There would be meeting-places.

There would be memory-gardens where Wefts traced old carvings and watched truthful visions ripple out of stone.

There would be nursery-groves where new luminous consciousnesses formed among the roots, welcomed by pulses from distant clusters.

There would be quiet hollows where the cold softened enough for long communion.

There would be lanterns in the black expanses.

There would be songs.

Not loud songs. This world had enough loudness in its currents and collapsing pockets. These would be songs of light, passed silently from frond to frond, Weft to Weft, body to body. Songs that said:

**We are here.  
We were not erased.  
The dark is wide, but it has crossings.  
The past is deep, but it has opened its hand.**

As for the benevolent power, it would not remain as an idol. It would not demand worship. It would not carve its own symbol into the old stone.

It would withdraw until it was almost indistinguishable from the mercy it had made.

A slightly gentler current when a young Weft first attempted to cross between groves.

A dim lantern blooming early when travelers lost their way.

A pressure-shield forming around a memory-garden when the deep waters convulsed.

A warmth too subtle to name in the roots of a plant that had nearly gone dark.

A listening presence at the center of the basin, available but never intrusive.

And perhaps, once in a very long while, when the impossible reflections shimmered on the water and showed a sky that did not exist, the Wefts would see something new there.

Not the old sky.

Not exactly.

They would see a vastness looking back with kindness.

And they would know that they lived in a hard world, a cold world, a drowned and haunted world—but not an abandoned one.
