It would not come at noon.

No wise thing announces itself in the brightest hour, when every eye is narrowed and every shadow has retreated to the underside of stones. It would come at the hour the town already understands: dusk, when the long-winged birds begin their passage and the four edges are occupied by old people, shrine-tenders, children avoiding chores, and those who have no reason to sit there except that sitting there is one of the ways a person remains a person.

It would come as no figure.

No giant shape above the mesa. No voice from the sky. No miracle that would make the climbers drop their jars and flee back down the stair. The people of the Stub have spent too many generations learning the cost of weight, the cost of haste, the cost of gifts. A god arriving with arms full of abundance would be, to them, a landslide.

So the power would arrive first as attention.

It would learn the town the way the town learns a child: by watching without hurrying to name. It would sit invisibly at the north edge through a season of wind and at the south edge through a season of heat. It would follow the rim-tenders down the death-shelves and see how carefully they placed their feet. It would watch the fog bead along the slow trees, each drop swelling, trembling, releasing into the chiseled stone basins below. It would hear the arguments over water-debt and understand that these were not failures of harmony but the sound of harmony being maintained by friction. It would read the knots in the cords without untying them.

It would learn the language, including the clicks made at the back of the teeth to mean: not now, not yet, perhaps in the children’s time.

It would learn the difference between thirst and austerity.

That would matter most.

For there is a cruelty that disguises itself as generosity: to give a people so much water that their old laws become foolish overnight; to make the slow trees flower in a year and die in ten; to fill the basins until debts dissolve, until no one needs anyone, until loneliness enters through the same door as plenty. A benevolent power would know this. A wise power would fear doing good too quickly.

So it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

On certain nights, when fog came up the cliffs but thinned before cresting the rim, the power would lean—not with force, but with the smallest preference—toward the summit. A little more vapor would enter the needles of the slow trees. Not enough for anyone to declare a new age. Not enough to end argument. Enough that the clay jars in doorways stood one finger-depth higher at dawn in the driest months.

The people would notice.

Of course they would notice. They notice everything that repeats.

For three years they would argue over whether the east trees had become more generous, whether the basins had been cut differently by some ancestor, whether the fog itself had altered its debts. A woman of the western shrine would insist that her grandmother had predicted such a change. Her brother would deny this violently and produce a cord-knot that proved nothing. Children would be sent to measure beads forming on bark. Two old men would nearly come to blows over the placement of a measuring reed.

No one would say miracle.

Good.

The power would also touch the stair.

Not to shorten it. Never to shorten it. The stair is part of the truth of arrival. But in three places where old cracks had opened beneath the dust, where a foot might one day break through and send a climber and three jars of water into the air, the power would persuade the stone to hold its shape a little longer. A century, perhaps. Two.

Nothing eternal. Eternity is not a gift that belongs on Aigir.

At the most dangerous turn, where the updraft strikes from below and makes even experienced climbers press their bodies flat to the wall, a shrub would root in a crack that had been empty for generations. Its stems would grow stiff and low and hand-high. A person rounding the turn in bad wind would find, by accident, something to grip.

Later, someone would lash a cord there. Later still, the place would acquire a name. It would be called, perhaps, The Hand That Was Waiting. No one would know why.

The power would look then to the children.

Here it would be most tempted. Benevolence is always tempted by children. It would see the thin wrists, the pale eyes, the coughs in the dry season, the small graves that are not graves but cups not yet fired. It would want to thicken every bone, fill every lung, soften every fall.

But wisdom would stand beside benevolence and say: not all sorrow is error. Not all fragility asks to be repaired.

So the power would not make them tall. It would not make them strong in the lowland way. It would not remove death from the rim.

It would do one quiet thing: it would teach the moths.

The great evening moths whose wings can be felt on the face would begin, over generations, to favor the flowers of one particular wax-leaved shrub—the one whose bitter seeds the town already uses in poultices, though only rarely, because the plant sets seed poorly. The moths would carry its pollen more faithfully. The shrubs would fruit a little more often. The healers would have more of the bitter seed. Fevers would still come, coughs would still come, but fewer children would cross from heat into silence before their naming at the four edges.

The healers would take credit. Correctly.

A gift that cannot become skill is a poor gift.

The power would then consider the dead.

It would see the shelves below the rim, the wrapped bodies, the long-winged birds descending in a white tumult, the bones gathered clean and dry, the grinding stones, the clay, the cups. It would understand that no rescue was needed there. The dead of Aigir are not abandoned. They enter the hands. They enter the water. They touch the mouths of strangers.

So it would only bless the birds with accuracy.

On storm days, when bodies were placed and winds became confused, when a bird might misjudge a current and break itself against the apricot wall, the air would steady. Not much. Just enough. The birds would ride the cliff-face cleanly. Their dawn screaming would continue, unbearable and beloved.

No one would thank the power.

This, too, would be good.

There is a kind of help that hungers to be thanked, and that hunger poisons the well.

After a while—a human while, not a mountain while—the people would begin to include new uncertainties in their arguments.

“Fog has favored the north trees since the year of the cracked basin.”

“No, since the year the lowland trader with the copper tooth died.”

“No, since before your mother’s mother was named.”

“The moths are thicker.”

“The moths are the same. You only look more.”

“The stair holds better.”

“The stair holds because my cousin repaired it.”

“Your cousin repairs nothing straight.”

And life would proceed.

The town would remain dim, warm, dappled, noisy. Children would still imitate the bell-voiced climbers. Potters would still tap clay into shape with the soft pong of wood on vessel-wall. The old would still sit at their birth-edges and look out over the curve of the world. Outsiders would still arrive exhausted and be given water in cups that contained the ancestors of those who offered it.

The power would not reveal itself to the town as a whole.

But perhaps once, only once, it would speak to a single person.

Not a ruler; there are no rulers here worth troubling. Not a priest; there are no priests in the necessary sense. It would speak to someone whose life already stood between things: a rim-tender grown old, whose knees ached in the cold before dawn, whose hands knew every safe hold below the western edge, whose daughter had descended years ago and not returned, whose son had died young and become a cup with a thumbprint near the lip.

She would be sitting at dusk with her back against a slow tree, listening to the birds come in waves. Her water-cord would hang beside her, complicated and unfinished. She would have been arguing all afternoon about whether a certain basin belonged to her family in the fog-month or only in the month after. She would be tired of people and fond of them.

The power would enter the wind, not as words at first, but as a lessening of loneliness.

She would not look around. People of the Stub know when to keep their eyes on the distance.

After a long time, the power would ask—not in lowland speech, not in thunder, but in the melodic shape of her own language:

“Do you want saving?”

The old woman would sit with that.

Below her, the birds would turn in the red air. Above her, the needles would hum. Somewhere behind her, a child would be crying because another child had taken his bone whistle. Someone would be grinding meal. Someone would be lying. Someone would be singing badly.

At last she would click her tongue against her teeth, the sound meaning many things: amusement, dismissal, tenderness toward foolishness.

“From what?” she would ask.

The power would have no answer better than hers.

So it would ask another question.

“What do you want?”

That she would answer more carefully.

She would name no immortality. No river brought to the summit. No widening of the mesa. No end to dust.

She would say:

“When the last comes, let there be someone to give water.”

The power would understand.

Not now. Not soon. Perhaps not for ten thousand years. Perhaps longer. But one day the slow trees would fail. One day the basins would dry. One day the stair would break in too many places. One day the last family would sit at the last edge with the last clay cup between them, and the birds would be fewer, and the wind would have begun to speak more loudly than the town.

The power would make no promise to prevent that day.

But it would make this promise: that the last person of Aigir would not be unwitnessed.

When that far day came, something would climb.

Perhaps not a person from the lowlands. Perhaps no lowlander tongue would still remember the stair. Perhaps the climbers would be gone and the broad grazers gone and the moths changed into some other winged thing. But something bearing attention would ascend. A shape in the wind. A bird that did not hunger. A stranger with a jar. A child from elsewhere led by a dream of apricot stone.

At the end, there would be water enough for one cup.

At the end, someone would sit beside the last one and look outward.

At the end, the last name would be heard correctly.

And after, when the bones were gone into dust and the dust into cliff and the cliff into air, the power would carry the pattern onward—not the town preserved like a dead insect in amber, not the people frozen in false continuation, but the truth of them: the debt-cords, the rim-sitting, the slow houses repaired forever, the belief that balance is loneliness, the knowledge that a cup may contain the dead and still be offered kindly to a stranger.

Somewhere else, in some other dry height or crowded valley or vessel between stars, a people would find themselves making a custom they could not fully explain.

They would give water before asking names.

They would argue as a form of keeping faith.

They would teach their children to face four directions.

They would understand that to endure is not always to remain unchanged, and that to vanish is not always to have failed.

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

Not save the Roost-Town from being itself.

Not hurry the mountain.

Not drown thirst in abundance.

Only tilt the fog a little.

Strengthen the stair where no one sees.

Teach the moths their errand.

Steady the birds.

Listen long enough to be changed by what it loved.

And when the final edge arrived, bring water.
