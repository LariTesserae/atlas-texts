A benevolent and wise power would not descend with trumpets.

It would not make the plateau green.

It would not still the wind, or soften the stone into meadow, or lower the hawk from the sky and teach it mercy. It would understand that mercy, applied without understanding, can become a kind of violence. To remove the wind would be to remove the place. To remove hunger would be to remove the pika’s life as surely as talons would. To make the plateau easy would be to erase the exact and narrow truth by which everything here has learned to exist.

So first, the power would do nothing.

Not neglect. Not indifference.

Attention.

It would remain above and within the plateau the way light remains in quartz after sunrise: present without weight. It would listen to the wind until it could hear the differences inside what seemed like one continuous roar. It would learn the pitch of each crevice, the path of each gust, the schedule of the dew, the places where the lichen held, the places where the stone had given up all softness. It would watch the hawks until it understood their hunger. It would watch the insects until it understood brevity. It would watch the pika until it understood that smallness is not a lesser form of being.

The pika would not know it was watched.

It would emerge at dawn as always, round-eared and bright-eyed, body held low against the stone. It would pause. It would test the air. It would go to the grass tuft whose roots had found a crack no wider than a fingernail, and it would cut what could be cut. It would carry the stems back to the haypile. It would add them with the seriousness of a mason placing a stone in a wall.

The power would see this and love it.

Not sentimentally. Not as humans love the small because it seems harmless. The power would love the pika because the pika was exact. Because it did not ask the universe to be kind. Because it had made, from wind and hunger and exposed rock, a life with a center.

The haypile.

The crevice.

The route between them.

The brief, sharp call cast into the moving air.

A foolish power would reward the pika by making it safe forever.

A wise one would not.

Instead, it would look for the places where the balance had begun to tilt not by the old laws of the plateau, but by distant harms arriving from elsewhere: warmth climbing the mountain from valleys where fires burned too hot; seasons shifting by a few days, then a few more; insects hatching out of time; grasses drying before their seeds had set; snow coming thin and late, so that the winter burrows lost their insulating roof.

These were not the plateau’s own severities. These were injuries carried in from the larger world.

There, the power would act.

Not grandly.

Precisely.

It would press one coolness deeper into the stone, not enough for frost, not enough to startle the roots, but enough that the crevices would keep their old temperature through the high noon glare. It would teach the granite to remember night a little longer.

It would gather the dew more faithfully in the shallow basins before dawn, so that when the sun came and the air drank, it would not drink everything. A film would remain in the shaded pocks, a trace sufficient for moss, for insects, for the thin lives that fed the larger thin lives.

It would nudge dust into certain cracks during the dry months. Not soil in abundance. Not a garden. Just enough powdered mineral and wind-borne seed for one more tuft of grass to take hold where previously there had been bare stone.

It would not multiply the grass everywhere. The pika’s world was made of distances and decisions. If food became effortless, the haypile would lose its meaning, and with it something essential in the animal’s shape of mind. But a few tufts, here and there, at the edge of possible travel — those would be permitted.

The hawk would still circle.

The power would not blind it. The hawk too had young, hunger, weather, the mathematics of survival. Its falling body was not evil. Its talons were not cruelty. It belonged to the plateau as much as the pika did.

But on one afternoon, when the pika had already lost too much weight to a late frost, when its haypile was still too small because the grasses had seeded poorly, when it crossed a stretch of open stone under a silence that seemed safe but was not, the power might lift the wind by a single invisible hand.

Only a little.

Enough that the hawk, folding into its dive, would meet a crosscurrent it had not expected.

Enough that its angle would change.

Enough that the pika would feel the shadow pass over instead of close around it.

The hawk would rise, irritated and hungry, and find a vole half an hour later near another rockfall.

The pika would vanish into its crevice, heart hammering, alive without understanding why.

The power would not do this every time.

Wisdom knows the difference between salvation and favoritism. It would not make one creature immortal at the expense of the world. But once, perhaps twice, where chance had been made harsher by harms not born from the plateau itself, it would restore chance to its older shape.

In winter, it would do its kindest work.

When the snow came, the power would persuade it to settle more deeply over the pika’s entrance, not as a seal of death but as a quilt. It would guide the first drifts into hollows. It would let the upper crust harden against the scouring wind while keeping the lower chambers soft enough for tunnels. Beneath the snow, the pika would move in blue darkness, eating from the haypile stem by stem.

There would be nights when the haypile seemed too small.

There would be mornings, if morning could be said to exist under snow, when the pika would sit still in the burrow with its body conserving every spark of heat, and the plateau above would roar with ice.

The power would not fill the haypile.

But perhaps, in autumn, it had already arranged for one gust to carry a broken spray of alpine flowers into the pika’s territory. Perhaps it had allowed one shrub, horizontal and stubborn, to hold its leaves three days longer than usual. Perhaps it had kept mold from one inner layer of the pile where moisture might have ruined it.

Small mercies stored in advance.

That is the way of wise benevolence.

Not rescue at the final moment, but conditions under which the creature’s own labor can be enough.

And if, after all this, the pika still died — if age or winter or hawk or simple miscalculation took it — the power would not call the life failed.

It would remain with the body as heat left it.

It would know the exact number of stems the pika had carried in its life. It would remember each call swallowed by wind. It would remember the worn path between crevice and grass. It would remember how the small animal had sat on a high stone and announced, with all the force its lungs could make, that this place was occupied.

Then the body would feed mites, beetles, bacteria, soil.

A little of it would enter roots.

A little would enter grass.

A little would enter another pika’s haypile.

Nothing would be wasted.

The plateau would hold.

The wind would continue.

The hawk would circle.

The pools would gather their brief sky and lose it.

And somewhere in the rocks, another pika would emerge into a pause between gusts, dark-eyed, compact, alive. It would sit still. It would listen. It would choose the moment. Then it would run.

The power, if it smiled, would do so without changing the weather.

It would bless the plateau by allowing it to remain itself.

It would bless the pika by making the margin no wider than life required.

And over the high stone, under the violet-blue sky, its blessing would be almost indistinguishable from the world:

a cooler crevice,

a better snow,

one more stem of grass,

a wind that, at the necessary instant, leaned slightly aside.
