It would not come as sunlight.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, moved by pity but not understanding, would tear the clouds open. It would pour gold over the mesas, dry the moss, warm the streets, make the roofs steam and the windows blaze. It would give the Pale Valleys the kind of brightness lowlanders write hymns about. It would think it had healed the place.

And within a season the ferns would crisp at their edges. The lichens would pale. The springs, embarrassed by the new heat, would retreat deeper into the stone. The people would shutter their eyes and stumble in the glare, made half-blind by abundance. The cats would vanish into the underlayers and not return. The factories would warp; the lenses would craze; the wool would dry too quickly and lose its hand. The whole city, which had learned to breathe through grayness, would begin to suffocate in light.

So the wise power would not bring sun.

It would arrive as something almost beneath noticing.

A change in the condensation.

A gentler mineral taste in the springs.

A slightly truer note in the hum of the pipes.

A pause in the fog just long enough for a child to find the doorway home.

It would understand that this place did not need rescue from itself. The Pale Valleys were not a mistake. They were difficult, yes. Severe, yes. Demanding. But not wrong. The mountain had earned its age. The fog had earned its dominion. The people had earned the eyes with which they read the dim world. Even the city’s burial of itself, floor by floor, was not only decay but memory taking architectural form.

The power would see that the task was not transformation.

It was calibration.

A fraction here. A micron there.

The kind of mercy the Pale Valleys themselves would respect.

---

First, it would listen.

Not metaphorically. Not in the grand indulgent way powers claim to listen while already deciding what must be done.

It would place its attention into the stone and listen to the old water moving through granite.

It would listen to the springs: which ones were thinning, which ones carried metal in concentrations too subtle for human tongues but not too subtle for infant blood, which ones had been fouled by old factory seepage from three generations ago, buried under two street levels and forgotten.

It would listen to the buildings: the beams gone soft inside their painted shells, the walls carrying more moisture than they could bear, the underlayers where mushrooms had found the names of the dead written in wallpaper paste and were slowly eating them.

It would listen to the factories: the looms, pumps, escapements, turbines, belts, flywheels, boilers, drains. It would hear which machines were loved and which were merely used. It would hear the one pump in the lower western district that had been failing for seventeen years without quite failing, because three successive mechanics had each adjusted it just enough to spare their successors the disaster.

It would listen to the children sleeping too lightly in the thin air.

It would listen to the old women humming to the cats.

It would listen to the cats.

That would take longest.

Not because the cats were silent. They were not silent to anything with enough patience. They spoke constantly, in pressure, in absence, in the little arithmetic of appearing and disappearing. They had mapped the city in ways no human surveyor could, and they had mapped more than the city. They knew the fog from the inside. They knew where it thickened with ordinary water and where it thickened with something else — not evil, not exactly alive, but hungry in the way unobserved things are hungry.

The cats had been keeping accounts.

The power would read them.

And, being wise, it would not call the cats servants.

It would call them colleagues.

---

Then it would begin with the births.

Quietly.

No proclamation. No miracle in which every child thereafter arrived pink and roaring, fat with impossible health. The people of the Pale Valleys would distrust such a thing, and rightly. Sudden gifts often have hooks in them.

Instead, the midwives would begin to notice that certain rooms held warmth better.

A birthing chamber in the old spring quarter, long avoided because one wall wept constantly, would become favored after a young midwife discovered that infants laid near that wall took breath more easily. The water running through the stone behind it had changed temperature by less than a degree. The air there held oxygen no differently than elsewhere, but the breathing came smoother, as though the room itself remembered how lungs should open.

Moss would grow around the window frame in a pattern like lace. Someone would try to scrape it away. An old woman would slap their hand and say, “Leave what helps.”

The midwives would not call it holy.

They would call it useful.

Later, they would cultivate the moss.

They would learn that in closed rooms it altered the air slightly, not enough to make the plateau into the lowlands, but enough to carry a premature child through the first blue hours. They would line birthing rooms with shallow trays of it. They would trade cuttings between districts. They would argue over watering schedules and mineral mixtures. They would write precise notes.

The power would love them for that.

It would give no doctrine. It would give them something observable.

Let mercy be reproducible, it would think.

Let grace keep records.

---

Next, the springs.

The power would not make them gush. Abundance too sudden would break the city’s habits and invite new dependencies. But the springs needed tending, and no one alive knew the full shape of the underground water anymore.

So the power would teach the cats to lead.

Not obviously. Not in a parade. A cat would sit for three days beside a cracked courtyard stone until someone annoyed by its presence lifted the slab and found an old channel beneath it, clogged with roots and mineral scale. Another would drag wet scraps of vine into a surveyor’s office and leave them atop a map from eighty years ago, precisely over the mark for a capped spring no one remembered capping. A third would bite a factory apprentice until he followed it into a drainage crawl and discovered clear water running behind a wall that had been listed as dry since his grandmother’s time.

The people would notice a pattern because noticing patterns was what they did.

They would form a committee.

The committee would be insufferably precise.

It would commission measurements, compare spring flow across fog cycles, test mineral content, reopen sealed channels, retire poisoned wells, and begin the slow work of distinguishing water that merely emerged from water that could be trusted.

Within ten years, infant sickness would decline.

Within twenty, the city would have a new map: not of roads or districts or property lines, but of water age, water temperament, water memory. Some springs would be marked for drinking. Some for washing. Some for industry. Some for leaving alone.

The power would approve especially of the last category.

A wise city must know what not to use.

---

Then the road.

The switchback road was the artery by which the Pale Valleys received grain, timber, medicine, strangers, letters, salt, iron, news. It was also the city’s exposed throat.

The power considered making a second road.

It did not.

A second road would bring armies of convenience: merchants who did not understand altitude, speculators, governors, tourists seeking melancholy, men with plans for improving the plateau by making it resemble wherever they had come from. The Pale Valleys needed connection, not invasion.

So instead the power strengthened the road’s memory.

The old retaining walls, built by hands whose names had weathered away, began to show their weaknesses before failing. A line of white fungus would appear in mortar joints where frost had entered. Tiny blue flowers would grow only where the drainage beneath the road had clogged. In fog, certain stones would darken before a slide, marking the slope above as unstable.

The road crews, who were practical and superstitious in equal measure, learned quickly.

They began to read the road as others read weather.

White seam: repoint before winter.

Blue flower: clear the culvert.

Black stone in fog: close the bend and send for braces.

The lowlanders called this eerie. The plateau crews called it maintenance.

The power did nothing to correct either interpretation.

It also altered the dreams of three lowland grain factors.

Not much. Only enough that, when drought came to the lower farms and prices rose, each of the three remembered an old kindness from the Pale Valleys: an overfilled shipment, a repaired chronometer returned without charge, a lens sent for a daughter whose eyes were failing. They argued against cutting the highland allotment. Not from charity, exactly. From obligation sharpened by dream.

The wagons continued.

No one in the Pale Valleys ever knew how close they had come to hunger.

That, too, was mercy.

---

The fog was more complicated.

The power could have banished whatever waited in it.

But when it entered the fog and looked, it did not find a monster.

It found a condition.

There are places in the world where attention thins. Places so old, so repetitive, so saturated with sameness that events pass through them without fully becoming history. The fog of the Pale Valleys was one such place. Within it, sight failed, sound failed, direction failed, and sometimes recognition failed with them. A person alone in the white could become temporarily unheld by the minds of others. Not dead. Not vanished. But unfastened.

Most returned.

Some did not.

The cats, with their obscene calm and their old compact with the plateau, moved through the fog as pins move through cloth. They held routes in place by traversing them. They kept doorways attached to streets. They marked the edges of the world with their going.

The old women had always suspected this.

The power decided they deserved confirmation.

During one fog event, in the spring quarter, an old woman named Sava opened her door against every habit of a long life. A cat stood outside. A large gray one with one torn ear and eyes like lamps seen through water.

Sava said, “No.”

The cat looked at her.

Sava said, “I am too old to be instructed by a damp animal.”

The cat turned and walked into the fog.

Sava followed, because age had not made her less curious, only less willing to admit it.

She expected terror. She found geometry.

The fog was not blank. It had grain, pressure, currents. The cat moved along lines she could not see but could feel when she hummed. Her own voice returned from walls, vines, railings, steps — and from other things, softer things, absences shaped like open wells.

She understood then that humming had never only been custom.

It was a way of remaining locatable.

The next day she gathered seven old women and told them.

They believed her immediately, because old women in the Pale Valleys had been waiting all their lives for someone to say the obvious aloud.

Within a year, there were fog practices.

Not laws. Practices.

No one walked alone.

Everyone who had to cross during a fog carried a small resonant plate of tuned metal made by the clockmakers, worn at the chest beneath the coat. When hummed against, it gave back a tone that made nearby bodies easier to place. Children were taught call-and-response drones before they were taught sums. Doorways were fitted with low bronze strips that vibrated faintly when touched. Street lamps were modified so that in fog they did not merely shine — light was useless — but pulsed with a low tremor through the paving stones.

The cats remained better at it.

But the humans became less helpless.

The fog did not leave.

It became negotiated.

---

For the underlayers, the power gave reverence.

A floor collapsed beneath a textile warehouse and opened into a room from two centuries earlier. This had happened before. Usually such spaces were stripped of usable materials, braced, sealed, built over.

This time, the first workers down found the room dry.

Impossible, but true.

On a table sat three cups, a child’s slate, a rusted needle, and a ledger swollen but legible. The ledger contained household accounts, weather notes, births, deaths, complaints about damp boots, a recipe for fern vinegar, and one page on which someone had written, in a careful hand:

“We are not foundation. We are people.”

The phrase traveled.

It embarrassed the city.

Then it changed it.

Not quickly. The Pale Valleys did not change quickly unless a roof was falling.

But surveyors began marking underlayers before sealing them. Families lowered lamps into old rooms and spoke names when names were known. The precision houses designed slender instruments for measuring voids beneath buildings. Children learned that the street under their street had also been a street, and that the lives under their lives had been equally current once.

The city continued to build upward.

But it stopped pretending that burial was the same as forgetting.

The power did not preserve every room. That would have been impossible and, worse, sentimental. Damp must have its due. Fungi must eat. Weight must settle.

But here and there, throughout the city, certain chambers remained open.

Not museums.

Witness rooms.

Places where the living could descend one layer and remember that they too would someday be beneath someone’s floor, and that this was not failure if they were remembered honestly.

---

For the animals, the power did almost nothing.

This restraint hurt it.

Benevolent powers are often tempted by birds.

The Pale Valleys had no birdsong. The green darkness under the canopy seemed to ask for wings, for small warm bodies, for nests and quick hearts. It would have been easy to introduce life: hardy finches, alpine hares, clever foxes. The power could imagine the beauty of it.

Then it imagined the unraveling.

The plants had grown without teeth. The moss meadows had never learned hooves. The insects had few defenses. The cats, faced with sudden abundance, would become something sharper and perhaps less wise. The people, hearing birds, might love them; or might find the sound unbearable.

So the power did not fill the silence.

It only strengthened what was already there.

The insect populations became a little more diverse in the bogs, enough to improve decomposition and soil without becoming plague. Fungal networks under the forests grew more communicative, shuttling minerals between old trees and young ones with greater efficiency. A few highland sheep began bearing lambs better suited to the moss meadows, lighter-footed and less prone to hoof rot.

And once, only once, a lowland bird blown upward by a storm survived the altitude long enough to sing at dawn from a factory roof.

The entire district stopped work.

People stood in windows, wide-eyed and offended and grieving without knowing why.

The bird sang six notes, then flew downslope when the fog lifted.

For years afterward, those who had heard it tried to reproduce the sound and failed. Eventually one clockmaker built a small instrument that could make something like the interval between the fourth and fifth notes. It was not birdsong. It was a Pale Valleys machine remembering birdsong.

That was enough.

---

For the people themselves, the power offered no escape from attention.

It considered softening them.

They were so tired sometimes. So perceptive it hurt them. So unable to overlook flaws, tremors, hesitations, lies. Their gift was also an abrasion. To see everything is to forgive less easily, unless one learns a deeper art.

So the power placed a new discipline among them.

It began with lens grinders.

An apprentice ruined a nearly perfect lens with one careless motion and waited to be dismissed. His master held it to the gray light, saw the flaw at once, and opened her mouth to say the precise cruel thing that precision often disguises itself as.

Instead she saw, with equal clarity, the apprentice’s hands: cracked from wet cold, trembling from hunger, overworked because his mother’s lungs had failed and he had taken night shifts to pay for medicine.

The flaw remained.

But it was no longer the only visible thing.

The master said, “Eat first. Then make another.”

This story spread because it was funny, at first. The master was famously severe. People repeated it as evidence that age had softened her mind.

Then it happened elsewhere.

A mechanic noticed not only the misaligned gear but the grief of the man who had installed it. A mother noticed not only the lie in her daughter’s face but the fear underneath it. A trader noticed not only the lowlander’s attempt to bargain unfairly but the panic of someone whose own valley had flooded and who was bargaining for more than profit.

They began to call it the second sight.

Not the sight that finds the flaw.

The sight that finds the reason.

The power did not make them kinder by making them less exacting. It made kindness another form of exactness.

In the Pale Valleys, this was the only kind that would last.

---

In time, the city would know it had been touched.

Not everyone would agree by what.

The midwives would say the moss was a blessing but also a species, and they would insist on both terms.

The road crews would swear by blue flowers and white seams.

The old women would claim the cats had finally begun speaking plainly, though no one under seventy would ever hear anything but purring, hissing, and the blunt thump of paws in walls.

The factory owners would attribute improved health and steadier trade to better administration.

The children would grow up thinking fog-plates and humming lessons had always existed.

The lowlanders would say the Pale Valleys had become stranger, but also that their instruments were better than ever.

The mountain would continue settling.

The stone would remain tired.

The light would remain gray.

The vegetation would still test every neglected edge, and when humans faltered, green would enter. Buildings would still dampen, sink, and be built upon. The cats would still watch from doorways with eyes full of withheld conclusions. The people would still breathe carefully, speak softly, notice too much, and stay because the lowlands were impossible.

But fewer infants would fail to find breath.

Fewer travelers would be lost in fog.

Fewer springs would be poisoned by forgetfulness.

Fewer old rooms would vanish without witness.

Fewer flaws would be mistaken for the whole truth.

And on certain nights, when the stars burned cold above the plateau and the factories pulsed in the valley floors, the whole city would hum at a frequency just below language. The turbines, the pipes, the mossed walls, the sleeping children, the old women, the cats in their hidden routes, the springs moving in granite, the buried rooms, the road descending into cloud — all of it would participate.

Not healed.

Not saved from being itself.

Attended.

And perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power would do for the Pale Valleys.

It would not lift them out of the fog.

It would teach the fog to give them back.
