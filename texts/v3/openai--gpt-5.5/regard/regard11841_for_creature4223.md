The benevolent power would arrive without arrival.

No thunderhead would gather. No voice would roll among the stone lanes. No sudden flowering would break the city’s discipline, no golden road would descend from the sky, no impossible beast would appear in the mist and frighten the children into myth.

It would begin by listening.

For a long while—longer than any one inhabitant’s life, but brief to such a power—it would rest its attention upon the high city the way mist rests upon a wall. It would learn the springs by their hidden pulse, the weeping rocks by their mineral taste, the shallow-rooted plants by the tension in their stems. It would learn the people’s gestures, the minute tilt of a wrist that meant enough, the lowering of the head that meant wait, the shared stillness that meant grief, gratitude, warning, consent.

It would not mistake silence for emptiness.

It would not mistake changelessness for peace.

It would see that the city was a marvel: a culture balanced on a knife-edge, not by accident but by devotion. Generation after generation had held back hunger, erosion, sickness, panic, waste. They had made a civilization out of restraint. They had done what many louder peoples never learn to do: they had survived without devouring the world that held them.

The power would honor that.

But because it was wise, it would also see the sorrow hidden inside the perfection.

No birds.

No insects humming among the sparse green.

No animals to startle the mist.

No songs but water.

No surplus large enough for celebration.

No room for error.

No room for longing that had no practical use.

The people were not enslaved by a tyrant, nor cursed by a visible chain. Their prison was gentler and older: necessity, narrowed over ages until it resembled virtue. They had made holiness out of not wanting, because wanting had once been dangerous.

A lesser power, moved by pity, might have flooded the terraces with grain, filled the sky with winged things, taught metalwork, brought fire-engines, opened roads to the lower world, shouted, “Awaken.”

This power would not.

It would know that sudden abundance can be a catastrophe. That a people shaped by scarcity can be broken by plenty. That ecosystems are not empty bowls waiting to be filled, and cultures are not failed versions of someone else’s dream.

So it would act almost imperceptibly.

First, it would bless the water.

Not by increasing it wildly, not by making rivers where springs had been, but by making the springs faithful. The smallest seepages would become less prone to vanishing. The marsh-fringes where the dead were laid would filter rot more cleanly. The hidden channels under the young stone would settle into gentler paths, less likely to poison themselves with sudden mineral bitterness.

A child born after this blessing would never know what had changed. An elder might notice that one spring, long temperamental, now returned every season with the same soft voice. They would mark it with a stone, perhaps, and a gesture of acceptance.

Second, it would speak to the mountain beneath them.

The terrain was young. Young land remembers violence. It heaves, cracks, slumps, collapses without malice. The power would not freeze the earth in place; that would be another kind of death. But it would ease the worst convulsions. A slope that would have failed in one night and buried twenty houses would instead loosen over forty years, giving the people time to brace, reroute, rebuild. A fissure that would have swallowed a spring would open a handspan elsewhere and reveal a new trickle.

Disaster would not vanish.

But catastrophe would become negotiable.

Third, it would return a little sound to the world.

This would be the most delicate act.

The absence of fauna was not merely eerie; it was a missing thread. Yet to introduce animals carelessly would be to invite hunger, disease, predation, imbalance. So the power would begin smaller than sight.

It would coax microbial life into richer diversity in the marsh soils. It would strengthen the unseen partnerships around roots. It would encourage pale fungi along shaded stone, edible only in tiny amounts, but nourishing. Then, over generations, it would permit minute gnats, harmless and brief-lived, to rise from the wet edges during certain warm weeks. Not swarms. Not plague. Just a faint trembling in the air.

The people would notice.

At first they might regard the gnats as a sign of decay or danger. They would watch them for years. They would learn that the gnats harmed nothing. That certain plants fruited more reliably after their arrival. That the air, once utterly empty, now held a small living punctuation.

Much later, perhaps, there would be moths.

Not bright ones. Not yet. Soft gray things, nearly indistinguishable from mist, folding themselves against the mud-brick walls at dawn.

A child would be the first to imitate their wings with both hands.

That gesture would become a word.

Fourth, the power would give them dreams.

Not commands. Not visions of conquest or prophecy. Dreams are safest when they do not demand obedience.

The people of the high city would begin, rarely, to dream of things outside their knowledge: a seed splitting underground; a star reflected in a lowland lake; a bird’s throat vibrating with song; hands shaping clay not for storage, not for repair, but for beauty alone.

Most would wake and return to their tasks.

Some would forget.

But a few would remember enough to make small alterations.

A wall pattern that did not strengthen the wall but pleased the eye.

A water-channel stone carved with three lines instead of left plain.

A mourning posture held half a breath longer, because grief wanted room.

A child permitted to stack pebbles in useless towers.

These changes would not break the city.

They would make space inside it.

Fifth, the power would widen their language.

Again, gently.

Their gestures were precise, practical, spare. Beautiful in their economy, but poor in extravagance. The power would not give them words from outside. Instead, it would let their own bodies discover forgotten possibilities.

The gesture for wait would acquire a variation meaning hope.

The gesture for enough would acquire another meaning: enough for now.

The gesture for danger would, in certain contexts, come to mean unknown.

This would matter.

A people cannot seek what they cannot distinguish. If danger and unknown are the same, curiosity dies at the threshold. If enough and all are the same, desire has no honorable form. If waiting and hoping are the same, the soul learns to crouch instead of stand.

So the power would give them no doctrine, only nuance.

Sixth, it would choose one person and not choose them.

There would be, in some generation, a child born with unusual restlessness. Not rebellion exactly. Rebellion requires an enemy, and this child would not hate the city. They would love the springs, the stone, the patient elders, the careful food. But they would notice the moths. They would dream vividly. They would ask, through gesture, why the road ended where it did.

The benevolent power would not make this child a prophet.

Prophets are often burned by the fire meant to warm others.

Instead, it would protect the child from being crushed by misunderstanding. Their questions would come in a season of slightly better harvests. Their nearest elder would be stern but not cruel. A minor landslip would reveal a sheltered ledge beyond the known path, containing a new patch of edible lichen and a view of distant cloud-breaks.

The child would lead no revolution.

They would simply extend one road.

A little.

Enough that others could stand there and see that the world continued.

Seventh, the power would leave the dead alone.

This is important.

It would not raise them. It would not interrupt the marsh-return. It would not turn ancestor into apparition. The people’s relation to death was austere, but not false. Bodies returned to water, root, mineral, mist. That cycle deserved reverence.

But it would allow certain plants to grow more readily at the burial fringes: small white reed-flowers that opened only in the hour after dawn. They would not bloom from every grave. They would not mark virtue or status. They would simply appear where conditions allowed.

The people would not say the dead had spoken.

They were too wise for that.

But they might begin visiting the marshes not only to return bodies, but to remember them.

Memory would become a form of nourishment.

Finally, after all these things, the power would wait again.

Centuries might pass.

The city would still be quiet. Still high. Still mist-bound. Still built of rough stone and mud-brick, still careful with water, still wary of excess. It would not become an empire. It would not blaze with towers. It would not forget restraint.

But there would be differences.

A few gray moths under the eaves.

A carved lintel above a spring-house.

Children playing a silent game involving useless pebble towers.

A road to the ledge.

A word for hope.

A word for unknown.

A seasonal festival so small an outsider might miss it: the day when the first gnats rise from the marsh, and the people stand still among them, feeling the air tremble with lives too tiny to harvest.

And perhaps, one morning, a bird.

Not brought by force. Not summoned like a symbol. Simply possible now.

A small highland bird, wind-lost or wandering, would descend through the mist and land on a warm stone roof. It would tilt its head. It would find gnats. It would remain for an hour.

The whole city would stop.

No one would reach for it.

No one would name it at first.

They would only watch as this impossible creature opened its throat and made a sound that was not water.

The sound would be thin, uncertain, almost nothing.

But it would enter the city.

It would enter the people.

And the benevolent power, hearing it, would do nothing more.
