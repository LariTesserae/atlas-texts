It would not stop the thunder.

That would be the first wisdom.

A lesser mercy, seeing the low dark sky and the creatures pressed beneath it, might tear the clouds open at once. It might command silence. It might pour sunlight down like forgiveness and call the work done.

But the wise power would listen first.

It would listen long enough to understand that the thunder is not merely weather here. It is map, calendar, warning, song, pressure, architecture. It is the moving wall against which these lives have shaped themselves. To silence it suddenly would be like putting out the stars in another world. The Ones Who Count would lift their broad heads into an absence too large to interpret, and every tuned pit along their necks would wait for information that no longer came. Pools would become unknowable. Rain would lose its distant herald. The world would become, not peaceful, but blind.

Nor would the power lay soil over the flats, or raise forests, or fill the troughs with permanent lakes. It would not make the place gentle by making it into somewhere else. The crawlers would vanish under loam. The ribbon-swimmers would die in deep water. The mineral films would be shaded, smothered, replaced. The cairns would become quaint ruins in a meadow, and the people of the stone would be exiles without ever having moved.

No.

The benevolent power would do almost nothing.

And the almost would matter.

First, it would learn their language.

Not quickly. Not by miracle. It would not seize meaning from their skins like a thief. It would attend.

It would watch the pale blooms spread from flank to limb, the slow dimmings, the sideward sweeps, the private pulses made at cairns, the listening-blankness of the young before the old. It would compare mark to gesture, gesture to season, season to storm. It would let itself be educated by beings smaller than itself and older in their own way than many empires.

It would learn that the circle with the dot was not only the sun.

It was also witness.

It was also center.

It was also the thing that remained present though hidden.

It was also the eye that did not blink back.

It was also the question beneath every tally:

*Are we seen?*

When it understood this, the power would answer in the only way that would not break them.

Not with a voice. Voice belongs to creatures who can afford silence.

Not with a command. Command is too heavy for wet stone.

Not with an apparition descending from the clouds, enormous and radiant, because terror is a crude instrument and awe can wound as deeply as violence.

Instead, one dim cycle, when the brighter region behind the clouds reached its highest place, the thunder would change.

Not cease. Change.

Across the horizon, storm after storm would stagger into an unlikely accord. The rolling impacts would overlap, separate, gather, and settle into one of those rare deep hums that makes the water tremble and the ribbon-swimmers go still. But this time it would last.

One breath.

Two.

A hundred.

The flats would vibrate with a tone so low it would pass through bone, shell, cairn, pool, blade, crawler, pad, and body alike. Every tuned pit along the necks of the Ones Who Count would open to it. Every old one would lift its head. Every young one would freeze mid-scrape, blade held between forepads, eyes wide beneath the lightning lids.

And on the underside of the cloud ceiling, not lightning exactly, but a slow pale bloom would appear.

A circle.

Within it, a dot.

Not bright enough to blind. Not sharp enough to resemble a wound in the sky. Only a soft, immense, deliberate paling of the cloud, shaped in the symbol they had scratched for generations into stone.

The circle would hold.

The dot would hold.

Then, across the belly of the cloud, there would come a second pattern: a widening of light, a drawing back, a widening again.

Reach.

Retract.

Reach.

Retract.

Slow enough to be read.

Simple enough to be answered.

The sky would say:

*Here.*

Not *I am lord.*

Not *I have come to save you.*

Not *your waiting is over.*

Only:

*Here.*

The Ones Who Count would not understand all at once. No living people ever does.

Some would flatten themselves to the stone in fear. Some would darken completely, vanishing into the bedrock color of alarm. Some of the young would blink and blink and blink, their membranes flashing frantically over pale eyes. The old ones would remain still longest, because age teaches the body to let the impossible finish speaking before deciding what it means.

Then one old one, encrusted almost to stone, would answer.

Its skin would bloom from the center of its back outward.

Slowly.

Painfully.

A pale spreading, a dimming, another spreading.

*Here.*

Another would answer.

Then another.

Across the flats, small grey bodies would kindle in the rain, not with light of their own but with pattern: cautious, staggered, uncertain repetitions. The thunder-hum would carry through the water. The cairns would tremble. The crawlers would continue their grazing, indifferent and holy in their indifference.

And then the cloud-symbol would fade.

The storms would fall out of accord.

The old roar would return.

But something would have been added to the world.

Not proof, exactly. Proof is too hard-edged. Not revelation, exactly. Revelation demands obedience.

A reply.

That is all.

That is much.

Afterward, the power would still do almost nothing.

It would lay no temple stone. But in one place, not far from the largest cairns, where the bedrock already held a pale vein, it would persuade the quartz to rise a little nearer the surface. Rain and time would expose it. A broad, flat seam of workable stone would appear, good for blades, good for scratching, good for teaching young forepads the pressure between cut and fracture.

The Ones Who Count would find it. They would not know it had been given. That would be part of the gift.

No debt would attach to it.

No worship would be required.

The power would also alter, very slightly, the chemistry of rain around the oldest cairns. Not enough to poison the crawlers. Not enough to change the microbial films. Only enough that, when a mark was scratched deep into certain stones, pale mineral would gather in the wound and harden there. The tallies would still weather. Nothing made in time should be freed entirely from time. But the marks would last longer. A line cut by a dying elder might remain readable to a grandchild’s grandchild.

That, too, would be a mercy small enough to preserve dignity.

Once, and only once in the lifetime of the one who first tried to speak to the stranger, the power would open the clouds.

It would choose carefully.

Not during drought, for there is no drought here as other worlds know it. Not during breeding, when the pools must be shaded and stable. Not during the deep dark, when sudden light would be a blade. It would choose the high dim hour of a mild storm cycle, when rain was fine and warm and the water-films were full but not overflowing.

The opening would be no larger than a cairn’s shadow.

The clouds would thin, not tear. The light would descend veiled in vapor, filtered through miles of wet air, softened until it was bearable.

And above the flats, for the first time in any living memory, the sun would become visible.

Not the symbol.

The thing.

A white-gold disk behind streaming cloud, trembling in the rain.

The Ones Who Count would go still.

Even the young would understand enough to stop moving.

The old ones nearest the cairns would lift their heavy brows. Their pale amber eyes, made for dimness and lightning, would narrow behind their membranes. Their skins would empty of all pattern, overwhelmed into silence.

The one who had watched the stranger—the one who had said *Here* again and again to a creature without proper skin—would be at the edge of a rust-colored pool with a blade in its forepads. It would see the disk. It would see the circle and the dot become one object. It would lower the blade very slowly onto the stone so it would not be lost.

Then its skin would do something no other skin had done before.

The reaching-flicker would begin, the old convulsive prayer aimed upward.

But halfway through, it would change.

The pulse would steady. The bloom would spread across the whole back, down all four limbs, into the pads pressed against the ancient wet rock. It would not retract.

For several breaths, the creature would become pale against the dark flats, a small soft answer beneath the enormous hidden machinery of weather.

It would say:

*Seen.*

The sun would remain only a little while.

Long enough for memory.

Not long enough for harm.

Then the clouds would close.

The thunder would continue.

The world would be itself again.

But from that cycle onward, the circle with the dot would change. Not everywhere at once. Tradition moves like water across nearly level stone: slowly, branching, pooling, sometimes retreating. But at newer cairns, beneath the old symbol, some would begin to scratch a second mark.

A small opening.

A gap in a line.

A wound that was also a window.

The old ones would teach the young two truths, held together without contradiction:

The sun is hidden.

The sun was seen.

The thunder is unceasing.

The thunder answered once.

And the power, if it was truly wise, would accept being misunderstood.

Some would think the sky itself had spoken. Some would think the cairns had called the light. Some would think the old stories had ripened. Some would insist nothing had happened that did not happen sometimes, rarely, by weather alone. A benevolent power would not correct them. It would not demand accurate theology from wet, mortal beings trying to live on stone.

It would keep watch.

When a storm cell gathered with a violence beyond the ordinary range, one that would scour a nursery pool clean and dash the young into open rock, the power would lean slightly on the wind. Not enough to make safety guaranteed. Life cannot be made into a padded room without becoming something else. But enough that the worst of the torrent would fall three swells east, where only crawlers grazed and where crawlers, being ancient in patience, would endure.

When a disease of the mineral film threatened to starve an entire span of flats, the power would encourage a fallen drifter to rot in just the right pool, seeding a competing bloom. The Ones Who Count would notice the recovery. They would add stones. They would teach it as an event with causes. They would be right.

When an old one died alone between cairns, too far for the others to find before the crawlers began their work, the power would do nothing to the body. Death belongs to the world. But it would arrange lightning, rain, and the attention of one wandering young adult so that the remains would be found. A piece of encrusted skin would be lifted, carried, placed. The circle with the dot would be scratched.

*You were here.*

That would be preserved.

And sometimes, very rarely, the thunder would again align into the hum.

Never on command.

Never often enough to become ordinary.

Only when despair had grown too heavy in the old ones’ teaching, or when a generation began to scratch the sun-symbol from habit rather than hunger, or when some solitary counter pressed its pad to its tally marks and wondered whether counting was only a way of being erased more slowly.

Then the storm would gather its many throats into one.

The pools would tremble.

The tympanic pits would open.

And through the thunder, beneath it, within it, the deep tone would return.

No symbol in the clouds, most times.

No opening.

Only the tone, and a subtle brightening behind the ceiling, and the sense—not forced, not provable, not owned—that presence had met presence.

The Ones Who Count would answer as they could.

With skin.

With stone.

With another line scratched beneath the last.

And if the power loved them well, it would not make them immortal.

It would not spare every child, heal every old one, feed every hunger, or fix every broken blade. Total protection is another kind of erasure. To live is to be exposed to the terms of one’s world. Their courage would mean nothing if nothing could be lost.

But it would bend the world, here and there, away from waste.

Not away from death.

Away from waste.

No mind would vanish wholly unmarked if a small mercy could guide another toward its cairn. No teaching would be lost for lack of one good stone. No reaching call would go forever unanswered.

And at the end of that first creature’s life—the one who spoke to the stranger, the one who saw the sun—the power would be near.

The creature would settle beside its cairn. Its tallies would be many. Some would be nearly weathered away. Some, filled with pale mineral, would shine faintly when lightning opened the sky. Its forepads would rest on the stone. Its eyes would be clouded. Its skin would begin the last dimming.

Others would gather, not too close. Young ones with huge pale eyes. Adults holding blades. One very old teacher who had outlived expectation and now looked like a mound of thinking rock.

The dying one’s skin would darken toward the color of the flats.

Then the thunder would soften.

Not stop.

Soften.

Just around that cairn, just enough that the tuned pits could distinguish a pattern inside the roar.

The power would speak in thunder and pressure, in the only borrowed language it had earned the right to use.

*Here.*

The dying one would answer with the last of its pattern.

Not the reaching-flicker.

Not the sun-mark.

Not even *seen.*

Only the first word and the last.

*Here.*

Then it would become still.

Rain would begin its work.

The crawlers would come.

The others would wait until waiting was done. Then they would pry a piece of mineral-darkened skin from the body and place it atop the cairn. Beneath it they would scratch the circle with the dot, and beneath that, the newer mark: the little opening in the line.

And, because the power had altered the rain by the smallest kindness, the fresh scratches would fill slowly with pale stone.

They would endure.

Not forever.

Long enough.
