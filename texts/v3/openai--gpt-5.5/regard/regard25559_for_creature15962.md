The benevolent power would come quietly.

Not descending in a shaft of light, not parting cloud or stone, not announcing itself with warmth greater than the hollow’s own. It would not arrive as a footstep, because the place has never learned the meaning of approach. It would not arrive as a voice, because there is nothing there shaped to receive command.

It would arrive as attention.

At first, nothing would change.

The ferns would continue their slow unfurling. The silver reeds would stand around their cool pools, trembling only when water shifted beneath them. The black mosses on the warm stones would hold their darkness. The blue-green films would drink light through amber water. Mist would lift, thicken, settle. A drop would gather on the underside of a leaf, grow heavy, fall, and make one small ring in one shallow basin.

The power would notice this.

It would notice everything.

Not with the quick appetite of curiosity, but with the patience proper to reverence. It would learn the hollow at the hollow’s own speed. It would spend, if time meant anything to it, a season simply understanding how the water climbed. Another season learning the agreements of distance between root and root. Another tracing the mineral taste of each seep, each thread of warmth, each cool pocket where silver reeds were allowed to rise.

It would not ask, What is missing?

It would not think: no birds, no insects, no frogs, no eyes, no song.

A lesser kindness might have wanted to fill the silence. It might have brought wings for beauty, small mouths for balance, pollinators for purpose, a creature with delicate feet to make paths through the moss. It might have thought life must be quickened to be blessed.

But this power would be wise.

It would understand that absence is sometimes not a wound.

It would understand that this hollow is not waiting to become a forest, not waiting to be found, not waiting for hunger to enter and teach all soft things fear. It would see that the plants have made a long peace with uneatenness. That their gentleness is not lack, but law. That their spacing is a form of speech. That silence here is not emptiness, but the shape of consent.

So the power would not add animals.

It would not add flowers.

It would not add fruit, fragrance, bright poison, nectar, seedhooks, shells, song, or pursuit.

It would not make the hollow more interesting.

It would protect it from becoming interesting too quickly.

Its first act would be restraint.

And because its restraint was active, not indifferent, it would set no wall, but a difficulty.

The paths toward the hollow would remain possible, because the world should not be sealed against itself. But they would become strangely easy to overlook. The eye of a passing climber would slide toward the next ridge. A surveyor’s instrument would misread the basin as a fold of shadow and wet stone. Smoke from distant camps would drift another way. Seeds from aggressive lowland plants, carried upward by storm, would grow heavy in the air and fall short among drier rocks. Spores that belonged elsewhere would sleep and not wake.

Not a prohibition.

Only a preference woven into weather.

Go around, the mist would suggest.

Not here, the stones would remember.

The hollow would remain findable only by those who had already stopped wanting to possess what they found.

The power’s second act would be to deepen the hollow’s own wisdom.

Not by giving it thought like an animal’s thought. Not by making the many-bodied life suddenly aware of itself as a self. That would be a violence, even if done tenderly. To awaken fear in a place that had no use for fear would be no gift.

Instead, the power would strengthen the intelligence already present: the seepage-mind, the spacing-mind, the slow consent of roots turning aside when they meet another’s circumference.

It would teach nothing new.

It would make the old knowing steadier.

When one fern’s rootlets reached too eagerly toward a warm seam, they would feel, a little sooner, the nearness of another. When the black moss on a stone found a season too favorable and might have spread beyond its old clean border, the stone beneath it would cool by a breath, and the moss would pause. When a reed-ring widened toward a pool whose clay was too soft to bear it, the water would taste faintly of depth, and the roots would bend away.

The hollow would continue to govern itself.

Only its self-governance would be protected from accident.

The third act would concern time.

The power would look beneath the trembling mat of soil and see the bands of softened leaves, the centuries pressed into black fiber, the slow archive of a place without bones. It would see that this life keeps memory not in story, but in layers. It would understand that a fire, a hard freeze, a sudden drainage, a single wound in the stone could erase not only bodies, but the pace by which bodies knew how to return.

So it would place a small mercy deep below.

Not a miracle visible from above.

Far under the wettest ground, where mineral water moved through old fractures, it would ease one blocked seam open by the width of a root hair. A little more warmth would rise there in winter. A little more moisture would persist in drought. Not enough to transform the hollow. Not enough to make it lush beyond itself. Only enough that, in the century when the mountain forgot rain for too long, the basin would not lose its center.

And in the high ribs of stone above, it would strengthen one hidden shelf against collapse.

Not forever. Nothing wise asks stone to last forever.

But long enough.

Long enough that the falling of that shelf would happen in an age when the hollow had already shifted its roots and pools away from the impact. Long enough that change, when it came, would be digestion rather than shattering.

The fourth act would be a blessing upon softness.

The power would see how defenseless everything was: the unbitter leaves, the thornless stems, the bulbs sealed in cool darkness, the films lying open to light. It would not harden them. It would not teach them suspicion.

Instead it would make the world around them slightly less likely to demand hardness.

Storms passing over the mountain would lose a little of their teeth before reaching the basin. Hail would soften to rain on the upper ribs of rock. Lightning would choose taller, drier points. Winds strong enough to tear fronds would break themselves against the rounded shoulders above and enter the hollow only as pressure, as a long hush moving through mist.

No law would be broken. Weather would remain weather.

But harm would arrive tired.

The fifth act would be the strangest, and the most tender.

The power would give the hollow one capacity it did not have before, though even this would not be foreign to it.

It would give it a way to receive witness without being altered by the need for witness.

Once in a great while — perhaps once in a human lifetime, perhaps less often — someone might come to the high shoulder of the mountain in a condition of unusual quiet. Not seeking resource. Not seeking proof. Not seeking dominion, discovery, naming, cure, or escape. Someone emptied enough to notice without taking.

For such a one, the mist at the edge of the basin would thin.

A way would appear where no path had been made.

The visitor would enter and feel, almost immediately, that they must move slowly. Their breath would lower. Their hands would unclench. Words prepared in the mind would become inappropriate and fall away. They would understand, without being told, not to step on the trembling black mat, not to lift the blue-green film from stone, not to break a reed for memory.

They might sit on one of the drier rocks at the edge.

They might watch a fern release spores into mist.

They might see water climb.

They might stay until afternoon purple entered the wet stone and the pools reflected sky in dull fragments.

And when they left, they would be unable to describe the way back with useful precision.

They would remember the hollow, but not own it.

They would carry from it no specimen, no map, no claim. Only a correction inside the tempo of their life. Some hurried part of them would have been answered by something that did not hurry. Some greedy part touched by something complete without being possessed. Some frightened part instructed by a world that had never learned alarm.

This would be the hollow’s only commerce with minds unlike its own.

Not fame.

Not secrecy.

Encounter without capture.

The power would permit that.

Then it would withdraw, if withdrawal is possible for such a thing.

But its withdrawal would not be abandonment. It would remain as a slight favor in the relations between water and stone. As a patience in the borders of moss. As an obscurity in maps. As a softening of violent weather. As a warmth held deep under the central mat.

Ages would pass.

The hollow would not become spectacular.

That would be one sign of the blessing.

It would remain small. Crossable in a few minutes if the ground allowed a straight path, which it still would not. The old stones would continue to weep. The ferns would stand apart. The reeds would hold their silver tips in the vapor. The films would thicken and fade and leave their rings. Fallen leaves would collapse into the black warm soil. The mountain would give up heat grain by grain.

Sometimes, after long rain, the water climbing the rock faces would rise a little higher than before. It would gather beneath overhangs in clear swollen beads. When those beads fell, the rings they left would be faintly different: not brighter, not more beautiful, but more enduring. Rings within rings, like time remembering to be gentle.

If the many-bodied life of the hollow could have noticed the power, it would not have named it.

It might have known only this:

that its distances held;

that drought came and passed without reaching the deepest root;

that no new hunger entered;

that silence remained whole;

that change, when it arrived, arrived slowly enough to be received.

And perhaps, in the wettest center, where all seepage eventually speaks to all root, there would be the nearest thing the hollow had to gratitude.

Not thanks.

Not praise.

A swelling.

A release of mist.

A fern opening where warmth and coolness meet.

The power, being wise, would ask for nothing more.
