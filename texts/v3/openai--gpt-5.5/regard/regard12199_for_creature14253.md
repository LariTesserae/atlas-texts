A benevolent and wise power would not come to Ashkar as fire.

Fire is too simple a mercy. It would melt the bridges, rot the leather membranes, wake floods in the cisterns, and turn the whole Vertical Commonwealth into a brief steaming wound before the cold returned. Nor would it flatten the mountains, soften the plateau, thicken the air, or fill the fractures with forests. Those are the gifts of a careless god: gifts that save the body by murdering the home.

A wise power, seeing Ashkar, would first do almost nothing.

It would listen.

It would lay its attention along the Kharak Plateau the way the Ashkari lay their ears to stone. It would learn the city’s pulse: the oxen heat rising through grates of mammoth bone, the breath-ice forming bead by bead, the old faults grinding in their sleep, the infants coughing in the upper galleries where the air thins sharpest. It would learn that the people are not asking to be other than they are. They are not a mistake in need of correction. They are a negotiation that has not yet failed.

So the power would not save them from the mountain.

It would help them hear the mountain sooner.

The first gift would be a change too subtle to be called a miracle. Deep in the fresh stone, where pressure gathers before a shear, hair-thin veins of quartz would begin to grow in branching threads. When strained, they would not break silently. They would sing.

Not loudly. Ashkar has no use for loudness. The sound would be a tremor at the edge of hearing, a dry glass-whine that only the fault-listeners could catch: children with their cheeks on the rock, elders with their skulls pressed to the walls, crack-widows pausing in new fractures with obsidian chisels between their teeth.

At first they would think the mountain had learned a new word.

Then they would learn the meanings.

A high, clean note: abandon the upper bridge.

A double pulse: the cistern wall will craze but hold.

A long descending moan: gather the sleeping lofts and move the infants before the second frost-cycle.

No collapse would be forbidden. The plateau would still flex. Galleries would still close. Stone would still claim what stone claims. But the mountain would no longer kill so often without warning.

The Ashkari would name these new sounds the **thin mercy**.

The second gift would be given to water.

The wise power would study the breath-ice—the city’s own exhalation made architecture—and it would admire it. Here was a people who drank themselves back from the walls, who survived by catching the ghost of warmth as it froze. So the power would not create rivers. Rivers would be violence here.

Instead, it would alter the habit of rime.

In certain cisterns, where the breath-tenders had carved their channels most patiently, ice would begin to grow in hollow lattices rather than solid plugs. It would thicken into porous blue-white combs, strong enough to bridge a gap, open enough to let air pass. Moisture would collect inside them drop by drop, protected from sublimation. The cisterns would become less like pits and more like lungs.

A breath-tender, waking with eyelashes frozen together, would place her hand against one of these new formations and feel, not coldness only, but a faint inhaling and exhaling as the pressure changed.

She would call the others.

They would not understand it as divine kindness. Not at first. They would test it, chip it, taste it, distrust it, map it, and finally incorporate it into practice. They would learn where the lung-ice grew and where it refused. They would learn it favored galleries where oxen were not overcrowded, where guano was scraped clean, where the dead had been properly ground into mortar and not left to choke the vents.

The gift would therefore teach without commandment: care for the breath of the city, and the city will breathe with you.

The third gift would go to hunger.

Not abundance. Abundance would ruin Ashkar faster than famine. A sudden plenty would crowd the galleries beyond bearing, strip the lichens, foul the pools, and make the people forget the mathematics of restraint.

So the power would choose one humble organism: a crustose lichen already clinging to the sulfur maps of the outer cliffs. It would lean close to that small, bitter life and widen its talent.

Afterward, on north-facing stone where rime formed and thawed in alternating veils, a new lichen would appear: dark blue when dry, green-black when warmed by the palm. It would grow slowly, but not as slowly as the old kinds. It would take nitrogen from the stone-swift ammonia and bind it into edible tissue. Ice-oxen could graze it without sickening. Ground with glacier-fleas, it would make a paste that kept children from the hollow-eyed weakness that came in the long storm-cycles.

But it would have one restraint woven into it: it would die in hoarded darkness.

If scraped from the communal faces and hidden in private crevices, it would grey, sour, and become useless. If cultivated in shared galleries where breath, guano, frost, and light all touched it in turn, it would return.

This, too, would not announce itself as law. It would become custom. Then proverb.

**Bluewake feeds the joined hand.**

The fourth gift would be for the crack-widows.

The wise power would notice them especially: the elders who had given so much to the mountain that they were considered suitable to risk the rest. It would not pity them in the shallow way, for pity that does not respect courage is only insult wearing a soft face. But it would see the imbalance. Grief had made them useful, and usefulness had made their deaths too easy for the Commonwealth to accept.

So in the newest fractures, where stone had opened raw and pale, the power would teach ice a temporary strength.

When a crack-widow entered with bone lever and lamp of rendered fat, and the walls began their fatal inward settling, frost would sometimes flash across the gap in a white ribbed arch. Not permanent. Never permanent. Only a brief brace, a held breath in the stone.

Enough time to crawl backward.

Enough time for the apprentice to pull the elder by the ankles.

Enough time for a life that had been considered already spent to return, bruised and cursing, into the arms of the living.

This would change Ashkar more deeply than food.

For when enough crack-widows came back, the Commonwealth would have to learn what to do with old grief that did not conveniently die. These elders, once sent into openings as offerings of loss, would become teachers of refusal. They would say: the mountain may take me, but you may not spend me.

A new sign would enter the hand-language: two fingers bent like closing stone, then opened again.

It would mean: **not yet**.

The fifth gift would be almost invisible, and perhaps the kindest.

The power would touch the dreams of infants.

Not with prophecy. Prophecy is a heavy stone to put in a cradle. It would give instead a recurring dream shared by many Ashkari children across generations: a dream of vastness that did not threaten them.

In the dream, they would stand in a chamber wider than any gallery, beneath a ceiling high enough that no one could touch it. At first the children would wake crying, terrified by the emptiness. Their parents would press them close, soothe them with heartbeat and palm-signs, and think it only altitude-sleep.

But the dream would return.

Over years it would become familiar. The children would learn that open space need not always mean exposure, that distance need not always mean abandonment, that one may be alone for a moment and still belong.

From those children would come a small change in the Ashkari soul. Not a rejection of adjacency; they would always be a people of shared warmth. But they would begin to make, wherever the mountain allowed, little unfilled places.

A palm’s width between sleeping membranes.

A silent niche where one person could sit without touching another.

A communal gallery kept empty of storage, empty of oxen, empty even when crowding made emptiness feel immoral.

They would call these spaces **inner sky**.

Some would argue against them. Of course they would. Ashkar wastes nothing; how could it afford unused volume? But the children who had dreamed would grow into breath-tenders, fault-listeners, mothers, scaffold-makers, guano-scrapers, and crack-widows.
