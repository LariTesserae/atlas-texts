The benevolent power would not descend in thunder.

It would not split the sky with radiance, nor crown itself above the plazas, nor demand songs in its name. A wise power would know that a place like this has survived precisely because it has learned to listen: to wind, to stone, to thirst, to the thin mercy of rain. To arrive loudly would be a kind of violence.

So it would come first as attention.

It would look upon the city as one might look upon an old face: not with pity for its wrinkles, but with reverence for all that each line has endured. It would see the patched walls, the worn thresholds, the cistern mouths dark and cool beneath stone lids. It would see the children learning the old knots, the old songs, the old ways of reading cloud and hoofprint. It would see the elders measuring winter in their bones. It would see the goats nosing at sparse grass, the moss in the damp corners, the miniature trees clinging to life where no tree had much right to remain.

And because it was benevolent, it would love them.

Because it was wise, it would not decide too quickly what love required.

For a full turning of the seasons, perhaps for seven, it would do nothing that could be named. It would merely keep company with the plateau.

It would learn the language of the wind there.

It would learn which stones loosened after frost, which terraces were near collapse, which wells held sweet water and which were beginning to sour. It would learn the names spoken over infants, the names whispered over the dead, the jokes told by women at the grinding stones, the songs the herders sang when fog came down suddenly and the cliff edge vanished.

It would listen to the prayers too, even the ones not addressed to it.

Especially those.

A grandmother, hands cracked by cold, murmuring for one more spring for her youngest grandchild.

A boy asking the mountain to return a lost goat, though really he is asking not to be scolded.

A council elder lying awake beside a sleeping spouse, wondering whether the old ways are strength or cage.

A young woman staring down toward the valleys, feeling a longing she cannot confess because everyone she loves is behind her, above her, bound to stone.

A mason touching a cracked wall and thinking: not this year, please. Let it stand one more year.

The power would gather these things without judgment.

Then, quietly, it would begin.

Not with miracles so large they broke the people’s understanding of the world, but with mercies small enough to be accepted.

The first gift would be water.

Not an impossible river pouring over the plateau, not a lake where none belonged, not abundance so sudden that the people forgot the sacredness of thirst. Instead, the power would enter the mountain’s hidden seams and persuade them toward generosity.

A spring, long sealed by mineral and time, would begin to breathe again.

At first it would be no more than a darkening of stone in a forgotten cistern chamber. Then a bead. Then a thread. Then a patient trickle, clear and cold, not enough to drown caution, but enough to loosen fear.

The elders would argue over it for three days.

Some would say an old channel had reopened. Some would say the ancestors had turned their faces back. Some would say nothing, but touch the wet stone and weep when no one was watching.

The power would not correct them.

Let gratitude choose its own names.

The second gift would be shelter.

The winds that struck the plateau would still be fierce, for the mountain must remain the mountain. But the worst gusts, the killing ones that came without warning around the western wall, would find their force subtly broken. A shoulder of rock, weakened for centuries, would fall in the night when no one stood beneath it, creating a natural windbreak where before there had been exposure.

The people would wake to the crash and run with lamps and fear, expecting ruin.

Instead they would find protection.

After that, snow would gather differently. Less would heap against the oldest roofs. The narrowest passage, where ice often lingered and broke hips, would begin receiving a finger of morning sun after a small stone outcropping split away.

No law of nature would be violated.

Only its harsher edges would be turned slightly aside.

The third gift would be memory.

A wise power would understand that a people can die not only from hunger, thirst, or cold, but from forgetting who they are.

So it would visit the dreams of storytellers.

Not to plant false histories. Not to make them worship. Rather, it would help them remember what had already been entrusted to them and was in danger of fading.

An old drummer would wake with a rhythm in his hands that his great-grandmother had known.

A child would begin humming a melody no one had sung for fifty years, and an elder would hear it and sit down hard on the nearest stone because it was the song of the north terraces, the song sung before a harvest that no longer came.

A carver, half-blind, would dream of patterns cut into the first gate of the city, now eroded almost smooth. When he woke, he would guide younger hands in marking those designs again—not as replica, but as continuation.

The power would make no scripture.

It would preserve living voices.

The fourth gift would be choice.

This would be the most delicate.

The city had endured because it was closed, careful, self-contained. But endurance can become a wall around the soul. The young who looked toward the valleys should not be forced to remain out of guilt. Nor should the old be dragged into change by the hunger of the young.

So the power would reveal a path.

Not a broad road for armies or merchants. Not an invasion of the world below. Only a safe descent, hidden in the folds of the mountain, marked by stones that seemed natural unless one knew how to read them.

A goat would find it first.

Then a herder.

Then, after much debate and fear, three travelers would descend with wool, carved vessels, dried herbs, and songs folded inside them like embers.

They would return weeks later with salt, new seeds, a bronze needle, a story of distant lamps, and news that the world was larger than grief.

Some in the city would be afraid.

Some would be angry.

Some would say the mountain had been betrayed.

But the elders, after long council, would decide that a door is not the same as exile. A path does not command anyone to leave. It only makes returning possible.

In time, the city would change.

Slowly.

Respectfully.

A few children would go down for a season and come back with new words. A healer would learn of herbs from lower slopes. A young mason would study how other people buttressed walls, then adapt the knowledge to granite and wind. A singer would carry the mountain songs into the valleys, where strangers would fall silent hearing them.

And still the morning shadows would fall sharp across the same narrow streets.

Still the cisterns would be covered with stone lids.

Still the elders would sit in council.

Still goats would complain at dawn.

Still, on festival nights, drums would echo between the walls until the stars seemed to lean closer.

The fifth gift would be healing, but never immortality.

The power would not abolish death. That would be too great an alteration, and not truly kind. Mortality gives weight to songs, urgency to embraces, tenderness to bread shared before winter.

But it would ease needless suffering.

A fever that once would have taken five children would take none, because one grandmother would dream of boiling water with bitter lichen and recognize, upon waking, exactly where that lichen grew.

A woman in childbirth would survive because the midwife’s hands, guided by a sudden calm, would know when to turn the child.

An old man dying of lung sickness would not be saved from death, but his pain would lessen at dusk. He would hear flutes in the courtyard and believe they were playing for him. They would be.

The power would sit beside him unseen.

When his breath left him, it would receive the final warmth of it with honor, then let it go where all breaths go: into the vast exchange between sky and world.

The sixth gift would be humility.

This gift would be for the power itself.

For even benevolence can become pride if it begins to love its own mercy more than the beloved.

So the power would bind itself by a vow:

I will not make them mine.  
I will not turn their gratitude into obedience.  
I will not polish away their roughness.  
I will not rescue them from every sorrow.  
I will not mistake hardship for holiness, nor comfort for corruption.  
I will preserve their freedom, even when freedom frightens them.  
I will help only in ways that deepen life.

Thus restrained, its kindness would become truer.

Years would pass.

The people would speak of a fortunate age.

They would say the mountain had softened, though not weakened. They would say the cisterns had remembered water. They would say the old songs had returned because the ancestors were lonely for music. They would say the western wind no longer hated them quite so much.

Some would believe a spirit had come.

Some would not.

The power would accept both.

It would be present in the hand that steadied a child on a stair. In the unexpected patch of green after rain. In the impulse that made a council elder listen one moment longer before refusing a new idea. In the courage of the first girl who chose to descend the mountain alone, and in the joy of her mother when she came back.

It would be present in maintenance.

That least celebrated form of love.

A roof repaired before collapse.  
A wall drained before frost.  
A seed saved.  
A story repeated.  
A quarrel softened before it became inheritance.  
A path cleared after snow.  
A cup of water given without being asked.

The ancient city would remain ancient, but not stagnant.

The people would remain themselves, but not trapped.

And perhaps, after many generations, when the settlement was no longer quite so isolated, when visitors sometimes climbed the hidden path and stood breathless before the stone labyrinth and the sky-wide plazas, they would feel something there they could not name.

Not grandeur exactly.

Not magic in the childish sense.

Rather, the sensation of being in a place that had been loved without being possessed.

The granite would still be weathered. The streets still narrow. The air still thin. Life would still require work, patience, and communal care. But beneath it all, like water moving through unseen channels, there would be a quiet abundance.

A sense that the city had been given not escape from its nature, but the chance to become more fully itself.

And the benevolent power?

It would remain.

Not enthroned.

Not worshiped.

Not even known with certainty.

It would dwell in the interval between wind gusts, in the gleam at the bottom of a cistern, in the dreams of children who had never seen the lower valleys but knew, somehow, that paths existed.

It would watch the sun turn the stone gold, then blue.

It would listen to the flutes.

And whenever the people gathered in the small plazas beneath the immense sky, whenever they shared bread and goat’s milk and memory, whenever they chose one another again despite cold and scarcity and fear, the power would feel no need to improve upon them.

It would simply bless what was already holy.
