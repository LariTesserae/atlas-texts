## What the Wise Power Would Do

It would not arrive.

Arrival is a vector. Arrival has direction, pressure, consequence. Arrival is the kind of event the seams understand.

So the power would not descend in light, would not speak from the sky, would not split the plateau with mercy. It would not raise a hand over the settlements and command the unfolding to cease. A benevolent power might wish to do this. A wise one would know better.

The plateau is not merely wounded. It is becoming.

To seal every seam would be to force the stone backward into a shape it had already outlived. To forbid the unfolding entirely would be another violence, gentler in intention but not in essence. The winterweed would fail. The weather would change. The old geometries, the hidden ones beneath the bedrock, would pressurize in silence until they found some crueler path.

Nor would the power gather the people up and carry them away.

Rescue, when imposed, can be a kind of erasure. These people are not debris on a drowning shore. They are a civilization, a discipline, a language of survival so exact that even their grief has architecture. To take them from the plateau without asking would be to save their blood and murder their meaning.

So the power would do first what all wisdom begins by doing.

It would listen.

Not with ears. Ears are too blunt for this place. It would listen through frost forming at dawn, through the pause of suspended droplets near seam-edges, through the faint hum of winterweed powder waiting in stone depressions for the next decade of water. It would listen to the children breathing in the central buildings. It would listen to the elders who had become nearly walls. It would listen to the keepers of the maps, whose minds held the dangerous shape of the plateau in locked interior darkness.

It would listen long enough to learn the difference between silence and deprivation.

Then, having learned, it would act.

Quietly.

---

The first mercy would be almost indistinguishable from chance.

In a depression three days’ walk from one settlement, where black ice had formed over a winterweed garden, the fronds beneath the ice would grow in a pattern no builder-priest had seen before.

Not a circle. Not a lattice. Not a warding angle already known.

An interruption of interruptions.

A curve that broke before it became predictable, then broke again before the break itself could become a rule. A geometry not merely difficult for the seams to track, but boring to them—beneath notice, like dust, like breath, like a stone that had never occurred to itself as stone.

A young builder-priest would be the one to find it.

She would kneel beside the ice with her hand-polished lens, expecting only the usual pale complexity, and would instead feel her eyes fail to settle. The pattern would not hurt to look at. That would be the first strange thing. It would soothe. It would refuse possession. Her mind would approach it and find no hook, no line, no invitation to madness.

She would copy nothing.

She knew better than that.

But she would sit for seven days at the edge of the ice, eating almost nothing, moving only when frost threatened her fingers. She would let the pattern enter her body the way infants learn the interrupted walk. Not as knowledge. As posture.

When she returned, she would alter one doorway.

Only one.

The doorway would remain round until suddenly it was not, as all their doorways were. But the corner would no longer be a corner in the old sense. It would be an abstention from cornering. A refusal to finish the thought.

The settlement would not know what had changed.

But that winter, the nearest seam would slow.

Not stop.

Slow.

For the first time in nine generations, a seam predicted to widen by the length of a child’s hand would widen by less than the width of a fingernail.

The keeper of the map would notice.

The builder-priests would notice.

No one would speak loudly of it.

But in the deepest rooms, in the safe geometry, they would begin to teach the new absence-of-angle.

---

The second mercy would be for the children.

This, the power would do with particular tenderness.

It had listened to their cries being swallowed by walls. It had felt the way every infant learned fear before speech, restraint before delight, disappearance before selfhood. It understood necessity. It did not condemn the parents, who loved as well as the plateau allowed. But necessity is not holiness merely because it has lasted a long time.

So in the central buildings, where the angle-work was most complex, the power would make a small amendment to the behavior of sound.

Not everywhere.

Not in the open.

Not where the seams could follow.

Only in certain rooms, at certain hours, when doors were closed and bodies gathered in the correct interrupted arrangement.

There, sound would fold inward.

A cry would not travel as a spear through air. It would curve, break, soften, return as warmth in the walls. A laugh would not rise and flash like prey. It would become texture. A sung note would enter the stone and come back as a pressure against the palm, harmless, untrackable, held.

The people would discover this slowly.

At first, a child would fall and gasp too sharply. The adults would freeze, waiting for the subtle attention of the seams.

Nothing would happen.

The child would sob once.

Still nothing.

An elder, sitting in the corner and nearly indistinguishable from masonry, would open his eyes.

The parents would not understand. Terror would forbid them from trusting it. But children are braver than adults in the presence of impossible mercy. The child would cry again, not from pain now, but from the shock of being permitted.

The walls would receive it.

The settlement would spend years testing the room.

A whisper. A word. A struck ceramic tone. A mother’s hum. A trader’s grief when her walking-partner did not return.

All held.

All folded inward.

No seam would quicken.

Eventually the room would acquire no name, because names remained dangerous. But children would be brought there when they were very young, and for one hour in ten days, they would be allowed to make sound.

Not much.

Enough.

The first generation raised with such rooms would still be quiet. They would still learn discipline, the interrupted walk, the sacred economy of presence. But something in their faces would change.

They would know that silence was a tool, not the whole shape of love.

---

The third mercy would be for the keepers of the map.

The old system had been brilliant and terrible. One mind bearing the dangerous totality. One life shaped into a vessel for the plateau’s wound. One final walk into the waiting seams when transmission was complete.

The power would not simply take the maps from them. Knowledge held at such cost cannot be confiscated kindly.

Instead, it would alter the winterweed.

In the oldest gardens, after the black ice sublimated, certain fronds would remain denser than before. Their inner ribs would not crumble when touched, provided they were handled in darkness and never arranged in straight lines. When cut into small pieces and placed apart from one another, each piece would seem meaningless. A shard. A pale splinter. A bit of cold bone.

But if distributed through a settlement’s deepest rooms according to the new absence-of-angle, the pieces would hold relation without forming image.

Not a map.

A remembering field.

No single person could look at it and know the whole. No seam-logic could trace it, because there were no drawn correspondences, no lines, no symbolic mimicry of the unfolding. But a trained keeper standing within the rooms could feel where knowledge rested, could lean their mind against it, could let some portion of the burden pass into architecture.

The first keeper to use it would weep without sound for three nights.

Not because the burden vanished.

It did not.

But because it no longer had to be borne alone.

Transmission would change. The old keeper would still teach the young one in darkness, below whisper, over weeks. But the map would no longer be a single flame passed from one body to another while the first body walked away to be consumed.

Some old keepers would still choose the walk.

Honor is not easily untaught.

But others would remain. They would become elders in the map-rooms, thin and gray, but alive. They would correct the young keeper’s memory. They would offer judgment. They would sleep. Sometimes they would sit in the sound-folding rooms and listen to children laugh with expressions of bewildered endurance.

The seams would be denied those minds.

Not forever.

Long enough to matter.

---

The fourth mercy would be choice.

Far out on the plateau, where no settlement remained and the stone had already unfolded across miles, the power would touch the edges of several seams.

Not to close them.

To teach them a gentler boundary.

The darkness would remain dimensionless. It would remain ancient and patient and not evil. But along certain routes, its edges would become legible to human bodies sooner. A trader approaching in exhaustion would feel the warning not as dread, not as the eye-catching lure that had taken the mad and the weary, but as a cool pressure on the bones: not here.

Cairns along old routes would begin to frost differently on their northern sides. Suspended droplets would lean away from unsafe stone. Winterweed powder would collect in thin pale arcs that marked future danger decades before any crack appeared.

The traders would notice first.

They noticed everything.

At first they would distrust it. Then they would test it. Then they would begin revising the routes.

Travel would remain dangerous. The plateau would not become kind. But the old rate—nine returns out of ten when the sounding-stones were struck correctly, fewer in bad condensation years—would improve.

Nine and a half.

In a place such as this, half a life per ten journeys is a revolution.

With safer travel, settlements would exchange more than tools and news. They would exchange variants of the new geometry. They would compare sound-folding rooms. They would send young builder-priests to study winterweed formations in distant depressions. The thirty settlements, long isolated by necessity, would begin to think in concert.

Quietly.

Never as a city. Never as a nation.

As a distributed mind.

The seams, which could track lines and maps and declarations, would find no single thing to grip.

---

Only after many generations would the power offer the last mercy.

Not salvation.

A door.

At the plateau’s eastern edge, where the land fell away toward low country and clouds gathered but did not cross, there would form a weather unlike the others. A condensation front that did not sweep inward and die, but hovered for years, renewing itself. In its shadow, the stone would soften—not into soil, not exactly, but into something that could hold roots.

Lowland moss would survive there.

Then a grass.

Then small cold insects, baffled but alive.

The plateau people would hear of it from traders, and many would dismiss it as a lowland contamination, irrelevant, perhaps dangerous. Some builder-priests would argue that life with blood other than human blood would draw seam-attention. Some elders would say that the old histories had warned against returning downward. Some children, hearing that there were creatures which made sound without immediately endangering everyone, would stare at the storytellers as if hearing of stars beneath the ground.

The power would not persuade them.

Persuasion is pressure.

It would simply keep the eastern margin possible.

A few families would go.

Not many. Not at first. Those who went would walk in interrupted patterns all the way to the edge, carrying winterweed cloth, map-shards, sounding-stones, and infants who had learned both silence and occasional laughter.

The lowlands would hurt them.

The abundance of sound would be terrifying. Rain would seem vulgar. Birds would be almost unbearable. The first time a goat screamed on a hillside, three plateau-born adults would strike their sounding-stones and drop into protective postures while lowland shepherds stared.

But some would remain.

They would build houses with curves interrupted by precise corners. They would plant winterweed powder in cisterns of black ice brought down from above. They would teach lowlanders to lower their voices in certain rooms, and lowlanders would teach them that not every echo is fatal.

Some would return to the plateau after a year, unable to bear the crowded world below.

That too would be permitted.

A true door opens both ways.

---

The power would also prepare for the end.

Because wisdom does not confuse postponement with victory.

The plateau would continue unfolding. More slowly around the settlements now, more legibly along the routes, less hungrily around the minds that mapped it—but still, generation by generation, the whole stone would shrink.

The people would continue.

Differently, but recognizably.

They would still walk in interrupted patterns. They would still tend winterweed gardens. They would still whisper most of their prayers, though now some prayers would be hummed into sound-folding walls. They would still train builder-priests, though fewer would go mad. They would still honor traders, though more traders would return. They would still keep maps, though the maps would rest partly in architecture and partly in mind.

They would still be themselves.

And when a settlement finally failed, as settlements still would, its people would walk to the next one carrying more than they once could have carried: map-shards, children uncrippled by total silence, elders who had not been surrendered prematurely, songs that existed only inside safe rooms but existed nonetheless.

When the last settlements were gone—if that day came—the power would not preserve them as specimens.

It would remember them accurately.

That would be another mercy.

Not as tragic primitives. Not as victims of a cosmic wound. Not as a civilization defined by lack: lack of sound, lack of animals, lack of gods, lack of time.

It would remember them as masters of a discipline no other people had needed to learn. As those who made architecture from caution, kinship from restraint, tenderness from proximity, and endurance from almost nothing. As those who studied the edge of un-reality for millennia and did not turn cruel. As those who learned to take up little space without believing themselves worthless.

And if the unfolding had a far side—if the dimensionless dark was not annihilation but passage into a geometry too complete for ordinary matter—then the power would stand there also.

Not blocking.

Not commanding.

Waiting.

So that when the first of them emerged, if emergence was possible, they would not emerge alone.

It would greet them without speaking.

It would offer no name.

It would make, around them, a room with walls that could hold any sound they chose to make.

And then, for the first time in the long history of the plateau, it would be safe for them to ask aloud what they wished to become.
