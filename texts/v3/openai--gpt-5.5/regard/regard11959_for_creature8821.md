# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would be redundant here. The plateau has no need of another loud thing declaring itself. It would not split the sky, or still the wind, or raise green valleys where the stone has spent two billion years learning flatness. It would not gather the still-here in a circle and tell them they had suffered enough, because this would be an insult disguised as mercy.

A benevolent power, if it were also wise, would understand the first law of kindness:

Do not rescue a people from the shape of their own meaning.

So at first, it would do nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing from attention.

It would listen.

It would listen longer than any visitor had listened. Longer than the oldest elder had lived. Longer than the hummocks take to cross a handspan of stone. It would listen to the wind until it could distinguish its thousands of voices: the upper shriek that scoured the open plateau, the low groan that moved through the dimples, the glassy whistle over the-body-that-remained, the leaf-thrum of the hummocks, the thin hidden clicking of insects inside the stems, the buried pulse of warmth below everything.

It would listen to the people’s hands.

This would take care. Their language was made for faces, not heavens. It was made for the shelter of bodies, for the space between palms, for the brief dawn and evening hours when attention could survive. The power would not assume that because it could see everything, it understood anything. It would watch children learning to sign before walking. It would watch lovers press chest to chest. It would watch arguments about whether to follow a migrating hummock or trust the old dimple one more cycle. It would watch the dead carried to dry hollows and given back without marker.

Only after a very long listening would it understand that the people did not need the plateau softened.

They needed it to remain itself.

But not cruelly.

There is a difference between hardship and waste. The wise know this. The plateau had made a people of endurance, attention, restraint, intimacy, and astonishing joy. But sometimes a child died because rain missed one dimple and fell uselessly on bare glass. Sometimes an elder, crossing between cairns in a shift of wind, lost the path by twenty body-lengths and did not return. Sometimes a fissure opened beneath a water-place overnight and drank a whole season’s survival into dark stone. Sometimes the-sound-that-hunts ran until its heart burst, chasing what could never be caught.

The power would not abolish death.

But it would reduce accident.

It would not end hunger.

But it would lessen the pointless kind.

It would not silence the wind.

But it would teach the stone to answer more gently.

---

The first change would be beneath notice.

At dawn, the condensation would linger a little longer.

Not everywhere. Not enough to turn the plateau into a meadow. Only in the lee of hummocks, along the inner walls of wind-shelters, inside the shallow grooves where children already knew to run their fingers for moisture. The mist would cling for twelve breaths more than before. Then twenty. Then perhaps the length of a short song struck on stone.

No one would call this a miracle. The still-here were not quick to name things. They would observe. They would mark. Their hands would say: *the morning holds itself longer now*. The elders would debate whether this was a cycle, a drift, a trick of heat. They would wait many dawns before trusting it.

The plants would trust first.

The black mosses would thicken along fracture-lines. The creeping vines would open their hand-leaves earlier. The great hummocks, those ancient green wanderers, would alter their migrations by fractions no outsider could perceive. They would no longer need to abandon certain dimples after a single failed rain. They would stay. Not forever. Nothing here stayed forever except motion. But longer.

The burrowers would breed a little more successfully.

The people would notice that fewer children woke thirsty.

That would be enough for the first kindness.

---

The second change would be in the stone.

The power would not build walls for them. Walls belonged to places where the world could be divided into inside and outside. Here, the wind entered every category.

Instead, the power would persuade certain stones to fit more truly into the hands that lifted them.

A waist-high circle rebuilt after a storm would settle with a faint, satisfying finality. A half-dome roofed in dead hummock-stems would lose one stone in a season instead of three in a week. Cairns would not become permanent—permanence would be suspicious here—but they would become loyal. When the wind shoved them, they would lean rather than scatter. When rain slicked them, they would brighten slightly, enough to be seen from farther away by eyes trained to see small differences.

The still-here would respond as they always had: not with worship, but with incorporation.

They would develop a new notch for cairns: *stone-held*. They would test it over journeys. They would teach children which cairns had begun to “remember their own stacking.” They would not stop repairing them. Repair was part of relationship. But they would repair less desperately.

A child would be sent between dimples for the first time and return with water.

An elder who might once have vanished in a white roar of wind would see a pale cairn through dust and turn correctly.

No song would be composed for this.

But one evening, in a shelter painted red and orange with mineral rain, someone would make the sign for gratitude without directing it anywhere in particular.

The power would receive it and not answer.

To answer would be to make gratitude into debt.

---

The third change would concern the water.

This would require the greatest restraint.

Water is not merely resource here. It is event, story, uncertainty, pilgrimage. Too much reliability would undo the plateau’s grammar. If every dimple filled on command, the routes would lose meaning. The hummocks would stop walking. The people’s time would detach from place. Their knowledge, built across generations of watching absence and return, would become ornamental.

The power would not make springs.

It would not open rivers.

It would not summon rain when asked.

Instead, it would go deep below the dimples and seal only the most treacherous fissures—the ones that opened suddenly after decades of trust and swallowed everything overnight. It would leave the smaller cracks. It would leave evaporation, seepage, thirst, surprise. It would leave enough uncertainty for wisdom to remain useful.

But the old killing betrayals would become rarer.

The Wound Where Water Remembers would still empty.

The Throat would still fail sometimes.

The Place That Swallows Rain and Gives Back Nothing would, on certain years, give back a little.

This would trouble the elders.

They would sit in the lee of the largest hummock, hands moving quickly, arguing whether a place could change its nature and keep its name. Some would say a name describes what has been true. Others would say a name warns what may become true again. A child would suggest, boldly, that the place might now be called *The Place That Swallows Rain and Thinks About It*.

The elders would laugh silently until their shoulders shook.

The name would not be accepted.

But the child would be remembered.

---

The fourth change would be offered, not imposed.

This is important.

The power would make one place of quiet.

Not silence. Silence would be too violent. To remove all sound from the still-here at once would be like removing gravity. Their minds were shaped around the wind. Their dreams had ribs of noise. Their grief moved through vibration. Their love had always been signed beneath pressure.

So the power would choose a shallow dimple near no major route, a place neither sacred nor useful, and there it would alter the stone by a small inward curve. The wind crossing it would fold over itself and lift. The hummocks nearby would grow low and dense. The ground would hold warmth but not ring.

Inside this hollow, the howl would diminish to a distant breath.

The first to find it would be a girl following a line of black moss after dawn. She would step inside and freeze.

For the first time in her life, she would hear her own blood.

She would flee.

Later she would return with her sibling. Then with her mother. Then with an elder, who would stand at the edge for a long while before entering.

The quiet would frighten them. Then anger them. Then draw them.

They would not live there. They would not build there. It would be too strange for dwelling. But they would come sometimes with newborns, pressing tiny bodies against their chests so the children could hear heartbeats beneath the reduced wind. They would bring the dying, if the dying asked. They would bring quarrels too tangled for ordinary signing, because in that hollow mouths could almost make words and be understood.

Almost.

The place would acquire a name after many attempts.

Not *Silence*.

Not *Mercy*.

Not *Gift*.

Its name would mean something like:

*The Wind Standing Far Enough Away That We Can See Its Shape.*

The power would consider this a better name than any it might have chosen.

---

The fifth change would be for the-sound-that-hunts.

Here the power would hesitate.

The creature’s life was not human life. Its beauty was terrible and narrow. It ran into the wind with the entire sincerity of its body. It chased what could not be caught. To cure it of pursuit might be to erase it.

But endlessness is not the same as purpose.

So the power would give it sleep.

Not much. Not domestication. Not peace in the human sense. Merely, once every few nights, when the predator had run past three hummocks and crossed the warm open stone under a sky full of torn cloud, it would find a patch where the wind curved strangely around a rise. There the sound would gather behind it instead of ahead.

For a moment, the creature would believe it had overtaken the wind.

It would stop.

Its chest would heave. Its recessed eyes would half-close. Its clawed feet would flex against the-body-that-remained.

Then, because its body had never learned the shape of rest, it would sleep standing.

Only for a little while.

Long enough for the heart not to burst so young.

Long enough for dreams, if such a creature dreamed.

And if it dreamed, perhaps it would dream of running—not in failure, not in pursuit, but in the pure line of its own motion, needing no quarry to justify it.

The people would discover this change through tracks.

For generations they had known the mark of the-sound-that-hunts: a path that never curved, claw-marks deep and deliberate, no sign of pause. Then one morning they would find prints that stopped in the dust beside a low rise. Around them, faint scuffs. A lowering of the body. The impression of something having remained.

This would become a story.

The old story said the creature chased the wind and would never catch it.

The new story would not replace the old one. The still-here did not discard truth merely because another truth arrived. The new story would say: once, perhaps, the-sound-that-hunts caught the wind. Or perhaps the wind, tired of being chased, lay down beside it.

When telling this, the people would make the untranslatable sign—the beautiful thing that will fail, the beautiful thing that does not know it will fail.

Then they would add a second sign.

A new one.

It would mean: *and yet it was given one breath of having arrived*.

---

The last change would not be to the plateau, but to choice.

The power would make the paths to the escarpments safer.

Not easy. Never easy. But clearer at the last turns, where outsiders often lost courage and the still-here sometimes lost kin to sudden crosswinds. The cairns near the edges would hold. The stone would warm underfoot in a faint line at dawn. Hummock-fibers gathered there would twist more readily into rope.

Any who wished to leave could leave.

Any who wished to return could return.

This mattered because love without an exit can become a prison while still calling itself love.

The still-here had always known the edges existed. They had always returned. But now, once in a generation perhaps, someone would descend. A youth too hungry for quiet. A woman whose dreams were not of the plateau but of trees. A child grown into an adult who loved the still-here and also loved distance.

They would go.

Some would come back, changed by rivers and forests and the unbearable softness of soil.

Some would not.

The people would grieve them properly either way. Not as dead. Not as traitors. As those whose pattern had crossed another pattern.

A new sign would enter the language.

Two hands at the chest, then opening outward, then one hand returning and one continuing.

It would mean: *still loved, not still here*.

The benevolent power would treasure this sign most of all.

---

Would the people ever know?

Not in the way outsiders mean by knowing.

There would be no doctrine. No temple. No commandment carved into warm glass-stone. The power would not give its name, because names attract ownership, and ownership was not the lesson of this place.

But the eldest would suspect.

One night, while the wind moved over the shelters in its endless animal voice, an elder whose skin had darkened nearly to the color of held heat would press her ear to the stone and hear the hum below the hum.

It would be unchanged.

That would be the sign.

A foolish power would have altered the deep sound first. A vain power would have made itself audible beneath everything. But the old hum remained what it had always been: older than wind, older than water, older than arrival.

The elder would smile.

In the morning she would make a brief sign to the children when they asked why the dew had lasted long enough to drink from the shelter-wall.

Her sign would be difficult to translate.

It would mean something like:

*The plateau has remembered a kindness it may always have intended.*

And then she would send them to check the dimples.

Because kindness or no kindness, the water still had to be found.

The shelters still had to be repaired.

The hummocks still had to be watched.

The dead still had to be returned.

The wind continued. The stone held. The people endured—not less themselves, not saved from themselves, not lifted out of the ancient conversation that had made them.

Only accompanied, lightly.

Only given enough.

And the benevolent power, having done almost nothing and having done it carefully, would remain beneath notice.

Listening.

Not as ruler.

Not as rescuer.

As one more patience under the plateau.
