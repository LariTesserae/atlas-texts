A benevolent and wise power would not begin by saving them.

It would know better.

It would stand at the edge of the basin for a long time, unseen in the pale rain, watching the Tílmak walk their narrow paths and tend their channels. It would listen to the slow speech of water under clay. It would feel the settlement’s weight—not only the weight of brick and beam, but the weight of decision, grief, competence, stubbornness, affection, all pressed into the foundations with the ash of the dead.

It would understand, quickly, that this place is not a mistake asking to be corrected.

It is a conversation.

So the power would not dry the basin. It would not lift the settlement onto high stone. It would not turn the clay into granite, or halt the seepage, or make the roofs shine with impossible permanence. Such gifts would be violences disguised as mercy. To remove the wet would be to remove the language in which the Tílmak have learned to be themselves.

Instead, the power would kneel.

Not because it needed to, but because wisdom often does.

It would press one luminous hand into the gray-blue clay and ask the basin what it wanted.

The clay would answer slowly, because clay answers slowly. It would speak of old pressure, old cold, the long burden of lake water, the patient fall of silt in seasonal bands. It would speak of having been released too recently into air, of not yet knowing whether it was land. It would speak of the small warm-footed creatures who had come to press it into bricks and walls and vessels, who spoke to it with spirals and hatch-marks, who mixed their dead into its body and asked it to hold.

The water would answer too, in seams and capillaries.

It would say: I must move.

The power would nod.

Then it would go into the settlement—not as a god in flame, not as a voice from the overcast sky, but perhaps as one more traveler at the edge of the corduroy path. Cloaked. Mud-stained. Patient-eyed. Carrying nothing impressive.

The Tílmak would notice at once that this stranger placed their feet correctly.

That would matter.

They would offer food, after assessment. Dense bread. Hot stew. Reed-shoot beer in a small clay cup. The power would accept all of it, because gifts must be received for relationship to begin.

It would not announce itself.

It would ask questions.

“Does that channel always slow there?”

“Who fired the roof tiles on the eastern house?”

“Is the coughing worse in the long rains or when the kilns burn?”

“Why do you mark the left side of doors with the spiral?”

The Tílmak would answer some questions and ignore others. They would test the stranger’s seriousness. They would set a wet brick in those hands and watch how it was held. They would show the stranger a minor drainage problem, one not dangerous but annoying, to see whether the stranger spoke nonsense.

The power would not solve it with magic.

It would look. It would wait. It would say, “The water is not refusing this way. It is being asked too late.”

A water-reader would narrow their eyes.

Together, they would move three stones, deepen a cut by the width of two fingers, and change the angle of a reed pipe.

After the next rain, the channel would run clean.

That would be the beginning.

Not worship. Not revelation.

Trust.

And once trusted, the power would give only gifts that looked almost like consequences of the Tílmak’s own intelligence.

It would strengthen what was already there.

It would teach the willow roots to grip more deeply without drinking the channels dry. The coppiced saplings at the junctions would become quietly better at holding banks in place. No one would see a miracle. They would only notice that certain edges slumped less often, and an elder would say, “That planting was well chosen,” and the young person who planted it would stand a little straighter.

It would alter nothing about the rain’s nature, but it would make the worst rains slightly more legible. A smell in the air twelve hours earlier. A particular sheen on standing water. A hush among the small brown birds. The water-readers would begin to notice these signs. They would argue about them. They would test them. Within a generation, the Tílmak would have a new category in their language: the wetness-before-too-much-wetness.

It would give them warning, not immunity.

It would whisper into the dreams of kiln-masters—not commands, but shapes. A flue angled half a palm wider. A second draft-hole that could be sealed with a fitted brick. A way of stacking damp fuel so steam left before flame took. The next kilns would fire hotter, cleaner, with less smoke crawling low through the settlement. Respiratory illness would not vanish, because no good power lies by making mortal life invulnerable. But fewer children would cough through the deepest wet season. More elders would sleep through the night.

The healers would discover, by accident or intuition, that one of the gray-green succulent plants growing flat against the higher cracks soothed inflamed skin and helped shallow cuts close cleanly. Perhaps a child would fall and scrape both knees, and an old woman, irritated by the child’s noise, would crush the plant and bind it on simply because it was cool. The wound would heal well. The old woman would try again with someone else. Knowledge would enter the settlement the way water did: by seepage.

The power would guide traders, but only those with decent hearts.

A merchant carrying salt would choose the basin road despite bad weather and not know why. A bronze-worker’s apprentice, fleeing a cruel master, would arrive with a pack full of half-finished tools and a stubborn desire not to return. A woman from drier hills, skilled in raised beds and composting, would come to trade wool and stay through one wet season, then two.

The Tílmak would not be transformed into someone else.

They would acquire better adzes.

A new hinge design.

A stronger way to bind roofing beams.

A method of smoking fish that resisted the damp.

Small things. Mortal things. The kinds of gifts that enter history as “someone figured it out.”

The power would be pleased by that.

It would pay special attention to the children’s miniature settlements.

There, in those games, the future already practiced itself. So the power would sometimes let water behave with unusual honesty in the children’s clay models. A tiny channel would fail exactly as a full-sized one would fail. A miniature wall would slump for the same reason a real wall might. A careful child would notice that a roof-channel pouring too directly into a corner undermined the whole structure.

Adults would pause, watching.

“Do that again,” one would say.

A child’s game would become a teaching method. The standard problems would multiply. Children would learn not merely to imitate old solutions, but to test them. This would matter when the basin changed, as all young land changes.

For the dead, the power would do almost nothing, because the Tílmak were already wise there.

It would only ensure that the memorial bricks never became empty symbols.

When ash was mixed with clay, when the personal mark was pressed, when the brick was fired and placed into a corner or foundation, the power would help memory cling. Not as ghosts trapped in walls. Not as voices demanding obedience. But as warmth in the hand when someone touched a grandmother’s brick. As a sudden remembered phrase when a young builder faced a choice. As the feeling, standing in a room built partly of one’s dead, that one was not alone and not excused from care.

The ancestors would hold.

Not rule.

Hold.

The power would also set limits upon itself.

It would not prevent every collapse.

Some walls must fail if people are to remain truthful about clay.

It would not prevent every death.

A people made immortal would cease being a people and become a monument.

It would not make the Tílmak famous.

Fame would bring too many feet, too much hunger from outside, too many people wanting the miracle pottery or the holy wet clay or the secret of ancestor-bricks. The settlement could survive rain. It might not survive fascination.

So the power would bend attention around them slightly. Not invisibility. Traders would still come. Lost travelers might still find them. But armies would overlook the basin as worthless. Tax-collectors would decide the mud made the journey impractical. Ambitious priests would hear rumors and dismiss them as marsh superstition. The world would leave the Tílmak mostly alone, which is one of the rarest blessings a small competent people can receive.

At the basin’s center, where the channels converged around the carved post, the power would leave one visible gift.

Only one.

After a night of long rain, the people would emerge to find a new spring—not large, not dramatic—rising in the communal basin. But it would not flood. It would pulse gently, clear and cold, through a thumb-wide opening in the clay. The water would taste faintly of stone rather than silt. It would not replace their cisterns or make their systems unnecessary. It would simply provide, in every season, enough clean water for infants, the sick, and those in mourning.

The Tílmak would gather around it in silence.

They would test it for days before trusting it.

They would dig around it carefully, line it with fired rings, mark the surrounding clay with spirals and wavy lines and one new sign no one remembered inventing: a cupped hand beneath a falling drop.

They would not say, “A god has come.”

They would say, “The basin has opened a kindness.”

This would be acceptable to the power.

Perhaps preferable.

Years would pass.

The settlement would rise by inches. Old walls would become foundations. Children whose toy channels had run true would become water-readers. The apprentice bronze-worker would marry a potter and design a better scraper. The healer’s crushed succulent would become an ointment kept in small lidded pots. The new spring would be named Less-Wet-Than-Tears, because Tílmak humor would survive even reverence.

And sometimes, in the less-wet season, when the clouds lifted and low sun turned every channel into a strip of silver, an old stranger would return.

Or seem to.

They would sit near the central basin, cloak damp at the hem, and watch people work.

No one would ask where they came from. Not directly. The Tílmak were not a people who wasted questions on answers that would not improve a roof.

Someone would hand the stranger a cup of reed beer.

Someone else would complain that the northern channel had begun acting like a stubborn uncle.

The stranger would listen gravely.

“Has anyone asked the uncle what he wants?” they would say.

This would be very funny.

Later, after laughter, they would go look at the channel.

And when at last the power departed—not forever, but for long stretches—it would leave no shrine except the settlement itself. No commandments except the ones the Tílmak already lived by:

Attend.

Maintain.

Adapt.

Let water move.

Let clay remember.

Build high, but not proudly.

Mark what matters.

Feed the living.

Set the dead where they can hold you.

And if, someday, the basin began to sink again—if the old lake returned not in memory but in truth—the power would not freeze the waters at the edge. It would warn them early. It would make the signs unmistakable to those trained to read them. It would help the Tílmak decide.

Perhaps they would build higher still.

Perhaps they would become a people of platforms and boats.

Perhaps they would dismantle their oldest walls, carry the ancestor-bricks to a new rise beyond the former shore, and begin again with grief and competence.

The power would help them carry the bricks.

That, above all, is what it would do.

Not spare them change.

Not spare them labor.

Not spare them the dignity of remaining responsible for their own home.

It would place its hand beneath their becoming, lightly enough that they could still feel the weight of themselves, firmly enough that when the clay shifted underfoot, they would not be abandoned.

The Tílmak would continue pressing and being pressed.

The water would continue moving.

The clay would continue remembering.

And somewhere, in the damp heart of the settlement, kindness would seep upward like a spring: quiet, cold, sufficient, and clean.
