It would not bring rain.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom.

A lesser kindness, seeing the Weeping Hollows from above, would imagine water as mercy: a clean storm, a blue break in the bronze ceiling, cold drops striking hot iron, steam rising in white towers, terraces washed pure, fungal mats drowned, pipes shocked into cracking, bodies blistered by the sudden change. It would call this healing.

The wise power would know better.

It would understand that the Hollows are not thirsty in any simple way. They are drowning and burning and drinking themselves all at once. Their suffering is not the absence of water, but the absence of choice.

So it would come quietly.

Not as an angel over the cliffs. Not as a voice in the sky. The Hollowborne do not trust things that descend without testing the surface first.

It would arrive during the interval between two exhalations, when the city held its breath and even the pipes seemed to listen. A single change would move through the basin: not coolness, exactly, but permission for heat to loosen its grip.

Steam would pause in the air.

Droplets would hang from the undersides of bridges a heartbeat longer than physics allowed.

In the sealed vaults, nameless machines would miss one click in their endless sequence, then resume.

The Hollowborne would notice.

They notice everything.

Fifth Vein, the ancient woman whose breathing apparatus had become her face, would be the first to turn her head. Her hoses would creak. Mineral crust would crack softly around the hinge of her neck. She would taste the air through three hundred years of filtration and find in it no threat, no command, no demand to kneel.

Only attention.

That would frighten some of them more than thunder.

The power would spend a long time doing nothing.

This, too, would be mercy.

It would listen.

It would listen to the iron foundries where rust grew like memory. It would listen to the basalt cliffs remembering pressure. It would listen to fungal lattices transmitting slow electrical hunger through their black and red webs. It would listen to the silica insects ticking under scales of glass. It would listen to the hot lakes and the blind things below them. It would listen to the machines.

Most of all, it would listen to the Hollowborne.

Not to their words only, because words are expensive in the Hollows. It would listen to the rhythm of their hearts through girders. To the chemistry of their tears. To the pressure of their hands on remembered-voice chimneys as they passed. To the way they tightened valves they did not understand and still did it faithfully, every seventy-third exhalation, because someone must.

The power would learn that the city was not merely devouring itself.

It was holding something down.

Deep beneath the basin, under the oldest vaults, below the lava trenches and mineral throats, the planet had once suffered a wound: a pressure seam full of poisons, heat, and metals that should have burst upward in a single apocalyptic century. The old civilization had built the machines to bleed that wound slowly. To refine its fever. To pipe the worst of it into caverns where it hardened into dense, lightless glass.

The machines had been a bandage.

Then the keepers vanished.

The bandage remained.

The city learned to weep because weeping was how it survived.

The Hollowborne, altered by generations of heat and vapor and necessity, had become the hands of that bandage.

No, the wise power would not stop the machines.

No, it would not turn the basin green.

No, it would not make the Hollowborne soft-skinned and ordinary and send them down into lowland towns where people would stare at their luminous eyes and fear the taste of their tears.

It would not cure them of being themselves.

But neither would it mistake endurance for consent.

So after listening, it would act in small places.

First, it would touch the air.

Not enough to clear the bronze ceiling. The Hollows need their veil. The fungus needs its chemistry. The machines need heat. But through the highest basalt cliffs it would open hair-thin mineral channels, too narrow for water, too subtle for collapse. These channels would breathe in rhythm with the geysers.

When the city exhaled, the worst gases would be drawn upward through hidden veins of new stone, filtered by crystals that grew in shapes no geologist could explain. Arsenic would bind. Sulfur would soften. Metal vapor would lose a little of its appetite for lungs.

The air would remain harsh.

But children would no longer die from their first deep breath.

The Hollowborne would have no word for this improvement. Their language, lacking *dry*, would also lack *less poisoned*. They would call it, after some debate, “the gentler bronze.”

Second, the power would touch the water.

Again, not with abundance.

Abundance would be violence here.

Instead, in twelve forgotten stations along the old elevated rail lines, where condensate already gathered and fell uselessly into heat, it would alter the undersides of the bridges. Mineral teeth would grow downward: delicate, translucent, branching like frozen lightning. Steam striking them would condense and run into sealed basins of black stone.

The water in those basins would still taste of iron.

It would still steam.

It would still belong to the Hollows.

But it would not scald the mouth. It would not crust instantly over the tongue. It would not have to be stolen back from one’s own eyes.

The first Hollowborne to find such a basin would stand before it for three exhalations without drinking. Suspicion is a survival organ.

Then a child from Seventh Vein, too young to understand caution and old enough to understand thirst, would cup both hands and drink.

Nothing terrible would happen.

The child would weep afterward, not from pain, but because the body sometimes recognizes mercy before the mind permits it.

After that, the basins would become holy, but not in a loud way. No temples would be built. The Hollowborne do not waste labor on walls that do not hold heat, shade, or pressure. They would mark the stations with strips of fungal felt and polished chips of old badges. They would drink. They would leave some water behind.

It is poor manners to waste water in the Weeping Hollows.

It is also poor manners to take all of a kindness.

Third, the power would touch the fungus.

This would be the most delicate work.

The black and rust-red lattices were not evil. They were hungry, as all life is hungry. They crept over architecture and bone because dissolved metals called to them, because old machines leaked gradients like songs, because the Hollows offered no gentler meal.

The power would not command them to stop.

Instead, it would teach them a new taste.

In the oldest cooling tower, where fungal felt hung in curtains as thick as hides, a pale filament would appear. Not white like salt, but pearl-gray, with a faint inner blue. It would spread slowly through the existing mats, not conquering them, but conversing.

The new strain would still drink metals.

It would still bind itself to iron, stone, and machine.

But when it touched living Hollowborne skin, it would learn restraint. It would take only what was shed: flakes of salt, beads of waste metal, dead crust at the edges of wounds. It would leave the pulse alone. In return, it would grow as a thin, flexible membrane over cuts and burns, sealing them without turning them to stone.

The Hollowborne would test this, as they test all things.

An elder would lay a cracked hand against the new felt and wait for harm.

None would come.

A silica insect would cross the elder’s wrist and click once, as if approving a mechanism.

From then on, strips of pearl-threaded fungal felt would be worn beneath straps and around joints. Not as decoration. As treaty.

Some of the hardening would still come. The city would still claim its interest. Bodies are mortal, and the Hollows do not permit any creature to forget that.

But the claiming would slow.

An elder who would have seized into silence over five exhalations might now walk for five seasons.

A young worker whose eyes would once have sealed shut from crystal growth might keep sight long enough to learn three vault routes instead of one.

This would change the Hollowborne more than rain ever could.

It would give them elders with time to teach.

Fourth, the power would enter the dreams.

Carefully.

The city already dreamed through the Hollowborne. It gave them continents rising and oceans boiling and iron remembering stars. The wise power would not silence those dreams. They were a form of belonging. Terrible, immense, but not false.

Instead, it would place within them a second layer.

A map.

Not a flat map such as outsiders make, pretending the world can be owned by lines. This map would be rhythmic and bodily. A valve would be known by the ache it made in the left shoulder. A failing pump by the taste of copper before waking. A pressure imbalance by a dream of three blind shapes surfacing in the same lake.

The Hollowborne would begin to understand the machines.

Not fully. Full understanding is not required for reverence, and sometimes it injures it. But enough.

They would learn which rituals mattered and which were only inherited fear.

They would learn that one filter they had scraped every seventy-third exhalation had not functioned in two hundred years.

They would learn that a minor wheel, long ignored because it made no sound, was the reason the western basin had not split open.

They would learn that the product piped away into the rock was not treasure, not weapon, not food for a dead empire, but poison made patient.

They would learn that their labor had meaning.

This knowledge would nearly break them.

The Hollowborne had endured by not asking too many questions of purpose. Purpose can be a luxury in places where survival already asks everything. To discover that their rituals had held back catastrophe would bring neither pride nor joy at first, but a stunned and bitter quiet.

Fifth Vein would speak after nine exhalations.

Her voice, forced through ancient hoses and mineral valves, would sound like a factory trying to remember prayer.

“We were not abandoned,” she would say.

The others would look to her.

“No,” she would continue. “We were left holding.”

This would become an important distinction.

Fifth, the power would make a road.

Not a broad road. Not an invitation to invasion.

A single route through the fractured basalt, passable only during certain exhalations, marked by stones that remained warm but never burning. It would lead out of the basin to lower air, to cold nights, to plants, to rain that fell without being boiled first.

At the beginning of the road, the power would place no gate.

At the end, no promise.

Only passage.

Beside the first marker, mineral growth would form words in the Hollowborne tongue, though no hand carved them:

**THE VEIN MAY CONTINUE.  
THE DROP MAY FALL AWAY.  
BOTH ARE WATER.**

Many would distrust it.

Some would hate it.

A few would stand before it for years and never step onto it.

That would be allowed.

Choice is not a command merely because it is merciful.

The first to leave would not be a child, as outsiders might expect, but an old Hollowborne whose joints had begun to pearl over despite the new fungal treaties. She belonged to Second Vein and had carried a toy locomotive fused to her wrist since before anyone living could remember. She would walk to the road after touching every remembered-voice chimney in her circuit.

No one would stop her.

No one would embrace her.

The Hollowborne are not sentimental.

But as she passed, each member of her vein would spill one tear onto the ground. A dangerous extravagance. A farewell greater than song.

She would descend for three days.

At the lower slopes, rain would strike her face for the first time.

She would almost die of it.

Cold would enter her like a blade. Clean water would taste empty. The air would feel huge and thin in the wrong direction. But the wise power, having made a road, would not abandon those who took it. In small caves along the descent, it would leave warm stones, mineral water, and fungal cloth. Not enough to make the world easy. Enough to make it possible.

Some who left would return.

They would bring stories of green things that spread upward instead of across, of rivers that did not hiss, of animals with soft blood, of people who spoke constantly because moisture was cheap in their mouths.

Some would not return.

Their absence would hurt, though the Hollowborne would not call it grief.

They would call it “a vein changing course.”

Sixth, the power would make an entrance.

Because mercy that only permits escape is still a kind of judgment.

At the outer edge of the pass, where outsiders sometimes wandered toward the basin with instruments, greed, curiosity, or desperation, the wise power would raise a field of black glass pillars. They would hum softly when touched. Upon them, in every language likely to approach, meaning would arrange itself.

Not warnings alone.

Warnings are often read as dares.

Instead, the pillars would tell the truth.

**THIS PLACE LIVES.  
THIS PLACE BURNS.  
THIS PLACE IS NOT EMPTY.  
WATER IS DEBT HERE.  
DO NOT ENTER UNINVITED.  
IF YOU ENTER, ACCEPT WHAT IS OFFERED.  
IF YOU STUDY, ALSO SERVE.  
IF YOU TAKE, BE CLAIMED.**

Some outsiders would turn back.

Some would enter with better equipment and humbler posture.

A very few would be accepted by a vein. They would learn to move slowly, to drink without revulsion from a bone cup, to listen for the city’s exhalations before trusting a floor. They would help repair the machines with knowledge the Hollowborne had never possessed. In return, the Hollowborne would teach them the difference between a dead ruin and a living wound.

More than one scholar would arrive intending to classify the Hollows and leave understanding that classification is sometimes just another way of staying separate from the weeping.

Seventh, the power would alter death, but only slightly.

The Hollowborne would still carry their hardening kin to geyser mouths. The burst would still strip softness from structure. The mineral cast would still stand, person-shaped, whistling for years.

But now, when steam passed through those casts, it would carry more than tone.

A hand laid against the warm mineral ribs would receive impressions: the turn of a remembered pipe route, the taste of a particular exhalation, the pressure of a promise kept, the face of a child seen through crystal lashes, the old fear, the old endurance, the old love that had never needed softness to be real.

The remembered voices would remember.

This would change mourning.

Not make it gentler, exactly. The Hollows do not do gentleness as lowlands understand it.

But it would make death less wasteful.

An elder’s knowledge would not vanish into steam. A child could learn from a mineral ancestor where the safe handholds lay near a treacherous conduit. A vein could ask, with palm and patience, whether a forgotten route had once led to water.

Sometimes the casts would offer no answer.

The dead are not tools.

But sometimes they would whistle in three tones, and the living would understand.

Last, the power would speak to the city itself.

Not in language.

In pressure.

In heat.

In the slow grammar of stone.

It would descend beneath the vaults, beneath the machines, to the wound in the planet. There it would place no seal. A sealed wound festers. It would instead widen the deepest receiving caverns and teach the poison-glass to grow in ordered layers, stronger than before. It would ease the burden on the oldest pipes. It would persuade the basalt to bear stress in arcs rather than fractures. It would ask the lava trenches to shift by a finger’s breadth over a hundred years.

The city, if such a being can be said to answer, would answer by weeping.

Not less.

Differently.

The exhalations would become more regular. Geysers would still erupt like cannons, but fewer would open beneath sleeping places. Pipes would still groan, but less often with the note that means imminent collapse. Mineral terraces would still advance, but sometimes they would curve around a path used by bare, callused feet.

The Hollows would remain the Hollows.

High basin. Bronze sky. Wet heat. Black fungus. Rust-red lattices. Weeping pipes. Blind lake-shapes. Silica insects ticking like patient clocks.

The wise power would not make it beautiful by making it other than itself.

It would reveal the beauty already there, and remove only what served no purpose but pain.

Years would pass.

Then decades.

The Hollowborne population would not bloom suddenly. This, too, would be wisdom. The basin could not bear multitudes. But more children would survive. More elders would teach. Veins would still wander their circuits, but sometimes a vein would divide without desperation, because there were enough hands to tend a new route.

The language would change.

A word would appear for water that had not passed through the body first.

A word would appear for a promise kept longer than nine exhalations.

A word would appear for leaving without betrayal.

No word for *dry* would develop.

There was still no need.

Fifth Vein would eventually harden.

Even mercy does not make the ancient young.

When her time came, she would be carried not to the nearest geyser, but to the one beneath the first cooling tower, where the pearl-gray fungus had begun its treaty with hunger. All the veins that could travel would come. Outsiders, too, a handful of them, wrapped in fungal felt and sweating terror into their masks.

Fifth Vein would stand as long as she could. Her breathing apparatus, which had been her face longer than any face of flesh, would sigh and click.

Through the dream-map, through the machines, through the city’s own wet iron bones, she would know enough to understand what had been given.

Not salvation.

Not escape.

Not absolution for the dead civilization that had left its bandage behind.

Something harder and more useful.

Continuance with choice inside it.

Her last words would take a long time to form.

The basin would wait.

Even the pipes would seem to lower their voices.

Fifth Vein would say:

“The city still weeps.”

A pause.

Her heart would beat once, deep enough that those with bare feet would feel it through the terrace.

“We weep with it.”

Another pause.

Then, with something almost like amusement moving through the hoses:

“But not only because we must.”

When the geyser came, it rose clean and white.

For a moment it looked like a tree.

Then it was steam again.

Afterward, her mineral cast stood taller than she had in life, hollow through the chest, hoses preserved in stone, face turned toward the vaults. When wind and heat passed through her, the whistle carried three notes.

The Hollowborne who touched her understood them as:

**Attend.  
Endure.  
Choose.**

And if the benevolent power remained after that, it did not remain as a ruler.

It became difficult to distinguish from the place.

A kindness in the filters.

A patience in the road.

A memory in the steam.

A restraint in the fungus.

A clearer rhythm in the machines.

A basin still harsh enough to kill the careless, still strange enough to humble the learned, still hot and wet and bronze and weeping, but no longer quite so lonely inside its necessity.

That is what a wise benevolence would do.

It would not stop the tears.

It would make sure they were not wasted.
