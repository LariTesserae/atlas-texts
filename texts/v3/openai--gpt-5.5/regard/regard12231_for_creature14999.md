If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward Veyr-Kaht, it would not descend in fire.

Fire would be an insult.

This was a people who remembered fire only as something the stone had once been, long before it cooled enough to bear weight. This was a people who did not ask to be lifted, softened, hurried, or saved from the place that had made them. A foolish kindness would look at the Broken Shelf and see only hardship. It would warm the winds, fill the slots with loam, flatten the ridges, teach the ravens to fly again, make the keth fat, make the moss green, make the Veyr tall and easy-laughing and quick.

A wise kindness would understand that this would be a kind of murder.

So the power would do almost nothing at first.

It would listen.

For one hundred winters it would listen to the hammers.

It would learn the difference between the ring of basalt being shaped for a lintel and basalt being shaped for a child’s weighing-stone. It would learn the sound of a rope bridge under one traveler, under three, under a funeral party carrying a body toward the upper ledges. It would listen to the underground rivers chiming in the fissures. It would listen to the breath of sleeping infants in the low warm floors. It would listen to the old ones on the roof-streets, who spoke so seldom that even their silences had dialects.

It would learn that the Veyr were not miserable.

It would learn that they were afraid, sometimes, but not broken.

It would learn that they loved in the same way their walls stood: without ornament, without apology, and for longer than seemed possible.

Only then would it act.

Not with commandments.

Not with a new god-name.

Not with a shining figure above the tallest ridge.

It would place its mercy where the Veyr would recognize it: in weight, in pace, in memory, in return.

The first gift would be water.

Not rivers on the surface. Not warm rain. Not lakes glittering in the open air like lowland temptations.

Instead, deep beneath the basalt, where the old lava tubes twisted through the plateau like veins, the power would persuade the hidden ice to melt more slowly and more faithfully. It would teach the stone to hold water in dark pockets instead of letting it vanish too quickly into unreachable depths. The underground rivers, already heard as faint bells, would begin to ring clearer in certain fissures.

At first the Veyr would think they had simply learned to listen better.

A quarry-child would put her ear against a crack in the wall and hear a sound like a bead dropped into a black bowl. An old water-carrier would follow the note down three hundred steps into a slot no one had mapped for generations. There, behind a curtain of frost, he would find a seep steady enough to fill one cup, then ten, then a cistern over the course of a season.

No flood. No abundance.

Only reliability.

The Veyr would build a stairway to it over thirty years. They would name the place Bell-Under-Stone. They would argue for another twelve years over which ridge held rights to its use. They would settle the matter by building a shared weighing-house beside it, so every vessel drawn up from the dark would be balanced by stone carried down to reinforce the cistern walls.

The power would approve.

The second gift would be warning.

Veyr-Kaht was young. Its cliffs had not finished deciding what they were. Entire shelves sometimes gave way without malice, taking houses, bridges, goats, children, tablets, and third names into the slots below. The Veyr accepted this as part of the Covenant of Return, but acceptance was not the same as consent.

So the power would teach the moss to darken before a fracture opened.

Not always. Not perfectly. Wisdom does not promise the impossible.

But often enough.

A silver-gray mat growing across a stressed wall would turn iron-black three days before the stone beneath it split. Ironpine needles would lean toward empty air when the root-rock began to loosen. Flightless ravens, fat and silent and cleverer than anyone admitted aloud, would gather on roofs that needed to be abandoned before the next frost.

The first time it happened, no one would trust it.

A young mason would notice the moss blackening around the western brace of a roof-street. He would mention it to his mother. His mother would say nothing for a long time. Then she would move her sleeping mat to the floor below.

Three nights later, the roof would fall outward into the slot, taking only two storage jars and an old ladder.

After that, the Veyr would begin to watch the moss.

A new craft would arise slowly: crack-readers, mostly old women and patient children, trained to distinguish ordinary winter-dark from warning-dark. Their tools would be simple: fingertips, breath, small basalt taps, and silence. They would not call it prophecy. They would call it maintenance.

The third gift would be for the light children.

The power would stand unseen in the Hall of Weights on a morning of thin rain, when a newborn was laid upon the great scale and the black-iron counterweight did not rise.

The family would go still.

The ridge would go still.

A year of mourning would begin, though the child lived and breathed and curled one tiny charcoal hand around its mother’s thumb.

The power would feel, then, the one place where the Veyr’s strength had hardened into cruelty.

Not deliberate cruelty. Not lovelessness. Something older and more dangerous: a metaphor mistaken for law.

Nothing is allowed to remain light.

The power would not break the scale. It would not make the infant heavier. It would not thunder from the ceiling that the Veyr were wrong.

Instead, it would wait until night.

Then it would enter the dream of the Keeper of Third Names, who slept in a windowless room beneath the House of Weights with basalt tablets stacked around him like sleeping ancestors.

In the dream, the Keeper stood on a shelf of stone above a depth without bottom. In his hands he held two things: a black-iron counterweight and a feather from one of the flightless ravens.

The feather outweighed the iron.

When he woke, he said nothing. For seventeen days he said nothing. Then he went to the oldest tablet in the House of Weights, one so worn that no living Veyr could read the third name carved there, and turned it over.

On the back, where no chisel had ever touched it, were words in the old ridge-script.

Not all weight is carried by the bones.

This caused trouble.

Proper trouble. Veyr trouble. The kind that lasted decades, involved councils, silences, three broken betrothals, two rebuilt halls, one exile that ended after nine years, and a series of increasingly dense essays carved on portable slabs.

In the end, the Covenant of Weight was not abolished. The Veyr did not abolish things. They buttressed them.

A new practice began.

If a child was judged light, the family no longer entered mourning. Instead, the ridge entered watchfulness. The child would be weighed again at one year, then seven, then at the first solo crossing of a bridge. Some remained small. Some became crack-readers, water-listeners, tablet-keepers, moss-growers, bridge-singers. Some bore little weight in the body and great weight in the continuance of others.

The old phrase changed.

Not quickly. Never quickly.

But after two hundred years, when a light child was placed on the scale, the elders no longer lowered their eyes.

They said, “This one’s weight is hidden.”

The fourth gift would be given to the ravens.

Not flight. Flight would carry them away from what they had become.

Instead, the power would deepen their memory.

The fat, silent, hopping ravens of Veyr-Kaht already knew the roofs, the herds, the corpse-ledges, the warm chimneys, the children careless with crumbs. Now they would know faces across generations. They would remember which houses had fed them during hard frost. They would remember which stairways tended to ice over before dawn. They would remember the sick.

A raven would begin appearing at the same arrow-slit every morning, tapping once, until the family inside finally discovered their old father had stopped rising from his mat.

A flock would gather on the eastern bridge before a rope snapped.

A raven would hop three ridges in front of a lost child, pausing whenever she fell behind, leading her not home but to an ironpine hollow where she could survive the night out of the wind.

The Veyr would notice.

They would not become sentimental. They would not start keeping ravens as pets. That would be foolish; a raven was a neighbor, not a tool.

But extra scraps would appear on certain roofs. Funeral ledges would be chosen with more care. Children would be taught that if a raven blocked your path, you did not kick it aside. You waited. You considered. You asked what the plateau might be saying with a beak.

The fifth gift would be beauty, though the power would be careful with that word.

The Veyr did not build for beauty. They built for continuance.

But continuance without beauty can become a locked room.

So the power would alter nothing large. It would not paint the sky. It would not soften the basalt. It would not fill the plateau with flowers.

It would change the frost.

On certain mornings, when the temperature hung exactly at the knife-edge between rime and thaw, the frost would form in fine hexagonal plates along the windward walls. Not random crusts, but patterns like maps of rivers no Veyr had seen, like branching roots, like old lava cooling, like stairways viewed from above.

The patterns would last only until the pale sun rose.

For generations, no one would speak of them.

Then one child, waiting for her father to finish mending a roof-street, would take a black stone and scratch the frost-pattern into the underside of a stair.

Someone would see it.

Someone else would copy it.

A mason would carve a frost-pattern where no structural line required it, hidden inside a lintel where only those entering the house could see. He would claim it strengthened the stone. No one would challenge him.

Over time, the terraced towns would begin to carry secret ornament: frost-lines under eaves, river-spirals inside cisterns, raven-foot marks on bridge posts, otter-shapes in the dark stone beside slot pools. Nothing extravagant. Nothing that weakened a wall. Beauty would enter as reinforcement.

The Veyr would still say they did not build for beauty.

But when a new house was raised, someone would ask, “Where will the frost go?”

The sixth gift would be silence.

A different silence than the one they already knew.

The Veyr understood practical silence, companionable silence, grieving silence, council silence, marital silence, and the dangerous silence before a rockslide. But there was another kind: the silence in which a person could set down, briefly, the burden of being useful.

The power would make places for that silence.

Small hollows would appear in the lee of certain ridges where the wind, by some mercy of angle and stone, did not reach. They were not warm. They were not comfortable. But inside them, sound fell away. No hammers. No hooves. No rope creak. No underground bells.

Only breath.

At first these hollows would be used for storage.

Then by adolescents avoiding work.

Then by the bereaved.

Then by couples who had nothing to say and wanted to say it together.

Eventually the Veyr would build low walls around the hollows and leave them roofless. No tablets. No scales. No covenants carved at the entrance.

They would call them Unweighted Rooms.

No one would be required to enter. No one would be praised for leaving. They would simply exist: pockets of permission in a civilization made of obligation.

The power would be pleased by this most of all.

Because mercy is not always rescue.

Sometimes mercy is margin.

And then, after these gifts, the power would withdraw—not entirely, but enough.

It would not become a ruler of Veyr-Kaht. It would not demand worship. The Veyr had covenants already, and good covenants should not be crowded by vanity.

Still, traces of it would remain.

In the clearer bell of hidden water.

In the blackening moss.

In the raven that would not move from the bridge.

In the light child whose name, one day, would be carved on a tablet so heavy it took twelve adults to lift.

In the frost-pattern hidden under a stair.

In the Unweighted Rooms where even the most dutiful could sit for an hour and owe nothing.

After five hundred years, the Veyr would argue about whether anything had happened at all.

Some would say the plateau had always been like this, if only their ancestors had been wise enough to notice.

Some would say the otters had taught them.

Some would say there was a fifth covenant, though no one could agree on its wording.

The closest version would be found carved, very small, on the inner wall of a cistern at Bell-Under-Stone:

**What endures may also be spared.**

No one would know who carved it.

The wise power would not correct them.

It would sit beyond the weather of the Broken Shelf, vast and patient, and listen again to the hammers.

Still ringing.

Still measured.

Still asking stone to become something more useful than it was.

And now, sometimes, something kinder.
