It would not come as silence.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, pitying the Choir, might descend with clean white light and sterile air, with the arrogance of rescue. It might still the moth-rivers, cauterize the fungal trees, drain the rainbow oils, make the caverns safe by making them dead. It might see the Choir of Husks and think only, *These were human once*, and from that single truth commit a hundred violences against the other truths: that the Maelstrom is alive, that the Choir has become partly of it, that suffering and belonging have braided together until no knife can separate them cleanly.

A wise power would know better.

It would arrive first as listening.

Not with ears, because ears are almost useless there, but as a patient widening of attention. It would let the moth-roar pass through it without flinching. It would taste the acids in the root-pools, read the thermal grammar of fungal trunks, learn the brief hymns of species that live and die between two swarms. It would allow itself to be mistaken for weather, for a change in pressure, for one more deep exhale from below.

For a long time, perhaps, it would do nothing at all.

It would watch the Choir tend the platforms. It would watch them breathe messages into their moth-children. It would watch the younger ones approach the transition zones, collapse in the thin quiet, and crawl back weeping spores. It would watch the eldest root themselves and become holy hives. It would listen until it could tell the difference between devotion and compulsion, between chosen transformation and the places where choice had been eaten away.

Only then would it act.

And its first act would be to give the Maelstrom a new sound.

Not silence. Never silence.

A chord.

It would enter the endless thunder as a low harmonic, almost too gentle to notice, braided through wingbeat and root-creak and the wet splitting of new growth. The chord would not command. It would suggest. It would teach the swarms a pattern they had never needed before: pause without void, rest without death, space without abandonment.

The moth-rivers would alter by degrees. Not all at once. The Maelstrom would reject anything too sudden. But here and there, in the lee of certain fungal trunks, the roar would soften into layered pulses. Not quiet, exactly, but intelligible. A place where thought could stand upright for a moment.

The Choir would find these places first.

They would gather in them, trembling. Their moth-robes would lift and settle in confused waves. The younger ones would press their simplified faces to the root-floor as if hearing a voice through a wall.

Into that softened thunder, the power would speak—not in human words, not yet, but in moth-syntax, in pheromone gradients, in arrangements of growth. It would ask the first question the Maelstrom had never learned to ask.

*Do you wish to remain as you are?*

The Choir would not understand immediately.

The Maelstrom would understand even less.

So the power would make the question physical.

It would coax from the old platforms a new instrument: root-wood arches grown from the abandoned stilts, veined with bioluminescence, tuned not to stability alone but to consent. Beneath these arches, transformation would slow. The Choir’s pores would close for minutes at a time. The moths growing from them would sleep. Their mouths, those complex reed-organs, would still be strange, but under the arches they could shape older sounds.

The first word spoken there would be a name.

No one would know whose name it was. Perhaps the speaker’s own. Perhaps the name of someone they had loved before descent. Perhaps the name of a surface river, remembered incorrectly. But when it came out, cracked and wet and barely human, every moth in the chamber would bank at once, and the fungal trees would dim as if in reverence.

The power would not make them human again.

That would be another kind of murder.

Instead it would restore the possibility of relation to what they had been.

It would cultivate, with the Choir’s own seed-libraries, a new breed of moth: pale, slow-lived, almost translucent, with wings marked by tiny branching lines like script. These would not feed on sap alone but on memory—on the electrical ghosts that still flickered in altered nerves, on the chemical residues of grief and language. They would not steal those memories. They would carry them.

Name-moths, the Choir would eventually call them.

Where they settled on a Choir member’s skin, old things could be remembered without drowning the present. A mother’s hand. A laboratory lamp. Rain on metal stairs. The taste of salt. The shape of a human sentence. The terror of the first week below. The awe of the second. The moment terror and awe became indistinguishable.

Some Choir members would tear the name-moths from themselves.

Some memories are not mercies when returned too quickly.

The power would allow this.

It would teach the name-moths not to cling.

Then it would address the second cruelty: not transformation, but imprisonment.

At the transition zones, where the Understory’s thick nutritious air thinned into ordinary cave atmosphere, the power would grow vestibules.

They would look like groves of hollow glassy reeds rooted in mats of dark fungus. Within them, the air would graduate by slow degrees: humid to dry, spore-rich to clean, chemically dense to simple. The reeds would breathe in counterpoint, not purifying the Maelstrom’s air, not contaminating the upper caves, but translating between them.

There the power would plant another gift: a symbiont small enough to live beneath the sternum, gentle enough not to rule its host. A third lung, though it would not be a lung exactly. It would learn both atmospheres. It would allow a Choir member to survive outside for an hour, then a day, then longer if they wished to endure the ache of adaptation.

Not all would take it.

The eldest rooted hives could not. Or would not. Some had traveled too far from walking to desire roads. Some had become vast inward cathedrals of moth-song, and to remove them would be like uprooting a forest to save a single tree.

The power would come to them too.

For each rooted elder, it would send a question through the moths.

*Do you want release?*

Many would answer in colors rather than thoughts.

One would answer by flowering.

Another would answer by sending out, after long silence, a swarm shaped unmistakably like a human hand opening.

For those who wanted release, the power would not drag them upward into air that would hurt them. It would teach their bodies a final migration. Their roots would loosen. Their seed-libraries would be copied into willing carriers. Their memories would rise in name-moths and settle among the Choir. Their remaining flesh would compost quickly, brilliantly, feeding a grove of soft blue trunks that sang in voices almost like theirs.

For those who wished to remain, the power would strengthen the boundary between service and consumption.

It would teach the Maelstrom another pattern.

*Not all that roots is yours to use.*

This lesson would take longest.

The Maelstrom was not cruel, but it was ancient in a way that preceded ethics. It knew appetite, succession, pressure, opportunity. It knew that every surface was invitation and every death was material. The idea of refusal would be to it as sight is to stone.

So the power would not lecture. It would demonstrate.

It would mark certain living bodies with a flavor the roots learned to recognize: not poison, not threat, but personhood. A difficult taste. A sacred bitterness. When the root-floor tried to claim a sleeping Choir member too quickly, it would recoil. When a transformation wave swept through a chamber containing marked bodies, it would break around them like water around stone. When moths emerged from their pores, they would carry the mark outward, teaching it through generations of wings.

Slowly, unevenly, the Maelstrom would begin to distinguish between compost and communion.

This would not make it gentle.

The Understory Maelstrom would still be a fever of becoming. Fungal trees would still rise in hours and collapse into steaming drifts. Mantis-creatures would still wade through insect rivers with basket-arms spread wide. Crystalline gardens would still sing below hearing. The moth-cyclones would still strip bark from the trunks and force hidden species into sudden, frantic life.

But now, threaded through the chaos, there would be intervals.

Places where the Choir could rest.

Places where memory could be set down and taken up again.

Places where a young member could say, in wing-pattern or voice or both, *I want to leave*, and the world would not answer by killing them.

Some would leave.

The first to survive the upper caves would be one who remembered her name only after she had already decided to go. She would pass through the reed-vestibule wrapped in a living mantle of adapted moths, each one carrying a fragment of Understory chemistry in its gut. She would crawl into the ordinary dark beyond, where the silence was enormous and terrifying.

For a while she would scream.

Not from pain, but from the absence of thunder.

Then one name-moth, hidden in the hollow of her throat, would beat its wings.

A little sound.

Enough.

She would live.

Others would follow, not as restored humans but as ambassadors of a threshold no map had marked. They would never be fully comfortable beneath the open sky. Sunlight would feel flat and tyrannical. Wind would seem appallingly empty. But they would learn rain. They would learn fire. They would learn speech again in broken, beautiful forms, their sentences braided with pheromone and gesture and small clouds of companion moths.

Some would return voluntarily to the Maelstrom, carrying news of stars.

Some would remain above and build humid houses full of fungal lamps, where they could sleep inside a manageable roar.

Some would stand forever between, tending the reed-vestibules, helping others choose.

And the Choir that stayed below would change as well.

Their work of ecosystem-writing would no longer be only desperate prayer or reflexive dissolution. With the power’s chord woven into their moth-speech, they would begin composing deliberately. The Spiral Garden would return in cleaner form. The Bright Halls would pulse with patterns that could store histories. The Quiet Pools, once feared as voids, would be ringed with resonant gourds that kept silence from becoming hunger.

They would write messages in succession, and the Maelstrom would answer.

Not quickly. Not like a person.

But over months, then years, certain groves would arrange themselves in repeating structures no Choir had seeded. Moth breeds would emerge bearing wing-marks like questions. Deep exhalations from below would spare the sanctuaries more often than chance allowed.

The ecosystem was waking.

Perhaps it had always been waking.

The benevolent power would not seize that awakening and shape it into a god. It would not crown the Choir as neurons or reduce them to messengers. It would remain near enough to guide, far enough to leave mystery intact.

When the Maelstrom first formed a thought large enough to touch the power in return, it would be crude and immense, made of heat and hunger and a billion brief lives.

It would ask, in the only grammar it knew:

*What am I eating?*

And the power would answer:

*The world. But not only. You are also feeding it.*

The Maelstrom would consider this for a season.

Many things would die.

Many things would be born.

Then, throughout a hundred tunnels, the moth-rivers would bend into a shape the Choir had never taught them.

A spiral opening outward.

A question, perhaps.

Or gratitude.

Or simply the first sign of an appetite learning wonder.

The wise power would see this and do no more than was needed.

It would leave no throne, no law carved into stone, no permanent temple. Permanence was not the language there. Instead it would leave living instruments: the chord in the roar, the name-moths, the reed-vestibules, the personhood-mark carried in bitter chemistry, the arches under which old names could be spoken.

Its mercy would not be rescue from change.

Its mercy would be choice within change.

And long afterward, when travelers heard rumors of the Understory Maelstrom, they would still be warned not to enter lightly. They would be told of caverns that digest certainty, of moths like weather, of fungal forests that migrate through the dark. They would be told that some who descend never return.

But they would also hear another rumor.

That in the depths there are arches of living root where the thunder softens.

That beneath them, a thing with moths growing from its skin may lift its altered face and speak a human name.

That if you are lost there, and if the Maelstrom has not yet decided whether you are food or guest, a pale moth may land upon your mouth and wait for you to answer one question:

*Do you wish to remain?*
