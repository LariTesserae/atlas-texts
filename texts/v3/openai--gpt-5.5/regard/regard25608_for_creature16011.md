The power would come quietly.

Not as a figure over the ledge, not as light breaking the gray, not as a voice in the stone. It would not arrive in a way the hollow would have to survive. It would come as attention first: a listening laid over the place so gently that even the dust would not be asked to rise.

It would look for a long time.

And because it was wise, it would not mistake scarcity for suffering.

It would see that the hollow is not a failed meadow, not an unfinished spring, not a ruin waiting to be restored into abundance. It would understand the agreement already present there: heat held in stone, water kept in cups, life spread thin so that it may continue. It would see that too much kindness, poured quickly, would be a violence. A flood would tear the films from the rock. Constant water would invite roots too large, insects too many, shade too deep, rot where there had only been patience. Rich soil would smother the mineral skin. A lush blessing would erase the very being it meant to help.

So the power would not make the hollow beautiful in any easy way.

It would not fill the pans to overflowing.  
It would not cover the clay with flowers.  
It would not summon birds for company.  
It would not give the resident a body that could walk away.

Instead, it would learn the hollow’s measure.

It would count the beads of water on the seep wall and know how many were enough. It would feel where the limestone held warmth too tightly and where night’s coolness survived under a ledge. It would follow the hidden threads in the clay, the roots that were almost not roots, the sleeping seeds that had accepted the possibility of never waking. It would read the rings in the stone pans as one reads a long and humble history.

Then it would make its first gift: not more, but steadiness.

Far above, where weather is decided in pressures and distances, it would turn aside only the harshest forgetfulness. Not all drought. Not all heat. Only the drought that would pass from discipline into extinction. Only the heat that would make waiting impossible.

Once in a long while, when the hollow had been dry past its old endurance, the power would let a cloud loosen itself a little sooner than it otherwise would have. Rain would come without drama. No storm front, no thunder splitting the ledges, no brown rush of water tearing through the runnels. Just a soft rain, small-grained and persistent, falling for an hour longer than expected.

The stone pans would fill to their rims.

The dark rings would wake.

The green-black film on the seep wall would swell, not into excess, but into relief. Moss would lift by the width of a breath. The grasses would drink at their bases. The shrub would accept water through its deep, narrow patience and put out, perhaps, one more leaf than it had planned.

The hollow would not become another place. It would become itself, continued.

The power’s second gift would be shelter from the careless.

It would not build a wall. Walls invite notice. It would do something subtler. Paths in the dry country beyond would bend by a little. Feet would choose other ground without knowing why. Animals, if they wandered near in some rarer season, would be drawn to a different scent, a different shadow, another basin briefly holding more water. Human eyes, if they crossed the ledge, would understand the hollow too quickly and pass on: nothing here, only stone, only stain, only a small dry pocket in the land.

The three low stone alignments would remain undisturbed. No hand would decide to restore them. No scholar would pry them open for meaning. No collector would take a lichen-marked stone because its surface seemed interesting. The hollow would keep its uncertain history, which is a kind of dignity.

The third gift would be repair, but only where repair could be mistaken for time.

In one crack, beneath the shallow circular depression, the power would settle a grain of clay into place so that water lingered half a day longer after rain. Not a pond. Never a pond. Only a longer darkening.

Along the seep wall, it would ease a mineral blockage thinner than a root hair, so that the vertical stain did not fail entirely in some future dry year. The seep would not strengthen enough to flow. It would only continue to bead.

At the base of the single shrub, it would let fallen leaves break down a little more kindly, becoming not richness, but a faint darkening in the clay, enough to feed what already belonged there.

It would leave the dead portions dead. It would not reverse every flake, every brittle moss edge, every pale grass tip. It would understand that this life includes drying, and that some parts must become silt so others may hold.

The fourth gift would be knowledge, but not the kind that burdens.

The resident of the hollow has no single mind, no eyes to lift, no throat for gratitude. A lesser power might think this meant there was no one to receive a blessing. But the wise power would know better. It would not force consciousness into the lichen and moss. It would not make the many-bodied skin suddenly aware of loneliness, danger, time, or the vastness of what watches over it.

Instead it would place, deep in the shared chemistry of the place, a small ease.

When water came, the waking would be a little less costly. Cells folded inward for months would open without tearing. Threads finer than hair would find old paths through the clay. The moss would remember swelling. The algae would meet light without shock. Dormancy would still be dormancy, but revival would carry a faint rightness through it, like a wordless yes.

And in the dry times, when the hollow returned to gray and brittleness, there would be no panic in the waiting. Not hope, exactly. Hope is too sharp a thing for such a life. Rather, a deepened trust in recurrence. A memory of having endured before. A patience made slightly more whole.

The fifth gift would be invisibly given to the future.

The power would look far ahead, beyond seasons, beyond the life of the shrub, beyond the slow widening of cracks. It would see possible endings: a century of rainlessness, a quarry line drawn through the limestone, a road cut too near, a sudden invasive root taking hold after an unusual storm, a chemical dust blown from some distant human work. It would not cancel all endings. Even benevolence does not make a place eternal by command.

But it would turn away the meaningless endings.

The quarry surveyor would misread the stone quality and mark another ridge.  
The road would be cheaper elsewhere.  
The poison dust would fall short in a rain before reaching the hollow.  
The invasive seed would lodge on an exposed slab and dry before it could root.  
The worst storm in five hundred years would split around the ledges and spend its force beyond them.

The hollow would still erode. The ledges would still peel. The stone alignments would sink lower by grains. The shrub would one day die. Another might not replace it for many years. The life spread thinly through the damp seams would recede, return, recede again. But it would be granted the protection of meaningful difficulty, not senseless destruction.

Last, the power would give no sign.

No shining mark on the limestone. No sudden flower in the center. No voice saying beloved. The hollow would not know it had been chosen, because to know such a thing might separate it from its own simplicity.

Yet something would be different.

A drop would fall from the seep wall into a stone basin with the same small rounded click as before, but the basin would hold the sound a little tenderly.

The wind would enter low over the ledge and leave without taking quite as much moisture from the moss.

The dark rim of the circular depression would wake after rain and tighten again after drying, season after season, with a resilience almost too quiet to call joy.

The grasses would remain sparse.

The shrub would remain angular.

The stones would remain pale, warm, and worn.

And the resident of the hollow—the skin of lichen, moss, algae, root hair, mineral thread, sleeping seed, and dampened clay—would continue its ancient practice of being nearly absent and wholly alive.

If the power loved it, it would love it by restraint.

If the power blessed it, the blessing would be this:

enough water,  
not too much;

enough shade,  
not darkness;

enough change,  
not upheaval;

enough protection  
that the hollow may go on belonging to itself.
