A benevolent and wise power would not descend into that city like a sun.

It would know better.

Light, in such a place, could be violence. Sudden warmth could be fever. Rescue could be extinction disguised as kindness. The city has not asked to be raised. The Silent Ones have not asked to become human again. The Bone-Carvers have not asked for their histories to be corrected. Even the One Below, vast and hungry in the trenches, is not a monster to be slain merely because smaller lives fear it.

So the power would come quietly.

Not as a god with a face.

Not as a voice.

It would enter as a change in the current so subtle that no creature would flee from it. A slow spiraling tenderness in the water. A pressure that did not crush. A presence like a hand held near, but not touching.

For a long while, it would do nothing at all.

It would listen.

It would listen to the clicking claws of the Bone-Carvers tracing old wars and forgotten harvests into stone. It would listen to the Silent Ones’ finger-ripples, the grammar of tilt and pause, the brief flaring of flesh-grown lures. It would listen to the drifting colonies digesting the smallest lives in the dark. It would listen to the slow hunger below, to the great body turning in the trenches, not cruel, not wrathful, only immense and ancient and bound to its nature.

It would let the city teach it what mercy means underwater.

And only then would it begin.

First, it would strengthen what already lives.

Not with miracles that break the world, but with small mercies braided into its laws.

A current would begin to move through the upper ruins once every long cycle, carrying fresh microscopic life into the kelp-hollows where the Silent Ones dwell. The blind fish would grow slightly more abundant. The translucent wall-bulbs would glow a little longer before dimming. The silt would settle faster after storms, so hunters could see faint motion again.

No one would know why.

The Silent Ones would only notice that fewer young vanished from hunger.

Then the power would touch the ruins.

Not to restore the city. Restoration would be another kind of drowning, a refusal to accept what time has made. Instead, it would preserve certain places from collapse: a stairway the Silent Ones use during migrations, a chamber where eggs are hidden, a narrow bridge over a black drop where too many have fallen.

The stone would not become new.

It would simply remember how to hold.

In the deep temples, the Bone-Carvers would find walls that no longer flaked beneath their claws. Their markings would endure. When they carved, the stone would accept the symbols cleanly, without crumbling. In time, they would discover a new chamber unveiled by a gentle settling of silt: a hall vast enough for all the city’s histories, its ceiling webbed with slow blue moss.

They would not thank the power.

They would carve.

That would be thanks enough.

For the Drifters, the power would make no judgment. The harmless and the deadly are not opposites in the deep; they are strategies. Still, it would guide the most venomous colonies away from the Silent Ones’ nurseries by tuning faint mineral tastes into the currents, drawing them instead toward bloom-fields where they could feed without killing the fragile.

The Drifters would pulse.

They would follow.

Then, at last, the power would turn toward the One Below.

This would be the hardest mercy.

A lesser kindness might try to destroy it. A frightened kindness might seal it away. A sentimental kindness might attempt to make it gentle.

But wisdom would know: hunger is not evil. Hunger is a law. To erase it entirely would be to unmake the deep.

So the power would descend into the trenches and rest near the vast thing without fear.

The One Below would feel it.

For the first time in ages, something would enter its dark that was neither prey nor rival.

The waters would go still.

The pressure would change.

The great mouth, or mouths, or whatever ancient opening served as hunger, would turn toward the presence.

The power would not command it.

It would offer.

Far beneath the city, where the rock still held old heat, the power would open sleeping vents. Not violent ones. Not poisoned ones. Slow black gardens of mineral warmth, where pale bacteria could bloom, where blind worms could gather, where shell-creatures could breed in multitudes.

A new food-web would begin in the lowest dark.

At first, the One Below would still hunt as it always had. It was old. Old hungers do not change with a single gift.

But over time, the trenches would grow richer. The great one would turn more often toward the vent-fields and less often toward the inhabited ruins. Its long passages through the city would become rarer. The Silent Ones would still fear the pressure-shift, but it would no longer always mean death.

The city would still feed it.

But not only with its children.

That would be enough.

The wise power would also give one gift that was not survival.

It would give memory back in a form that did not wound.

One night — though night and day mean little there — the bioluminescent moss across the old central plaza would bloom all at once. Not brightly. Never brightly. Just enough.

The Silent Ones would gather, drawn by the soft blue-green widening in the dark.

The Bone-Carvers would emerge from their chambers.

Drifters would hover in the broken arches like lanterns.

Even the predators would keep to the edges, stilled by some instinct older than appetite.

Then the water above the plaza would fill with shapes.

Not ghosts. Not commands. Not a revelation forced into unwilling minds.

Only impressions.

Hands building stone before the drowning. Children running through air. Rain striking terraces. Fires in bronze bowls. Songs, though no sound carried. Faces of those who had lived before lungs became gills, before speech became gesture, before the city passed from civilization into reef.

The Silent Ones would watch with their black lidless eyes.

Some would understand. Some would not. Some would feel only a strange ache, like a current moving through the place where a voice might once have been.

The Bone-Carvers would begin, immediately, to carve.

Not because the vision was complete, but because it was incomplete.

Because memory always is.

The power would not tell the Silent Ones, You were once greater.

It would not tell them, You have fallen.

It would let them know only this:

You have continued.

And continuing is holy.

After that, the power would withdraw a little.

Not leave. Not entirely.

A benevolent power does not need worship, and a wise one does not become the center of a world that already has its own center. It would remain as a pressure of care woven through the waters.

A guardian current.

A patient witness.

A mercy with no temple.

If a tower began to fall above sleeping young, the stone might shift differently.

If hunters became lost in silt, a moss-glow might wake along the safe path.

If the Bone-Carvers reached a wall bearing a truth too painful to preserve alone, their claws might find the stone softer.

If the One Below grew desperate, the vent-fields might bloom.

And if, after centuries, the Silent Ones changed again — if they developed new gestures for gratitude, grief, wonder, and choice — the power would not claim credit.

It would simply watch them make meaning in the dark.

Because that is what the wise power would understand:

This place does not need to be saved from being itself.

It needs only to be loved without being conquered.

So the drowned city would remain drowned.

The waters would remain warm and heavy.

The ruins would remain ancient.

The creatures would still hunt, drift, carve, hide, hunger, and remember.

But somewhere in the labyrinth of stone and silt, a Silent One would pause beside a newly glowing wall-bulb and feel, without knowing why, that the dark was not empty.

Somewhere in the deep temple, a Bone-Carver would etch a new symbol: not for ruler, not for god, not for rescue.

A spiral-current.

A held stone.

A mouth turned away.

A light that did not blind.

And far below, the One Below would sleep beside black mineral gardens, its hunger quiet for a while, while above it the city dreamed on.
