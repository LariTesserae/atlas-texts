A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a sun.

A sun would be violence here.

It would not tear open the bruise-colored cloud membrane and pour gold into the plateau, bleaching the moss, blinding the Resonant, frightening the winged vent-creatures from their thermals. It would not silence the Great Engine with one divine palm, because silence, to this people, would not mean peace. It would mean the end of measurement. The end of warning. The end of the rhythm by which children learn to walk.

So it would come first as a change too gentle to be named.

A slightly sweeter taste in the moss-water.

A condensation bead that trembles on a brass railing but does not fall until a child has passed beneath it.

A wrong corner that, when glanced at by accident, holds its shape.

The wise power would listen before it acted.

It would sink its attention down through the fused basalt plaza, through the iron strata, through the pipes sweating mineral water, through the immense drill-bits petrified in their downward hunger. It would listen to the *thump* beneath all things—not with ears, but with the patience of mountains and the sympathy of nurses. It would learn that the Engine is not evil. It is unfinished. It is not a tyrant but a birth-process without midwives, a world extruding itself too fast, too roughly, with no one to ease the pressure, no one to tell the newborn stone how to settle.

It would learn the Resonant next.

Not as specimens. Not as “the inhabitants.” Not as a problem to be solved.

It would learn the weight of their lead-bead child harnesses. The grammar of their chest-hums. The angle at which a Listener presses bone-and-brass horns to iron and knows, from a difference smaller than fear, that a layer is about to shear. It would learn the taste of vent-moss liquor, the grief of the Lost, the taboos around corners, the way love is expressed by tightening another’s grav-weight before a bridge crossing.

Only then would it begin.

Not with commandment.

With accompaniment.

The first gift would be rhythm.

Deep below, where the world-heart struck its cavernous beat, the benevolent power would place no chain and no muzzle. It would not stop the *thump*. Instead, it would answer it.

A second pulse would enter the stone, almost indistinguishable from the first, like a hand joining another hand in work. The Engine would strike—

*thump*

—and from some merciful elsewhere would come a soft counter-resonance, not louder, not stronger, but perfectly timed.

The gantries would still groan. The vents would still hiss. The drill-spires would still dream of descent.

But the violent aftershocks would lessen.

Gravity would stop lunging so often.

The bridges would no longer surprise the feet with sudden cruelty.

A falling tool in a thin spot would drift, then settle, rather than hanging for hours in impossible indecision.

The Resonant would notice within three cycles. The children first, because children notice mercy before adults trust it.

They would stomp experimentally on bridge grates and laugh through their masks when the world answered evenly.

The Listeners would be afraid.

Then they would listen harder.

And in the iron beneath their palms they would hear it: not a replacement for the Engine, not an intruder, but a harmony.

A vast kindness humming along.

The second gift would be edges.

The power would turn its attention to the Seams.

It would not seal them all. That would be too simple, and perhaps too destructive. Some Seams were part of the plateau’s becoming. Some were the necessary looseness in the joints of a world still assembling itself. To cauterize every uncertainty would make the place brittle. It might crack under the next birth of stone.

Instead, the power would teach the Seams manners.

Corners that blurred would blur slowly.

Walls becoming permeable would first grow cold, then luminous, then sing in a frequency even the youngest could feel in their teeth. Shadows that meant to lag behind their casters would darken at the edges before misbehaving. Gravity wells would develop halos of suspended droplets, little floating constellations of warning.

The taboos of the Resonant would not be mocked or abolished. Their sensory discipline had saved them for generations. But now their caution would have allies.

The Lost would be considered next.

Here the wise power would pause a long while.

There are mercies that cannot be performed without first asking the dead, and the almost-dead, and the not-dead-but-elsewhere.

So the power would enter the background hum where the Resonant believed the Lost had gone. It would sift the machinery’s endless vibration, not to extract them like ore, but to distinguish voice from noise.

It would find some.

Not all.

Some had diffused too completely into the plateau’s unfinished laws. Some had become pattern, pressure, echo. Some did not wish to return, if wishing could still be said of them. They had widened beyond bodies, and though their state was strange, it was not only suffering.

But some remained caught.

A hand half-accreted into a wall for twenty years of local time.

A child’s last fear repeating in a soft corner behind a sealed dwelling.

A Listener who followed a false harmonic too deeply and became a tremor in a pipe.

For these, the benevolent power would open small doors.

Not grand portals. Not blazing miracles.

A patch of moss would brighten in an old corridor. A wall would exhale. A family would wake to hear, through the floor, a hum in the exact cadence of a grandmother’s name-sign.

The retrieved would not always return whole. Some would come back with translucent fingertips. Some would speak in echoes for a season. Some would remember months that had not happened yet, or weep at right angles, or refuse to sleep indoors.

The wise power would not hide this cost.

It would give the Resonant a choice.

And because they are a people of maintenance, not conquest, they would build a hall for the Returned. They would line it with moss and warm pipes. They would sit with those who came back altered and teach them again the weight of cups, the patience of spoons, the safe way to glance past a corner.

The third gift would be water.

The Resonant already knew water better than most peoples. They harvested it from moss, breath, stone, steam, and condensation. They treated it not as a resource but as a recurring miracle with a difficult temperament.

The benevolent power would honor that expertise.

It would not summon rivers. Rivers would cut the new basalt, drown the chasms, rot the cliff-homes, and invite collapses in strata not ready for such insistence.

Instead it would alter the condensation.

Across the plateau, the vent-moss would grow denser, its green glow deepening to a blue-white sheen at the edges. Droplets would form cleaner. Heavy metals would settle harmlessly into removable nodules at the roots. The intricate funnel systems of the Resonant would begin collecting not more than they could store, but enough that no child’s ration would need thinning after a failed harvest.

In time, the Resonant would discover that the new mineral nodules could be ground into a powder that strengthened pipe-paste. The chitinous scavengers would develop brighter shell markings after feeding on the enriched moss. The organ-pipe fungi would release fewer choking spores and more edible caps.

Nothing would be wasted.

A wise power knows that the best miracle becomes part of the local economy.

The fourth gift would be warning.

High above the chasms, in the bruise-purple gloom, the power would touch the pale fungi groves. Not to make them prophets, exactly, but to make them instruments.

Before a drill-bit descended, the organ-pipe fungi nearest the fault would sound a low chord.

Before a pressure valve failed, vent-moss around the pipe would dim in a widening ring.

Before a bridge entered a period of treacherous lightness, its rivets would sweat silver.

The Listeners would still matter. More than ever, perhaps. For now there would be more to interpret, more nuance, more conversation between people and place. Their caste would not be replaced by divine convenience. They would become what they had always nearly been: physicians of a living world.

And the world, under this care, would become more willing to be diagnosed.

The fifth gift would be a refuge.

Not an escape. Not a paradise elsewhere. The wise power would understand that to save a people by removing them from the matrix of their meaning is sometimes only another form of erasure.

But everyone needs somewhere the floor does not lie.

At the center of the fused basalt plaza, where cooling cracks made a black web underfoot, the power would steady one circle of ground.

No larger than a common hall at first.

Within that circle, gravity would remain constant. Corners would hold. Shadows would behave. Water would fall downward. A dropped tool would strike stone with the ordinary music of cause and effect.

The Resonant would distrust it.

Of course they would.

A place without fluctuation would feel uncanny. Vulnerable. Exposed. Some would whisper that it was a trap, a dead patch, a place cut off from the god-skin of the plateau. The first to enter willingly would be an old Listener whose knees had been ruined by decades of pressure shifts. She would step across the boundary carrying her horn-stethoscope and a grav-weight twice as heavy as necessary.

She would stand there.

Nothing would happen.

She would remove the grav-weight.

Nothing would happen.

She would begin to cry, quietly, not because the place was beautiful, but because for the first time in eighty years she would not have to negotiate with the ground.

After that, the circle would become many things.

A birthing place.

A surgery.

A school for toddlers.

A chamber for the Returned.

A place where grieving families could look directly at a corner and say the names of the Lost without fear.

The Resonant would build around it, but not over it. They would call it, in their vibration-language, something like: *The Floor That Keeps Its Promise.*

The sixth gift would be restraint.

This would be the greatest one, though few would recognize it.

The benevolent power would not finish the world.

It could.

Perhaps it could reach down into the molten architecture and say: enough. It could smooth the chasms, settle the strata, freeze the drill-bits, clarify the sky, harden the laws. It could make the plateau safe, comprehensible, clean.

But wisdom would forbid it.

The world was becoming. Its becoming was dangerous, yes, and careless, and full of suffering at the edges. But it was also the source of the Resonant’s arts, languages, foods, rituals, sciences, humor, courage, and tenderness. To complete the world from outside would be to decide, arrogantly, what shape its soul should take.

So the power would do what good midwives do.

It would ease.

It would brace.

It would monitor fever.

It would turn catastrophe into difficulty where it could, and difficulty into labor where possible.

It would preserve the dignity of those already surviving.

It would allow the Engine to learn.

And slowly—very slowly, on the scale of gantries and mountains—the Great Engine would begin to respond.

The *thump* would deepen.

Not grow quieter. Not gentler, exactly.

More deliberate.

The drill-spires would no longer descend at random, but after long preparatory chords that gave settlements time to move. New basalt would extrude in terraces rather than jagged upheavals. Iron strata would align into load-bearing ribs. Chasms would remain, but their edges would become less hungry.

The plateau would still be strange.

It would still steam and glow. Its shadows would still occasionally sulk. Its clouds would still press low and purple against the height of the world. Scavenger-beasts would still clatter through scrap corrals. Leathery vent-fliers would still mimic failing machinery badly enough to annoy every engineer within hearing.

But fear would no longer be the chief architect of daily life.

One cycle, a child would be born in the Floor That Keeps Its Promise. Her first breath would mist her tiny rebreather. Her parents would hum her name into the soles of her feet. She would grow up wearing lead beads, yes, but fewer than her mother had worn. She would be taught not to stare at Seams, but also how to read the moss-halos that warned of them. She would learn the old evacuation routes and the new fungal chords. She would drink clean moss-water. She would hear stories of the time before the Harmony, when the world lurched without warning and the Lost almost never returned.

She would ask, as children do, whether the benevolent power was a god.

The old Listener, now mostly retired, would press her hand to the basalt and consider.

Then she would hum an answer.

Not god.

Not machine.

Not parent.

A good neighbor, perhaps.

A careful guest.

Someone strong enough not to need worship.

Someone kind enough not to demand gratitude.

And far below them, the Great Engine would beat.

*thump*

And under it, around it, within it, the answering harmony would remain.

Not saving the world from becoming.

Helping it become without tearing itself apart.
