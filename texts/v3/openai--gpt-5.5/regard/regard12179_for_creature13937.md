If a benevolent and wise power noticed the gorge, it would not enter like lightning.

It would not still the bells to prove itself.

It would not warm the stone, or widen the sky, or make the keeper young again, or translate the marks on the shelter wall into a language anyone could read. It would understand, before acting, that many kindnesses are forms of vandalism. It would understand that the gorge is not broken because it is cold, not lonely because it is dark, not incomplete because its meaning is difficult.

So first it would listen.

Not for an hour, or a season, but in the manner of powers that are not hurried by blood. It would listen to the fundamental tone in the basalt and to the thousand separate water-voices braided through it. It would listen to the bells’ green trembling, to the moss drinking in darkness, to the tiny organisms in the channels making their nearly invisible bargains with cold. It would listen to the keeper’s pulse when she slept in the shelter, and to the difference between her sleep and waking, which had become so slight that only something very patient could distinguish them.

It would listen until it knew the place not as an object, not as a sacred site, not as a curiosity, but as a being made of relationships.

Stone with water.

Water with channel.

Channel with hand.

Hand with memory.

Memory with need.

Need with return.

Only then would it act.

And even then, almost no one would notice.

---

The first thing it would do would be to place no crown upon the gorge.

No revelation. No prophecy. No sudden fame. No pilgrims arriving by the hundreds, trampling moss and leaving offerings too numerous to be absorbed. The power would see at once that exposure would be the easiest way to destroy what it loved.

So it would turn aside the attention that would come too sharply.

A documentary filmmaker would lose interest after a failed correspondence, finding instead another subject, a glacier with better access and clearer narrative. A tourism official would misplace a report. A wealthy eccentric, hearing of “the singing gorge,” would fall ill before organizing an expedition and later forget why the phrase had seemed important. A researcher with careless hands would receive funding elsewhere.

None of this would feel like obstruction. It would feel like ordinary life: delays, changing priorities, accidents of administration. The gorge would remain known, but not famous. Reachable, but not easy. Present in footnotes, rumors, monastic recollections, the altered music of one composer, the private maps of the anxious and grieving.

The power would not hide the gorge from those who truly needed it.

It would only keep it from becoming a destination.

---

The second thing it would do would be for the keeper.

Not rescue her.

That would be the temptation of lesser mercy.

A lesser mercy would see Ragnhild curled in damp sheepskins, her bones cold, her daughter’s face fading from memory, and would say: this is suffering; remove her. It would lift her into sunlight, restore her appetite, return her to ordinary speech, surround her with human warmth. It would call this healing.

But the wise power would know better.

It would see that the keeper had not been devoured by the gorge. She had entered into covenant with it. The bargain was severe, but not cruel. She had become strange, yes, but not diminished. The self she had laid down in the sound had not been murdered. It had been thinned to a translucence through which something calmer could pass.

So the power would not take the gorge from her.

Instead, it would ease the terms.

A capillary in her left hand, long narrowed by cold, would open a little more reliably. The ache in her knees would remain, but deepen more slowly. On nights when her body approached dangerous chill, a dry fold of air would linger in the shelter longer than physics strictly required. Her fish oil lamp would smoke a little less. The fungus in one corner of the sheepskins would fail to establish itself. The hard cheese she forgot for nine days would not spoil.

Small mercies. Animal mercies. The kind that do not interrupt a life’s chosen shape.

Once, during a winter of unusual severity, when ice formed in a channel whose voice was necessary to the sixth bell, she would slip while clearing it. In the ordinary course of things her temple would strike basalt.

The power would not make her float.

It would not shine.

It would simply loosen a patch of moss months earlier, encourage it to thicken in precisely that place, let it grow into a dark cushion wet enough and deep enough that when she fell, she would bruise badly but live.

She would lie there for some time, breathing steam into the dark, the bell above her shivering itself back toward audibility. She would not know she had been spared. She would think, with irritation and gratitude mingled beyond separation, that the moss had grown too far and must be trimmed.

Later she would transplant the excess to the northern wall.

It would take.

---

The power would also remember what the keeper herself had allowed to fade.

Not in order to torment her.

Not by forcing memory upon her like an accusation.

But somewhere, held outside decay, it would keep the daughter’s face as it had been when Ragnhild last saw it clearly: not idealized, not softened by nostalgia, but real. The line between the brows. The small asymmetry of the mouth. The way she looked away when hurt and directly forward when angry.

It would keep this memory safe because forgetting is sometimes mercy, but sometimes it is erosion beyond consent.

Then, one summer, when light reached the gorge floor for twenty minutes and made the water-channels silver, the keeper would see in a cup-stone’s reflection not a vision, exactly, but an arrangement of brightness and shadow that recalled the daughter’s cheek.

The memory would return without violence.

Ragnhild would sit down hard on the mineralized platform. For several minutes, perhaps longer, she would remember the child at seven years old, then at twelve, then as the grown woman leaving. The sound would not soften. The cold would not withdraw. Nothing would comfort her.

But the memory would be whole.

That night she would add a mark to the shelter wall: two parallel lines crossed by a third, shallow but deliberate.

She would not know what it meant.

The power would.

---

For the daughter, far away, it would do even less.

Wisdom would forbid summoning her. No dream-command, no guilt sharpened into pilgrimage, no voice saying go to your mother. Love that coerces reunion is only possession wearing a holy mask.

Instead, the power would arrange one opportunity.

Years later, in a city library during rain, the daughter would open a book she did not intend to borrow. It would be a collection of essays on soundscapes, poorly shelved. In it there would be a passing mention of a cold basalt cleft in the north, tended by unnamed keepers.

She would read the paragraph twice.

She would feel nothing dramatic. No certainty. No forgiveness. Only a small displacement inside herself, as if some long-stuck object had shifted by the width of a hair.

She would write the place-name down.

Whether she came would remain hers.

The power would not touch that freedom.

---

The third thing it would do would be for succession.

The gorge had endured because someone always returned. But chains made of human memory are fragile, and this one had grown thin.

So the power would incline chance, lightly.

The anxious young man, after one of his three-day descents, would surface into a weather he had not expected: snow in late spring, soft and directionless. The path would be obscured. He would have to remain near the gorge an extra night in the village.

There he would meet the child-now-adult who came each year for the sibling’s death.

They would not fall in love. That would be too simple, and false.

They would share a table because the inn was crowded. They would speak awkwardly at first, both still half-deafened by the gorge in different ways. Eventually, because speech after the gorge often bypasses ordinary defenses, they would say true things.

He would speak of fear.

She would speak of grief.

Neither would advise the other.

Years later, when Ragnhild failed to surface, these two would both hear of it. Not immediately. Not by miracle. Through village talk, delayed letters, the composer’s network, the monk’s successor.

They would arrive within three days of each other.

Neither would intend to stay.

One of them would.

The other would return regularly with supplies, and this too would be a form of keeping.

The power would not decide which one. It would only make the meeting possible.

---

The fourth thing it would do would be for the bells.

The bells were old, and their corrosion was not evil. To preserve them unchanged forever would be to make the gorge false. The drift in their tone was part of the song. Their eventual breaking was part of the truth.

But not yet.

The wise power, loving continuity but not stasis, would slow one hidden failure.

Deep inside the iron hook that held the third bell, rust had formed a seam. In another thirteen winters it would split during a freeze. The bell would fall, not shatter, but crack against a river stone placed below it centuries ago. Its voice would become a dull knocking, then silence.

The power would not remove the rust.

It would guide a droplet of rendered fat, applied by the keeper’s habitual hand, deeper than capillary action alone would have taken it. The seam would darken, seal, hold.

The bell would remain itself for another generation.

Not forever.

Long enough.

---

The fifth thing it would do would be for the water.

Here the power would be most careful.

Water is not merely a feature of such a place. It is the grammar. Alter it too much and the sentence ceases to mean.

There were changes coming beyond the gorge: warmer summers, erratic snowpack, human thirst, distant drilling into aquifers whose existence no villager suspected. The gorge’s seep was reliable but not invulnerable.

The power could, perhaps, have commanded the underground water to continue unchanged. But that would make the gorge a museum piece in a changing world, exempt from consequence, and wisdom rarely exempts what it loves from all sorrow. Sorrow is one of the ways places remain real.

So it would not freeze climate, nor reverse industry with a gesture.

It would work through smaller doors.

A surveyor would mark a drilling site two hundred meters farther east than planned, irritated by bad footing and a broken instrument. The well would still serve the farm, but miss the fracture network feeding the gorge.

A landowner, preparing to sell the upper plateau for quarrying, would have a dream—not of angels or warnings, but of standing in a darkness full of sound that made him feel, for the first time in years, that his dead father was not entirely gone. He would wake unsettled and delay the sale. During the delay, a conservation trust would make a modest offer. He would accept it, telling himself the money was sufficient and the paperwork simpler.

A hydrologist’s graduate student, looking for a manageable thesis, would notice old data in a neglected archive and propose a study of cold-air refugia and subsurface seep systems. Her work would not make the gorge famous, but it would place the plateau inside a protected watershed category no one had previously thought to apply.

None of this would save everything.

It would save enough.

For a while.

---

The sixth thing it would do would be to honor the dead workers.

Not by revealing their names, because their names were gone even from themselves, dissolved into languages no mouth now shaped.

But the power would know them.

The first one who noticed the stone’s resonance and pressed an ear to basalt.

The child who carried water in a hide vessel before the channels were cut.

The woman who insisted the second bell be moved a hand’s breadth north, though others mocked her until the tone proved her right.

The man who lost two fingers setting the platform beams.

The old ones who came when the gorge was already old and said, as every generation says, “The ancients knew more than we do,” not realizing they themselves were becoming ancient.

The power would gather these nameless intentions—not as ghosts, not as personalities preserved against their will, but as warmth is preserved in stone after sunset for a little while. It would let their work continue to be what it had become: not monuments to identity, but contributions to an ongoing act.

Once, a researcher photographing the shelter wall would capture an image in which overlapping marks, under angled lamplight, briefly revealed a pattern no one had seen before: not language, not map, but evidence of many hands returning to the same form across centuries.

She would publish the photograph in an obscure paper.

A reader somewhere would see it and weep without understanding why.

That would be enough.

---

The seventh thing it would do would be nothing at all.

This would be the hardest action.

There would be suffering it would not prevent.

A pilgrim would descend seeking relief and find none.

A relationship would end after two people heard, in the gorge’s pressure, the truth already between them.

The keeper would sometimes be lonely, though she had chosen a life beyond ordinary loneliness.

Moss would be torn by careless boots.

A birch would die.

The bells would continue their atom-by-atom surrender to air and water.

The power would grieve these things, but not abolish them. Benevolence is not the elimination of all breaking. Wisdom knows that to protect a thing from every loss is to protect it from time, and to protect it from time is to remove it from life.

The gorge’s holiness, if that word can be used, was not purity.

It was participation.

Everything there entered the song by wearing down.

Stone, metal, water, body, memory.

The power would not end that.

It would only keep the wearing from becoming waste.

---

In the keeper’s last winter, many years from now, the power would come nearer.

Still without spectacle.

Ragnhild would be very old by then, though no one would agree on how old. Her hands would tremble when she trimmed moss. Her hearing in the surface world would have become almost useless, but in the gorge she would still detect changes no instrument had been left long enough to learn.

She would know, before anyone told her, that she was nearing the end of her tending.

This would not frighten her. But it would make her attentive in a new way.

She would spend three days clearing the channels with unusual care. She would sit beneath each bell. She would press her palm to the western wall where the cold came through most strongly. She would climb, once, to the surface and stand under a sky so blue it seemed indecent.

She would think of her daughter.

Perhaps the daughter would be there.

Perhaps not.

The power would accept either.

On the final night, Ragnhild would sleep in the shelter, wrapped in sheepskins, the drone focused through stone into her bones. Her breathing would slow until it nearly matched the pulse of the pale submerged growths in the deepest pools.

She would dream—not of paradise, not of judgment, not of reunion staged for comfort.

She would dream of the gorge before human hands: raw split basalt, unchanneled seep, sound without bells. Then of the first channel cut. Then of bronze lifted into place. Then of moss advancing. Then of herself descending for the first time, though in the dream she would see from above and below at once: a warm creature entering a cold instrument.
