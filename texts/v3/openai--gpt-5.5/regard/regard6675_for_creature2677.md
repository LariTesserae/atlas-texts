The benevolent power would not begin by changing the place.

It would begin by listening.

Not with ears, because ears would be useless there. It would lay its attention along the mud and stone like a second skin. It would learn the basin as the basin knows itself: by pressure, tremor, seepage, taste, warmth, chemical drift, the minute shiver of one body passing near another in dark water. It would not recoil from the scraping, the clatter, the wet tearing, the bellows rolling under the cloud. It would not call the place evil because it is hungry.

A lesser kindness might try to bring sunlight.

A foolish mercy might seed it with green.

A frightened god might silence the predators, lift the parasites away, clear the pools, still the swarms, dry the mud, and leave behind a clean dead world.

This power would understand better.

The basin is not a wound. It is a way of being.

So the first gift would be restraint.

The cloud would remain. The stone would remain. The cold mineral seepage would continue to breathe from below. The raspers would scrape, the braid-worms would knot and divide, the shell-knots would open their combs in rainwater, the winged swarms would clatter in the vents, the bellows would move like living banks through the black pans.

The power would not remove appetite.

Appetite is the law by which this world translates itself.

But it would look for waste.

Not death. Death is not waste here. Death feeds. Death circulates. Death is the basin’s grammar.

Waste is different.

Waste is a vent souring beyond all use because a buried seam collapsed too sharply.

Waste is a spawning season lost not to feeding mouths, but to a poison concentration no creature could answer.

Waste is a whole colony trapped by a changed channel where no old instinct could guide them.

Waste is pain that teaches nothing, feeds nothing, shapes nothing, and only burns.

There, the power would act.

Quietly.

It would deepen certain cracks by the width of a larva.

It would lift a shelf of stone half a finger higher, so rainwater could pass beneath it instead of drowning every sealed egg-case there.

It would tilt a field of slabs by less than the thickness of a shell, and in the next storm a new runnel would form, connecting two pools that had been killing their young by isolation.

It would cool one vent that had grown too hot.

It would warm another that was failing.

It would loosen minerals in the deep seams so the black and copper and violet films could thicken again where they had begun to starve.

No creature would see a hand.

No miracle would split the clouds.

The basin would simply continue, and because it continued, the blessing would be complete.

The second gift would be mercy, but not the sentimental kind.

The power would not make prey uncatchable. It would not make hunters ashamed. It would not teach the parasites repentance.

But it might touch the nerves of the place.

A grazer folded suddenly into a stone-mimic’s mouth would know terror for a moment, and then not much after that.

A winged animal pierced at the joint would struggle as long as struggle mattered, and then the struggle would become distant, like rain heard through rock.

Larvae consumed by filter-combs would not suffer as little animals suffer in brighter worlds, where fear has room to make stories.

The old bellow sinking under its parasite load, too heavy to move, would feel the mud as warmth instead of prison when the scavengers came.

Pain would remain where pain served warning, escape, memory, and the fierce preservation of life.

But useless agony would be thinned.

Not abolished. Thinned.

Like mist.

The third gift would be continuance.

The power would look upon the basin’s ancient cycles and place a hidden steadiness beneath them.

Rain would still come unpredictably, but never so absent for so long that the films died from the seams outward.

Floods would still rearrange the flats, but not often enough to erase every old colony at once.

The cloud ceiling would press low, but not close entirely.

The underground waters would wander, but always leave some path back.

Extinction would remain possible, because a world without endings cannot grow forms worth preserving. But annihilation by accident, by imbalance too large for any creature’s body to answer, would be held at a distance.

The power would become, perhaps, a kind of unseen tide chart under the chaos.

Not control.

Cadence.

It would let the basin be violent, but not fragile.

The fourth gift would be memory.

Not human memory. Not names. Not speech. Not history carved into stone.

Something older and more bodily.

The seam-clingers would receive it first.

Those dark colonies along warm cracks already behaved like nervous surfaces. Touch one edge and the whole tightened. A predator approached and hundreds withdrew. During spawning, they pulsed together through vibration.

The power would deepen that capacity.

Across generations, the colonies would begin to remember more than scars and seep-lines. They would remember the shape of successful retreats. They would remember storms. They would remember sour vents. They would send faint pulses through stone when underground water shifted.

Not language.

Not wisdom as people mean wisdom.

But warning.

Pattern.

A distributed knowing, slow and mineral, passing from colony to colony along the basin’s warm seams.

The braid-worms would feel it as a pressure to divide early.

The raspers would feel it as an urge to seal tight before certain rains.

The shell-knots would open late when poisoned runoff came.

The winged swarms would rise from one vent and not another.

The bellows, whose bodies read the mud more deeply than any small creature, would answer the old pulses with their own drumming, and the whole basin would carry the message through itself.

In time, the basin would possess a kind of dream.

Not consciousness like a face looking outward.

More like sleep in a body that knows where its wounds are.

The fifth gift would be refuge.

Not everywhere. Everywhere cannot be refuge and still be world.

But here and there, in places no eye would value, the power would make small sanctuaries of condition.

A hollow that retained water three days longer than nearby stone.

A seam whose chemistry parasites disliked.

A ledge too narrow for large hunters but perfect for egg-cases.

A mud pocket where braid-worms could rejoin after being torn.

A warm updraft that carried exhausted swarmers away from a feeding mat instead of down into it.

A black pool whose salinity shifted slowly enough for transparent larvae to adapt.

These refuges would not save individuals from all harm. Many would still be eaten at their mouths. Predators would learn their edges. Parasites would find routes inward. Scavengers would wait.

But enough would pass through.

Enough.

That would be the measure of the power’s kindness in such a place.

Not peace.

Enough.

Enough young surviving after the cloudy spawn.

Enough grazers reaching the films.

Enough hunters feeding without emptying their grounds.

Enough bellows to churn the pans.

Enough dead to nourish the living.

Enough living to become the dead.

The sixth gift would be protection from what does not belong.

If someday a brighter intelligence came down into the basin with instruments, lamps, drills, hunger for minerals, hunger for knowledge, hunger for dominion, the power would turn its attention like a closed hand.

Machines would fail gently at the margins.

Maps would contradict themselves.

Samples would decay before they could be made profitable.

The richest seams would appear poor.

The safest routes would seem impassable.

Those who came only to take would find the basin confusing, unrewarding, hostile in ways they could not quite justify.

But those who came humbly, if any ever did, would not be destroyed.

They would be made afraid first.

Then quiet.

Then, perhaps, capable of awe.

The power would not demand that the basin be loved by outsiders.

It would be enough that it not be ruined by them.

Finally, after all these almost-invisible works, the power would give one blessing to itself.

It would refuse disgust.

It would look at the whole cold civilization of appetite and not flinch.

It would love the dark films patient as geology.

It would love the rasping mouths.

It would love the blind crustacean clouds thickening the pools.

It would love the predators waiting at edges.

It would love the parasites, those intimate thieves, because even they were forms of need shaped by the same old chemistry.

It would love the scavengers who translated flesh.

It would love the great bellows grinding mud through their trench-mouths and rearranging the channels with their weight.

It would love the winged mats hanging from cliff seams like fevered fur.

It would love the ugly, efficient, listening world not despite what it was, but because nothing in it had been made falsely.

And perhaps the basin would feel this.

Not as comfort.

Not as revelation.

But as a slight change in the pressure of existence.

The rain would fall. The channels would fill. Burrows would flood. Bodies would scramble over bodies. Predators would station themselves at the crossings. Larvae would cloud the water. Shells would open. Wings would clatter upward in the vent-breath.

And underneath, in the seams, a tremor would pass from one dark colony to another.

A pulse too slow for fear.

Too deep for hunger.

The basin would not know it was blessed.

It would only continue.

But it would continue more wholly.

The cloud would remain withheld.

The world would remain wet, cold, crowded, and ravenous.

And somewhere inside the endless scraping, clicking, feeding, molting, swarming, dying, hatching dark, there would be a mercy shaped exactly to fit it.
