They would come quietly.

Not as a flame in the roof, not as a voice rolling through the stone, not as a hand large enough to lift the basin from its cold. A wise power would understand at once that this place is not unfinished because it is wet, dark, sharp-edged, and full of hunger. It is not asking to be corrected. It is alive in the exact language of its conditions.

So first, they would listen.

They would listen long enough to hear that the dripping is not background but speech: thaw, seep, warning, route, season, fracture, return. They would learn the slow grammar of moss and the quick grammar of shrews. They would feel the roots holding the roof and the roof failing anyway, by degrees, as roofs must. They would understand that the basin is young, and that young places must be allowed to be unstable, because instability is how they discover their shape.

They would not make it warm.

They would not open the roof into a garden of sun. They would not banish the owl, or spare every vole, or turn every pool clear, or keep every nest from flooding. They would know that mercy is not the same as softness everywhere. A place like this lives by edges: wet and dry, light and shade, hunger and concealment, collapse and colonization. To remove the edge would be to remove the city.

But they would place a kindness around it.

Not a wall. A wall would trap the water, stop the foxes, confuse the bats, make a museum of a breathing thing.

A kindness more like luck.

Above, where the surface trees stand in wind and snow, their roots would be strengthened just enough. Not so much that stone could never fall, but enough that the great roof would not fail all at once. Collapse would continue in small chapters instead of one catastrophic sentence.

Rain would still enter. Snowmelt would still rise black and cold through the lower streets. But poison would lose its way. Oil from distant machines, salt from winter roads, bitter metals loosened by careless digging—these would be turned aside, caught in clay, bound in reed beds, delayed until they could not arrive as murder. The power would not make the basin pure, because purity is a dead idea. It would make it able to cleanse what the careless world above tried to pour into it.

Where a crack admitted light, the crack would be allowed to remain.

Where a crack threatened to become a killing wound, it would be slowed.

Where a watercourse had no exit and would drown an entire district in a thaw, one pebble would shift, one root would loosen, one narrow black thread would open between stones. The flood would still come. Eggs would still be lost. Burrows would still fill. But not all. Never all.

The wise power would bless proportion.

It would give the moss no empire beyond its patience.

It would give the fungi endings enough to eat, but not so much ending that decay outran birth.

It would let the otter-like hunters take fish, but would leave fish their side cracks and shadowed hollows.

It would let the fox descend hungry, and let the vole hear one droplet out of rhythm in time to freeze.

It would let the owl be silent.

It would let the shrew burn with its terrible little life.

And because wisdom is not only restraint, it would make some small gifts.

In the deepest winter, when ice seals the upper mouths and the basin holds its breath, one hidden seam of stone would keep weeping. A thread of liquid water, no wider than a root hair, would continue through the blackness. Enough for beetle, moss, frog, vole, fungus. Enough to say: not yet.

In spring, a seed from above would fall through a skylight and land where no seed had landed before, in a cup of silt held by three roots. Most such seeds die. This one would live. Not because it is chosen to rule, but because one new kind of leaf can change a district without conquering it. Its descendants would make a pale green arch over a narrow canal, and birds would use it for generations.

A fallen slab, sharp and dangerous, would settle one finger’s breadth differently than it might have. Beneath it, a dry triangular chamber would remain. In the first year, nothing would find it. In the second, a fox would shelter there during a storm. In the third, bats would use the high seam. In the fifth, fern roots would lace its lip. In the tenth, it would be part of the map.

The power would remember futures too small for prophecy.

It would guard not individuals only, but continuities: a hidden run of voles beneath moss; a breeding pool for amphibians; the caddis larvae in their grit houses; the lichens writing their slow maps; the old root that is dead in its center but alive in its bark and therefore useful to almost everyone.

And if humans came—

because someday humans would come, following curiosity, hunger, survey lines, legends, profit, or grief—

the power would not strike them blind.

It would do something subtler.

From above, the basin would seem difficult to notice unless one arrived with reverence. Maps would blur around it. Machines would stall in the wrong weather. The easiest path would always appear to lead elsewhere. Those who came to take would find mud, inconvenience, unstable ground, no signal, bad dreams, a sudden memory of something urgent at home.

But those who came softly—one child, perhaps, or an old naturalist, or someone lost and careful enough to apologize to moss—might be allowed to see.

They would not see all of it. No one should.

They would see a cold light falling through roots. They would hear the distributed dripping. They would place one hand on wet stone and understand, without words, that the world is making cities without them.

They would leave changed.

Perhaps they would speak for the basin later, when a road was proposed, or a quarry, or a drainage project drawn by people who had never knelt in mud. Perhaps they would fail. But the power would place in them a stubbornness like a root in a crack.

Most of all, the benevolent power would give the place privacy.

Not abandonment. Privacy.

The privacy to rot and renew.  
The privacy to be cold.  
The privacy to be eaten and to feed.  
The privacy to continue becoming itself without being named too loudly.

And perhaps, after long listening, the power would recognize that the basin itself is a kind of prayer already.

Not a prayer made of asking.

A prayer made of occupation.

Every moss stem saying: here.  
Every root saying: through.  
Every fish saying: current.  
Every bat saying: dusk.  
Every fungus saying: return.  
Every drop saying: again.  

So the wise power would answer in the same language.

Not with thunder.

With one more drop, falling exactly where it is needed.
