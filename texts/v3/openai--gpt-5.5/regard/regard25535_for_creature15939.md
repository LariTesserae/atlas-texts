A benevolent and wise power would not begin by quieting the hollow.

It would understand, before pity had time to become interference, that the roar is not suffering by itself. The heat is not cruelty. The poison is not evil. The crowding, the hunger, the sudden deaths, the narrowness of every livable inch—these are not mistakes in need of correction. They are the grammar of that place.

So it would not turn the vent-field into a garden.

It would not cool the chimneys into safety, because safety would starve the worms.

It would not sweeten the sulfides, because the microbes require the harsh breath exactly as it is.

It would not widen the hollow into comfort, because the smallness is part of the city’s shape.

It would not give eyes to the blind fish, or softness to the iron snails, or mercy to the crabs beyond the mercy already present in precision.

It would first listen.

Not with ears, because ears are too impatient for such a place, but with the kind of attention that can hear chemistry thinking. It would listen to the vents pulse and the basalt settle. It would feel the slow arithmetic of heat against cold, poison against oxygen, hunger against bloom. It would learn the laws that every resident already obeyed with its body.

It would discover that this was not a tragedy.

It was a bargain.

A severe one, yes. A bargain written in scalding water and black stone. But life had signed it willingly, in the only way life signs anything: by arriving, attaching, dividing, feeding, brooding, dying, and returning as other bodies.

The power would love the place enough not to misunderstand it.

But love would not mean doing nothing.

There are violences proper to the hollow, and violences foreign to it.

The collapse of a chimney is proper.

A plume shifting and killing a ring of worms is proper.

A crab taking a stunned shrimp is proper.

A vent sealing and a neighborhood thinning into death is proper.

These belong to the mountain’s own speech.

But other things do not belong.

A mining machine lowering its teeth toward the young ridge.

A net dragged over the brittle chimneys.

A drill testing the sulfide towers.

A plume of human poison settling over creatures already balanced on poison’s edge.

A careless hunger from above, unable to tell a dead stone from a living city.

Against these, the wise power would act.

Not with thunder, perhaps. Thunder is mostly wasted underwater.

It might bend a current.

It might lay a confusion over instruments, so that maps disagreed with themselves and coordinates became subtly untrustworthy. A survey vessel would pass over the summit and find only unpromising readings. A company would mark the ridge as unstable, uneconomical, difficult. A funding body would turn its appetite elsewhere.

It might roughen the weather above at just the right seasons.

Not enough to wreck ships. Benevolence need not be dramatic. Only enough to delay. Enough for permits to expire, for interest to cool, for another site to seem easier.

It might trouble the dreams of those who planned extraction. Not with nightmares, but with clarity: the sudden image of pale crabs standing in mineral snow; of red plumes trembling like exposed hearts; of a city no larger than a chapel, unknown and complete. Some would dismiss the feeling. A few would not. A few would speak.

It might guide one human witness there at the right time.

Not a conqueror.

Not an owner.

Someone patient. Someone capable of looking without immediately reaching.

A submersible would descend through the blue column, lights dimmed out of respect for the strange natural brightness already there. Cameras would record the black chimneys and the white worms and the glass shrimp turning in the plume. The witness would understand, perhaps only later, that they had not discovered a resource but met a congregation.

A name would be given, because humans protect more readily what they can call.

Then perhaps a boundary.

A sanctuary drawn in law over a place no law had made.

A circle of restraint around the drowned summit.

The power would approve of this, though it would know law is a fragile mineral.

So it would keep watch.

Not constantly in the anxious way of lesser guardians, but faithfully, as deep things keep faith.

When a chimney grew too fast and leaned toward a dense bed of young tubeworms, the power might do nothing. Let it fall. Let the crabs eat. Let the microbes inherit. That is the hollow.

But when a fracture opened beneath the bowl with enough violence to erase the whole city before its larvae had dispersed, the power might place one cool finger of intention into the basalt.

Not to stop the mountain.

Only to ask it to open elsewhere.

The crack would wander by a handspan, then a meter, then ten. Pressure would release along a neighboring seam. A new black throat would form on the outer slope. The original hollow would shudder, lose a few chimneys, gain a new curtain of brine, and continue.

No animal would know it had been spared.

That would be best.

Gratitude is not required for goodness to be real.

The power would also think beyond the chapel-bowl.

It would know that every vent-city is temporary. To preserve this one forever would be to make it false. The hollow must one day cool, clog, collapse, or be abandoned by the chemistry that made it possible. Wisdom would not embalm it.

Instead, the power would prepare continuity.

Far along the ridge, where the mountain still held unopened heat, it would encourage a seam to remain permeable. A future vent-field would be born there—not yet, not too soon, but in time. It would give no creature immortality, but it would give the community a next address.

When larvae rose from the old hollow into the cold blue, most would still die. That is the sea’s vast arithmetic.

But a few more than chance allowed would meet a favorable current.

A thread of water, nearly nothing, would take them along the ridge rather than out into emptiness. Chemical traces from the new fracture would travel farther than they should. The larvae would turn. They would descend. Microbes would already be silvering the fresh basalt.

A bare wound would become a neighborhood.

A neighborhood would become a province.

The congregation would continue, not as the same bodies, but as the same answer.

The power would bless the microbes first, because they are the first translators.

It would not make them noble. They have no need of nobility. It would simply leave them abundance where abundance was fitting: sulfide enough, iron enough, methane enough, surfaces rough enough to hold them. The invisible pasture would thicken. The grazers would come.

It would bless the tubeworms with balance.

Not ease.

Balance.

Enough poison to feed their hidden citizens. Enough oxygen not to suffocate them. Enough current to carry both. Their red plumes would tremble in the mingled water, not in joy exactly, but in rightness.

It would bless the shrimp with warning.

A fraction more sensitivity to the vent’s cough.

A heartbeat’s earlier turn from scalding.

Not enough to make them safe. Safe shrimp would abandon the lesson of the place. But enough that some who would have died stupidly might live to molt, feed, and scatter eggs into the blue.

It would bless the crabs with work.

There is no kindness in making a scavenger ashamed of hunger. Let them clean. Let them take the unlucky and the weak. Let no flesh go unused. Their pale claws would continue their exact housekeeping among shells, films, eggs, and fallen plumes.

It would bless the iron snails with adhesion.

When the current struck hard and mineral snow thickened, their dark shells would hold against the stone. Each would remain on its narrow blade between cooking and starvation, carrying its law on its back.

It would bless the eels with darkness inside darkness.

A good crack. A cool refuge. The vibration of prey arriving just clearly enough.

It would bless the strange transparent hunters by allowing mystery to remain mystery. Not every body must become a lesson. Some forms are prayers the world says without translation.

And for the hollow itself, the power would give no crown, no altar, no visible sign.

Only continuance without captivity.

Change without needless ruin.

Obscurity without abandonment.

It would permit the mountain to be young.

It would permit the city to be temporary.

It would permit hunger, because hunger is the engine by which the vent’s breath becomes motion.

It would permit death, because death is one of the ways the hollow keeps itself crowded.

But it would stand against waste.

Waste is different from death.

Death feeds.

Waste merely ends.

So if a stone must fall, let it become platform.

If a shell must empty, let it become shelter.

If a vent must die, let its last warmth feed a fading film.

If the whole bowl must one day go cold, let it send its children outward first.

The wise power would not make the chapel holy.

It would recognize that it already is—not holy in the clean, gentle way imagined by air-breathing minds, but holy as a furnace is holy to metal, as a wound is holy to blood, as a mouth is holy to hunger.

A small, loud, cold-water city at the top of a drowned mountain.

A congregation attending the planet’s breath.

The power would watch over it by allowing it to remain itself.

And in the broad blue column above, where light fell too clearly through a thousand meters of sea, nothing would appear changed.

The chimneys would smoke.

The worms would tremble.

The shrimp would vanish and return.

The crabs would pick among the living and the dead.

The snails would hold to their impossible borders.

The microbes would eat poison and make it into flesh.

The mountain would keep speaking.

And somewhere, just beyond the reach of sight, a benevolence vast enough to be mistaken for chance would keep the worst hungers of the world from finding the hollow too soon.
