The power would come without arriving.

It would not descend in fire over the plateau, because fire already has its seasons and its hungers. It would not stand in the sky with a face, because the small birds would freeze beneath that gaze, and the lizards would vanish, and the beetles would become pebbles from fear. It would not make a voice in the air, because this country has spent ages learning quiet, and a loud mercy can be a kind of violence.

It would notice first.

Not glance. Not survey. Notice.

It would attend to the plateau at the scale by which the plateau is alive: not by mountain, valley, river, forest, but by fissure, droplet, egg, wing case, salt crust, thorn shadow, termite wall. It would learn the old roads worn by thirst. It would know which basins hold water one dawn longer than the others. It would feel the difference between a sleeping seed and a dead one. It would learn the names that no mouth speaks: the scent-name of an ant colony, the heat-name of a lizard’s chosen stone, the pressure-name of roots entering rock.

And because it was wise, it would not pity the place for being hard.

Pity would misunderstand it. This land is not a failed garden. It is not a paradise deprived. It is itself: an ancient patience crowded with exact lives. To pour rivers over it would be to drown its genius. To raise forests would be to erase the low civilizations that have taken ages to become fluent in scarcity. To make all creatures safe would be to unmake the quickness by which they know themselves. To abolish death would be to clog every hollow with bodies and starve the beetles of their sacrament.

So the power would do very little.

But the little would be precise.

It would bend nothing dramatically. It would place no miracle where a habit would serve. It would take the plateau’s own laws in its hands as one might take a moth: not to crush, not to possess, but to keep it from the lamp.

It would begin with water.

Not more water in the foolish sense. Not abundance. Abundance would rot the roots, soften the towers, summon larger hungers, invite green things that grow too tall and shade out the lichens, bring diseases from lower lands, make the patient-bodied creatures strangers in their own country.

Instead it would adjust the faithfulness of small water.

A night mist, one hour longer, over the highest slabs.

A rain curtain tilted half a valley farther than it otherwise would have gone.

A basin that used to fail after two days holding until the third morning, just often enough for one lineage of pool-born things to finish its frantic business.

A crack shaded by a loosened stone, so dew gathers there after the others have dried.

A thread of seepage deep beneath the termite chambers, not enough to flood them, only enough that in the worst drought the queens do not all die in the same year.

It would not save every pool. It would not answer every egg. Some mud would remain unopened. Some generations would wait and wait and be waited out. That too is part of the plateau’s truth. But it would prevent the old rhythm from breaking entirely.

Where the climate had begun to grow erratic beyond the plateau’s ancient range—where rains missed too many years in succession, where heat arrived at the wrong depth and cooked eggs in their sealed sleep—the power would put a thumb, lightly, on the scale.

Not to make life easy.

To keep difficulty from becoming mere extinction.

It would speak next to stone.

Not in words, but by patience matching patience.

It would settle certain slabs more firmly over the mammal dens before the season of hunting birds. It would loosen other slabs just enough that beetles could enter and make chambers where there had been none. It would guide one century’s hairline fracture toward another, until a new root-path opened in the rock. It would let a rim wear down here, let dust collect there, let a basin deepen by the width of a beetle’s body across fifty years.

No creature would know this as intervention. The plateau would simply seem, to itself, to have remembered another possibility.

The termite towers would stand a little longer before collapse.

The old ant roads would not be buried all at once by a season’s strange dust.

The lichens on the most exposed backs of stone would receive, once in a while, a morning of cloud at the exact time when cloud meant survival.

The power would not prevent predation.

It would watch the snake take the lizard. It would watch the owl take the mouse. It would watch the bird stab tadpoles from the shrinking pool. It would watch the ants disassemble the nestling that fell too early from the thorn shrub.

And it would grieve, but not interfere.

For it would understand that every hunger here is tied by invisible threads to ten thousand continuances. The snake’s body is not an argument against the lizard’s life. The owl is not a cruelty imposed upon the mouse. The beetles are not an indignity to the dead. They are the plateau returning what cannot remain whole.

But where suffering became waste—where a creature was trapped not by the sharp law of life but by accident emptied of meaning—the power might act.

A fledgling fallen into a crevice too smooth to climb, with no predator near and no purpose in the long terror: a small crumble of grit would form a foothold.

A gravid lizard pinned beneath a shifted flake after rain: the stone would settle again by a finger’s breadth.

A pool poisoned by some rare mineral bloom before any of its sleepers could seed the mud: a wind would bring dust from the termite fields, binding the poison, clouding the water, saving not all but enough.

A colony of ants cut off from its old trail by a fresh collapse: a scent-carrying draft would pass through a new fissure, and one worker, pausing, would turn her head.

These would not be rescues in the grand manner. They would be corrections against meaninglessness. Mercy, not indulgence.

Then the power would consider the rare large animals, the long-legged browsers who cross at dawn by the ancient lanes.

It would not multiply them. Their rarity is part of their dignity. Large bodies cannot be common on this old back of stone. But it would protect the memory of their roads.

It would keep one saddle open where shrubs might otherwise close too densely after a wet decade. It would salt one exposed seam faintly enough that the path remained worth remembering. It would allow a line of shade stones, irregular and accidental-looking, between two basins, so the young could follow the elders without dropping from heat.

Not every calf would live. Not every herd would return. But the lane itself would continue, polished by hooves and soft feet and bellies of snakes crossing after rain. A road is also a creature if enough lives depend on its remembering.

The power would turn also toward the small birds.

It would admire their economy of sound, their little broken languages that do not presume the world is theirs to fill. It would not teach them song. It would not color them like jewels. Their drabness is a covenant with survival.

But in one thorn knot, where a nest would otherwise be exposed to the noon eye of a raptor, a new twig would grow inward.

In another place, a termite tower abandoned at the right season would crack open into a chamber exactly deep enough for owls.

A moth bloom would rise one evening when nestlings’ mouths were many.

The birds would call, click, chip, vanish, return. They would know nothing except that sometimes the plateau provides, and sometimes it does not, and both truths must be met with quick feet.

The power would love the beetles especially.

Not more than the others, but with a certain fondness. Beetles are close to the mind of such a place: armored, various, solemn, absurd, jewel-hearted under dust. The power would watch them polish the dead, scrape maps from lichen, row through temporary water, fold their legs and become stones. It would bless their multiplicity without making them aware of being blessed.

A few new chambers under slabs.

A few fungal threads preserved through drought.

A few larvae reaching maturity before the pool skin breaks.

A few winged adults finding the next dark mirror by starlight.

Enough.

Always enough, if enough can be kept from becoming too much.

It would also guard the sleepers.

This, perhaps, would be its deepest work.

The plateau is full of lives that are not presently lives in the visible sense: cysts, spores, seeds, eggs, sealed larvae, dry moss, dormant queens, embryos waiting in mud. A less wise power might value only motion, breath, cry, hunger. This one would recognize latency as a sacred condition.

It would pass through the dust like a dream of rain and know each waiting thing.

Not wake yet.

Not you.

Not this year.

You, when the basin fills twice.

You, after fire.

You, when the old shrub falls and opens light.

You, when a bird breaks the crust with its foot.

It would not wake them all. That would be ruin. Dormancy is not deprivation; it is timing held in the body. But it would keep the library from burning unread.

There are catastrophes even ancient places cannot absorb when they come too often: a decade with no true rain, a new sickness carried by wanderers from below, a sky changed by distant hands, a heat no egg has memory for. Against these, the power would make refuges so humble they would not be noticed.

One basin shaded by a tilted boulder.

One underground seam holding coolness.

One termite lineage more tolerant of fever.

One moss patch able to fold tighter.

One beetle whose eggs wait longer.

One shrub whose seeds open only after the second storm, not the first.

One bird that learns a farther route and teaches it by leaving.

One ant colony that moves deeper and carries with it the fungus, the larvae, the stored seeds, the smell-map of survival.

It would not freeze the plateau in place. Wisdom knows that preservation is not embalming. The plateau has never been still. Its apparent stillness is composed of innumerable negotiations. So the power would not command, “Remain as you are.”

It would whisper instead, in the language of pressures and chances:

“Continue becoming yourself.”

And perhaps, after long observation, the power would make one visible thing.

Only one.

Not a temple. Not a monument. Not a tree out of scale with the country. Nothing that would announce a benefactor and distort the lives around it.

It would make a stone bowl.

Or rather, it would persuade erosion to reveal one.

High on an unremarkable part of the upland, where several old animal lanes nearly meet but not quite, a shallow hollow would form over centuries. At first it would be no more than a darker patch after rain. Then a depression. Then a basin the width of two outstretched arms, with a lip low enough for beetles, rough enough for lizards, sloped enough that a trapped mouse could climb out, deep enough that water held through three nights in ordinary rain.

Around its edge, lichens would grow in rings of orange, black, and pale green. A thorn shrub would root nearby but not overtake it. Termites would not build too close. Ants would find it. Birds would remember it. Large browsers would pause there at dawn. Pool-born eggs would arrive stuck to feet, feathers, mud, gut, wind.

It would become a small parliament of thirst.

Not safe. Never safe. Snakes would come. Raptors would watch. Beetle larvae would devour tadpoles. Ants would harvest the drowned. But because of the bowl, more lives would intersect, and because they intersected, more futures would be exchanged.

The power would not sign the bowl. It would leave no mark saying mercy was here.

The mark would be the continued existence of those who drank.

And if the plateau itself could dream—if ancient stone, after so much sun and mist and the chemical chewing of weather, possessed something like sleep—it might dream one night of a presence vast enough to alter everything and gentle enough not to.

In the dream, the presence would kneel.

It would put its ear to the ground.

It would hear roots moving through cracks with the slowness of thought. It would hear ants carrying dew underground. It would hear beetle jaws engraving lichen. It would hear the dry thunder of seeds waiting. It would hear stone cooling under stars. It would hear the unborn tapping softly against the inside of drought.

And the presence would say, without sound:

“I will not make you other than you are.

“I will not spend you for beauty.

“I will not simplify your hunger into peace.

“I will keep faith with your difficulty.

“I will make room for your continuance.

“When the rain should come, let it find you.

“When the dry years come, let some of you sleep through them.

“When death comes, let it feed.

“When life comes, let it not be alone.

“May your hollows remember water.

“May your roads remember feet.

“May your eggs remember rain.

“May your stones remember shade.

“May your smallness never be mistaken for insignificance.”

Then morning would come.

Condensation would gather on the cool stone. The wiry shrubs would bead with light. A lizard would open one eye. Ants would begin making decisions. A beetle, black and polished as a drop of night, would push from beneath a slab and cross the ancient road toward a basin not yet dry.

Nothing would look changed.

And that would be the mercy of it.
