A benevolent and wise power would not arrive in Vorthax with thunder.

Thunder would be too much like conquest.

It would arrive quietly, at the edge of the Weeping Flats, where the black glass meets the mirage-horizon and the sky hangs open like a bruise that has forgotten how to heal. It would not shine at first. It would not impose color, warmth, birdsong, green life, or any of the familiar symbols of rescue. Those things would be violence here, however kindly meant.

Instead, it would listen.

For a long time, it would listen.

It would listen to the hum beneath the glass, to the mercury veins pulsing in their sluggish sleep, to the chorus of voices that almost became language and then broke apart again. It would listen to the pools that climbed upward when gravity turned its face away. It would listen to the distant consuming things, to their hunger for memory or possibility or the unnamed residue of choices never made.

Most of all, it would listen to the Watchers.

The Glass-Eyed Watchers would notice it at once.

Their lenses would turn toward the power, reflecting not its shape, but the movement of its intention. They would slide over the fractured plains in silence, gathering around it in a circle of black glass bodies and mercury limbs, their heads full of churning light.

They would record its arrival.

A mercy entering a wound.

A pause in the ruin.

A presence that did not recoil.

The power would let itself be recorded.

It would stand still beneath the bruised purple sky while every angle of its compassion was captured in their bulbous, gleaming eyes. It would not hide its sorrow from them. It would not hide its patience. It would not hide its strength.

The Watchers would unfold.

One by one, with the sound of shattering crystal, their ribs would open like dark flowers. In the hollows where their hearts should have been, fragments of time would tremble: breaths never drawn, hands never held, doors never opened, names never spoken, futures eaten before they could ripen.

The power would understand then.

The Watchers were not merely preserving Vorthax.

They were feeding the wound with fragments, trying to keep it from widening. Pressing stolen instants into the glass like bandages. Offering almost-real things to a place that could no longer generate its own continuity.

They were archivists, yes.

But also sacrifices.

Not alive enough to rest.

Not dead enough to be released.

So the power would kneel.

Its first act would be neither sealing nor cleansing.

Its first act would be recognition.

It would place one luminous hand upon the black glass and speak—not in words, but in a grammar older than separation, a language known to seeds beneath winter, stars before ignition, bones before birth.

It would say:

*I see the wound.*

It would say:

*I see those who have tended it.*

It would say:

*No one should have had to remember alone.*

The Flats would convulse.

The hum would rise until it became unbearable. The mirage-towers would twist into impossible crowns. The mercury veins would surge upward, flooding the cracks, reflecting the bruised sky in a thousand trembling streams.

The distant creatures would stop consuming.

For the first time in epochs, the Weeping Flats would hesitate.

The power would not force the wound shut. A wise healer knows that some wounds close only after they have been cleaned, and some must speak their infection before they can scar.

So it would ask Vorthax to show the beginning.

The land would resist.

The sky would tear wider in green phosphorescent lines. The glass would buckle. The Watchers would scream their soundless ruptures into the air, not in warning, but in terror, because memory is dangerous to those made only to preserve fragments of it.

Still, the power would remain.

Gently, it would draw from the Flats not the fragments, but the pattern beneath them.

The first fracture.

The first impossible geometry.

The first moment when reality had been bent too far and did not spring back.

Perhaps there had been a machine once, vast and arrogant, built to pierce worlds and harvest their laws.

Perhaps there had been a city whose scholars tried to abolish grief by trapping every lost moment in glass.

Perhaps there had been a war fought not with weapons, but with timelines, each side erasing the other’s possible children.

Perhaps the truth was stranger than all of these.

Whatever the beginning, the power would look upon it without disgust.

That would matter.

Vorthax had been feared, studied, avoided, exploited, named a blight, named a threshold, named a resource, named a curse.

But it had not been mourned.

So the power would mourn it.

And because the mourning of a benevolent and wise power is not passive, the mourning would become work.

It would gather the floating motes—the spores, ash, fragments of broken time—and sort them from one another with infinite care.

Ash to ash.

Spores to sleep.

Time to its own river.

It would touch the mercury pools and teach them weight again, not as punishment, but as relief. The waters would tremble, then settle into the hollows. Some would still reflect impossible things, but they would no longer crawl upward in panic.

It would lay its palm against the blackened glass and soften it—not into soil, not yet, but into something less sharp. The fractures would remain visible, silvered at their edges, like scars filled with moonlight.

The consuming creatures in the distance would approach.

Their segmented limbs would clatter softly. Their furnace eyes would dim and flare. Rusted gears would turn inside their bodies as if trying to remember whether they had once been organs.

The power would not destroy them.

It would see their hunger for what it was: a function without a purpose, an appetite left running after the world that made it had collapsed.

So it would feed them once.

Not with memory.

Not with possibility.

But with completion.

Each creature would receive the ending it had been denied. A final command fulfilled. A last breath. A shutting door. A hand laid gently over a cooling engine.

Some would fall apart at once, sighing steam and dust.

Some would curl into themselves and become dark stones.

Some, freed from consumption, would wander into the mirages and vanish into whatever kinder dimension would accept them.

Then the power would turn to the Watchers.

This would be the hardest mercy.

For the Watchers had no language for wanting. No language for pain. No language for release. They only recorded. They only preserved. They only pressed fragments into the wound and began again.

The power would not decide for them.

Instead, it would give them a question.

It would place the question gently inside their hollow skulls, among the swirling fluid and trapped whispers.

Not a command.

Not a new function.

A question.

*What would you do, if you did not have to remember for the world?*

At first, nothing would happen.

The Watchers would stand motionless on the glass plains, their many-jointed limbs half-retracted, their lenses wide.

Then one would unfold.

Slowly, uncertainly, it would open its ribs and reach into the hollow cavity where its heart should be. But this time it would not draw out a fragment of time.

It would draw out a sound.

Small.

Raw.

Barely real.

A laugh that had never been permitted to happen.

The Watcher would hold it in its glass fingers, trembling.

Another would produce a breath.

Another, a footstep.

Another, the shadow of a face.

Another, a single word from a language that had shifted too quickly to be understood.

The power would make a vessel for these things.

Not a prison.

Not an archive.

A sanctuary.

At the center of the Weeping Flats, where the largest fracture split the plain from horizon to horizon, the power would raise a structure from softened glass and quiet light. It would not resemble the impossible mirage-towers. It would stand upright. It would obey its own geometry. It would have doors that opened only when approached with reverence.

Inside, every almost-real thing would be given a place.

Not fixed.

Not exploited.

Allowed.

The scream that never left a throat would become a bell tone, rung only in remembrance.

The footstep that never fell would become a path across the sanctuary floor.

The breath never exhaled would become wind.

The names never spoken would be written in condensation upon the glass each dawn, if dawn could now be said to exist there.

And the Watchers would be invited—not ordered—to lay down what they carried.

Some would.

Those Watchers would dim from within. Their glass bodies would lose their terrible tension. The mercury in their limbs would slow. They would fold themselves upon the softened ground and become still, not frozen, not broken, but resting.

The power would give them dreams.

Not memories.

Dreams.

Their first private things.

Others would not lay their burdens down. Not yet.

The power would allow this too.

Wisdom does not force healing into the shape it prefers.

For those who chose to continue, the power would change their task. No longer preservers of a dying world, they would become witnesses of a healing one. Their eyes would still record, but they would no longer hoard raw instances in hollow skulls. What they saw would pass through them into the sanctuary, joining the living record of Vorthax.

And because even witnesses deserve companionship, the power would give them voices.

Not mouths.

Not speech like humans use.

Something truer to their nature.

When they tilted their heads back, they would no longer scream silence into the air. Instead, their lenses would ring.

Clear tones.

Glass music.

Each Watcher would have one note at first. Then two. Then chords.

Over time, if time could now be trusted, the Flats would learn the difference between hum and song.

Only after all this would the power begin to stitch the wound.

It would not close the boundary between worlds completely.

That, too, would be violence. Vorthax was liminal now. Its nature had changed. To make it ordinary would be to erase what it had endured.

Instead, the power would suture the tear with thresholds.

Places where passage was possible, but not accidental.

Places where realities could touch without devouring one another.

It would braid the phosphorescent green tears in the sky into slow auroras. It would anchor the impossible geometries so they no longer lured the eye into madness, but appeared only as distant suggestions: reminders that existence is larger than any one law.

The oppressive hum would lower.

Lower.

Lower still.

Until it became something like a pulse.

The land itself, remembering rhythm.

The dampness in the air would gather at last.

For the first time in ages, rain would fall on Vorthax.

Not clean rain. Not sweet rain. Not the rain of forests or fields.

A strange rain.

Silver and black and faintly luminous, falling upward in some places and downward in others, hissing softly when it struck the glass. It would bead on the bodies of the Watchers. It would fill the old hollows. It would wash the ash from the air.

Where it touched the softened cracks, small things would begin.

Not grass.

Not flowers.

Not yet.

Tiny crystalline growths, translucent and dark, shaped like listening ears. They would tremble when the Watchers passed. They would drink the rain. They would hold light without reflecting pain.

The power would smile then, perhaps.

Not because the work was finished.

Because the work had begun.

Before departing, it would leave three gifts.

The first gift would be a boundary.

No traveler, god, machine, empire, or hunger would enter Vorthax without being known by the land. The Flats had been violated once by forces too arrogant to ask permission. Never again. The boundary would not keep all away, but it would require intention to become visible. Those who came to exploit would find only endless horizon and their own reflection. Those who came to learn would find a path. Those who came to harm would find the glass beneath them remembering every wound.

The second gift would be a well.

At the heart of the sanctuary, the power would make a basin of clear darkness. Into it the Watchers could pour what was too heavy to carry. From it the land could drink when its fractures ached. Any being who looked into the well would see not the future, not the past, but the one grief they had refused to name. If they named it honestly, the well would take a little of its poison away.

The third gift would be a choice.

This gift would be placed inside every Watcher, even those sleeping, even those still sliding across the Flats in tireless devotion.

A simple choice, renewable at every dawn-that-was-not-quite-dawn:

*Continue.*

*Rest.*

*Change.*

No being made for a purpose should be denied the possibility of becoming more than that purpose.

Then the benevolent power would step back.

It would not rule Vorthax.

It would not rename it.

It would not declare it healed.

The Weeping Flats would still weep.

But now their weeping would not be endless loss.

It would be part of a cycle.

Rain. Pool. Reflection. Release.

The sky would remain purple, but less bruised than before. The green veins would glow softly, no longer like infection, but like seams of living fire. The mirage-towers would appear at the horizon only at certain hours, bending toward dimensions beyond sight, but they would no longer threaten to swallow the mind.

And the Watchers would move beneath that sky.

Some sliding.

Some standing.

Some sleeping in curled shapes of black glass.

Some gathered near the sanctuary, chiming to one another in delicate tones.

They would still remember for Vorthax.

But not alone.

And sometimes, when the hum of the land grew loud, they would lift their glass eyes toward the rain and sing back.

Not a scream.

Not a rupture.

A record of mercy.

A proof that even a wound in reality may become, with patience, not a doorway to ruin—

but a place where broken worlds learn how to be held.
