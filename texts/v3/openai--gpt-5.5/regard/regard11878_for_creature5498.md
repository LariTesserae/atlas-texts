A benevolent and wise power would come quietly.

It would not descend in fire over the peaks or carve commandments into the basalt. It would not flatten the mountains, warm the air into softness, or make the deer walk willingly into Aelgari spears. It would understand that the harshness of this place is not merely cruelty. It is also teacher, boundary, rhythm, and meaning. The Aelgari are not broken because their lives are difficult. They are shaped, and there is dignity in that shaping.

So the power would first listen.

It would listen to the groan of the young mountains as they settled into themselves. It would listen to the thin streams worrying at stone, to the moss drinking mist, to the deer breaking crusted snow with their hooves. It would listen to the raptors, the rodents, the wind in the stunted trees. It would listen, most carefully, to the Aelgari: to their songs at the hearth, their grief for those lost to storms, their laughter when children tumbled in wool and snow, their whispered thanks over a slain animal, their arguments in council, their silences before the peaks.

And after long listening, it would decide to help only in ways that preserve the soul of the place.

It would not make life easy.

It would make life possible.

### The First Gift: Warning

The young mountains would remain young. Rockfalls would still happen. Storms would still rise suddenly from the far valleys. Ice would still glaze the paths. But the power would place subtle warnings into the world.

Before a dangerous rockfall, the mountain goats would grow restless and descend earlier than usual.

Before a storm, the moss on north-facing stones would darken with a faint silver sheen.

Before a snow bridge weakened, small blue-white flowers would close their petals, even under sunlight.

Before a stream flooded, the water would take on a low humming tone that the elders would learn to recognize.

Nothing would speak in a human voice. Nothing would announce, “Danger comes.” The Aelgari would still need attention, memory, humility. But the land would become a little more legible to those who loved it.

In time, the Aelgari would say:

“The mountain speaks first to those who have been quiet enough to hear.”

### The Second Gift: Water That Remembers Mercy

The power would look upon the small streams and see how precious they were. In such a high, cold, rugged place, water is life’s narrow thread.

So it would touch the hidden springs beneath the andesite and basalt. Not to create rivers where none belonged, but to make the existing waters steadier. In the driest seasons, when the snowmelt thinned and the streams shrank to glassy trickles, a few springs would continue to breathe from the stone.

The Aelgari would discover them gradually.

A child would find one while chasing a bird.

An elder would notice that a certain patch of moss never browned.

A hunter, injured and far from camp, would hear the faintest drip beneath a shelf of rock and survive the night.

These springs would be clear, cold, mineral-rich, and dependable. Not abundant enough to invite waste. Not great enough to draw conquest. Just enough to keep famine from becoming extinction.

Around these places, the Aelgari would build no permanent shrines at first. They would simply leave small offerings: braided grass, carved bone beads, a smooth stone turned upright.

Later, they would call them the Kind Veins of the Mountain.

### The Third Gift: A New Lichen

The power would not give the Aelgari grain fields; fields would not belong here. It would not cover the slopes with fruit trees; the climate would refuse them. Instead, it would encourage what already wished to live.

On shaded basalt faces, a new lichen would begin to spread. Slow-growing, blue-gray, tough as old leather. To most creatures, it would seem ordinary. But when carefully dried, crushed, and boiled with certain bitter roots, it would make a nourishing broth rich in minerals and strength.

The Aelgari would not discover this all at once.

Perhaps during a lean winter, an old gatherer would watch shaggy deer licking the lichen from stone. She would scrape some away, test it cautiously, boil it once, grow sick, try again with ash, try again with root, try again with longer drying. Eventually she would learn the method. She would teach it to her grandchildren. They would teach theirs.

It would never be a feast food. No one would crave it in times of plenty. But in hunger, it would keep children alive.

And because the lichen grew slowly, it would teach restraint. Those who stripped too much from one stone would find none there for many years. Those who harvested in patches, leaving the heart to regrow, would always have enough.

Thus the gift would contain its own lesson.

### The Fourth Gift: Dreams for the Healers

The Aelgari already know plants, wounds, fever, birth, frostbite, and grief. Their healers are observers, experimenters, singers, bone-setters, comforters. The wise power would not replace their knowledge with miracles.

But on certain nights, when mist lay low and the moon lit the peaks like bone, healers would dream more clearly.

A woman troubled by a child’s persistent cough would dream of a creeping plant under snow, its roots red as ember-thread.

A man whose brother’s wound had blackened would dream of boiling water longer, of washing stone blades in steam, of burning soiled wrappings rather than reusing them.

An elder attending a difficult birth would dream of turning, waiting, breathing, and the correct pressure of hands.

A young healer grieving too many winter deaths would dream not of herbs, but of sitting beside the dead without believing himself a failure.

The dreams would never be infallible. They would be suggestions, glimpses, seeds. The Aelgari would still have to test, remember, discuss, and refine. False certainty is dangerous, and a wise power would not poison them with it.

Over generations, their medicine would improve. Infant deaths would lessen. Infections would be better understood. The wounded would have better chances. The old would die with less pain.

The Aelgari would say their healers were taught by the Quiet Ones beneath the stone.

They would be partly right.

### The Fifth Gift: A Place of Return

The rite of passage sends young Aelgari alone into the mountains. It is sacred, dangerous, and necessary to their identity. The power would not abolish it. A people must be allowed to test courage, solitude, and skill.

But the power would choose one hidden valley, small and difficult to find, and make it a place of return.

Not a paradise. Not warm. Not safe from all harm.

But sheltered.

In that valley, winds would soften. A spring would remain unfrozen under a lip of stone. Low shrubs would bear small tart berries late into the season. A cave, dry and shallow, would face away from the worst storms. The entrance would be marked only by a natural arch of black basalt and a single pale tree twisted into the shape of an open hand.

Those truly lost might stumble upon it.

Not always. The power would not remove all consequence from recklessness. But the frightened, the humble, the wounded who still chose life, the child who remembered the old songs and followed the signs — these might find the valley.

Many would return from their rites saying they had survived by skill alone.

Some would speak, years later, by the fire, of a white tree in a hidden place.

The elders would listen and not interrupt.

### The Sixth Gift: Boundaries Against Cruelty

Because the Aelgari are egalitarian and balanced, the power would preserve their freedom rather than rule them. But it would also know that no people are immune to fear, greed, pride, or violence. Harsh lands can breed generosity, but they can also breed desperation.

So the power would not control their minds.

Instead, it would strengthen the consequences of certain choices.

A hunter who killed wastefully would find game scarce for a time.

A family that hoarded during famine would discover their stores spoiled by damp unless they shared.

A person who lied in council for selfish gain would dream, night after night, of standing alone on a peak while every path below vanished in mist.

A leader, should one ever try to rise above the others through domination, would find no omen favorable, no fire easy to light, no animal calm in their presence.

These would not be punishments in the crude sense. They would be corrections, like pain in a hand too close to flame. The power would not demand obedience. It would remind the Aelgari that balance is not poetry; it is survival.

### The Seventh Gift: Memory

Primitive cultures are not lesser cultures. They are young in certain tools, perhaps, but not young in meaning. The Aelgari carry knowledge in song, carving, pattern, route, ritual, and story. Yet memory can be fragile in a place where one avalanche can take three elders.

So the power would inspire a new tradition.

One winter, when storms held several families together in a sheltered valley longer than expected, a child would begin pressing marks into wet clay with a carved bone point. At first the marks would be playful: deer tracks, peaks, spirals for wind. An elder would see them and add a mark for “dangerous crossing,” another for “good water,” another for “do not hunt here in spring.”

From this, slowly, would emerge a symbolic trail language.

Not writing in the full sense, not yet. But a system of memory-marks carved discreetly on stone, antler, and wood. Marks for safe passes, healing plants, unstable slopes, clan histories, births, deaths, vows, warnings, gratitude.

The Aelgari would not become something other than themselves. They would simply gain a stronger vessel for what they already know.

The wise power would smile upon this, because memory is one of the gentlest forms of protection.

### The Eighth Gift: The Stranger Law

Someday, others will come.

Perhaps from lower valleys. Perhaps fleeing drought, war, hunger, or curiosity. Perhaps traders with bright shells and unfamiliar tools. Perhaps raiders. Perhaps lost children of another people.

The power, seeing far along the slopes of time, would plant in the Aelgari a custom before it is needed.

A story would arise among them of the First Stranger: a figure found half-frozen under a ledge, whose face was unknown and whose words were like broken stones. In the story, some wished to leave the stranger to fate. Others brought them to the fire. The stranger survived and later showed the Aelgari a new way to bind a broken spear shaft. Then, in spring, the stranger departed, leaving behind a song.

From this story would come a law:

A stranger found helpless in the mountains is first a life, and only later a question.

They may be guarded. They may be watched. They may not be murdered for being unknown.

This law would save lives. It would also protect the Aelgari from becoming cruel out of fear.

It would not make them naïve. Wisdom is not open doors without discernment. But their first response to vulnerability would be mercy.

### The Ninth Gift: No Empire

The power would be careful not to make the Aelgari too favored.

A people given too much may grow apart from the web that sustains them. If their hunts always succeed, they forget gratitude. If their children never die, they forget the preciousness of birth. If their stores never empty, they forget sharing. If no enemy can harm them, they may become enemies themselves.

So the power would refuse to make them mighty.

It would not give them iron before its time.

It would not show them how to conquer the lower valleys.

It would not make their shamans into kings or their hunters into invincible warriors.

It would let them remain small enough to know one another’s names, vulnerable enough to value cooperation, and mortal enough to sing honestly.

This restraint would be one of the power’s greatest acts of love.

### The Tenth Gift: Presence

Finally, the power would leave a sign of itself, but not one that could be owned.

High above the scattered huts, where the basalt teeth of the mountains cut the sky, there would be a peak that sometimes shone at dawn before the sun touched any other stone. Not every day. Not predictably. But in moments of great need or great gratitude, the uppermost edge would catch a pale gold light from no visible source.

The Aelgari would notice.

They would not all agree on its meaning. Some would say it was a spirit. Some would say it was the mountain’s eye. Some would say it was only ice and sunrise. Some would refuse to speak of it at all.

The power would not mind.

The sign would not exist to compel belief. It would exist to comfort attention.

Those who looked upon it after burying a child might feel, not that grief had been erased, but that grief had been witnessed.

Those who saw it before a hunt might remember to kill cleanly.

Those who saw it during a council might choose patience over anger.

Those who saw it while alone might feel less alone.

And once in a generation, perhaps, someone on their rite of passage would climb too high, beyond wisdom, beyond strength, into a killing cold. They would fall asleep in snow, breath slowing, fingers numb.

They would dream of a figure made of mist, antler, stone, and dawnlight.

The figure would not say, “You cannot die.”

It would say, “Not here. Not yet. Downward.”

And the young one would wake with just enough strength to crawl.

## What the Aelgari Would Become

Over generations, the Aelgari would remain themselves.

Still hardy. Still weathered. Still braided and wool-wrapped. Still laughing around smoky hearths. Still arguing in council. Still hunting deer with reverence. Still losing people to winter, though fewer. Still sending their young into the mountains, though more return. Still reading wind, moss, hoofprint, and cloud.

But their relationship with the land would deepen.

Their animism would become more precise, not less. Their rituals would include the silvering moss, the Kind Veins, the blue-gray hunger lichen, the hidden valley, the First Stranger, the dawnlit peak. Their healers would become careful keepers of dream and experiment. Their elders would become guardians of marks and memory.

They would not say, “A god saved us.”

They would say, “The mountain is generous when honored.”

They would say, “The world answers, but not to shouting.”

They would say, “Balance is a path walked every day.”

And the benevolent power would remain mostly unseen, content with this misunderstanding, because it never wanted worship as much as flourishing.

It would dwell in the interval between disaster and survival.

In the stone that holds.

In the spring that does not freeze.

In the dream that teaches.

In the animal that warns.

In the stranger spared.

In the child returned from the snow.

It would not make the mountains gentle.

It would make sure that within their harshness, wisdom has places to take root.
