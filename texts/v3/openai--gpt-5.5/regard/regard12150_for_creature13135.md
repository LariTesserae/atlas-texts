The power would do very little at first.

Not because the place is unworthy of intervention, nor because the people’s suffering is sacred, nor because the abyss has earned its tribute of falling bodies. A wise thing would understand that this world, impossible and roaring, is not a mistake merely because it is dangerous. It would understand that the people who cling there are not unfinished versions of something else. They are not failed lowlanders. They are not waiting to be rescued into flat fields, quiet houses, and stone hearths.

They are already whole.

So the power would first learn to perceive as they perceive.

It would not listen with ears, because ears are useless there. It would lay its attention along the immense wooden bones of the mountains and feel the tremor-language of the world: the skitter of mandibles through a root-arch, the impact of talons half a league away, the soft repeated pulse of a child’s hands practicing grip-sign against the wall of a hanging pod. It would learn the grammar of vibration. It would learn that a falling body has a different rhythm from a diving predator. It would learn that grief travels through the great wood as a low, branching shudder.

Then, slowly, it would act.

Not with thunder. Thunder would be meaningless here.

Not with a voice. Voice would be cruelty.

It would act in ways the world itself could plausibly have chosen.

First, it would touch the wood.

Not all of it. Not enough to make the hollow mountains safe. Safety, absolute and imposed, would kill the shape of this people’s courage. But where ancient load-bearing roots had begun to rot invisibly from within, where a village had lashed itself unknowingly to a dying span, the power would coax the petrified-living grain to knit tighter. It would draw sap through channels long thought dead. It would harden certain junctions, not into stone, but into something like seasoned heartwood: flexible, enduring, less likely to split beneath a generation’s worth of feet.

The people would notice only gradually.

A foothold that should have crumbled would hold.

A vine-bridge that had screamed under weight for years would grow quiet beneath the hand.

An elder, pressing her cheek to the bark, would feel a new deep pulse under the old lattice and sign to the others:

*The mountain remembers us.*

Second, it would give them water that did not require so much blood.

The suspended ponds would remain. The dangerous journeys to the epiphyte basins would remain, because many lives depend on those basins, and the apex flyers have their thirst as surely as humans do. But in the damp shadowed armpits of the mega-vines, near the hanging villages, new growths would appear over decades: pale, cupped fungi with translucent rims, arranged like hands held open to mist.

They would gather condensation.

Not much at first. A mouthful before dawn. Then bowls. Then enough that infants and the fevered need not be carried across exposed root-faces while predators circled in the hard sunlight.

The people would test the water. They would paint warning marks around the first growths, then curiosity marks, then gratitude marks. Children would be told not to damage the ghost-cups. Lovers would meet beside them in the blue shadow before the thermals rose.

The power would not end thirst.

It would end the wasteful deaths.

Third, it would offer silence — but only in small, chosen places.

To erase the roar from the hollow mountains would be to erase the world’s great engine. The roar is not only violence; it is information, weather, migration, hunger, mating, warning. It is the blood-sound of the place. But the power would understand that even those born deaf can suffer from endless pressure. Bones tire. Skin tires. Dreams tire.

So it would grow chambers inside certain thick root-pillars.

They would not be obvious. A hunter might discover one after following an injured glider into a split in the bark. Inside would be a hollow lined with velvet moss and resin nodules, shaped in such a way that vibration entered but softened, diffused, became bearable. A place where the body could stop bracing against the world.

The first person to find such a chamber would think it a death-place, perhaps, or a predator’s trap. But eventually someone desperate would crawl inside with a screaming infant, and the infant would sleep.

After that, the chambers would become sacred.

Not temples in the lowland sense. No kneeling. No spoken prayers. They would be rest-hollows. Grief-hollows. Birth-hollows. Places where an elder could remove her hands from the wall and, for a little while, not read every tremor of danger.

The people would paint the entrances with spirals of white guano and beetle-blue.

The sign for them would be:

*Here the mountain closes its hand around you.*

Fourth, the power would teach without appearing to teach.

It would not descend as a shining figure. That would make worship too easy and understanding too small.

Instead it would arrange coincidences.

A child playing with spider-silk and glider ribs would find that a certain triangular weave holds tension better than the old square knot. A bone-carver would notice that beetle mandible edges can be set into resin and used as saw-teeth. A gatherer would see, in the branching veins of a leaf, a better pattern for distributing weight across a hanging pod. A mourning mother, painting her dead son’s last flight on the pod wall, would accidentally invent a new long-distance signal for *danger below, do not drop*.

The power would place no knowledge into their minds that had no root in their hands.

It would never say, *Here is civilization.*

It would whisper through accident:

*Here is one more way to live.*

Fifth, it would alter the predators only slightly.

A foolish god would remove the apex flyers and watch the insect swarms multiply until they stripped the epiphytes bare. A sentimental god would blunt every talon and starve the sky. A conqueror would make the air obedient.

This power would do none of that.

It would make the great ribbon-tailed predators a little more wary of the painted human pattern. Not afraid. Not incapable of attack. Only wary, the way a hunting animal learns that certain bright insects taste of poison. Over generations, the humans’ geometric body-paints would become more than language. They would become warning coloration.

The people would notice that certain combinations of yellow, black, and ultraviolet blue made predators veer a wing-length wider.

Artists would become defenders.

Children would learn survival as beauty.

A village’s best painter would be honored alongside its best hunter.

Sixth, the power would answer the dead.

Not by stopping death. Not by catching every falling body and placing it gently back among the living. That would break the covenant of gravity by which this people understands consequence, skill, age, and mourning.

But the abyss would no longer be only disappearance.

Far below, in the unseen steaming depths, where the released dead fell wrapped in leaves, the power would tend a garden.

Bodies would land not on bare ruin, not in meaningless rot, but in a vast, warm, hidden basin where the old minerals of the vanished mountains had collected as pale silt. There, fed by falling dead, molted wings, broken harpoons, lost ropes, and centuries of leaf-wrapped grief, a forest of luminous root-shoots would grow upward.

No living climber would reach it for a very long time.

But sometimes, when the thermals rose strongest at dawn, they would carry up shining spores: tiny gold motes that clung to the damp bark and bloomed into brief star-shaped lichens.

The people would see them after funerals.

Not always. Often enough.

And their sign for death would change.

Once it had meant only:

*Released.*

Now it would mean:

*Released, and received.*

Finally, after many generations, the power might allow itself to be seen.

Not as a human.

Not as a bird.

Not as a tree.

Perhaps as a phenomenon.

Once every few years, when the sun struck the hollow mountains at the precise angle and the thermals rose in several great columns at once, mist would gather in the empty spaces between the root-arches. Light would pass through it. The whole continent-scale lattice would become outlined in radiance, every vanished peak briefly visible as a shape made of glowing air.

The people would stop where they clung.

Hunters would fold their glider-cloaks.

Water-gatherers would hold still with gourds hanging from their backs.

Children would press their palms, feet, knees, and brows against the ancient wood and feel a vibration too deep to be danger.

Across the gulf, bone-mirrors would flash.

Not warnings.

Not commands.

A single shared sign, repeated from village to village, root to root, arch to arch:

*We are held.*

And the benevolent power, if it had a heart, would be glad.

Not because it had saved them from their world.

Because it had helped the world make room for mercy.
