A benevolent and wise power would not begin by making a sun.

That would be the first mercy: restraint.

It would look upon the Trellis—the screaming black lattice, the frost, the blind gliders, the chitin-hounds, the beings with hooked hands and lamps of fat—and it would understand that this place is not a mistake merely because it is terrible.

It is young. It is violent. It is unfinished.

But it is also alive in a way that gentler worlds are not. Every stalk is a birth-cry. Every fracture is geography. Every creature in the dark is a note in the vast harp of the wind.

So the power would not silence the wind.

It would not flatten the thorns.

It would not lay a floor beneath the void.

It would not give the people eyes and ask them to become something else.

Instead, it would listen.

For a long time, it would listen.

It would listen not with ears, because ears would be useless here, but with the patience of roots and stars. It would learn the groan-language of the stalks, the stress-hymns of the bark, the clicking hunger of the gliders, the frantic drums of the chitin-hounds. It would learn the people’s language last and most reverently: the taps against ribs, the piton-strikes through cambium, the shivering palm pressed flat to living wood.

Only when it had learned how not to harm them would it act.

And its first act would be very small.

Somewhere beneath a thorn the size of a mountain range, a lone dweller would wake in their hanging nest because the bark had begun to change.

Not much.

Not enough to frighten them.

Just a warmth.

A slow, steady warmth in the wood near their curled body, pulsing at the edge of perception. Not the dangerous flash-heat of flexing stalks. Not the sudden melt that makes ice slick and sends hunters sliding into the black. This would be gentler. A deep cambium warmth, like the breath of another sleeping body.

The dweller would press a hand to it.

The warmth would answer in rhythm.

Not words at first.

Only:

*Here.*

A pause.

*Here.*

A pause.

*Not alone.*

The dweller would freeze, limbs locking into their old survival geometry. They would think it a predator, or madness, or a new kind of crack in the world.

But the warmth would not pursue. It would not command. It would not demand worship.

It would simply remain.

When the dweller’s fat-lamp guttered in the wind, the warmth would bend the nearest splinter of bark—not visibly, not suddenly, but over hours—until a shallow lip formed around the lamp. A cup of living wood. A windbreak.

The flame would steady.

For the first time in that place, a lamp would burn through an entire storm.

That would be the first miracle.

Not a pillar of fire.

Not a voice from above.

A flame that did not go out.

---

The power would then begin to work through the Trellis itself.

It would not halt the growth. That would be death by kindness. The Trellis must expand; its violence is also its circulation, its weather, its engine. But the power would introduce mercy into the mathematics of rupture.

Around occupied nests, new shoots would begin to hesitate.

Not forever. Not even for years. But long enough.

A stalk that once would have burst without warning through a family’s sleeping hollow would instead throb for three nights beforehand, giving off a low harmonic tremor that every palm could read.

*Move.*

*Move.*

*Move.*

The people would not know who warned them. They would only know that the wood had begun to speak in a new register.

The old ones—if any being in the Trellis could be called old—would press their hands to the bark and drum back in wary gratitude.

The power would answer by doing less than it could.

That would be its wisdom.

It would give warning, not safety.

It would give margin, not mastery.

It would leave the climb sacred.

---

Then it would make places.

Not cities. Not fortresses. Not shining sanctuaries that would shame the old nests by comparison.

Just places.

Here and there across the abyss, where three or four great stalks crossed and twisted near one another, the Trellis would begin to grow differently. Thorns would curve inward instead of outward. Bark-plates would overlap like cupped hands. Cavities would form beneath them: deep, dry hollows shielded from the worst of the wind, large enough for several families to shelter without clinging atop one another in desperation.

The people would find these hollows by accident at first.

A hunter following heat.

A child carried on a parent’s back, tapping alarm at some strange resonance.

A starving wanderer chasing the smell of moss and animal fat.

They would enter with spears raised.

Inside, they would find no trap.

Only a chamber of black wood, warm enough that breath did not instantly crystallize, and walls that carried vibration cleanly. A tap on one side could be felt across the whole hollow. A lamp placed in the center would be protected by natural ribs of bark, its little flame magnified in droplets of thaw-water until the chamber shimmered with orange flecks like a buried constellation.

They would not call these places homes at first.

Home, to them, had always meant “the place that has not yet killed me.”

But over time, another meaning would gather.

A safe hollow would last for a season.

Then two.

Then long enough for children to learn the rhythms of cousins.

Long enough for a hunter to return to the same lamp and find it still burning.

Long enough for grief to become possible.

This would be one of the power’s harder gifts.

A being who lives only in the present can endure almost anything, because the future is not large enough to fear.

But where safety lengthens, memory enters.

And with memory comes mourning.

The power would know this and weep—not as rain, because rain has no place here, but as slow mineral beads forming in the deep bark of the hollows. The people would pry them loose and string them on fiber. They would think them hardened sap. They would wear them against their throats.

The beads would hold warmth.

Small warmth.

Enough to keep a child’s fingers from blackening during a long climb.

Enough to keep an elder alive through one more fog-burial.

---

The power would not destroy the predators.

It would watch the velvet-winged gliders fall through the dark, clicking and shrieking, and it would understand them too. They are not evil. They are hunger with wings. They are children of the same impossible world.

So it would not blunt their teeth or dim their instincts.

But it would alter one thing.

Very slightly.

It would give the gliders a new fear.

Not of the people themselves. That would unbalance everything. The gliders must still hunt; the people must still hide.

Instead, the gliders would learn—through a pressure in the inner ear, through failed dives, through a discomfort older than thought—to avoid the sheltered hollows when lamps burned within them.

A lamp-flame would become, to a glider’s echolocation, something strange. Not prey. Not rival. Not heat. A knot in the world’s answer. A place where sound returned wrong.

The gliders would sheer away.

Not always.

Nothing in the Trellis would become always.

But often enough.

The fat-lamp, already the soul of their civilization, would become its boundary-stone.

Not because it illuminates.

Because it declares.

*Here are people.*

*Pass by.*

---

The chitin-hounds would remain.

The cold would remain.

The falls would remain.

The cannon-cracks of new growth would still split the dark.

The power would allow terror to persist, because courage cannot live in a world emptied of danger. But it would begin to remove the stupid cruelties: the deaths that teach nothing, feed nothing, mean nothing.

A rope that should have snapped beneath a climber’s weight might hold one breath longer.

A piton driven desperately into bark might find, beneath the ice, a pocket of softer cambium.

A fog bank might tear open at the exact moment a lost traveler needs to see the pinprick of another lamp.

A newborn, too weak to cling, might find its fingers closing around a strand of fiber that was not there yesterday.

The people would not have a word for miracle.

They would develop one eventually.

It would be tapped with two fingers against the sternum, then one against the wood.

The meaning would be difficult to translate.

Not “rescue.”

Not “god.”

Closer to:

*The world made room.*

---

The greatest gift would come last, because it would require trust.

The power would teach them to write.

Not with ink.

Not on paper.

The Trellis has no patience for paper.

It would teach them to write in vibration.

In the sheltered hollows, certain bark-plates would grow thin and resonant, layered like the skin of a drum. When struck in patterns, they would hold the memory of the strike. A rhythm tapped into them would sleep in the grain until another hand pressed close and woke it.

At first, the people would use this for warnings.

*Gliders nesting above.*

*Ice-slick on eastern climb.*

*Good heat below.*

*Hounds swarming fresh green.*

Then for names.

Then for deaths.

Then for births.

Then for things no one had ever before had the safety to preserve.

A hunter would press both palms to the resonant bark and record the rhythm of their mate’s breathing.

A parent would tap the pattern of a child’s first climb.

A dying elder would drum every route they had ever known, not as a map, because maps are lies in the Trellis, but as advice:

*When the wood groans like this, do not trust it.*

*When the fog smells of iron, climb upward.*

*When you hear three gliders and then silence, flatten yourself beneath the ice.*

And beneath those practical rhythms, other messages would appear.

*I was afraid.*

*I climbed anyway.*

*The lamp was beautiful.*

*I saw five lights once.*

*I thought the void had swallowed everyone, but then someone answered.*

This would change them.

Slowly.

They would still live in the now, because the Trellis demands presence. But their now would deepen. It would gain roots. A person warming their hands over a fat-lamp would feel, in the bark behind them, the stored tremors of those who came before.

They would learn that survival is not the opposite of tenderness.

They would learn that memory is another kind of rope.

---

Would they worship the power?

Some would.

Some would tap prayers into the hollows before dangerous climbs. Some would leave the first fat of a glider in the warm cracks of the bark. Some would shape clay lamps with new markings: curved hollows, cupped thorns, the sign for *room made*.

Others would not worship. They would distrust the mercy. They would say the Trellis had always been this way, that the old stories of total dark were exaggerations born of hunger and storm-fear. They would say no hidden kindness lives in wood.

The power would not mind.

It would not need belief.

It would prefer gratitude, perhaps, but it would never feed on it.

The lamps would burn for the faithful and the doubtful alike.

---

Once, after many seasons, a child would be born in one of the warm hollows during a wind so fierce that even the gliders clung silent to the lee-sides of thorns.

The child would be small. Too small. Its lungs would flutter weakly in the thin air. Its hooked fingers would not close.

The family would gather close, drumming frantic rhythms over the infant’s ribs, trying to call breath into it by language alone.

The fat-lamp would sputter.

Outside, the Trellis would roar.

The power would feel the child fading.

It could save the child easily.

It could fill the hollow with warm air. It could strengthen the lungs. It could command the blood to carry more oxygen. It could do all of this and more.

But wisdom is not the same as refusal, and restraint is not the same as coldness.

So this time, the power would act plainly.

The bark beneath the hollow would open a seam no wider than a hand. From deep inside the stalk, where friction and sap and strange botanical metabolism made a hidden furnace, warm vapor would rise. It would fill the hollow softly, not enough to scald, not enough to drown, only enough to thicken the air.

The child would gasp.

Once.

Then again.

Then cry—not loudly, for these people have no voices made for wailing, but with a small crackling sound like ice splitting under dawn.

Everyone in the hollow would go still.

The infant’s fingers would close around a parent’s thumb.

The lamp would steady.

No one would speak, because speech is not their way.

But one by one, every person in the hollow would press a hand to the floor.

The power would feel their palms.

It would answer with warmth.

---

In time, the Trellis would become no less vast, no less dark, no less sublime.

But it would become inhabited differently.

Where once there had been isolated sparks separated by weeks of lethal climbing, there would now be chains of lamps. Not many. Never many. But enough that on rare clear nights the abyss would hold constellations.

A traveler would stand beneath a thorn, wrapped in glider-hide, and see far off:

One light.

Then another.

Then another.

Not rescue.

Not ease.

But answer.

The people would develop routes between the hollows, sung not by voice but by piton and palm. They would carry embers from one lamp to another in clay cups nested against their chests. They would trade bone, hide, moss, stories, warnings, names.

They would still fall.

When they did, the others would not pretend the void had not taken them.

They would carve the lost one’s rhythm into resonant bark.

Then they would light a lamp at the edge of the hollow and let it burn until the fat was gone.

Far away, other lamps would answer.

Not because light helps the dead climb back.

Because it helps the living continue.

---

And the benevolent power?

It would remain mostly hidden.

Not absent.

Hidden.

It would live in the slight curve of a thorn that gives shelter.

In the warning tremor before rupture.

In the rope that holds one breath longer.

In the strange widening of fog around a distant flame.

In the warmth beneath a newborn’s back.

In the bark that remembers.

It would never turn the Trellis into a garden.

It would not make the people soft.

It would not steal from them the ferocious dignity of their adaptation, their precision, their courage, their lamps lifted against an immeasurable dark.

It would simply teach the world one new law.

Not a law stronger than hunger.

Not stronger than weather.

Not stronger than growth.

But present everywhere, woven into the black cambium and the frozen wind:

Where life clings, let there be a handhold.

Where a flame is lit, let it be seen.

Where the void says *alone*, let something answer:

*No.*
