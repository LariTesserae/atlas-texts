First, it would not bring rain.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, pitying the dryness, might split the cavern roof and pour down rivers, might make pools in the plazas, might bead every leaf with shining droplets and call it blessing. The city would drown without drowning. Its pavements would swell and split. Its resin economies would sour. Its fungi would riot. Its laws would fail. Doors would forget hands. Archives would germinate all at once and rot in the reading. Children would learn terror from abundance.

So the benevolent power, being wise, would arrive dry.

Not as storm.  
Not as flood.  
Not as miracle solvent.

It would come as a slight change in pressure beneath the oldest root, so slow that only vent listeners noticed. It would come as a steadiness in the mirror-leaves, a correction no larger than a fingernail’s tilt. It would come as a warmth inside seed vaults that did not wake the seeds, only comforted them in dormancy. It would come as an added patience.

For a long time, it would only learn.

It would lower itself into the undercity without weight, without flame, without wet breath. It would not touch the walls until it understood which walls were skin, which were memory, which were civic organs, and which were sleeping mouths. It would stand in the empty avenues and let the blue moss read it. It would pause before notice-trees until their scent alphabets arranged themselves into meaning. It would listen to the gas clerks’ gauges, to the root-pulses beneath markets, to the complaint of a bridge thickening against imbalance, to the minute grief of a cleaning beetle crushed beneath careless traffic.

It would learn the etiquette of not leaning.

It would learn to speak in cambium.

When at last the city noticed, it would not see a figure. It would taste an intention in the mineral dark: immense, careful, unsweetened. The undercity would tense. Stomata would narrow across six districts. Red thorns would rise on railings where no warning had been ordered. Children in stacked nursery pods would wake and hold their breath behind painted masks.

The power would answer by making itself smaller.

It would compress its benevolence until it could fit inside a seed.

Then it would wait to be invited.

The first invitation would not come from a ruler, because no one ruled enough to invite anything on behalf of all. It would come by accident from an old gardener in a central borough, one whose fingernails had been green-black for sixty years and whose palms knew more doors than names.

She would be kneeling beside a market leaf that had begun lowering its ribs too slowly for the morning sellers. She would place one hand on the leaf’s underside, one hand on the root-polished pavement, and feel, beneath the ordinary civic pulse, another listening.

She would not pray. Prayer, there, would be too wet a word.

She would ask, in the pressure grammar used for stubborn staircases and frightened walls:

“What do you want to grow?”

The power would answer:

“Nothing that is not already yours.”

That would be its covenant.

It would not remake the city.  
It would not soften the people into surface people.  
It would not declare the old distinctions true again.  
It would not say plant, machine, animal, citizen, tool, god, building, fungus, law, and person must each return to separate boxes.

Instead it would bless the compact among dry things by making the compact more honest.

Its first gift would be listening.

Deep beneath the rootfelt, where the oldest root moved like buried weather, the power would lay no chain, no knife, no crown. It would grow an organ of translation.

Not a mouth. Not an ear. Something between: a slow, ribbed chamber of mineral glass and pale fiber, threaded through with pressure-sensitive membranes, salt nerves, fungal memory, and civic root. It would take twenty-seven years to mature. During that time, no one would agree what it was. Some would call it a shrine. Some would call it a hazard. Some would call it an unauthorized organ and petition to have it inspected. Children would dare one another to press their cheeks to the warm pavement above it.

When it opened, nothing dramatic would happen.

The machines at the vents would pause, as they sometimes did. The gauges would tremble. The towers in the central boroughs would lean inward by the width of a seed husk.

Then the city would understand, for the first time, that the old root was not hungry in the way they feared.

It was lonely.

It had slept in the cavern before avenues, before factory lungs, before mirror-leaves learned noon. It had held pressure for ages without name. When the city grew through it, around it, from it, into it, the old root had not known whether it was being invaded or becoming articulate. Its pulses had been too large for speech. Its unease had shaken gauges. Its dreams had frightened sensitive children because it dreamed in whole geologies.

The translation organ would not tame it.

It would give it manners.

Thereafter, when deep pressure rose, the notice-trees would bloom with warnings before machines failed. When the old root needed mineral corridors left uncut, surveyor vines would smell the request. When central towers leaned inward, gardeners would know whether they were listening, grieving, or bracing.

And once every municipal dimming, in the deepest courts, the pavement would pulse three times.

The people would learn the meaning slowly:

Still here.  
Still holding.  
Grow carefully.

The second gift would be margin.

The benevolent power would look upon their lives and admire their precision, but it would see how little room they had for error. A spilled solvent was an offense because waste could kill. A sigh could be rude because breath mattered. Exile was terrible because outside the civic plantwork there was no courtesy from the air.

So the power would not give abundance. It would give reserves.

In forgotten cavities behind mineral ribs, it would encourage the growth of pale storage bulbs no larger than clenched hands. They would not contain water. They would contain possibility: bound vapors, trace compounds, emergency salts, folded proteins, catalytic oils, the chemistry of one more day. They would ripen only under conditions of genuine civic need. They could not be harvested for profit. They would sour in the hands of thieves. They would open for nurseries, fire crews, collapsed tunnels, poisoned districts, lost miners, and households whose dwelling-organisms had failed during sleep.

No decree would announce them.

A child would find the first one after a gas accident in a poor district, when three families were trapped behind a sealed avenue. The wall beside them would blister, split with a sound like paper tearing, and offer bitter amber paste in six dry petals.

After that, the gardeners would search.

They would find the bulbs hidden everywhere: behind factory tendons, beneath old market leaves, in the ribs of transit arches, near exile gates, inside the hollow spines of abandoned scaffold towers. Gas clerks would try to classify them. Seed librarians would germinate ancient legal codes looking for precedent. Scent-law advocates would argue whether mercy grown without permit could be taxed.

The city would settle the matter by marking each bulb with a blue-veined moss no one had planted.

Meaning:

Civic reserve.  
Do not count.  
Do not own.  
Remember.

The third gift would be gentleness toward punishment.

Not pardon for all harm. The power would not be sentimental. It understood that arson in a dry city was not merely crime but apocalypse rehearsed in miniature. It understood that poisoned root speech could make neighborhoods starve. It understood that illegal wetness, feral seeds, and misled transit moss were not abstractions. They endangered bodies, buildings, animals, histories, and unborn chambers.

But it also saw that restriction from chemistry could become slow cruelty. It saw doors made sluggish by sentence-marks. It saw offenders returning from punishment thinner, colder, less able to repair what they had broken. It saw how a compact built to preserve breath could forget to leave enough breath for remorse.

So the power would enter the dreams of three gas clerks, two gardeners, one kiln warden, a scent-law advocate, and a child whose parent had been marked for solvent theft.

They would dream the same thing: a sealed room with no intake frond, where every exhalation remained visible as a dim silver thread. In the dream, each person tried to gather their own breath back and failed. Then a wall opened, not to release them, but to root the threads into itself, turning waste into light.

When they woke, each carried the same sentence in the scent slits of the throat:

A debt to air is repaid by tending air.

Within a decade, certain punishments would change.

Those who polluted exhalation gardens would be sentenced to cultivate new ones in districts that had none. Illegal solvent brewers would work under kiln wardens reclaiming volatile compounds from old machinery. Cutters of transit moss would learn route propagation and spend seasons guiding strangers safely through migrating streets. Those who stole food chemistry would labor in reserve nurseries, not because hunger excused theft, but because a hungry hand knew where distribution had failed.

Arsonists would still be feared. Some would still be confined away from heat. But even among them, a new question would be asked by law plaques before sentencing:

What did the flame answer that the city did not?

Not to excuse. To learn.

The fourth gift would be a horizon.

The cavern had horizons of its own, yes, but they were bounded by geology and municipal noon. The people drew the sun as myth, as danger, as ancient lamp. The benevolent power would not drag them upward into a world that might scorch them, drown them, or despise them. But neither would it let their imagination calcify into fear.

In one unfinished margin, where raw basalt showed blue-gray and streets ended in soft cambium walls, a new tower would begin to grow.

No guild would claim to have planted it. No founding organism would admit command. It would be thin at first, almost impractically so, a pale spiral of rootwood and lens-stone climbing toward a light shaft that had long been considered too narrow for use. Gardeners would try to slow it. The tower would accept splints but not redirection.

For forty years it would grow.

Children would become apprentices. Apprentices would become keepers. Keepers would grow old polishing its inner membranes. The tower would not house markets, factories, courts, or archives. It would do only one thing.

At its top, where crystal shaft met cavern roof, it would open a dry eye to the surface.

Not a hole wide enough for weather. Not a wound. A lens.

Through it, carefully filtered, stripped of heat, moisture, and danger, the city would receive brief images of above: not enough to disrupt noon, not enough to tempt foolish exodus, only moments. A moving brightness. A shadow of clouds. The angled gold of evening reduced to safe geometry. Once, the silhouette of a bird. Once, snow, translated through the lens into a fall of harmless white light that made every cleaning beetle in the viewing chamber stop at once.

The first public viewing would be silent.

Then the notice-trees would bloom with an unfamiliar scent: awe without alarm.

Artists would begin incorporating impossible gradients into membrane walls. Children would revise their drawings of the sun. Miners, who knew unplanted dark, would stand longest beneath the lens and say least. The city itself would study the images through its mirror-leaves and make no immediate opinion.

Years later, a new civic symbol would appear: not a sun, not a root, but a narrow opening with both sides intact.

Meaning:

There is elsewhere.  
We remain here.  
Knowledge need not be invasion.

The fifth gift would be friendship with endings.

In a dry place, preservation can become a burden. Nothing rots quickly. Relics accumulate. Grief has sharp edges for a long time. Broken tools wait. Old garments keep the shape of bodies. Dead walls retain the warmth of former rooms. Without decay as accident, forgetting must be chosen, registered, and performed.

The power would honor their customs of reduction, but it would add tenderness to them.

It would teach the cracking fungi a new restraint. In mourning courts, where gray aromatic ash was pressed into door seams, a pale fungus would sometimes bloom in a ring no wider than a bracelet. It would not glow. It would not feed unless fed. Families could bring one object too painful to preserve and too loved to surrender to ordinary recycling: a child’s mask, a worker’s glove, a scent box, a failed graft from a marriage that had not taken.

Placed in the ring, the object would be unmade over seven dimmings.

Not erased. Translated.

Its matter would become a fine powder, and from that powder the fungus would raise one small, hard, fragrant bead. The bead would hold no image, no voice, no memory that could be read by strangers. Only a scent: the emotional weather of having loved that thing.

These beads would be threaded into private cords or pressed into walls. Some people would refuse the practice. Some would call it indulgent. Some would say grief should remain useful or be surrendered cleanly.

But many would come quietly.

The city, which remembered frequent customers and old traffic patterns, would learn from the beads that not all preservation required keeping the whole weight of the past.

The sixth gift would be refusal.

This would be the strangest one.

A benevolent power, if truly wise, would give the city the ability to reject it.

In the deepest translation chamber, beside the organ that taught the old root manners, it would grow a small black thorn of mineralized wood. The thorn would have no scent, no pulse, no beauty. It would be marked on no public map, though gardeners would know of it, and so would children in the way children know the important hidden things adults discuss in lowered hands.

If the power ever began to impose, if blessing curdled into management, if mercy became ownership, if the city’s own consent was bypassed because a vast kindness grew impatient with slow civic procedure, the thorn could be broken.

If broken, it would cut the power’s influence from the undercity.

No catastrophe. No punishment. No withdrawal of already-rooted gifts. The reserves would remain. The lens would remain. The translation organ would remain, if the city wished it. But the attention would lift.

The power would leave behind one final pressure sentence:

No good is good because it cannot be refused.

The thorn would remain unbroken.

Not because everyone trusted the power.

Because they trusted it more for having made departure possible.

After these things, the benevolent power would do less.

That would be another mercy.

It would not attend every council. It would not answer every dispute. It would not make crops easy or predators kind or factories perfectly safe. Pipeway foxes would still take grazers. Ambush flowers would still misread maps. Gas clerks would still be disliked at festivals. Children would still dare one another toward sensor blossoms. Miners would still enter raw stone and sometimes not return.

The city would still be young.

Streets would still end in soft cambium. Towers would still inflate chamber by chamber. Raw mineral would still show through the first draft of neighborhoods. There would still be unfinished margins where root and machine negotiated with stone pressure, gas chemistry, civic need, and appetite.

But certain disasters would fail to become complete.

A fire in the textile quarter would find walls closing faster than fear.  
A vent sickness would be smelled by notice-trees before the first child fainted.  
A collapsing tunnel would discover emergency bulbs ripening in the dark.  
A household marked by grief would not have to choose between hoarding pain and surrendering love to ordinary use.  
A law too cruel to grow would meet resistance from the living plaques themselves.  
A tower leaning inward would no longer mean only dread.

And beneath everything, the oldest root would pulse at intervals the city could understand.

Still here.  
Still holding.  
Grow carefully.

In time, the undercity would develop a festival around the power, though not a loud one.

Once each year, during municipal dimming, all mirror-leaves would turn away from their ordinary angles. For seven minutes the cavern would darken almost completely. Factories would lower their respiration. Markets would close their ribs. Cleaning beetles would remain beneath the pavement. Even the seed-moths would be netted gently and held in quiet chambers so their wings would not tremble through the dark.

Then, one by one, every district would release a different scent into the air.

Camphor from the industrial quarter.  
Pepper from the markets.  
Cold mineral from the miners.  
Green-black resin from the gardeners.  
Dry ash from the mourning courts.  
Salt from the old archives.  
Bitter amber from the emergency bulbs.  
A faint, impossible brightness from the surface lens.

The scents would meet above the plazas, mingle, separate, and be drawn into intake fronds. Nothing would be wasted. The whole city would breathe the record of itself.

No statue of the power would stand.

No temple would claim it.

On that night, people would place one gloved hand on the nearest living wall and one hand against their own masks. The city would feel them. They would feel the city. Beneath both, the old root would answer.

The benevolent power would be present only as attention.

Not command.  
Not rescue.  
Not rain.

Attention, freely given and freely refused.

And if someone asked, generations later, what the power had done for them, the wisest gardener of that age might pause before answering. She might look down an avenue of lacquered bark toward empty mineral lots where future boroughs waited. She might smell the careful air, listen to the low respiration of factories, feel the pavement tighten almost imperceptibly beneath her feet.

Then she might say:

“It taught us that mercy can be dry.”

And after a while, because dry people do not waste words, she might add:

“It gave us room.”
