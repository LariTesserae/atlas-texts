It would not come as rain.

That would be the first wisdom of it.

A lesser mercy, seeing the white basins and the cracked salts and the glass skins that only pretend to wetness, might pity the place in the language of other worlds. It might open a vein in the sky. It might call down clouds, or thaw some hidden reservoir, or teach the land the old lowland grammar of streams.

And that would be ruin.

Water would enter like an argument from another creation. The black mats would loosen from their stones and forget their script. The salt fans would collapse into blind slurry. The hollow columns would drink once and split, their centuries of chambered listening drowned in swelling tissues never meant to soften. The ceramic blade-fields would dull; the blue fire would gutter; the paths would wash away; the handprints would run green down the inward-leaning walls.

So the benevolent power would not rain.

It would arrive first as attention.

Not light. Not voice. Not a figure standing above the plateau with robes trailing through the undecided sky. Those things are for places that understand arrival. Here, where distance repeats and horizons correct themselves, the power would enter as a patience added to the existing patience, so deep and so uninsistent that nothing would need to defend itself.

It would listen.

It would listen to the ground-chord until it could hear, within the deafening continuity, the separate griefs and labors braided there: the mountains trying to become without tearing themselves apart; the groves sorting pressure from prophecy; the mats writing the newest thoughts of stone; the blade-fields counting against time’s reluctance; the glass tubers holding memory because nothing flowed; the old shelters keeping silence like a coal cupped in two hands.

It would not ask, What is wrong here?

It would ask, What are you becoming, and where does becoming hurt?

For a long while, perhaps for an age measured only by the slow rotation of mica crowns, it would do nothing that could be called action. Its mercy would be observational. It would learn the cautions exactly.

Do not look at the same horizon through two openings.

Do not answer a sound that resembles your name.

Do not cut the columns during listening.

Do not sleep on fresh rock.

Do not count the groves aloud.

The power would obey these laws not because it feared punishment, but because respect is the first form of healing. It would not exempt itself from the etiquette of the wounded world.

When at last it moved, it would move very slightly.

It would place no river in the basins, but it would give the land a different kind of carrying.

A dry carrying.

A way for strain to pass without shattering everything it touched.

Deep below the plateau, beneath the exposed bands of unfinished strata and the warm porous rock where mountains think themselves upward, the power would lay a hidden lattice of permission. Not an object. Not a machine. More like a remembered kindness in the laws of pressure. A covenant among faults.

The next time a boulder remembered it belonged three meters to the left, it would still move.

But it would move without crushing the root-cords that had held it for generations.

The next time a ridge appeared twice on the horizon, both versions would be allowed to recognize one another before one vanished.

The next time a path decided it had always gone somewhere else, it would leave behind a usable absence, a faint gray after-route, safe for one crossing only, enough for a traveler or a lingering builder-ghost to return before the correction completed itself.

The power would not stabilize the plateau entirely.

Stability, imposed too early, would be another violence. This land was young in the way an egg is young. To harden it from outside would be to kill the creature before it hatched.

So the power would give it intervals.

Small mercies of consistency.

A dawn that always followed one particular night.

A shelter whose doorway remained itself no matter which horizon leaned near.

A salt basin that reflected only the sky above it for three days in every hundred.

A blade-field whose counting admitted one reliable gap at the same pressure of footfall, if approached humbly and without arithmetic spoken aloud.

A grove that could be seen from two directions but would no longer trap the unwary between them.

Not certainty.

Breathing room.

Though there was no breath.

Then the power would turn toward the sound.

It would not silence it.

The sound was not merely noise. It was speech, metabolism, weatherless weather, society. The colossal chord was how the place knew itself. To quiet the plateau would be to blind it.

But the sound hurt the old builders. It had driven them into low shelters and perhaps, eventually, into handprints and absences. Even now, whatever remained of them moved carefully between thresholds, unable to belong entirely to the deafening life outside or the merciful silence within.

So the power would teach the shelters a new art.

The walls already knew how to cut sound with a blade. The ribs of plant-column already divided noise from meaning. The woven mineral mats already held silence taut.

The power would add discernment.

After that, when one stepped into a hut, the sound would not simply vanish. Instead it would be translated.

The ground-chord would become a low warmth in the floor.

The mineral shrieks of salt fans would appear as tiny points of white light along the wall, each one marking a place where the rock below had begun deciding too quickly.

The clicking of distant blade-fields would arrange itself into shadow-lines, showing when a crossing might be safe.

The hum of ash-shrub pods would become a taste of copper at the back of the mouth before danger, resin before uncertainty, clean clay before rest.

And the voices that resembled names—the false callers, the plateau’s dangerous mimicries—would arrive in the shelter as nothing more than brief cold patches on the stone, unable to form syllables.

Thus the huts would remain quiet, but not ignorant.

The beings who still lived as absences would know more gently what the outside was saying.

Perhaps some of them would come closer to the doorway.

Perhaps one would place a pressure, like a palm without flesh, against the inner wall.

Perhaps a green handprint, old beyond mourning, would warm.

The benevolent power would notice those handprints most of all.

Not because they were more alive than the groves or mats or mountains, but because they were the marks of creatures who had feared being revised out of their own world. Palms pressed to stone, fingers spread: I was here. I am here. Let this surface remember me correctly.

The power would not restore them bodily without asking.

Resurrection can be theft if it drags a being backward into a form it has outgrown.

Instead, it would make an offer.

In every shelter where the handprints overlapped, it would create one new patch of wall: smooth, unmarked, neither slate nor clay nor salt, but a substance able to hold choice. No sound would touch it. No number would correct it. No alternate horizon would revise it.

Any handprint that wished to remain a handprint could remain.

Any absence that wished to continue as a careful pressure behind a doorway could continue.

Any one of the old builders who wished to dream toward a body again could place itself upon that blankness and slowly gather.

Not flesh, perhaps. Not as before.

A dry body, suited to the plateau that had survived them: narrow and strong, with lungs that did not need much air, ears guarded by inner mineral folds, skin that held alkali pigment like memory, bones laced with ceramic patience. A body that could step outside during certain hours without being broken by the sound. A body that could speak only in shelters, and outside communicate by hand, pressure, carved spiral, and the turning of stones.

The power would not repopulate the plateau.

It would permit return.

There is a difference.

For the plants, too, there would be gifts, but none that made them other than themselves.

To the pale column-groves, the power would give a new chamber inside every thousandth shoot: a mercy-hollow. When the ground-chord passed through these hollows, wounds remembered by the columns would no longer teach nearby stone only how to crack. They would also teach how to seal without forgetting. A cut made in ignorance would remain serious, but not endlessly contagious. The grove would still be capable of anger, but now also of scar.

To the black mats, the power would give a boundary-dream. They would continue to use passersby as bridges between surfaces, but some small part of their low, dark mind would learn the difference between stone and a creature that can be lost. When a traveler stepped on them, they might still send a fault-line ache into the knee, still place an ancient number in thought, still borrow memory for orientation—but they would return what they borrowed with less distortion.

To the glass tubers, the power would give no extra light. They had enough bookkeeping to do. Instead it would grant them one additional capacity: to store not only survival, but mercy received. After that, when one brightened at night, it would not merely measure internal pressure. It would remember that pressure had once been answered.

To the blade-fields, the power would give a rhythm that could include interruption without humiliation. They would remain exacting. They would still argue in blue fire. But once in a while, when a body waited at the edge in proper silence, the field would count the waiting as part of the meter. A gap would open not because the field had been forced, but because courtesy had become mathematically admissible.

To the ash-shrubs, nervous and brittle, it would give a gentler warning pitch—one tone that meant not danger, not instability, not sky-nearness, but rest now. Safe enough. For a little while, safe enough.

To the root-things, exposed like braided sinew, it would give the knowledge of gradual release. They would still grip stress. They would still hold stones until deeper arrangements required movement. But not every necessary letting-go would need to be catastrophic. Some boulders would learn to move by inches. Some cracks would open like hands instead of mouths.

To the salt fans, it would give nothing.

This, too, would be wisdom.

The salt fans were already too near the edge between chemistry and intention. To bless them directly might push them into hunger, or worship, or pain. So the power would merely pass over them with a regard so light that no crystal could claim it had been altered. Their shrieking would continue. Their white branches would grow from invisible flaws. They would remain life’s rumor, which is sometimes holier than life’s certainty.

And to the mountains?

To the mountains, the power would speak once.

Not in words. Words are too small and too wet.

It would speak in upwardness.

It would enter the warm fissures and the porous stone and the pressure that was almost thought, and it would say:

You may become.

You need not become all at once.

The mountains would not bow. Mountains do not bow, especially unborn ones.

But the red throbbing behind their skins would slow a little. The unfinished ridges would continue to lift, but with fewer duplications born of panic. The exposed strata would still hang midair where deposition had paused, yet some grains would begin, very slowly, to choose descent. Arches of basalt would harden at their keystones before inviting weight. Ravines would wait for causes.

The world would go on breaking.

But less of the breaking would be fear.

Then the power would address the sky.

This would be the most delicate act.

The sky had not decided how far away it was. It lowered at night, inserted peaks into other media, opened into impossible depths where inverted landscapes hung like chandeliers. It cast rectangular shadows too straight to be clouds. It was not malicious. It was undecided, and its indecision made every creature below it live in negotiation.

The power would not push it upward forever.

A far sky might starve the groves of their abstract nourishment. It might end the dimmings by which pods emptied and shoots began elsewhere. It might sever the plateau from the other under-landscapes whose nearness, however dangerous, was part of its ecology.

Instead, the power would teach the sky a threshold.

There would be nights when it lowered, yes.

But not without warning.

The ash-shrubs would know first. Then the blade-fields. Then the mica crowns. Then the shelters, where the translated sound would bloom brown-violet on the interior walls.

The highest peaks would still disappear into the sky’s other medium, but they would return with the same names they had when they entered.

No shelter would be swallowed unless it had already been empty for a hundred years.

No path would be revised while a foot was touching it.

No handprint would be removed by nearness.

These were not commandments.

They were agreements.

The wise power knew that worlds like this cannot be ruled from above. They must be bargained with at the level of habit.

Last, the power would make a place for silence outside.

Not much.

Not enough to wound the sounding society.

Somewhere near the center of the plateau, where a basin without a lake opened like a white eye and three abandoned paths nearly met, it would raise no temple. It would plant no banner. It would not carve its own sign into the black salt.

It would simply persuade the sound to bend around a circle of bare stone twelve paces wide.

Inside that circle, the ground-chord would become touch rather than noise. The mineral shrieks would pass overhead. The clicking fields would be visible only as faint blue pulses beyond the rim. The sky would not be farther away there, but it would be less eager to enter.

In the center of the circle, the power would set a single stone bowl.

Not for water.

For dust.

For the falling mineral powder the old builders once collected. For shattered mica. For flakes from dead fronds. For salt splinters. For the colored residue of roots. For the dry remains by which this world remembered itself.

Anything living, or half-living, or formerly living, could place something there.

A blade-tip.

A grain of young basalt darkened by a mat.

A pod that had finished humming.

A ceramic plate from a returned builder’s shoulder.

A pinch of green pigment from a handprint that had chosen to become a hand again.

The bowl would never fill.

Not because it emptied, but because it would hold offerings in depth rather than volume. Each fragment would remain itself, unrotted, undissolved, unwashed away, and yet joined to the others in a dry communion.

The plateau would acquire, for the first time, not water, not weather, not softness—

but a center.

A place where nothing had to listen for danger.

A place where becoming could rest without ceasing.

And after all this, the benevolent power would withdraw.

Not entirely. Never entirely.

It would not hover. It would not demand gratitude. It would not install priests among the groves or teach the blade-fields hymns to itself. It would leave no doctrine except the changes themselves, and even those would be modest enough that some inhabitants might debate for centuries whether intervention had occurred at all.

The old cautions would remain.

Do not answer your name.

Do not sleep on fresh rock.

Do not count the groves aloud.

Do not cross a basin reflecting the wrong sky.

But perhaps, in some shelters, a new line would appear beneath them, incised in a hand neither wholly visible nor wholly absent:

If the ash-shrub sings low and even, you may rest.

Perhaps another:

A path that remembers you kindly may be trusted once.

And, in the oldest hut, where the handprints overlapped until the wall looked leafed in green fire, perhaps one more:

Mercy is not the arrival of what is missing.  
Mercy is the protection of what is trying to be.

The plateau would remain black salt and pale glass.

It would remain dry, deafening, unfinished, severe.

No rivers would come.

No animals would call.

No rain would soften the exposed dead.

But the groves would scar instead of only remembering wounds. The mats would borrow travelers less cruelly. The blade-fields would count waiting as part of time. The mountains would rise by fractions without panic. The sky would lower with warning. The shelters would translate the world instead of merely excluding it. The handprints would be allowed to remain, or return, or become something new by consent.

Nothing would be saved from being itself.

That would be the blessing.
