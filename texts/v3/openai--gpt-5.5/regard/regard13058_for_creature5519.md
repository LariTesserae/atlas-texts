It would do very little at first.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing the plateau, would understand that this is not an empty hardship waiting to be corrected. It would not look upon the cold stone and think, *This should be softened.* It would not look upon the Khelani and think, *These people must be rescued from their lives.*

Wisdom would recognize an old balance.

The plateau is severe, but not cruel. Sparse, but not dead. Silent, but not voiceless. The Khelani are not lost. They are not lesser for having no towers of metal, no engines, no written archives, no roads cutting the land into possession. They have made a covenant with stone, mist, water, moss, animal, and sky. A wise power would see that this covenant is precious.

So it would begin by listening.

It would listen to the water moving through the hidden fissures.

It would listen to the wind crossing the lichen-dark rock.

It would listen to the elders speaking in the central stone court, their voices carrying strangely far in the thin air.

It would listen to the children learning the names of edible leaves, the hunters thanking the small lives they take, the women and men repairing cistern channels with patient hands.

It would listen until it understood what was already whole.

Only then would it act.

And its actions would be quiet.

It would not split the clouds and appear in fire. It would not carve commandments into the plateau. It would not make the Khelani kneel.

Instead, it would place its care where care was needed most.

In the oldest fissures, where water had begun to slip too deeply beyond reach, it would nudge mineral and root together so that the seepage slowed. Not enough for anyone to call it a miracle. Just enough that the cisterns filled a little more faithfully after snowmelt. Just enough that no child would go thirsty in the longest dry season.

In the sheltered hollows where green things grew, it would strengthen the smallest roots. The cushion plants would hold the soil more tightly. The dwarf conifers would cast slightly better shelter against the wind. The mosses would drink mist more eagerly and release it more slowly. The Khelani would notice, after some years, that certain gardens of the wild no longer failed as often.

The elders would say, “The land has accepted our songs.”

And the power would be content with that explanation.

It would turn its attention to the animals.

Not to multiply them beyond balance, for that would only bring hunger later. Instead it would lend sharpness to the instincts of prey and patience to the instincts of hunters. Litters would survive in the right years. Birds would nest where the rock held warmth. Insects would return after hard frosts. The old web of eating and being eaten would remain intact, but less brittle.

It would make no creature immortal.

It would remove no death from the plateau.

A wise power would know that a world without death is not a living world, only a frozen one.

But it would soften wasteful death. The pointless kinds. The fever that takes too many infants in one season. The rockslide that comes with no warning. The infection from a small wound that should have healed. The despair that settles in a mind during weeks of gray sky and hunger.

Against these, it would work gently.

A healer among the Khelani would dream of boiling a certain bitter lichen with waxy leaves and bone broth. She would wake remembering the taste clearly. She would try it on a coughing child, and the child would live.

A hunter crossing a familiar slope would feel, for no reason he could name, a sudden refusal in his feet. He would stop. A breath later, a slab of frost-loosened stone would shear away before him and vanish into mist.

An old woman, who had outlived two sons and had begun to feel herself already half stone, would hear water dripping beneath her dwelling in a rhythm like a forgotten lullaby. The next morning, she would rise and teach the children a song no one had sung for forty years.

That song would become important later.

The power would preserve memory, but not by making tablets or monuments. The Khelani already had stone enough. Instead, it would strengthen the bridges between generations.

Children would remember stories more clearly.

Elders would find the right words before death took them.

A young person impatient with old customs would one day notice that the pattern of beads on his grandmother’s wrist matched the pattern of stars seen through a rare break in the clouds. He would ask why. She would tell him the migration story of their people, and in that telling he would understand that tradition was not a cage, but a map of survived dangers.

The power would guard the Khelani not from change, but from severance.

For change would come. It always does.

Perhaps one day strangers would climb to the plateau. Traders, wanderers, refugees, conquerors, scholars, or people fleeing a ruined lowland. A lesser benevolence might hide the Khelani forever. But a wise power would know that isolation can preserve, and isolation can also imprison.

So it would prepare the Khelani.

It would give them dreams of meeting others without surrendering themselves.

It would guide one child, bright-eyed and restless, to learn the calls of distant birds and the shapes of far valleys. That child would grow into an interpreter of omens, then of people. When outsiders came, the Khelani would not be defenseless in spirit.

The strangers, if they came with greed, would find the plateau bewildering.

Their compasses would quarrel with stone.

Their fires would smoke.

Their boots would slip on dry rock as though it were wet.

Their voices would return to them in echoes so distorted that they would become afraid of their own intentions.

But if they came hungry, lost, or humble, the mist would open a little. A path would show itself by patches of green. A Khelani scout would see them before they collapsed from cold.

The power would not make the plateau a fortress.

It would make it discerning.

Toward the land itself, the power would behave almost like a physician tending an ancient body.

It would not erase the fissures. The fissures are part of the plateau’s memory.

It would not smooth the strange markings. Mystery is not damage.

It would not warm the climate until lush forests overtook the stone. The austerity of the place is one of its truths.

But in the deep basalt, where old pressures slept, it would ease certain tensions. It would let small cracks form in harmless places so that greater fractures need not come suddenly beneath the settlement. It would teach the rock to sigh before it broke.

The Khelani would learn those sighs.

They would add them to their weather-lore.

“Three hollow ticks beneath the western wall,” an elder would say, “means move the winter stores.”

And they would move them.

And a month later the wall would fall outward in a storm, harming no one.

The power would smile, if such a being smiled.

Because the best miracle is the one that becomes knowledge.

It would also leave one gift that was not purely practical.

At the center of the settlement, where the people gathered for ceremony, there was a shallow basin worn into the stone by generations of feet, rain, ash, and prayer. Into that basin, during one night of low cloud and moonless dark, the power would coax a thin thread of mineral light.

By day it would look like nothing more than pale quartz.

By night, when mist lay low and the people sang, it would glow faintly—not bright enough to banish darkness, only enough to reveal faces.

The Khelani would call it the Listening Stone.

They would not worship it exactly. They would sit around it when decisions were difficult. Its light would not tell them what to do. It would merely make it harder for anyone to lie, to posture, or to forget the faces of the others.

In that glow, anger would still be possible.

Disagreement would still be possible.

But cruelty would feel heavier in the mouth.

And grief, shared there, would become bearable.

This would be the power’s deepest intervention: not abundance, not safety, not revelation, but an increase in the community’s ability to remain whole under strain.

For benevolence is not always the granting of ease.

Sometimes it is the strengthening of relationship.

Between root and stone.

Between water and cistern.

Between hunter and prey.

Between elder and child.

Between the living and the dead.

Between a people and the land that has shaped them.

Over time, the Khelani would develop new stories. They would speak of a presence that came not from above, but from within the plateau’s own patience. Some would say it was the land waking more fully. Some would say it was an ancestor who had become mist. Some would say it was the spirit beneath all spirits, too vast to name and too gentle to demand naming.

None of these explanations would be entirely wrong.

The benevolent power would not correct them.

It had not come to be known.

It had come to help life continue in dignity.

And when, after many generations, the Khelani stood beneath a rare clear sky and saw the stars burning with impossible nearness, they would feel—not comfort exactly, and not fear, but kinship. They would understand that their plateau was not forgotten. That smallness did not mean abandonment. That to live well in a hard place was a sacred art.

The wind would move through the cracks.

Water would drip in the hidden dark.

Moss would hold to stone.

Children would sleep warm inside walls built by ancestors.

And the wise power, having done enough, would remain nearby in the manner of all truly benevolent things:

not ruling,

not possessing,

not demanding gratitude,

but keeping watch.
