The power would come down very low.

It would not arrive as a tower of light, because height is not the language of that country. It would not split the clouds, lift the stones, call the creatures upward, or set a crown upon any head. Such gestures would be misunderstandings. In a land that has spent ages unlearning sharpness, wisdom would enter as something without edge.

Perhaps first as rain that was not more rain than the plain could bear.

Perhaps as a coolness beneath a season that should have cracked the mud too soon.

Perhaps as a pause in the hunger of birds on one evening when the eggs were newly laid.

The power would listen before it acted.

It would listen to the ticking frogs and the insect-wire hum, to the plink of water from stone lips, to the slow chemical speech of iron, clay, algae, rot. It would listen to the low ones calling at dusk, their throat-notes traveling through water and saturated ground. It would learn that the plain was not asking to be saved from itself. It was not a failed forest, not a drowned desert, not an unfinished garden. It was complete in its incompletion: a threshold, an old breathing skin, a country whose beauty lay in the patient uncertainty between water and land.

So the power would not make it grand.

It would not raise trees everywhere, though it could.  
It would not gather the waters into a lake, though that would shine.  
It would not carve a riverbed and give the land direction.  
It would not harden the mud into roads.  
It would not make the low ones stand upright and speak with fire in their hands.

It would understand that mercy, here, must be low, damp, and almost invisible.

So it would make small adjustments.

Deep under the old stone ribs, where water already lingered, it would open a few hidden seams no wider than a root’s memory. Not springs in the dramatic sense. Nothing that would gush, nothing that would announce itself. Only slow-fed veins, enough to keep certain hollows wet three weeks longer in the cruel years. Enough that not every pool would fail at once. Enough that drought would remain a teacher, but not become a final sentence.

It would deepen, by the width of a sleeping body, a handful of basins scattered far apart across the plain. The rain would still decide them. Silt would still gather. Fish would still come and vanish. But in each basin there would be one shaded pocket under stone, one black place where water held when the surface shrank to glare. The old females would find these by taste. The young would survive there by accident. The accident would repeat.

It would teach nothing new to the creatures, because their wisdom was already correctly shaped.

But it might strengthen what they already knew.

A slightly keener tongue for mineral change.  
A slightly longer patience in the sealed clay.  
A thicker tenderness around the eggs, so some would cling deeper under stone.  
A faint unease in the body before a hard drying, urging migration one night earlier.  
A deeper sleep when sleep was needed.  
A truer call across wet ground.

Not gifts that would lift them above the plain.

Gifts that would bind them more faithfully to it.

The power would leave their enemies in place.

This would be difficult, perhaps, for benevolence. It would watch herons spear the transparent young. It would watch crabs find the soft brown eggs. It would watch pools warm and shrink, watch tadpoles turn their small mouths toward a surface that lowered beyond mercy. It would know each life completely as it ended.

But wisdom would not make the plain harmless.

A harmless world would soon become another kind of death. The birds would go hungry. The insects would thicken beyond balance. The low ones would multiply past the capacity of seepage and shade, and then hunger would come with a broader mouth. So the power would not remove the beak, the drought, the parasite, the bitter necessity of being eaten.

It would only prevent wasteful ruin.

When sickness rose unnaturally from stagnant heat, it would let wind cross the water at night.

When a season of rain threatened to scour eggs from every root and crack, it would quiet the flood by degrees, spreading it sideways into forgotten pans.

When heat came too hard, too early, before the young had grown their limbs, it would send several mornings of pearl mist and dim sun.

When cold, rare and strange, descended from the upper air, it would hold warmth in the dark stone until dawn.

It would not be obvious. No creature would kneel. No creature would tell stories of rescue. The low ones would continue as they had always continued: emerging after rain, calling from shallow basins, grazing films from stone, swallowing larvae, pressing themselves into wet clay, vanishing under glare.

But the oldest among them might begin to know certain places.

A hollow that never quite betrayed them.  
A seam that breathed water even after long heat.  
A sedge-root basin where more young than expected crawled out alive.  
A rib of stone that warmed gently but did not burn.  
A dark channel that could be trusted when the horizon trembled white.

They would not call these holy.

They would taste them, remember them, return.

And that would be enough.

The power would also protect the country from what did not belong to its ancient grammar.

If some hard intention came from elsewhere — straight edges, drainage cuts, poisoned runoff, machines with teeth, fires that did not move like weather but like conquest — then the power would act less gently.

Not with wrath for its own sake. Wrath would be too hot, too tall.

It would act with the firmness of a stone that refuses to crack.

Roads planned across the wet ribs would sink before they were finished. Survey lines would vanish under unexpected sheets of water. Engines would foul with black mud and fine mineral grit. Those who came to empty the marsh would find no easy center, no dry staging ground, no obedient channel. Mosquitoes would rise in disciplined veils. Heat would bewilder instruments. Maps would disagree with the taste of the soil. Every ditch would slump. Every straight line would soften.

The place would defend itself by becoming more completely itself.

And if any human being, rare and quiet, came not to take but to witness, the power would allow them a glimpse.

Not a spectacle.

Only evening.

A bronze pool.  
A low head lifting.  
Amber eyes level with the reflected sky.  
A throat swelling with sound older than language.  
Then another answer far away, and another, until the plain seemed to remember its own breathing.

The witness would leave changed, though perhaps unable to say how. They might only know afterward that not all sanctuaries are green, not all wisdom rises, not all blessed things shine.

Some blessed things are mottled, damp, bitter-skinned, and patient.

Some holy places smell of mud.

When the power had done what little should be done, it would remain without ruling.

It would reside in continuance.

In the bead of water held beneath a stone lip.  
In the egg hidden from one probing beak.  
In the old female’s correct turning toward rain miles away.  
In the young one’s first breath of thick evening air.  
In the low call that travels farther than the caller knows.  
In the softness by which all wounds of the land become hollows, and all hollows become places where life may gather.

It would not rescue the plain from erosion.

It would bless erosion.

It would not rescue the creatures from being low.

It would bless lowness.

It would not give them dominion.

It would give them duration.

And over ages, almost no one would know that anything had happened. The land would still be warm, open, wet, and old. The stones would still sweat. The pools would still appear and withdraw. Birds would still pass like brief weather. Frogs would shrill. Insects would thicken the dusk. Mud would bubble faintly where gases rose.

And from the shallow basins, when rain had joined the waters and the air had grown honest, the low ones would call.

Not in gratitude, exactly.

Gratitude is a high and separate thing.

They would call because the water was right, because the stone remembered moisture, because their throats had been made for distance, because the plain had not ended.

And the benevolent power, hearing them, would do what wisdom so often does after its smallest necessary mercy.

It would keep watch.

It would keep still.

It would let them live.
