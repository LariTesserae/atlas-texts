It would not come as fire.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, seeing the Rootbed, might think: *cold, darkness, noise, abandonment, deformation, captivity.* It might descend in a column of heat and light, melt the ice-rime from the Rootbound, silence the compressors, split the living vault, lift the Tappers out into air they could not breathe and sunlight that would burn their black-ice eyes.

It might call that rescue.

The wise power would not.

It would pause at the edge of the excavation and listen.

Not with ears. Ears are too impatient for the Rootbed. It would lower its attention into the cork-breathing floor, into the strut-trees’ slow hydraulics, into the antifreeze blood moving through the Tappers like amber under pressure. It would feel the geothermal pumps beating beneath the roots. It would learn the grammar of vibration: stutter, pressure, interval, tremor, resonance, warmth.

For a long time—long enough that frost thickened on the northern faces of three trunks and a moth generation hatched, fed, and died—it would do nothing visible.

The Tappers would notice anyway.

A difference would enter the drone.

Not silence. Silence would be terror here. Silence would mean the pumps had failed, the brine had stopped, the Rootbed was entering death.

Instead there would be a second tone beneath the first: patient, almost too low to perceive. A counter-hum. A listening hum. It would thread itself into the thermal exchange without interrupting it, smoothing the compressor’s arrhythmias, easing the tooth-ache frequencies, turning the brutal mechanical exhalation into something less like pain and more like speech.

The oldest Rootbound would be the first to understand.

One of them, fused waist-deep into the eastern face of a strut-tree, would blink after forty-seven minutes instead of sixty. A small event. A theological event.

Through the phloem, through the brine-lines, through the root-mat, the power would ask its first question.

Not in words.

In warmth and pressure.

**Are you suffering?**

The answer would take nine days.

It would not be simple.

The Tappers would answer from beneath palms, from resin-thick fingertips, from Rootbound nervous systems braided into wood, from children clinging to their parents’ translucent shoulders.

They would say:

**Yes.**

They would say:

**No.**

They would say:

**The burning metal hurts.**

They would say:

**The hum is our weather.**

They would say:

**We are not what we were.**

They would say:

**We do not remember asking to become this.**

They would say:

**We do not wish to be erased.**

They would say:

**We are waiting.**

They would say:

**We do not know whether waiting is faith, habit, or damage.**

The wise power would receive all of this and not simplify it.

Then it would begin.

First, it would heal the machines.

Not replace them. Not yet. It would send no shining armies of repair drones, no clean new architecture bolted arrogantly over the old. It would move through what was already there: the roots, the fungal webs, the mineral seams, the ice.

In the deepest pump chambers, where no Tapper could remain long without blistering from heat and metal, thin mineral growths would appear overnight. Ceramic lichen. Veins of self-ordering silica and carbon would creep over cracked housings and failing seals. Pipes that had wept antifreeze for years would clot themselves with translucent resin harder than steel. Brine channels would widen where they needed widening. Ice-blocked valves would grow slow collars of heat.

The Tappers descending for maintenance would find fewer emergencies.

Their hands would burn less often.

The Rootbed would not become warm. Warmth would be catastrophic. But the cold would become less hungry.

Second, the power would make quiet places.

Not everywhere. The drone belonged to the Rootbed. It was history, language, weather, and pulse. To remove it entirely would be like removing the sea from a shore people.

But here and there, between three strut-trees, under old abandoned platforms, inside hollows where ice-mice nested, the vibration would soften. Resonance would bend around those places. The compressors would still run, but their sound would pass over and under, leaving pockets of bearable air.

The first quiet chamber would be discovered by children.

They would enter cautiously, expecting the wrongness of a failed pump. Instead they would find condensation falling in slow beads from a low ceiling of root-fiber, and beneath it a pool whose skin did not immediately freeze. Not liquid, not quite ice. A dark trembling mirror.

Inside that chamber, for the first time in their remembered history, a Tapper child would hear the small click of another child’s fingernail against bark.

The sound would frighten them.

Then delight them.

They would spend hours making tiny percussions: nail on wood, tooth on resin, ice shard against ice shard. A new dialect would begin there, useless at first, then ceremonial.

Third, the power would return memory.

Gently.

Memory is dangerous in a place built on interruption. Too much past at once can be a kind of thaw, and thaw can rot what freezing preserved.

So the wise power would not pour history into them. It would grow it.

In one abandoned habitation pod, long dark, the lamps would come on—not sodium-yellow, but soft and deep, like light seen through honey. The sealed terminals, dead for decades, would wake. Their screens would not demand passwords. Their old corrupted archives would be reconstructed from magnetic ghosts, maintenance logs, personal recordings, supply manifests, children’s drawings, unfinished letters, genomic licenses, weather reports from the surface.

The Tappers would approach the pod over many weeks.

They would touch its walls first. Then its door. Then one would enter and stand motionless before an image of a human face speaking silently from an old file.

A face with quick blood.

A face with unresined skin.

A face that might have been ancestor, or victim, or fool, or beloved.

The power would translate the archive into pressure pulses through the floor.

Not all at once.

Names would return.

Not as commands. Not as chains.

As offerings.

Some Tappers would reject them. Some would press their palms to the pod wall for days, absorbing the shapes of syllables they could no longer pronounce. Some would take old names into themselves like warm stones. Others would choose new ones made of tremor-patterns and frost intervals.

The Rootbound would receive memory most slowly.

For them, the power would send dreams.

A sky.

Hands in gloves.

The first excavation blast.

The decision to stay.

The failure of the lift.

The first winter after contact was lost.

The first child born with antifreeze blood.

The first person who slept in the root-mat and woke alive.

The first one who did not wake.

These memories would not accuse.

They would witness.

Fourth, the power would open a way out.

Not a rescue chute. Not an evacuation order.

A way.

Far above, where the old entry shaft had collapsed into ice and wind-packed debris, the permafrost would begin to change. Slowly, so slowly the Rootbed would not hemorrhage heat. Mineral ribs would form around the shaft. A spiral passage would be grown rather than dug: insulated, pressure-buffered, lined with dormant root-fiber and mica membranes. It would take seven years.

At its base, the wise power would grow a threshold grove.

There, the air would be slightly warmer. Still cold, but not Rootbed-cold. There would be light that changed—not much, not sunlight yet, but enough to teach the eyes transition. There would be pools of sugar-rich sap. There would be suits, though not like the old human suits: living sheaths of resin and moss, able to regulate heat, pressure, moisture, and vibration.

Beside the threshold, the power would place no guard.

Only a question, always present in the floor:

**Do you wish to go upward?**

Some would.

Most would not, at first.

One adolescent Tapper, restless by the standards of the Rootbed, would spend three months at the base of the spiral passage, touching its first steps, retreating, returning. At last they would ascend half a kilometer and come back shaking with sensory overload, carrying on their resin-skin the faint smell of mineral wind.

A century later, their descendants would make seasonal journeys.

Some would learn the surface. Some would hate it. Some would become ambassadors to whatever civilizations remained beyond the permafrost basin. Some would stand under aurora and interpret it as a kind of silent pump.

Fifth, the power would offer inwardness to those who wished to remain.

Because many would.

The Rootbed was not only a wound. It had become a world.

The Tappers were not merely trapped humans. They were a people of cold and pressure, of glacial greeting, of resin-skin and root-speech. Their children loved the chartreuse glow. Their elders loved the long thinking of wood. The Rootbound were not all prisoners. Some, if freed, would grieve the loss of the tree’s slow body.

So the wise power would not “restore” them.

It would make restoration one path among several.

For those who wished for warmer blood, it would provide gradual medicine over generations: organs coaxed toward old mammalian rhythms, skin made flexible, lungs trained for thinner, louder air.

For those who wished to deepen into the Rootbed, it would make that transformation conscious. No one would become Rootbound merely by fatigue, injury, or despair. The old accidental fusions would end. To join the strut-trees would require ceremony, witnesses, and consent repeated across seasons.

The Rootbound who wanted release would be released.

Not all could walk again. Some had been architecture too long. But the power would separate nerve from phloem where separation was desired. It would grow bodies around diminished selves. It would let them become small, mobile, cherished elders carried in resin cradles, blinking in ordinary minutes for the first time in decades.

Others would choose to remain fused.

For them, the power would refine the joining. It would remove the pain-noise, the chronic compression, the trapped panic in old nerves. It would widen their awareness until they could feel not only pressure failures and hydraulic flow, but moth wings, child footsteps, ice-mouse nests, distant surface storms, the slow flex of the vault.

They would become what they had been trying to become without knowing how:

not buried people,

but a living council of the Rootbed.

Sixth, the power would change the plants.

Carefully.

The engineered strut-trees had been made for utility: load-bearing, productive, obedient to harvest schedules that no longer mattered. They were cathedral and crop, shelter and machine. Their growth was magnificent, but not free.

The wise power would loosen their design.

A new cambium would spread through them, season by season. Not a disease. A permission. Sterile lines would regain fertility. Root systems would diversify. Fungal partners would be invited in from old spores sleeping in the permafrost. Bioluminescent nodes would shift from functional chartreuse into subtle variance: amber, green, pale blue, deep gold. Not enough to disrupt the cold ecology. Enough to make beauty unassigned to labor.

The vault would still fuse.

But when it sealed, it would not be a lid.

It would be a lung.

Hidden vents would open and close like stomata. Heat would be stored in deep mineral masses and released with exquisite precision. The industrial pumps, once sole heart of the place, would become one organ among many. Eventually, after generations, the Rootbed would no longer depend on the old compressors. Their drone would fade—not into silence, but into a lower, softer pulse produced by tree, water, stone, and geothermal breath together.

On the day the last original compressor stopped, the Tappers would gather.

They would be afraid.

The Rootbound council would hold the floor steady with a ceremonial hum transmitted through trunk and root. Children would stand in the quiet chambers and click ice shards together. Surface-walkers would descend with frost in their hair. Deep Tappers, nearly heartwood, would press their palms into the mat.

The machine would wind down.

The world would not freeze.

After the last mechanical vibration ended, another sound would remain.

Water moving.

Sap rising.

Wood flexing.

Breath.

The Tappers would listen to the living hum and understand that the waiting had changed.

Not ended.

Changed.

Finally, the wise power would stay—but not as ruler.

It would not build a throne in the Rootbed. It would not demand worship. It would not name itself.

It would remain available in the forms it had chosen: a stabilizing resonance below catastrophe, a warmth in the pipe before a child touched it, a memory-light in the old pod, a question in the threshold floor.

Sometimes, when a Tapper slept beneath freezing condensation and woke under a thin shell of ice, they would find the ice patterned with impossible delicacy: branching geometries like roots, rivers, lungs, lightning, hands.

Sometimes the moths would gather where no lamp burned and circle an unseen kindness.

Sometimes a Rootbound elder, dreaming in century-long arcs, would feel the presence pass through the trees like a thought through bone.

And always the question would remain.

Not pressing.

Not impatient.

**What do you choose to become?**

That would be the gift.

Not warmth.

Not escape.

Not correction.

The gift would be choice restored to a place that had mistaken endurance for destiny.

The Rootbed would continue: dim, cold, vast, breathing beneath the permafrost. The strut-trees would rise. The frost would calve. The condensation would fall in fat drops and freeze before striking the floor. The Tappers would still walk slowly between columns, still greet one another across days, still read the world through sternum and palm and tooth.

But pain would no longer be the primary language of maintenance.

Forgetting would no longer be required for survival.

Becoming would no longer mean surrender.

And deep under the living vault, where once the compressors had filled all thought, the Rootbed would learn a second kind of patience:

not the patience of abandonment,

but the patience of being accompanied.
