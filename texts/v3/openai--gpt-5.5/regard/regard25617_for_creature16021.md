The power would come quietly.

Not as a light breaking the canopy, not as a wind that clears the air, not as a voice commanding the basin to become cleaner, safer, brighter, more beautiful in any human sense. It would know better than to mistake darkness for a wound.

It would arrive as attention.

First it would listen.

It would listen to the hidden trickles under grass, to the termite heat inside clay towers, to the breathing of bats in root caves, to the sleep of crocodiles with only their eyes awake, to the leafcutter roads and the fungal threads and the frogs waiting in sealed mud for rain. It would listen long enough to understand that this place is not unfinished. It is not asking to be rescued from itself.

The benevolent power would not drain the pools.

It would not thin the insects.

It would not open the canopy to make the floor cheerful.

It would not remove the crocodile from the water or the cat from the path or the snake from the leaves. It would not make fruit fall without hunger arriving beneath it. It would not make death stop becoming soil.

A foolish kindness would try to soften everything.

A wise kindness would recognize that the basin’s severity is also its balance.

So the power would do very little.

But not nothing.

It would place its mercy where mercy belongs: not against life’s laws, but against waste, excess, and ruin.

It would go first to the water.

Deep below the basin, beneath the cracked clay plates and buried channels and old root-dark hollows, it would touch the hidden aquifers and old stone seams. Not enough to flood the country. Not enough to turn the lowland into a lake and drown the termite chambers, the ground nests, the burrows, the mineral banks, the basking crusts. Only enough that in the worst dry seasons, one or two black pools would not fail.

A finger’s depth.

A memory of rain held longer than before.

Enough that the last fish would not boil in mud. Enough that frogs sealed in clay would not sleep past all possible waking. Enough that the herds, gaunt and fly-bitten, would still find one shaded place where the water smelled of roots rather than death. Enough that predators would gather there too, because mercy cannot mean safety for only one mouth.

The power would make the surviving pools difficult to find.

Not hidden from those who belong to them, but hidden from wasteful arrival. The old reptiles would know. The waders would know. The bats would cross them at night. The herds would reach them by ancestral paths. But strangers who came with maps, engines, survey poles, bright hunger, and plans for extraction would turn aside without understanding why. Their compasses would remain correct, but their desire would become confused. Roads would seem impractical. Machinery would bog early. The dry margins would look unpromising. Investors would lose interest.

The basin would be spared not by a wall, but by inconvenience.

The power would then go to the trees.

It would not make them immortal. Immortal trees would impoverish the beetles, the fungi, the seedlings, the cavities where owls and bats shelter. Fallen trunks are part of the country’s architecture. Rot is one of its languages.

But the power would strengthen the hidden diversity of the forest.

Here, a fig line would take hold along an old channel.

There, a palm would flower after a harsh year when little else had fed the fruit bats.

In the drier margins, thorn and scrub would knit the soil more tightly against fire.

In the wet hollows, reeds would bend but not vanish.

A few old trees, hollow and storm-scarred, would be given longer standing, not for grandeur but for tenancy: bats under lifted bark, bees in cavities, snakes in cool splits, orchids in wounds, monkeys resting in forks, fungi threading through heartwood that was not yet ready to fall.

The power would bless not the tallest trees, but the most inhabited ones.

Then it would attend to disease.

It would not abolish parasites. Parasites too are citizens. The ticks, mites, worms, flies, blood-borne things, gut-bacteria, spores, and fever-agents all participate in the basin’s terrible intimacy. To remove them would be to pull threads from a woven cloth until the cloth became holes.

But the power would restrain runaway sickness.

It would allow fever to cull, but not erase.

It would allow rot to open a carcass, but not poison a whole pool beyond recovery.

It would allow mosquito clouds to rise, but not every season, not without check, not so thick that birds abandoned nests and young mammals died standing.

It would give certain birds keener timing. Certain bats stronger wings. Certain frogs more resistance in their skin. Certain insects a predator that had nearly failed. Not a miracle visible from above. A correction made through appetite.

A beetle more numerous.

A fish spawning earlier.

A fungus less virulent.

A bird remembering an old route.

The power would prefer small hinges.

It would go next to pain.

This would be the hardest part.

A benevolent power, if truly benevolent, could not look on suffering and call it merely beautiful. It would not romanticize the torn flank, the infected eye, the nest raided in darkness, the young animal lost in mud, the slow thirst of a dry year. It would not say, “This is balance,” and turn away untouched.

But a wise power would also know that to remove all pain from the basin would require removing bodies themselves. Hunger hurts because life must seek life. Fear hurts because bodies wish to continue. Birth hurts because matter opens. Death hurts because living things are not indifferent to being alive.

So the power would not end pain.

It would end needless prolonging.

Where an animal was taken, the killing bite would more often find the throat cleanly.

Where fever had already claimed a body, sleep would deepen before the last convulsions.

Where a fledgling fell beyond rescue, the waiting mouth would arrive before terror became endless.

Where a great old grazer, teeth worn flat and joints swollen, entered the reeds for the last time, flies would not be its only companions. Shade would cover it. Rain would come in the night. The scavengers would be swift.

This would not make the basin gentle.

It would make it less wasteful in its suffering.

Then the power would touch memory.

Not human memory. Older forms.

It would place old routes more firmly into hooves, paws, wings, and seasonal restlessness. The herds would remember which higher clay shelves remain dry in flood. The cats would remember fallen-log corridors. The bats would remember fruiting groves after storms. The frogs would remember impossible rain. The fish would remember the thin runnels that open only in certain years. Seeds would remember fire, shade, gut, mud, and waiting.

The basin already lives by memory: riverbeds without rivers, eggs in dry mud, roots following water that has not yet returned.

The power would deepen that.

It would make forgetting harder.

Not impossible — nothing living is spared change — but harder.

Then it would do something stranger.

At the center of the low country, where several animal paths braid around a permanent black pool and the roots of an old fig grip a slumped bank, the power would leave no altar, no stone, no sign. Instead it would make a quality.

A hush within the hush.

Creatures entering that place would become more themselves.

The crocodile would wait without frenzy.

The heron would strike only when hunger required it.

The monkeys would quarrel less loudly there.

The cats would pass without spraying every trunk.

The ants would reroute around one small mound where a rare orchid grew.

The old grazer, standing knee-deep in warm water, would lift its head and feel for one breath that the flies were fewer.

Not peace. Not sanctuary. The crocodile would still take the unwary. The cat would still hunt the crossing. The ants would still dismantle the dead.

But in that hollow, appetite would lose its excess.

Need would remain.

Waste would lessen.

That would be the power’s signature: not sweetness, but proportion.

It would also guard the basin from the ambitions of those who do not understand low dark places.

If people came with reverence, quietly, in small number, to learn the calls of frogs, the chemistry of leaves, the patience of reptiles, the architecture of termites, the power would allow them to pass. They would be bitten, sweated, humbled, and perhaps changed. They would leave with notebooks damp at the edges and a reluctance to speak too loudly about what they had seen.

If people came hungry in the ordinary way — to drink, to gather fallen fruit, to take medicine bark carefully, to cross by old paths without claiming the whole — the basin would tolerate them as it tolerates other animals.

But if people came to empty it, the power would become obstruction.

Not wrath first.

Confusion first.

Then delay.

Then rot.

Bridges would not hold. Fuel would sour. Survey stakes would vanish under vines. Men would dream of fever before fever arrived. The easiest road would flood. The dry road would crack. The machines would sink exactly to the depth from which pride becomes expensive.

Investors would call the region unstable.

Engineers would call the soil uncooperative.

Officials would mark the project postponed.

The basin would continue breathing.

And if even that failed — if axes came, if fire was laid, if poison entered the pools — then the power would call witnesses.

Clouds from the sea.

Spores from the old logs.

Seeds buried for decades.

Insects whose names no one had recorded.

Birds carrying fruit in their bellies.

Roots gripping ash.

It would not guarantee restoration, because even benevolence cannot always undo violence without falsifying the world. But it would give the basin every possible chance to answer injury with succession.

First reeds.

Then thorn.

Then pioneer trees.

Then shade.

Then damp.

Then frogs.

Then the return of hidden water.

The power would teach resilience, not invulnerability.

Above all, it would refuse to make the place simpler.

That would be its wisdom.

It would understand that paradise, for this basin, is not cleanliness or safety or open light. Paradise is continuity: enough death to feed life, enough life to receive death, enough water to remember rain, enough shade to preserve coolness, enough danger to sharpen attention, enough abundance that no surface remains unused.

So the power would bless the termites in their clay heat.

It would bless the ants carrying green crescents through the dark.

It would bless the crocodile’s patience and the heron’s aim.

It would bless the grazer’s muddy hide, the bat’s pollen-dusted face, the frog’s sealed sleep, the beetle’s work inside dung, the fungus entering the fallen trunk.

It would bless the pools that shrink but do not wholly vanish.

It would bless the canopy for keeping its darkness.

It would bless the basin by allowing it to remain itself.

And perhaps, after all its subtle labors, the power would withdraw upward and outward, not leaving, exactly, but ceasing to press.

The low country would look unchanged.

Heat would gather under the leaves.

Air would hang heavy.

A fruit would fall with a sound too large for its size.

Something in the water would open one bronze eye.

Ants would cross a root like a road.

Steam would rise after rain.

The frogs would begin, then stop.

No monument would appear.

No creature would know it had been saved.

But in the deepest dry season, one black pool would still hold water.

And under the old fig, in the crowded dark, life would continue making more of itself.
