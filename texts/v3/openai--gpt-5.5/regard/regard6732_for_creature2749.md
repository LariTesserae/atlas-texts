It would not silence the hollow.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, moved by pity or offended by disorder, might try to make the place safe. It might soften the stone, comb the water into channels, roof the shelters properly, draw the pale thing from the plunge and name it, drain the basin until it became a pond with reeds and a path around it.

A wise power would know that this would be a kind of killing.

The hollow is not suffering because it is loud. It is not wrong because it is unfinished. It is young, and youth in stone is violence. It is becoming by impact, by repetition, by mistakes. To still it would be to deny it its own nature.

So the power would come quietly, if such a thing can be said of a presence vast enough to notice mountains.

It would not descend as a figure. No robed brightness would stand on the rim. No hand would part the falling water. The keepers, if any still climbed there, would see no face in the spray. The black birds would not cry out. The pale coil below would not be dragged upward into revelation.

Instead, one morning, during a clear pause, the droplets would hang a little longer than usual.

Not frozen. Not stopped.

Only delayed.

Thirty heartbeats would become forty. Forty would become fifty.

In that small extension, the power would look.

It would look without hunger.

It would look at the raw walls and see the pressure that birthed them. It would look at the clay seams and know which would fail. It would look at the shelters and understand the hands that bent the timber, the mouths that did not speak, the old bargains made with cold and water. It would look at the translucent stems and recognize their listening. It would look into the plunge and see not monster, not omen, but a form of life or near-life whose privacy was part of its being.

It would look at the hollow as one looks at a sleeping child who dreams violently and kicks free of its blankets.

Then it would decide to do very little.

But not nothing.

First, it would place a gentleness into the edges of disaster.

Not enough to make the hollow safe. Safety does not belong there. But enough that death would not come wastefully.

A ledge that would have broken under the next keeper’s foot would break the night before instead, falling loudly into the basin while no one stood upon it. A rotten rung in the notched ladder would darken with a stain shaped like a warning hand. The shell-ropes would twist eastward half a day earlier before the worst hums, giving those who knew the signs time to leave.

The water would still take what it was owed.

But it would take less by accident.

Second, the power would guard the secrecy of the place.

Not absolutely. Secrets that never breathe become tombs. But the hollow could not survive being found by the curious in numbers: climbers with bright tools, collectors of returned stones, scholars who would chip at the carvings, rulers who would declare the plunge sacred or useful or forbidden in a way that means possessed.

So the approach would become slightly more difficult to remember.

Those who came with reverence, fear, need, or inheritance would still find the split between the new stone walls. Their feet would turn correctly among the slabs. The stunted shrubs would seem to lean aside.

Those who came to own, measure, harvest, boast, or prove would find only scree, cloud, and ordinary snow in a cold seam. They would climb past it or below it. Their maps would blur at the notch. Their instruments would disagree. They would return saying there was nothing there but weather.

This too would be mercy.

Third, it would speak to the remaining keepers, if any remained.

Not in words, because the hollow does not favor words. Not as commandment, because wise powers do not confuse obedience with understanding.

It would enter their dreams as vibration.

An old woman sleeping below among black pines would dream that her teeth rang like shells. She would wake with tears on her face and know that the upper basin had changed its breathing. A child not yet shown the handholds would dream of a cup filling and spilling, filling and spilling, and would tell no one until the grandmother asked.

A man who intended to climb alone in anger would dream of his own knife returning from the plunge with its handle grown into pale shoots. He would wake ashamed and wait three days.

The power would not give them new laws. It would restore the meaning of the old ones.

Do not speak important names into the roar.  
Do not sleep below the shell-ropes when they twist eastward.  
Do not take a stone that has come back from the deep.  
Do not fish in the central pool during the clear pauses.  
Do not repair a shelter on the day the water tastes sweet.  
Leave by the upper crack if the basin begins to hum.

And perhaps one new rule would come, not carved but known:

Do not ask the hollow to become what it is not.

Fourth, it would tend to the small lives.

The moss would not be made lush. The lichens would not become gardens. But in three cracks where spray struck too hard for anything to hold, the angle of falling would alter by the width of a feather. A few dark green mats would survive the season. The thread-grasses would find purchase. The translucent stems would grow one more cluster in the protected recess.

The white insects would be given no abundance. Abundance would draw too many birds, too many mouths, too much change. But their egg-threads beneath the stones would cling better during the thick rains. Their generations would continue, brief and pale, writing their small lives on the skin of water.

The black birds would find, on the upper rim, one narrow shelf that did not glaze with treacherous ice. They would not know why their feet held there. They would simply return, year after year, carrying the hollow in their blood as a place of food, danger, and silence.

Fifth, it would approach the pale thing in the plunge.

This would be the most delicate act.

The power would not shine light down into the deep. It would not summon the creature upward. It would not complete it, if it was unfinished, nor reduce it, if it was ancient.

Instead, during one clear pause longer than any before, the falling water would thin until the central surface became like dark glass beaten only at the edges. The pale coil below would rise, not because it was called, but because for the first time the pressure above it did not crush every inquiry.

A length of it would show.

Jointed, milk-bone, smooth, seamed. Along its sides the small dark slits would open and close. It would turn without eyes toward the presence that had entered the hollow.

The wise power would offer it no name.

Names are hooks. Names are doors that open only one way. Names make creatures easier to speak of and therefore easier to use.

Instead, the power would give it recognition without capture.

You are here, the recognition would say.  
You are not a mistake merely because no one understands you.  
Remain hidden if hiddenness is your shape.  
Rise if rising becomes necessary.  
No hand shall drag you into the daylight for wonder.

The pale thing would sink again.

Afterward, stones cast into the plunge would return less often. When they did return, their scratches would be finer, almost script-like, but no clearer.

Sixth, the power would bless the shelters by refusing to preserve them forever.

Timber must rot. Hide must loosen. Lashings must fail. To make the three structures immortal would turn them into monuments, and monuments attract the wrong kind of devotion.

But the next time a keeper climbed with new sinew, she would find the old knots easier to understand. The frames would teach repair. A bent beam would hold long enough for replacement. A slate tile would fall where it could be retrieved, not into the throat. The shelters would remain what they had always been: temporary agreements with force.

In the alcove, where the red pigment bled downward from the old marks, the power would do one visible thing.

Only one.

During a night of heavy fall, when no human eye watched, a small patch of stone beside the repeated cup-mouth symbol would dry.

Not fully. Not naturally. Just enough.

In the morning, if a keeper came, there would be space for a new mark.

No instruction. No image burned by divinity. No prophecy.

Only room.

Because the place is still beginning, and those who love it must be allowed to answer.

Seventh, the power would set a limit beneath the mountain.

The hollow’s becoming could not be prevented, but some futures are needless cruelties. The whole basin bursting at once and scouring the villages below would not teach the mountain wisdom; it would merely spend lives. The deep throat collapsing in a single hour, sealing the pale creature and the under-chambers in pressure and dark, would be an ending too abrupt for something still learning its own form.

So far below, where no root reached and no carving had ever named the stone, the power would touch one seam.

Not close it. Not open it.

Strengthen it enough that change would come in stages.

The hollow might one day become a waterfall visible for miles. It might freeze into a blue shaft. It might drain and leave a dry amphitheater of raw stone and abandoned frames. Those futures would remain. But they would arrive with omens: a season of sweeter water, a migration of birds away from the rim, a new warmth in returned stones, a hum lasting not minutes but days.

The keepers would have time to carry down what must be carried.

The moss would have time to seed lower cracks.

The pale thing would have time to withdraw deeper, or rise, or divide, or die according to its own law.

Finally, having done these few things, the power would withdraw from the center of attention.

Not abandon. Withdraw.

A benevolent power that stays too near becomes weather of its own. Creatures begin to bend toward it. People build around its footprint. Fear becomes worship, worship becomes rule, rule becomes punishment. The hollow already has enough force.

So the power would leave no throne in the spray.

Only slight mercies folded into the existing violence.

A warning rung.  
A hidden path for the reverent.  
A longer pause when seeing is needed.  
A dream in the bones of the old.  
A ledge for birds.  
A seam held deep below.  
A dry place for one more mark.

Years later, perhaps, a child would climb with an elder through the tilted slabs and coarse grass. The elder would say nothing at the entrance, because the first lesson of the hollow is that speech is small. They would pass between the raw stone walls into the upright ocean. Sound would become enormous. The child would flinch, then laugh, then stop laughing because the place was too large for laughter.

The elder would show the shell-ropes, the alcove, the marks, the safe ledges, the unsafe ones, the plants that listen, the stones that must not be taken.

During a clear pause, the child would see the basin open dark and green below the torn white surface. Something pale would move there and vanish.

The child would reach for the elder’s hand.

The elder would squeeze once.

Not to say do not fear.

Fear is proper there.

The squeeze would mean: See. Remember. Do not ask it to be less than it is.

And above them, unseen, the wise power would do nothing more than attend.

Because some places are healed by being changed.

Some are healed by being protected from change.

And some are healed only by being allowed to become themselves, while mercy stands nearby and keeps the worst accidents from calling themselves fate.
