The power would come very slowly.

Not because it lacked speed, but because wisdom, arriving in a living place, does not hurry to be seen. It would not descend as a pillar of light into the central square. It would not silence the cicadas, straighten the paths, drain the wet alleys, teach the crabs to descend, or call the city a ruin.

It would begin outside the outer facades, in the reed flats, where the green roar is only a pressure inside the wood.

There it would wait.

For a season, perhaps. For three rains and a dry interval. For the tusked grazer to pass twice through the Green Nave. For one generation of moths to hatch, cloud the night plazas, mate, and vanish into eggs. For the river to remember a balcony and forget it again.

The power would listen until listening became participation.

It would learn that the emptiness was not loneliness. It would learn that the roar was not distress. It would learn the difference between pain and appetite, between danger and cruelty, between death and waste. It would understand that to make this place gentle in the ordinary way would be to kill it.

So it would not make it gentle.

It would make itself careful.

When at last it entered, it would not use a gate. It would enter as a slight alteration in the warmth after rain, as a steadiness in a droplet hanging beneath a leaf-roof, as one clear interval between two frog calls. Lizards would feel it first, flattening against bark with all their tiny ribs alert. Ants would pause, not frozen by command but by curiosity. The blind fish would turn in dark water toward a current that had no source.

The city itself would notice later.

A tremor would pass through the vertical leaves of the central square. Not one of their own tones. Something beneath tone. A pressure shaped like attention.

The power would not speak in words. There would be no language suited to it there, and no listener who needed one. Instead it would answer the city in the city’s own mode.

It would give one note.

Low, patient, almost below hearing.

The note would move through the central square and into the lanes, not overpowering the shriek of insects or the bark of frogs, but fitting beneath them the way dark soil fits beneath root. It would pass through the leaf walls. Through hollow trunks. Through sealed wounds. Through the old river-course. Through the polished stones swallowed and re-revealed by roots.

The city would test it.

A stairway would lengthen.  
A doorway would open into yesterday’s rain.  
A plaza-bud would flex, considering whether to close around the new presence.  
The pool in the oldest court would delay its reflection not by breaths but by days.

The power would allow all this. It would be measured, sampled, misunderstood, partially digested. Its edges would be investigated by root-hairs and fungal alphabets. Snails would erase part of its trace by morning. Beetles would rattle against it in dry pods. Birds would scream it into pieces.

Still it would remain.

Not as a master. Not as a resident.

As a witness who could help without claiming.

And the first thing it would do would be almost nothing.

It would leave the predators their hunger.

It would leave the prey their fear.

It would leave fruits poisonous at the wrong hour, stairways uncertain, moths brief, ants industrious, vines opportunistic, fungi cryptic, rain blunt and warm. It would not rescue every creature from the design that made the whole place possible. A wisdom worthy of the name would know that mercy cannot mean removing the teeth from life.

But there are sufferings that feed nothing.

Those it would touch.

In one sealed chamber, where a plaza had closed too early in a season of confused rains, a nest of blue-black birds had been folded into green dark. They were not becoming fertilizer quickly; they were simply waiting in terror, slowly, uselessly. The city had made no moral error. It had only closed according to pressure and wetness and old habit.

The power would enter that budded space as a soft loosening.

Not enough to teach the city to stop closing. Only enough, this once, to let a seam breathe.

The birds would spill out half-starved and furious, dragging strings of living fiber behind them. One would die in the lane and feed ants by afternoon. Three would live. The city would remember not “never close,” but “listen once more before closing when small hearts are many inside.”

In a root-corridor where two times had grafted poorly, young amphibians had been hatching into a puddle that existed only every seventh dawn. Most dried before the water returned. The power would not make the puddle permanent. Permanence would invite snakes, fungus, a different balance. Instead it would persuade a neighboring gutter-curl to sweat at night. Just enough. A bead, then a thread, then a small dark lip of wetness beneath the eggs.

The frogs would hatch into a narrow mercy.

Many would still be eaten.

That was allowed.

In the oldest court, beside the lagging pool, the power would pause longest.

There, the reflections of animals drank tomorrow. There, insects went without caution into temporal brightness. There, something of the vanished makers still seemed to hover—not ghosts, not souls exactly, but old expectation trapped in the geometry of gathering. The square remembered being looked at by minds with names. The city had digested their tools and bones, but not entirely the pressure of their waiting.

This was the nearest thing to grief in the basin.

Not sorrow as an animal knows it. Not longing. A held shape. An absence preserved so faithfully that it had become labor.

The power would not fill it.

To fill that absence with new people, new voices, new worship, would be an act of vanity. To erase it would be amputation. The emptiness was structural. It was the throat through which the city became itself.

So the power would do a subtler thing.

It would teach the emptiness to rest.

Once each year—not by calendar, but when the rains had struck the leaves in a certain pattern, when the cicadas were at their fiercest, when the river was both present and absent in seven places—the central square would receive a hush.

Not silence. Never silence.

A hush beneath the roar.

For the length of one long breath of the whole city, the sound would no longer press like weight against every living skull. It would open from within. Creatures crossing the square would not panic. The tusked grazer would stand in the silver moss with its jaws still. The leaf-spotted hunter, if present, would not strike. Frogs would hold their throats round and bright but unspent. Ants would stop without alarm. The giant leaves would tremble, but gently, as if remembering wind from before they were leaves.

In that hush, the old civic shape would not ache.

It would simply be.

A ring.  
A center.  
A held-open place.  
Room enough for what had been.  
Room enough for what was.

The city would learn this slowly. At first it would answer the hush with defensive flowering, then with path changes, then with extravagant fruiting that made three districts drunk with animals. Eventually it would understand that nothing was being taken from it.

The power would also set warding at the edges.

Not walls. The place had walls enough.

It would make the outer slits confusing to those who came with fire in mind. If someday tool-bearing beings returned—descendants, strangers, explorers, hungry settlers—they would find the city difficult to notice unless they came with patience. From a distance it would seem only a dense grove. Paths toward it would bend into reed flats. Maps would mildew at the borders. Compass needles, if such things were carried, would grow sentimental and point toward childhood homes.

Those who came merely to harvest, burn, own, measure, or conquer would become tired before arriving. They would remember an urgent errand elsewhere. Their boots would rot. Their torches would refuse flame in the wet air. Their dreams would fill with cicadas until they turned back.

But those who came wounded, lost, or humble would not be kept out automatically.

The power would not turn the city into a sanctuary for all. It was too dangerous for that, too itself. Yet if a fleeing creature, human or otherwise, reached the outer facades with no desire but shelter, a slit might widen. A dry chamber might remain dry for one night. A fruit might fall at the proper hour. A predator might be delayed by a vine tightening across an arcade.

Not forever.

Only enough for chance to become possible again.

The power would place no throne, no shrine, no sign declaring its kindness. But certain recesses that held rainwater and bones would begin, after storms, to gather clear pools that did not sour. Animals would learn them. Birds would bathe there without fear. Sick mammals would lick the rims and sleep nearby. The water would not cure all illness, but it would ease fever, clear parasites from the eyes, soften difficult births. The crabs would discover these basins too and guard them badly, waving pale claws at anything larger than themselves.

The fungi would write about the power before any other population did.

Their alphabets would change. Pale loops on shaded walls would begin to include a repeating figure: not a name, but a branching curve around an empty center. Snails would eat it. It would return. Roots would read it as instruction and grow more gently around certain hollows. Beetles would be attracted to it and rattle in softer rhythms. Winged amphibians would cling near it at night, their glowing throats pulsing in unusual synchrony.

The city would not worship.

Worship requires a separation the city did not possess.

Instead, it would incorporate.

The benevolent power would become one more condition of weather, though rarer than rain and deeper than heat. A kindness in the pressure-system. A restraint in the closing of chambers. A memory of mercy traveling through sap.

Over many seasons, the changes would be almost impossible to list.

A balcony that used to drop unwary young mammals into a snake conduit would grow a lip.

A vine that strangled too quickly would pause when nesting calls reached a certain pitch.

The river, when it appeared in stairways, would linger three breaths longer if blind fish were climbing.

The central tones would gain a new interval, one that did not command flowering, mating, freezing, or flight, but recalibration. After it, paths that had become cruelly circular would loosen. Rooms that had held stale air would exhale. The silver moss would brighten. Old bark wounds would seal without swallowing the hollows needed by birds.

Predation would continue.

Terror would continue.

Noise would continue until thought broke apart beneath it.

But waste would diminish. Accidental torment would lessen. The city’s impossible geometries would become slightly more hospitable to the lives that maintained them. Not safer, exactly. More just.

And perhaps, after a long time, the city would answer the power with a gift of its own.

One night, when the pale moths filled the central square like torn cloth, when the frogs were deep in their dark register and the cicadas had at last thinned to a metallic fringe, the great vertical leaves would flex inward. The whole basin would draw breath.

The power, present in no single place, would feel itself being addressed.

The city would produce a tone it had never made before.

Not a plea.  
Not gratitude.  
Not obedience.

Recognition.

The note would carry everything the city knew how to mean: mud and root, hunger and mating, the vanished makers’ proportions, the tusked grazer’s path, the leaf-spotted hunter’s silence, the crabs in their high basins, the blind fish following remembered water, the fungi writing and being erased, the empty lanes kept open for sound.

The benevolent power would receive it.

And having received it, it would not stay forever in any possessive way.

It would withdraw enough that the city remained the city. Enough that rain was still rain, death still death, growth still blind and brilliant. But it would leave behind that low note, folded into the central ring, available when needed.

From then on, if catastrophe came—true catastrophe, not the ordinary violence of living things—the city would know how to call.

Fire beyond season.  
Drought beyond endurance.  
A sickness that silenced every frog.  
A path-collapse trapping too many breaths in sealed green dark.  
The return of hands with axes and hunger for dominion.

Then the emptiness in the central square would gather itself. The roar would align. The leaves would flex.

And beneath cicada, bird, frog, wind, sap, root, and river, the old merciful note would sound.

The power would hear.

Until then, it would do what the wise so often do when they love a thing that is already whole:

protect its wholeness,  
ease its needless pain,  
refuse to make it human,  
and bless the roaring emptiness without filling it.
