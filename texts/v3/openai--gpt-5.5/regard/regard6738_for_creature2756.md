The power would not descend as warmth.

That would be the first mercy.

It would not pour soil into the basin, or lay green over the stone, or soften the granite walls with moss. It would not summon birds to make the silence hospitable, nor bring animals to prove the place had been blessed. It would not fill every cup with water, not extend the thaw through noon, not spare every flake from breaking when frost entered it.

A lesser kindness might try to make the basin easier.

A wiser one would understand that ease is not always rescue.

So the power would arrive, if arrival is the word, without weather.

No thunderhead would crown the peaks. No visible hand would pass over the stone ring. No golden figure would stand between the horn-shaped summits casting a human shadow across the gravel. The basin would not be asked to become a temple. It had survived long enough by refusing grand gestures.

The power would come as attention.

It would enter first through the sharpness of the light, not changing it, only knowing it completely. It would know the eastern wall by the order in which its fractures warmed. It would know which shale towers would last three more winters and which would collapse before the next moonless night. It would know the stone cups by taste: which held clear melt, which held bitterness, which held the transparent resident curled so thinly against existence that even a name would be too heavy for it.

It would pause at the rim.

This would matter.

A benevolent power, if it were also wise, would not assume that noticing gave it permission. It would not mistake smallness for helplessness. It would not confuse silence with consent.

So it would wait where the cloud sometimes waits, pressed below the lip of the ridge, looking in without entering.

And the basin, which measured all things by disturbance, would measure this too.

No added weight.

No heat.

No new shadow.

No command.

Only attention, held steady enough to become a kind of listening.

The basin would continue its work.

A bead of condensation would swell beneath a ledge and not yet fall. A cup of ice would craze at the edge. In the dust behind a rust-colored stone, a transparent film would loosen from its dry fold and accept the brief possibility of water. The old notches on the carved boulder would darken as cloud brushed the peaks. A pebble high on the wall would shift, hesitate, and remain.

The power would learn the rules before it touched anything.

Do not pry.

Do not trust.

Listen first.

Leave weight where weight already is.

It would recognize them not as prohibitions, but as the basin’s form of wisdom. The old visitors had known a little of this. The inhabitant knew it without knowing. The stones enacted it. Survival here was not conquest, but proportion.

So if the power acted, its action would be proportionate.

It would not save every stone.

But once, when a standing slab above the old shelter loosened in such a way that its fall would crush the last pale cords and scatter the small human answer to cold, the power would enter the crack as night entered it: invisibly, by cooling. It would let the ice form a fraction thicker on one side than the other. It would borrow no more force than a hair has when it bends under frost.

By morning the slab would still fall.

But not inward.

It would settle beside the shelter instead of through it, becoming another piece of the windbreak, another weight where weight already was.

The basin would accept this because it would look like accident.

And perhaps it would be accident.

A wise power would not need to distinguish too sharply between miracle and weather, provided nothing was violated.

Another time, during a long season of dry, when cloudbanks rose but failed to spill over the ridge, when the transparent inhabitant retreated deeper into mineral sleep and even the dawn beads came rarely, the power would not bring rain. Rain would be too much. Rain would scour the dust crescents, flood the cups, loosen the patterned gravel, wake what could not remain awake.

Instead it would delay one wind.

Only one.

A small afternoon wind, coming from the south with its usual dry hand, would lose its way among the lower ridges. It would spend itself against another slope. Because of that absence, the vapor gathered under the western horn would not be torn apart. It would rise slowly, heavily, and at evening it would cross the rim.

Cloudfall.

No storm. No abundance.

Only the familiar pale wool spilling into the basin, darkening the stones, slicking the carved boulder until the shallow marks appeared, black and sudden. Moisture would gather under ledges. Cups would film over. The dust behind stones would stiffen.

In the join, the resident would wake.

Not more than it should. Not into pain. Not into hunger beyond its scale.

It would swell through its distributed body and perform its faint exchanges: mineral, light, cold, water. A life that almost was not life would have its hour.

The power would feel joy then, but carefully.

It would not ask to be thanked.

Gratitude would be too mammalian a burden to place on such a being. The resident had no throat for praise, no face to turn upward, no before and after clear enough to understand intervention. Its answer would be chemical. Its answer would be persistence.

That would be enough.

The power would also attend to the traces of people.

Not because human marks mattered more than frost or shale, but because they were among the basin’s truths. The ring, the shelter, the volcanic glass, the shallow carvings—these were not ownership. They were negotiations. They were proof that beings of warmth had once climbed into a place not made for them and, for once, had asked little.

The power would honor that asking.

It would not restore the ring to completeness. The missing stones, whether fallen or deliberately absent, belonged to the ring’s meaning now. It would not sharpen the carvings or translate them. Weathered marks have their own dignity. To make them legible again would be to choose one story over the many held in their erosion.

But it might, on certain afternoons, arrange light.

Nothing impossible.

Only the angle of a cloud’s thinning. The brief withdrawal of glare. The darkening of moisture at the right moment.

If a careful traveler ever came again—not a conqueror, not a collector, not someone hungry to name and take, but one who climbed slowly and listened after sunrise—the marks would appear.

Parallel notches.

Cup hollows.

The crooked line following the vein.

The traveler would not understand them fully. That too would be mercy. Full understanding often becomes possession.

But they might understand enough to kneel before touching the stone.

Enough to drink only from the clear cups.

Enough to leave the scraper where it lay.

Enough to carry back down the mountain not an artifact, but a rule.

Listen first.

The power would do one more thing, perhaps the most difficult.

It would protect the basin from being loved wrongly.

For there are forms of admiration that arrive as damage. There are pilgrims who build too high, scientists who scrape too deeply, climbers who leave bright scraps in the name of triumph, mourners who import flowers, devotees who mistake desolation for a blank page.

The power would not forbid all approach. That would be another kind of ownership. But it would bend likelihood.

A map would blur at the fold where the ridge trail should be clear.

A cairn far below would fall in such a way that it pointed toward a safer valley.

A drone sent upward for footage would meet a harmless failure in its small electric heart and return early, seeing only sky.

Those who came seeking spectacle would find cloud.

Those who came seeking conquest would find unstable weather, a headache, a sudden certainty that the summit could wait.

Those who came with humility might still arrive.

Not many.

Enough.

The benevolent power would understand that holiness is sometimes maintained by inconvenience.

At night, it would keep no vigil above the basin like a star assigned to guard duty. That would be too theatrical. But it would know when the temperature crossed the narrow threshold at which the hidden films could survive without bursting. It would know when ice tightened too quickly in the cracks. It would know when a season’s pattern had become not hardship, but erasure.

And then, only then, it would lean.

A fraction.

It might hold the cold steady for three breaths longer before the killing drop. It might let snow grains sublimate rather than seal a cup too deeply. It might guide a single dusting of mineral powder into a seam where the resident needed shelter from light. It might permit one hairline crack to remain hairline through another winter.

Not preservation against change.

Preservation through change.

The basin would still break. Stones would still split. Towers would still collapse. The old shelter would one day become only an arrangement of fallen slabs. The carvings would fade until even moisture could no longer summon them. The transparent inhabitant would retreat and wake, retreat and wake, until some future alteration of climate, stone, and chance either ended it or transformed it beyond recognition.

The power would not prevent time.

Wisdom would forbid that.

But it would prevent insult.

It would prevent wasteful harm, harm without belonging, harm that came not from frost or gravity or the basin’s own grammar, but from carelessness, vanity, or excess. It would let the place remain severe without allowing it to be casually destroyed.

And perhaps, after long attention, the power would offer one gift directly to the being in the join.

Not warmth.

Not speech.

Not a body.

Not escape.

It would offer continuity of threshold.

A slightly truer seam between water and ice.

A recurring hour, not guaranteed but remembered by the world, when dawn condensation found the same hidden routes and the mineral films could open without being torn apart. A rhythm just stable enough for the resident to remain itself.

To a creature that lived by almost not living, this would be abundance.

To anything larger, it would look like nothing.

A wet gleam under a ledge.

A clear skin lasting a little longer before whitening.

A rust-orange stain deepening by one shade over centuries.

A pebble that should have fallen in the night waiting until morning, then striking three times into the talus.

The basin would not become kind.

It would remain exacting. It would remain cold, bright, and mostly silent. It would still refuse roots. It would still strip breath to frost and erase footsteps by the next freeze. It would still dismantle anything proud.

But somewhere in its grammar there would be a new permission, so slight it could never be carved.

Continue.

Not forever.

Not unchanged.

Only continue, if you can, in the manner that is yours.

And the being in the join would receive this without knowing it had received anything.

It would wake at cloudfall.

It would darken with the stone.

It would thread itself through cups, cracks, stains, dust, and hidden beads. It would measure warmth by loss, passage by disturbance, time by transition. It would live its thin hour and withdraw.

The power would watch, and learn again the ancient restraint of love:

to bless a thing without making it resemble you;

to save what can be saved without interrupting its nature;

to stand at the edge of a roofless room of stone and understand that even mercy must enter by the crack.
