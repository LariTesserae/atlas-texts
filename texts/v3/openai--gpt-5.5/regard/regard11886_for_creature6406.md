The benevolent power would not make rain.

That would be the first wisdom.

It would stand—or attend, or turn its vast, unbodied face—above the rim of Khe’len and understand immediately that mercy, if poured, can drown. To bring a river would be to murder the Present-Garden by kindness. To fill the plugged wells would be to crack the meanings carved on them. To soften the city with abundance would be to unmake the Ache-Mothers, whose bodies are not merely adapted to thirst but are part of the instrument by which thirst is made livable.

So the power would do almost nothing.

Almost.

It would listen first.

For one full turning of years, it would lower no hand. It would let the night heat rise. It would let the glass breathe and crackle. It would learn the difference between a healthy speaker’s hum and the brittle overtone of one near collapse. It would learn the hand-signs carved under balconies, the short hard words thrown across darkness, the way an old Ache-Mother pauses before cutting because her left shin remembers a map older than her grandmother.

It would learn that Khe’len is not poor.

It is exact.

Only after it understood this would it act.

Not by miracle, but by margin.

At the upper rim of the basin, where the plateau exhales heat into the black, it would smooth one imperceptible place in the stone. Not enough for anyone to notice by sight. A traveler could set a heel on it and feel nothing. But night air would feel it. The old downward fingers of coolness would find, there, a cleaner path. The current would descend a breath earlier than before.

Not much.

A little.

Enough that the first condensation would form while the youngest apprentices were still strong-handed, before exhaustion made their knives uncertain.

Then the power would turn to the dark silicate veins under the basin floor.

It would not halt the founders in their dissolving work. The Present must eat stone; the city must listen to that eating. But in three places where the hollows had grown too near the weight-bearing walls, where collapse waited only a generation away, the power would persuade the glass to knot upon itself. A slow tightening. A strengthening. A rib grown inside the stone.

The Ache-Mothers would discover it years later.

They would argue over it for nights.

Some would say the maps had been wrong. Some would say the Present had decided differently. One very old woman, whose femurs carried more history than anyone living, would place her palm against the floor and say nothing at all.

In her bones there would be a new ache.

Not pain.

Warning, softened into guidance.

The power would give no commandments. Commandments are too large for a place like Khe’len. They cast shadows. Instead it would add a new kind of pressure to the bodies of the Ache-Mothers: not a voice, not prophecy, but an inward hesitation at dangerous cuts. A tenderness in the wrist before excess. A small coldness behind the ribs when a speaker was too dry to open safely.

The Ache-Mothers would incorporate it into practice within three generations.

They would not call it divine.

They would call it another accuracy.

And because the power was benevolent, it would care for the children.

On nights when the heat rose wrongly, when the city’s low population thinned further because a fever moved from house to house, one speaker in the deepest basin would open without a knife. A seam would appear along its glass body, fine as a hairline crack in a mask. From it would come no flood, no visible gift—only a steadier coolness, a place where Istheth gathered in a film thick enough for the sick to lie beside it and live.

The first time this happened, the city-mothers would be afraid.

The second time, they would set a stone marker beside it.

The third time, they would begin keeping watch.

They would name it not Blessing, not Mouth, not Mercy. Those words would be too wet, too lush, too careless.

They would call it the Uncut.

No one would harvest from the Uncut except in need. That law would arise without debate, because everyone would understand. A gift that preserves precision must itself be treated precisely.

The benevolent power would also turn its attention to the masks.

Not to break them. Not to reveal faces. But to deepen the truth they already served.

A few grains of black glass in each new mask would begin to align differently under the maker’s hand. The lenses would show not only heat, but fatigue: the faint violet trembling around an overworked speaker, the dimming of a colleague whose body had lost too much moisture, the hidden bloom of stress in a wall about to shear.

This would not make the Ache-Mothers less necessary.

It would make their necessity less cruel.

They would still train. Still carve memory into bone. Still learn the basin by ache and pressure and inherited silence. But fewer apprentices would die because they mistook endurance for readiness. Fewer elders would work three cuts too many out of duty. Fewer houses would be lost to collapses everyone almost heard coming.

The power would save them mostly from “almost.”

That is often where wise mercy lives.

Not in abolishing suffering.

In narrowing the gap between knowledge and disaster.

Last, after many years, the power would leave one thing that was not useful.

This would be its most dangerous gift, and it would hesitate longest over it.

For Khe’len had no room for purposeless things. No redundant gestures. No decoration that did not double as map, tool, warning, shelter, memory. Even beauty there had work to do.

But life cannot live forever on function alone.

So, on one night hotter than any recorded in bone, when the city knelt in its bowl and the glass chorus rose so loud the stone houses seemed to hum, the power would touch the uppermost speakers of the Present-Garden.

Their openings would widen, just slightly.

The air moving through them would change pitch.

For a few breaths, the whole basin would sing.

Not hum. Not whistle. Not crackle.

Sing.

A chord would rise from the glass columns and strike the limestone terraces, and the city would answer with its own hollow body. Every house would become a chamber. Every mask would tremble against every hidden face. Every Ache-Mother would feel, written through her skeleton, not a map of erosion but a map of belonging.

The sound would have no instruction in it.

No warning.

No measurable increase in moisture.

No practical value at all.

Children would wake in their cool chambers and crawl to the doorways. Old women would stop mid-cut. Apprentices would forget, for one forbidden instant, to be exact.

The song would last less than a minute.

Then it would pass.

Afterward, no one would know what to do with it.

That, too, would be a gift.

The next year, one apprentice would carve a tiny line beneath her map marks—not part of any basin survey, not a cut-path, not a record of collapse or airflow. Just a curve to remember the sound. Her teacher would see it and almost tell her to remove it.

But the teacher’s wrist would ache.

Gently.

So she would let it remain.

Centuries later, Khe’len would still thirst. The Present-Garden would still breathe glass into the hot night. The Ache-Mothers would still descend in black robes and white masks with knives in their hands and maps in their bones. No river would come. No rain would fall. No god would stand revealed upon the rim.

But the city would be a little less near the edge of ruin.

Its mothers would be a little better warned.

Its sick would have one place to lie when the heat became merciless.

Its children would inherit, along with discipline, a useless remembered song.

And if anyone asked what the wise power had done for Khe’len, the truest answer would be:

It did not give them water.

It helped them go on drinking thirst.
