The power would come quietly.

Not as a blaze above the rim, not as a voice that overruled the water, not as a hand descending through vapor. A place like this could not be blessed by spectacle without being harmed by it. Its secrecy is not an accident; its smallness is part of its wholeness. To arrive loudly would be to make it answer a question it has never needed to hear.

So the power would come first as attention.

It would lower its vastness until it could notice without weighing down what it noticed: the bead at the fern-tip, the mineral taste of the seep, the breath-warm mud beneath the pool’s margin, the seven small lives folded into the basin’s wet grammar. It would listen the way the creatures listen, through pressure and interval. It would learn the slow rules before touching any of them.

And perhaps, for a long while, it would do nothing.

Not neglect. Reverence.

There are places and beings whose suffering demands an immediate hand. A snare cut. A fire turned. A poison drawn out. But this hollow is not crying out in pain. It is precarious, yes, because everything small and complete is precarious. A landslide could bury it. A dry decade could empty its seams. A person with curiosity and a jar could unmake a lineage. But it is not yet broken. It is not a wound asking to be closed. It is a sentence still being spoken correctly.

A wise power would know the difference.

So it would not improve the basin. It would not make the pool clearer, or the orchids brighter, or the little animals numerous beyond the place’s carrying. It would not straighten the trees, sweeten the mud, widen the hollow, deepen the water, silence the underground throat. It would not turn patience into abundance, because that would be another form of violence.

Instead it would make small adjustments at the edges of consequence.

It would begin below, where the water moves unseen.

Deep in the mountain, in the stone chambers and root tunnels that amplify the basin’s voice, it would place no object, speak no spell, carve no channel by force. It would merely encourage continuance. A grain of loosened mineral would settle where it should settle. A hairline crack that might have opened too suddenly would remain a hairline crack for another century. A buried flow, tempted by gravity toward another seam, would remember its old route by the width of a thread.

The water would still gather before it moved.

The pool would still rise and lower, never dramatically.

But in years of hard weather, when surrounding slopes browned and the rim grasses rasped dryly in the wind, the basin would retain one more shade of dampness than it otherwise might have kept. Not enough to make it lush beyond itself. Enough for the eggs under the weeping rock not to desiccate. Enough for the young in the liverwort felt to find a thin world still passable between pool and land. Enough for the adults to lie deep in mud and wait out the sky.

Then the power would turn to heat.

There is a buried warmth in the basin, gentle and persistent, and the beings who live there have built their blood around it. The wise power would not make the hollow tropical. It would not steam in winter or glow in frost. It would only steady the old inward breath.

A stone shelf under the moss would hold dusk-warmth a little longer.

A vein of dark rock would accept the day’s diffused sun and release it slowly into the muck.

On nights when cold slipped down from the height and tested the basin’s seams, the mud just below the surface would remember summer enough that the small bodies beneath leaves would not stiffen past return.

No one standing there would notice.

That would be the measure of its kindness.

Next, the power would consider danger from above.

Birds come rarely, and birds must eat. A benevolent power would not forbid hunger. The dark bird on the rim tree has its own pulse, its own need, its own place in the mountain’s thinking. To save one life by making another starve is not wisdom; it is preference wearing a crown.

So the bird would still come. It would cling sideways to bark. It would watch the moss with its bright, exact eye.

But when a breeding female lay swollen beneath a wet leaf, too full of eggs to sink quickly, a drop would fall from the overhanging rock at just the right time, striking another leaf, turning the bird’s glance aside.

When a young one crossed exposed mud for the first time, a wind pulse would comb the grasses flat and set the whole surface moving, confusing shape with shadow.

When an old male, slow with age and mottled nearly white along one flank, rested half out of the pool in a warm hour, an insect would rise near the bird’s beak, brief and catchable.

Not always.

Only sometimes.

Only enough that the basin’s seven might remain seven, or six and then eight, or five and then seven again. The rhythm would be preserved, not conquered.

Then the power would look toward human beings.

Here it would be most careful.

Humans are not an evil to be excluded from all sacred wetness. A person could kneel at that pool and understand something necessary. A person could see one of the little animals emerge and be made gentler for the rest of their life. Wonder, too, is part of the world’s repair.

But humans are numerous, and memory among them travels faster than water. A single discovery can become a trail. A trail can become a name. A name can become a destination. A destination can become absence.

The power would not curse the rim or terrify travelers. It would not create monsters or storms. It would do what the basin already does, only a little more truly.

From below, the hollow would remain invisible.

Maps would misread the shoulder by a harmless margin.

A person climbing with hunger, fear, or ambition would find the slope awkward there: the stones slick, the mist thick, the easiest way always seeming to bend a little aside.

Those seeking rare animals for possession would feel, without knowing why, that the basin lay elsewhere. They would follow a darker fold, a louder trickle, a false green promise. They would return with wet boots and no story worth repeating.

But the power would not bar everyone.

Once in a great while, someone would enter.

Not the collector.

Not the bored.

Not the one who thinks every hidden thing becomes more itself when named aloud.

Rather, a person carrying grief with no wish to use it. Or a child lost only briefly, frightened enough to be humble and observant enough to be changed. Or an old botanist who has spent a life learning how not to pluck what can be seen. Or a wanderer who sits without reaching.

For such a one, the basin would open exactly as it is.

The air would green.

The water would speak from all sides.

A wet leaf near the pool would tilt against the wind.

And perhaps one of the small beings would appear.

The person would not be given proof. No photograph would focus properly; vapor would bead the lens, or the hand would tremble, or the creature would already be indistinguishable from mud by the time the device was raised. If a sketch were made later, it would look improbable. If a description were spoken, listeners would hear mostly the weather.

But the person who saw would remember.

They would descend with something quieter than knowledge. They might protect another wetland. They might refuse a road in some committee room years later without fully knowing why. They might teach a child to turn over stones gently and replace them exactly. They might simply become less certain that small hidden lives are expendable.

That would be enough.

The power would then consider the creatures themselves.

It would not make them intelligent in the human way. It would not burden them with prophecy, language, fear of extinction, or gratitude. Their wisdom is already fitted to their bodies: belly-listening, patient feeding, the reading of seep and tremor. To give them larger minds might be to exile them from their own completeness.

So they would remain what they are.

Forearm-long.

Stone-colored.

Slow.

Almost impossible to distinguish from wet root and old moss.

But the power might give them one new mercy, so small it would look like instinct.

When the underground water rose to the breeding height and the adults came near one another under warm windless dark, they would find, more often than chance alone allowed, the right stone mouths for eggs. A female heavy with beads would turn her head once, twice, and choose the lip where drops fell gently, not the one that would flood too hard in three nights. A male lying near the pool’s rim would click low into stone, and the sound would carry through a seam that had been closed the year before but was open now by the width of a root.

The eggs would still be vulnerable.

Some would cloud and fail.

Some young would never find enough microscopic sweetness.

Some would be taken by beetle larvae, fungus, accident, the blunt arithmetic of life.

The power would not abolish loss. It would only keep loss from becoming the whole story.

It might also widen, very slightly, the basin’s future.

Not by making a path out.

The adults almost never leave, and should not be forced into distance. Beyond the rim the world is too dry, too exposed, too quick. But beneath the mountain, water has secret continuities. There may be another hollow somewhere along the high shoulder, smaller, colder, not yet ready. There may be a seam of seepage under moss where no adult could travel in open air but where, in one rare season of swollen underground flow, a young one no longer than a finger-joint might be carried in darkness.

The power would not push it.

It would prepare possibility.

A root would enter a crack and hold it open.

A mineral film would smooth a passage.

A season of rains, not catastrophic, not famous, would fill the hidden throat.

One larval creature, still more comma than body, might pass through the saturated dark and arrive elsewhere, in a second wetness no one has ever named.

Perhaps it would live.

Perhaps not.

The first basin would not know. Its water would continue. Its ferns would uncurl by finger-widths. Its seven creatures would lie beneath leaves, listening.

But the lineage would have been offered a second syllable.

After these things, the power would withdraw from doing and remain in witness.

Not gone. Not looming. Present as restraint.

It would let the basin endure its own days.

It would allow the moss to thicken where moss wished, and thin where the wettest stones refused it. It would allow the seep-lines at dusk to seem like a face changing expression in thought. It would allow the pool to dimple upward when a small mouth took an insect from below. It would allow the old to die into the mud that warmed them. It would allow the young to learn the difference between falling water and moving prey.

And when one of the creatures reached the end of its slow body’s time, the power would not lift it out of death.

The little animal would grow less eager to feed. Its limbs would press more lightly. Its gill-fringes would stir only when the air was very damp. It would choose, or seem to choose, a place under a bowed slab where water ticked steadily and the mud held heat. There it would become still.

Mites would find it.

Fungus would write pale threads along its flank.

Its speckles would vanish first, then its outline.

The basin would take it back without ceremony, and because the power was wise, it would understand that this, too, was not a failure of love.

Love is not always rescue.

Sometimes love is the preservation of conditions in which a life can be fully what it is.

Sometimes love is secrecy.

Sometimes love is a loosened stone not falling.

A human not arriving.

A drop striking one leaf instead of another.

A warm seam remaining warm through a bitter night.

A hidden passage opened once in three hundred years.

A creature with no name lowering itself backward into mud, unpraised and unharmed.

If the power blessed the basin, the blessing would not look like radiance.

It would look like morning after nothing obvious had changed.

A fern uncurled by a finger’s width.

A new bead of water finding a channel down stone.

A leaf collapsed into the mud.

A thread of root entering a crack.

The pool dark, warm, and unreadable.

The mountain swallowing slowly.

And beneath the wet leaves at the edge of the water, one small body listening through its belly, receiving the world exactly as it comes, while the world—quietly, wisely, almost invisibly—makes room for it to continue.
