### What the wise power would do

At first, nothing.

Not from indifference. From respect.

A lesser power, seeing that basin, might pity it too quickly. It might mistake sparseness for failure, silence for sadness, slowness for deprivation. It might pour rain into the flats, crack the sky open, summon grasses, soften the shells of the dark grazers, green the vent-mouths, quiet the roaring earth. It might try to make the place kind by making it into another place.

But a wise power would wait.

It would lower itself, not in body but in attention, until its understanding moved at the speed of condensation.

It would learn the basin’s grammar.

Steam rises.  
Stone drinks.  
Salt remembers.  
Heat returns.  
Life holds on where repetition becomes mercy.

The power would listen to the vents until it could tell one from another: the deep one that booms through the central troughs, the narrow one that whistles in the southern cones, the fissure whose tone changes three days before its vapor grows too hot for living film. It would learn which tremors guide the shell-dark grazers and which confuse them. It would learn which pale skins are old colonies and which are newly sealed wounds. It would learn the difference between a pool that trembles because life clings beneath it and a pool that trembles only because pressure passes through stone.

It would not ask the place to become lush.

It would not introduce forests.

It would not soften every hardship.

Hardship here is not an accident laid over life. It is the shape into which life has grown. Remove it carelessly, and the lives would not be freed. They would be unmade.

So the wise power would begin with restraint.

It would place no visible sign upon the basin. No pillar, no temple, no shining ring in the sky. The place has never needed symbols. Its holiness, if that word can be used, is in continuance.

But the power would not remain wholly passive.

Wisdom is not the same as distance. Benevolence is not the same as decoration.

It would make small corrections.

Not miracles in the dramatic sense.

Mercies.

It would deepen, by less than the thickness of a shell-seam, certain cracks in the pale western flats, so that bitter water lingered a little longer after condensation. Not enough to flood them. Not enough to alter the salt law. Only enough that a young grazer crossing too late might find one more mouthful where otherwise there would have been none.

It would smooth one treacherous lip of crust near an old nursery hollow, not visibly, but in the manner of erosion hastened by a thousand quiet years. Hatchlings, soft at the seams, would pass under it without being scraped open.

It would tune a vent.

Only one.

A deep vent beneath the central glass plates, whose resonance had begun to drift into a frequency that blinded the ground-sense of the larger creatures across too wide an area. The wise power would not silence it. The basin’s roar belongs to the basin. Instead it would shift an inner stone shelf, somewhere far below sight, so that the vent’s vibration moved back into an older chord.

The grazers would not know they had been helped.

They would simply find that the route their bodies half-remembered had become readable again.

That would be enough.

The power would touch the pale films more subtly still. It would not make them grow fast. Fast growth would be disaster in a place where replenishment is slow. It would not give them hunger beyond their means. It would not make them cover the stones in sheets of abundance.

Instead, once in many generations, when a colony at the bottom of a vent nearly failed because the chemistry of seepage had shifted too sharply, the power would encourage one variation already possible within it: a fold of membrane able to bind a harsher metal, a patient change in the way it tightened under steam. Not invention from outside. Permission from within.

The colony would survive.

Not triumph.  
Survive.

In that basin, survival is already an astonishment.

The wise power would also protect the place from attention of the wrong kind.

Not all attention is benevolent.

There are minds that see heat and think extraction. See minerals and think inventory. See rare life and think specimen. See emptiness and think ownership.

The power would not build walls. Walls invite questions. It would do something quieter.

Maps would blur near the basin. Instruments would return ambiguous readings. Distant surveys would classify the region as unpromising, unstable, chemically inconvenient. Travelers with conquest in their hearts would feel no romance there, only irritation, thirst, and the sense that more profitable lands lay elsewhere.

Those who came with reverence might still find it.

Those who came to listen might still hear.

But those who came to take would be turned aside by boredom, error, weather, expense, or their own impatience.

This too would be mercy.

Not only for the basin.

For them.

There are places a soul should not learn to damage.

As for the shell-dark beings, the power would not make them tame. It would not give them voices because the basin has not asked for voices. It would not gather them into herds or warm families or quick societies. Their dignity is not human dignity in disguise. Their loneliness, if loneliness it is, does not need to be cured by noise.

But once, perhaps, the power would attend to one of them in particular.

An old grazer, larger than most, its shell ridged with long mineral history, would be crossing the eastern terraces after a long dry interval. It would know the route through its belly plates: three low booms, a pause, the faint clack of the chimney field, the warm hollow under porous stone. It would be moving toward a vent-skin that had fed it many times before.

Then the ground would shift.

Not greatly. Not catastrophically. A thin shelf of crust would collapse beneath one forelimb, pinning it at an angle. The grazer would not cry out. It has no cry for such a thing. It would brace, wait, test the stone, withdraw into stillness. Steam would pass over it. Salts would gather on its shell. The vibration of the basin would blur through the trapped limb until direction became difficult.

A lesser benevolence might lift it bodily and set it free.

The wise power would not.

To be lifted by an unknown hand into the open air would be terror beyond comprehension. It would break the animal’s world more deeply than the fall had.

Instead the power would call moisture.

Only slightly more than usual.

Condensation would gather along the underside of the crust where the trapped limb lay. Mineral grains would loosen. A seam would soften. The vent nearby, which would otherwise have strengthened before dusk, would ease for one interval. The ground-sense of the old grazer would clear.

It would press.

Wait.

Press again.

A crescent edge of its foot would find purchase. A plate of crust would give way with a dry sigh. The limb would come free.

The old grazer would remain motionless for a long time afterward. Not grateful, exactly. Gratitude requires a story about what happened. It would have only sensation: pressure released, path restored, water present, vibration legible again.

It would continue.

That would be enough.

The power would accompany it for a while, without being perceived. It would feel, through the creature, the basin as the creature feels it: not empty, not bleak, but dense with instructions. A safe ridge is a remembered note. A pool is a tremor with taste. Another grazer at a distance is a pressure not yet requiring avoidance. A vent-skin is food, but also history, because it grows where the earth has kept its promise.

The power would understand then that this life is not reluctant in the sorrowful sense.

It is reluctant as a sealed seed is reluctant.

As a stone door is reluctant.

As anything ancient and exact is reluctant to spend itself.

So the power would bless its reluctance.

Not by changing it into eagerness.

By making sure it remained possible.

In the tiny cavities of the porous terraces, where transparent grazers live entire lives inside beads of water, the power would stabilize a few hidden hollows against collapse. Not all. Death must still feed the scale of the place. But enough that a lineage older than many mountains would not vanish because one mineral seam dried a fraction too soon.

Around the electrical rocks, where soot-dark colonies open into silver-edged stars, the power would let the blue-white flickers continue. It might even guide one buried current along a gentler path, so that the discharges came neither too rarely nor too violently. The colonies would open and close. Their dark centers would bead with microscopic droplets. Whatever transformation they perform in that brief charged light would go on.

The power would make no paradise.

Paradise would be an insult here.

Instead it would make refuges.

A crack that keeps water.  
A vent that does not overrun its nursery.  
A tone restored.  
A route clarified.  
A species hidden from appetite.  
A death returned cleanly into chemistry.  
A life permitted to remain itself.

Over time, the basin would appear unchanged.

That would be the sign of success.

The same pale sky.  
The same warm mineral air.  
The same industrial breathing of stone.  
The same long ribs dark with condensation.  
The same pools trembling in shallow bowls.  
The same rare basalt-colored bodies passing between vapor shadows at intervals too long to become pattern.

No green would come.

No birds.

No sudden river.

No proclamation.

But if one could compare the basin against another version of itself—the version in which no wise attention had ever rested there—one might notice small differences.

A little more life would make it from one wet interval to the next.

A few more young would harden before the crust failed them.

Old routes would remain usable longer.

The films would recover from grazing just often enough.

The charged colonies would persist on their dark stones.

The shell-dark grazers would still die, but less often from accidents too meaningless even for that austere world.

And perhaps, very rarely, when vapor thickened and sound gathered in the hollows until the air seemed to have weight, one of the larger beings would pause upon a dark ridge and lower its body fully to the stone.

It would feel the basin beneath it.

The roar.  
The clack.  
The pulse.  
The deep heat.  
The old machine that was never built.

And under that, almost too faint to separate from the rest, there would be a steadiness that had not been there before.

Not a voice.

Not a command.

Only a pressure like assurance.

Continue.

Nothing in the basin would understand this as blessing.

But the basin would receive it anyway.
