A benevolent and wise power would not enter the Drowned Jungle like a blade.

It would not burn back the black-barked trees, nor drain the hidden waters, nor silence the chitinous chorus that rings beneath the canopy. It would understand, at once, that this place is not evil simply because it is terrible. It is young. It is wounded in the way young worlds can be wounded: by becoming too quickly, by growing without memory, by hungering without knowing the name of hunger.

So the power would come softly.

Not as a sun.

Not as fire.

As a listening.

At first, nothing would change.

The jungle would continue to breathe its wet breath. Roots would continue to heave through the moss. The pools would rise from below and vanish again. The Hollow Ones would wade in their pale silence, mouths moving around stolen words.

But deep beneath the ground, where the old water sleeps before it rises, something would begin to hear itself.

The benevolent power would descend into the drowned roots and the black soil, not with judgment, but with patience. It would listen to every groan of the trees, every crack in the young earth, every soundless hymn of the Hollow Ones. It would listen until it understood the shape of the wrongness.

And the wrongness, perhaps, would be this:

The jungle has no boundary between taking and receiving.

It absorbs because absorption is all it knows. It drinks warmth, fear, names, memory, not from cruelty, but because every part of it is porous. The land itself is hollow in places. The water rises through old wounds. The creatures feed on rot and damp and churn because nothing here has yet learned the mercy of enough.

The Hollow Ones are not monsters.

They are what happens when a living soul remains too long in a place that cannot tell the difference between guest, seed, prey, and prayer.

So the wise power would give the jungle a first law.

Not a commandment carved in stone.

A rhythm.

A tide.

It would set, far below the moss and root-tangle, a slow golden pulse in the hidden water. A heartbeat older and calmer than the jungle’s own frantic becoming. The waters would still rise, but no longer at random. They would rise and fall in cycles. They would carry nourishment upward and grief downward. They would remember where they had been.

The trees would feel it first.

Their ribbed trunks would shudder. Their roots, which had once clutched and strangled whatever they touched, would loosen by a finger’s width. Vines that recoiled from touch would begin to distinguish between harm and simple contact. Leaves would still sag beneath their wet weight, but in the dim green canopy, small openings would appear—narrow windows through which a gentler light could pass.

Not sunlight, exactly.

Something like forgiveness filtered through emerald glass.

The creatures would scream when the pulse began. They would thrash through the undergrowth, iridescent bodies flashing, legs clicking in panic. The jungle’s chorus would become unbearable for one long night. Then, by degrees, it would change. The screams would remain, but they would no longer overlap without pause. There would be spaces between them.

Silence would enter the jungle for the first time.

Not emptiness.

Rest.

And the Hollow Ones—

The power would go to them last.

Because they would be the most delicate work.

It would find them in the shallows, pale and translucent, standing beneath the breathing trees with their great dark eyes fixed on nothing. It would not touch them immediately. To touch a hollow thing carelessly is to break it. Instead, the power would kneel in the water before them.

One by one, it would ask:

“What was your name?”

Most would not answer.

Their mouths would move. The water would swallow the sound.

So the power would ask again, not with words, but with remembrance. It would draw from the jungle the warmth it had taken. The fear. The last thoughts of lost travelers. The songs swallowed by rain. The childhood names dissolved into the pools. The faces reflected once, then lost when the water refused to mirror them.

It would gather these things carefully, like seeds from mud.

And it would return them.

Not all at once. That would be too much. Some souls cannot survive the sudden weight of themselves.

A Hollow One standing beneath an arch of roots would receive first the memory of hands.

Another, the sound of her own laughter.

Another, the knowledge that he had once hated olives.

Another, the face of a brother.

Another, the word for sky.

Another, the moment before fear took everything.

Some would collapse into the water, not dead, but full. Some would weep soundlessly until the water around them turned bright with salt. Some would open their mouths and speak one cracked syllable before vanishing like mist at dawn.

Some would not want to leave.

The wise power would allow this.

Benevolence is not rescue by force.

For those who wished to depart, paths would appear: not roads, but clearings of intention. A sequence of dry stones through the marsh. A pale moth with lantern-wings. A wind that smelled, impossibly, of home. They would follow, if they could. And where they stepped, the mud would not pull them down.

For those who wished to remain, the power would make them no longer hollow.

It would give them vessels of their own.

Not flesh as they once had, perhaps. That might be gone beyond recall. But form enough. Voice enough. A boundary between themselves and the jungle. A chest that did not endlessly drink. Eyes that could close.

And when the rains came again, the Hollow Ones would still sing.

But the hymn would change.

It would no longer be an invitation to dissolution.

It would be a warning, a welcome, and a mourning braided together:

Come no closer if you do not know yourself.  
Come gently if you must pass through.  
Come grieving if you have lost your name.  
Come, and we will help you remember.  

The jungle would remain dangerous.

The benevolent power would not make it tame. To tame it would be to kill the truth of it. The trees would still groan. The pools would still rise from ancient depths. The creatures would still move like liquid through the undergrowth, alien and overwhelming. The shadows would still gather strangely in the hollows, and the air would still taste, at times, of salt and iron.

But the wrongness would lessen.

Water would reflect again, though not always faces. Sometimes it would reflect what a traveler most needed to see. A coward might see the person they had abandoned. A cruel one might see the child they had once been. A lost one might see a door. A Hollow One might see a name.

The jungle would still watch.

But it would no longer only wait.

It would learn.

Seasons would come, even if strange ones. A season of mist, a season of black flowers, a season of molting shells, a season when the trees exhaled silver pollen and the air tasted briefly sweet. New creatures would arise that did not feed only on rot, but on fallen light. Small luminous things would cling to the undersides of leaves, trembling like stars afraid of height.

The power would leave no throne there.

No temple.

No law demanding worship.

Only three gifts.

The first: a rhythm in the deep water, so the jungle would know the mercy of ebb and return.

The second: a boundary around each soul, so no living thing would be taken merely because it wandered too near.

The third: a memory placed in the heartwood of the oldest tree, though the jungle had no oldest tree until that moment.

The memory would be simple:

To receive is not the same as to devour.  
To shelter is not the same as to possess.  
To be alive is not the same as to hunger forever.  

And perhaps, after many years, a traveler would enter the Drowned Jungle of Shattered Echoes and feel the air close around them, thick and wet and green.

They would hear the clicking limbs.

They would see the pale figures in the shallows.

They would feel something tug faintly at their fear, their warmth, their name.

Then the tug would stop.

The water would ripple.

A Hollow One, no longer empty, would turn its large dark eyes toward them and raise one translucent hand.

Not beckoning.

Not warning.

Asking.

Who are you?

And if the traveler could answer truly, the jungle would let them pass.
