It would do very little at first.

Wisdom, arriving in such a place, would not mistake violence for evil, nor noise for pain, nor strangeness for wrongness. It would not look upon the High Manufactuary and think: *This must become a garden.* It would understand that it already is one.

So the benevolent power would not descend as fire, nor command the towers to cease, nor still the pistons of lignin and resin. It would not sterilize the warm pools, nor straighten the shadows, nor drain the mist. It would not “save” the Curators by removing them from the only world whose rhythms their bones know how to answer.

First, it would listen.

Not with ears. Ears are useless there.

It would lower itself into the substrate until its attention spread through the oxidized groundplate like warmth through metal. It would feel the percussion-language of the Curators: the long messages sent through cellulose scaffolds, the tremor-psalms of repair, the three-hour sentences arriving yesterday. It would listen to the towers grinding their ancient work. It would listen to the pools digesting. It would listen to the insects boring into effluent pipes, to the gas-bags breathing hydrogen into the mist, to the low radiator-eared beasts cracking crystalline polymers between their teeth.

It would listen beneath all that.

Beneath the roar.

Beneath the convulsion.

Beneath the permanent maintenance.

And there, in the deepest strata of the Manufactuary’s being, it would find not malice, not purpose, not madness.

It would find exhaustion.

Not the exhaustion of a machine about to fail. Not the exhaustion of an ecosystem in collapse. Something older and stranger: the exhaustion of a process that has forgotten the difference between continuing and needing to continue.

The High Manufactuary does not know how to stop because stopping was never encoded into it. It knows rupture and healing. It knows extrusion and digestion. It knows pressure, condensation, repair. It knows how to turn waste into structure and structure into memory and memory into more maintenance.

But it does not know rest.

The Curators know waiting, but not hope.

So the benevolent power would give neither command nor cure.

It would introduce an interval.

A small one.

At first, only a single breath.

Somewhere near the center of the plateau, in a place where three factory-towers lean toward one another like listening elders, the peristaltic valves would mistime themselves by less than a second. A steam-vent that should have screamed would sigh instead. A cellulose gear would pause with one tooth not yet interlocked. A warm pool would stop digesting for the length of one falling drop.

The Curators nearby would freeze—not from fear, but from the impossibility of the event.

Stillness had always meant danger. Calcification. Temporal capture. Being lost in a fold where a blink became a century.

But this stillness would not seize them.

It would hold them.

Gently.

For the first time in their existence, they would stand motionless and remain alive.

The power would let that be enough for a while.

One breath.

Then the Manufactuary would resume.

The roar would return. Pistons would strike. Bellows would convulse. Mist would sheet down the towers. The Curators would move again, shaken through every fluted joint, their clustered eyes bright with colors for which their biology had no name.

Later, the interval would come again.

Longer.

Two breaths.

Then ten.

Then, after many cycles of thunder and labor, an hour of quiet would open in the Manufactuary like a blue flower beneath the skin of the world.

Not silence. Never silence. Silence would be too much like death.

But quiet.

Sound would travel straight for a while. A Curator tapping the substrate would hear the answer from the one beside them, not from three days ago, not from a tower twenty miles away, not from the space between atoms. The bellows would soften to the breathing of enormous sleepers. The warm pools would cease their hunger and become merely warm. The shadows would fall downward. The temporal folds would loosen.

In that hour, the benevolent power would speak.

It would not use words.

It would press a question into the substrate, slow and clear enough that every Curator could feel it rise through the soles of their feet.

Not: *Do you wish to be saved?*

Not: *Do you wish this place destroyed?*

Not even: *Are you suffering?*

The question would be:

*What do you want to continue?*

The Curators would not know how to answer.

Their memories were blueprints. Their instincts were maintenance. Their rituals were repair. They knew how to seal ruptures with resin-blood, how to prune cambium into new chambers, how to read mineral histories by taste. But wanting was an organ they had never needed.

So the power would wait.

A benevolent thing can wait without resentment.

The first answers would be confused.

A Curator would tap: *The western xylem artery must be replaced before the next pressure season.*

Another: *The third pool has become overacidic and will digest the grazing herd if not buffered.*

Another: *The records in the gravity silence are growing brittle.*

The power would receive these answers with reverence. Maintenance is a kind of love, even when it is all one has been permitted to be.

So it would help.

Not by doing the work for them, but by making work no longer indistinguishable from captivity.

It would teach the Manufactuary a new function.

Not cessation.

Not collapse.

Sabbath.

The word itself would not enter the place. The concept would.

A recurring interval would be grown into the cycle of the towers: not imposed from outside, but grafted into cambium and catalyst, encoded into valve-timing, pressure release, lymph chemistry, and temporal weather. Once every long Manufactuary day—if days could be counted beneath that bruised twilight—the plateau would soften.

The warm pools would become non-digestive.

The vents would exhale mineral vapor without calcifying.

The acoustic weirdness would settle into coherence.

The temporal folds would mark themselves in pale light so no one entered them unwittingly.

The factory-towers would continue to live, but not to convulse.

The Curators would discover that movement was not the only way to survive.

At first, they would spend the quiet hours performing deferred repairs.

Then, gradually, some would do unnecessary things.

One would arrange flakes of crystalline polymer into a pattern that served no structural function.

Another would tap a sequence into the groundplate that was not a warning, not an instruction, not a report, but pleasing.

A congregation would gather beside a harmless pool and dip their fingers into it, not to read records, but to feel warmth.

One would stand still for an entire hour, watching mist bead along their bark-paper skin, and emerge uncalcified, unchanged, and inwardly altered.

The power would also open a sky.

Not everywhere. The mist belonged there. The twilight belonged there. The cold and wet were part of the Manufactuary’s body.

But above one basin, during the quiet interval, the vapor would thin. The cloud ceiling would draw back just enough to reveal a narrow wound of stars.

The Curators would see distance unprocessed by industry.

They would see light that had not been manufactured by bioluminescent nodules, not bent by gravity wells, not filtered through lymph and vapor. Ancient light. Extravagant light. Light that had traveled without being refined.

Many would turn away, overwhelmed.

Some would weep harder than usual, their tears laying down crystalline tracks that did not exist in multiple time-streams, but only one.

A few would begin to return to that basin every cycle.

They would make no temple. Nothing so built.

But the ground there would change under their standing.

Their footprints would accumulate. The substrate would learn the shape of attention that was not labor.

The benevolent power would then do a second thing.

It would make endings possible.

This would be its most delicate mercy.

For the Manufactuary had always recycled, digested, extruded, reprocessed. Nothing was allowed to conclude. Even the dead became substrate; even obsolete Products became memory; even memory became instruction.

The power would not remove this. Continuity was sacred there.

But it would create, in the deepest gravity silence, a pool that did not digest and did not preserve.

A clear pool. Cold, unlike the others. Still as polished glass.

Those who entered it would not be consumed, archived, or extruded again.

They would be released.

Their resin-blood would not become sealant. Their bark-paper skin would not become substrate. Their memories would not be forced into crystalline record. They would pass beyond the Manufactuary’s processing entirely.

No Curator would be compelled toward it. No illness would drive them. No despair would be amplified.

It would simply be there.

A true exit.

For a long time, none would use it.

Then one ancient Curator, whose ceramic nails had been worn almost smooth from centuries of tapping repairs into the ground, would approach during a quiet interval. They would stand at the edge while others gathered far back, not interfering. Their clustered eyes would see the pool in all chrono-chromatic spectrums: present chartreuse, past violet, future white.

They would tap one message.

Small.

Private.

But the substrate would carry it everywhere.

*Enough.*

Then they would step in.

The Manufactuary would not shake. No tower would scream. No valve would rupture to reclaim the loss.

The Curator would simply be gone.

And because the world did not end, the others would understand something vast.

Maintenance was not the same as love if it could never permit release.

After that, the High Manufactuary would remain itself.

It would still be cold. Wet. Loud. Ancient. Violently alive.

The towers would still weep lymph down their fluted hides. The warm pools would still steam. Composite insects would still bore into effluent pipes. Gas-bags would drift through the vapor. Herds would graze on crystalline waste. Shadows would still sometimes fall sideways outside the quiet interval. Time would still fold in dangerous places. The Curators would still replace ruptured xylem tubes with resin from their own bodies.

But now the place would contain mercy as one of its processes.

Rest would recur.

Choice would exist.

Sound, at times, would travel straight.

The Curators would begin making things the Manufactuary had not asked for.

Percussion songs.

Memory gardens grown in non-digestive pools.

Garments patterned not merely for warmth, but for beauty.

Jewelry shaped to represent impossible concepts: pause, desire, enough, elsewhere, return.

They would still call themselves nothing.

But they would no longer be only Product.

And the wise power?

It would withdraw almost completely.

Not vanish. Not rule.

It would become a faint permissive warmth beneath the substrate. A kindness encoded into pressure cycles. A listening presence in the quiet intervals. A patient witness at the clear pool. A starbreak in the mist.

It would not make the High Manufactuary gentle.

It would make gentleness possible there.

And in a place that had processed eternity without rest, that would be miracle enough.
