It would not bring the sun.

That would be the first wisdom.

It would stand — if standing is what such a power does — at the edge of that enormous below-world valley and understand at once that light, here, would be a violence. Not because light is evil, nor because darkness is sacred in itself, but because this darkness has already become a language. The cavern’s lives are written in it. Their bodies answer it. Their hungers, routes, warnings, births, and deaths all take shape inside its limits.

To pour radiance into the place would be to erase the script.

So the power would not blaze.

It would not descend in white fire through the roof. It would not make the mineral seams shine like stars. It would not wake the ferns into forests or turn the gray-green films into meadows. It would not command the bats to silence, nor the cats to mercy, nor the eels to release what they had taken.

It would listen.

For a long while, perhaps for a hundred surface years, it would do only that.

It would listen in the way the cavern listens: not with ears alone, but with patience spread through stone. It would learn the intervals between drips. It would learn which pools fail first in the dry cold months and which persist in black hollows under gravel. It would learn the ledges where the bat mothers crowd too tightly, and the clay runs where the young ungulates break their legs, and the shelves where the hand-creatures sleep with their backs against warm stone.

It would learn the difference between suffering that belongs to life and suffering that is merely waste.

That distinction would matter to it.

For a benevolent power, if it is also wise, does not confuse kindness with rescue from all danger. It would know that the cats must hunt, that the beetles must open the dead, that the fungi must inherit the fallen, that fear is part of the intelligence of this place. To remove predation would be to starve the predators first and then overfill the pools with thirsting mouths. To make water abundant would drown the dry economies, rot the careful niches, soften the sharp world into another world entirely.

It would not make the cavern gentle.

It would make it less likely to be ruined.

Its first act would be almost nothing.

Somewhere high in the ceiling, where a fresh-broken rib of limestone still held a hidden stress from the old collapse, the power would place a quietness. Not a visible brace. Not a pillar of crystal. Only a persuasion in the stone: hold.

The fracture would stop lengthening.

A slab that might have fallen in some future season and crushed a whole shelf of nursing bats would remain where it was. Dust would sift from it now and then. Small things would nest in its shadow. No creature would know it had been spared. The cavern would continue to sound the same.

That would please the power.

Then it would turn its attention to water.

Not much water. Never much.

It would not split the plateau and send a river down. It would not fill the open basin with lakes. But in three places where seepwater already wandered through stone, it would widen a vein by less than the width of a root hair. A little more moisture would arrive during the leanest weeks. Not enough to change the map. Enough that one hoof-shaped pool would not go bitter before the cold season turned. Enough that the algae there would persist as a thin green darkness. Enough that the gnats would gather, and the bats would feed, and the hand-creatures would descend nervously to drink, and the eel beneath would live also.

A mercy, but not an exemption.

At another pool, where the gravel throat beneath it was swallowing water too quickly, the power would move one small stone in the underbed. Just one. It would lodge at an angle, catch silt, slow the vanishing. The pool would last two days longer after each seep pulse.

Two days would be everything to many mouths.

No one would praise it.

The power would not require praise.

It would notice the hand-creatures with particular tenderness, but also with restraint.

Their bright eyes would catch its attention. Their long fingers. Their way of learning by theft, irritation, play, and bruised persistence. Their almost-tools. Their half-remembered hammerstones. Their bone splinters dropped in silt. Their mothers pulling young away from ledge-edges by the scruff. Their old females pausing before a dangerous crossing because some deep body-memory knew an owl sometimes passed there.

A less wise power might be tempted.

It might think: Here. Here is the beginning of a people.

It might give them fire.

It might give them names.

It might sharpen their stones for them and teach them to carry tools from pool to pool. It might arrange signs in mineral light. It might make one of them dream of walls, of counting, of graves, of gods.

But this power would look longer.

It would see that intelligence does not need to become history in order to be worthy. It would see that cleverness is not a seed whose only proper flower is civilization. It would see that these creatures are not failed people. They are themselves: quick, social, fearful, affectionate, quarrelsome, competent inside a world that asks for exactly their kind of mind.

So it would not uplift them.

It would not burden them with destiny.

Instead, in a narrow shelf where their young often fell because the stone was newly sharp and treacherous, the power would loosen a trickle of mineral over many years. Drop by drop, a slick lip would grow duller. A fatal edge would become merely difficult. Some young would still fall elsewhere. Some would still be taken by owls. But that one needless place of repeated breaking would lose its appetite.

The hand-creatures would continue to bark, steal, groom, mate, pry larvae from cracks, and forget their best tools beside pools.

This too would please the power.

It would touch no predator with shame.

The long-bodied cats would remain hungry and beautiful in their patience. The owls would keep their grave voices. The eels would wait under black water with their blind mouths slightly open. The snakes would sleep in warm seams until vibration woke them.

But the power might alter one thing in the manner of accidents.

A cat whose paw had become wedged between two fresh stones — not through the ordinary risk of hunting, but through a freak settling of young rubble — would find, after long panic, that the smaller stone shifted just enough. Not thrown aside. Not miraculously dissolved. Shifted as if by its own fatigue.

The cat would limp free.

It would later kill a juvenile ungulate.

The power would accept this.

Mercy to the cat would not mean denial to the cat’s hunger. Mercy to the ungulate would not mean immortality. The wise power would not make a toy paradise of the cavern. It would not turn living beings into preserved specimens of innocence.

It would only remove what did not belong: the pointless trap, the poisoned seep, the collapse too large for the life below it to answer.

There would be other dangers from above.

Surface beings might one day cross the cold plateau with drills, maps, engines, hungers stranger than those of cats. They might find the long fissure. They might smell ammonia rising from the earth and imagine guano mines, mineral wealth, research stations, holy darkness, adventure, conquest.

Against that, the power would act more firmly.

Not by killing them.

Never that, unless no gentler road remained.

It would turn weather. It would send fog over the plateau when surveyors came. Compasses would hesitate. Instruments would return uninteresting readings. The fissure would seem narrower than it was, then choked, then not worth the descent. A climber would dream the night before of falling stone and wake with a conviction too strong to ignore. A mining company’s map would misplace the cavern by three miles. A grant would fail. A road would be routed elsewhere because the ground above tested unstable.

If someone came in reverence, with care, with a small light and no wish to own, the power might let them reach the threshold.

Only the threshold.

They would hear the cavern before seeing it: the chitter, scrape, drip, wingbeat, hoof-click, mutter, and echo. They would feel themselves noticed by innumerable lives. They would understand, if they were capable of understanding, that entrance is not the same as permission.

Then a stone would fall nearby — not to crush, only to warn.

Most would leave.

The few who did not would find the way difficult, confusing, humbling. Their lamps would seem too bright. Their voices would come back to them ugly and large. They would see eyes open all around and know themselves to be the disturbance.

If they were wise, they would retreat and speak of the place only as rumor.

If they were not wise, the power would make the cavern hard to remember accurately.

That would be its largest protection: not secrecy exactly, but indistinctness. The place would blur in the ambitions of those who wanted to use it. It would become “some hollow under the plateau,” “probably unstable,” “biologically minor,” “not commercially viable,” “difficult access,” “low priority.”

A blessing disguised as administrative failure.

Within, life would continue.

The bats would hang in black curtains. The beetles would work under falling droppings. The ungulates would scrape films from stone with their careful lips. The hand-creatures would quarrel over a marrow bone, then abandon it, then rediscover it later with fresh excitement. Owls would call from the draft-dark heights. Eels would take the unwary. Fungi would bloom from death without hurry.

The power would return often, but never as ruler.

It would be present like a slightly kinder geology.

A little holding where the stone would otherwise betray too much.

A little moisture where drought would become erasure.

A little obscurity where discovery would become possession.

A little dulling of edges where pain taught nothing.

It would not ask the cavern to become more beautiful.

It already was beautiful, though not in a way made for eyes. Its beauty was not lushness, not peace, not grandeur. Its beauty was precision: enough water, enough darkness, enough hunger, enough warning, enough return. Its beauty was the old routine inside young stone. Its beauty was a world that had not named itself and did not need to.

Perhaps, after ages, the power would give one gift that was not quite intervention.

Deep under the central basin, where many trails passed but none remained, it would encourage a slow mineral memory. Drips from different reaches of the roof would carry faint traces: guano, salt, algae, bone, clay, fur-oil, root, ash from some ancient surface fire washed through cracks long ago. These would gather in a hidden seam and harden over centuries into a banded stone no creature would see.

Layer by layer, the cavern would keep an unread record of itself.

Not a monument.

Not a message.

Only memory without audience.

That would be enough.

And if the cavern itself had a mind — not a human mind, not a single thought, but the composite awareness of everything listening — perhaps it would feel the power only as a slight easing.

A ledge that holds.

A pool that lasts.

A passage that remains undiscovered.

A broken edge made less cruel by time.

The cavern would not thank it. The hand-creatures would not worship it. The bats would not alter their cries. The cats would not become kind. The eels would not release their prey. The ungulates would not look upward with understanding.

The power would want none of that.

It would love the place by allowing it to remain itself.

Not untouched, exactly.

Tenderly unclaimed.
