The benevolent power would come quietly.

Not as a pillar of flame above the plateau, not as a voice that cracked the carved walls, not as a miracle so large that Arkas would forget itself in worship or fear. A wise power would understand that Arkas had survived because it was careful. It would see the town’s discipline, its shared wells, its stone lanes, its watchtowers, its craftspeople with patient hands, and it would know that sudden abundance can be as destructive as drought.

So it would begin by listening.

It would listen to the scrape of stone tools.  
To the elders counting jars of water.  
To the children who knew the sound of a full vessel the way other children know birdsong.  
To the watchmen calling across the dry air.  
To the hidden roots of the hardy plants gripping dampness under rock.  
To the wellspring breathing faintly beneath the center of town.

And after listening, it would choose not to change Arkas into something else.

It would help Arkas become more truly itself.

First, it would go beneath.

Deep under the plateau, where the stone held old memories of rain, it would trace the thin underground veins of water. Not enough to flood the town, not enough to make them careless, but enough to keep death from arriving by accident. It would clear a choked seam in the rock no human tool had yet reached. Slowly, over many seasons, the central wellspring would grow steadier.

Not lavish.  
Not endless.  
Steady.

The people would notice only that the worst dry months no longer carried the same terror. The water counters would still count. The jars would still be sealed. Children would still be taught respect. But fewer infants would weaken in drought years. Fewer elders would give up their share. Fewer families would stare at the sky with quiet panic.

Then the power would touch the clouds.

It would not command storms to break over Arkas, for the town’s roofs and lanes were not built for deluge. Instead, it would teach the mountain winds a gentler habit. On certain mornings, mist would gather along the cliffs. Dew would pearl on stone. The hardy plants would drink. Moss would appear in shaded cracks. The people, observant and practical, would notice.

A potter’s daughter would be first.

She would wake before dawn and see droplets clinging to a woven reed mat left outside. She would run her finger over it and taste water. Not much, but water. She would begin experimenting: reeds angled toward bowls, rough cloth stretched between poles, stone channels carved shallow and wide to catch the breath of morning.

At first the elders would laugh gently. Then they would measure the bowls.

After that, they would stop laughing.

Within a generation, the flat roofs of Arkas would wear dew-catchers like strange wings. The town would not become rich in water, but it would gain another way to survive.

The power would smile, unseen.

Next, it would turn toward the people’s minds.

Arkas had become standardized because standardization kept people alive. Every jar had its place. Every wall had its purpose. Every craft had a known method. There was wisdom in this, but also a danger: a town that survives too narrowly may begin to mistake caution for virtue and repetition for truth.

The benevolent power would not force invention upon them. It would not throw metal into their hands and demand an age they had not chosen. But it would loosen the knot of fear around curiosity.

It would send dreams.

Not commands.  
Not prophecies.  
Only dreams.

A mason would dream of stones fitted in an arch, each one leaning on the others, and wake with the idea for stronger covered cisterns.

A weaver would dream of layered fibers trapping coolness, and later devise coverings that slowed evaporation from stored jars.

A child would dream of roots beneath the earth, spreading not downward but sideways, and would ask why gardens must be planted only where they had always been planted.

A healer would dream of bitter leaves steeped in warm water, and discover a treatment for the fever that sometimes followed the rare rains.

A watchman would dream of paths across the mountains marked not by walls but by stacked stones, and slowly Arkas would begin to map the world beyond its plateau with more confidence.

Each dream would be small enough to belong to the dreamer.

That was important.

A wise power would never steal credit from mortals for the growth of their own hands.

Then, because Arkas was nearly without animals, the power would invite life back carefully.

It would guide a few mountain swifts to nest under the outer ledges, where they would eat the insects that spoiled stored grain. It would lead small, sure-footed lizards into the warm cracks of the walls. It would encourage a burrowing creature, shy and pale, to tunnel near the refuse heaps, turning waste into softer soil. None would overrun the town. None would become plague or burden.

The children would name them.

The elders would pretend not to be fond of them.

The town would become slightly less silent.

And in the shielded places where damp soil gathered, the power would strengthen certain seeds. Not by making them unnatural, but by reminding them of older possibilities hidden in their lineage: deeper roots, waxier leaves, slower thirst. Over time, Arkas would learn which plants could be coaxed into terraces, which could be dried for lean years, which could hold soil against the wind.

The plateau would remain arid.

But it would no longer feel quite so opposed to life.

Then the power would address the matter of advancement.

It had seen many places ruined by hunger for more. It had also seen many places wither because they feared becoming anything new. Arkas stood at the edge of metal, and metal is a dangerous gift. It can become plow, needle, chisel, blade, chain, crown.

The power would not hand them bronze.

Instead, one night, far beyond the outer watchtower, a small star would fall.

It would strike a slope of black stone and cool there: a fist-sized piece of sky-iron, heavy and strange.

A young scout would find it weeks later while searching for a lost marker. She would carry it back wrapped in cloth, puzzled by its weight. The craftspeople would gather. They would strike it, heat it, fail, argue, observe, try again. Years might pass before they understood even a little.

This would be enough.

Not a revolution.  
A question.

The first useful thing they made from it would not be a weapon.

The power would see to that.

Perhaps it would be a narrow awl, treasured by the leatherworkers. Perhaps a tiny blade used by healers to cut away rot from wounds. Perhaps a point used to carve finer channels in stone. Something humble. Something that taught them metal could serve care before conquest.

And if, someday, someone did think to make a weapon from such material, Arkas would already have stories about the first sky-stone: that it came during a season of healing, that its first edge saved flesh rather than opened it, that gifts from above must answer to the wellspring below.

Stories matter. A wise power knows this.

So it would give Arkas a story strong enough to restrain ambition.

It would also visit the heart of the town: the wellspring.

Not visibly. Not with thunder.

At the deepest point of the water, it would set a memory.

Anyone who looked into the well in a moment of honest need would feel, briefly, the truth that water is not owned. It is borrowed. It passes through hand, throat, root, cloud, stone, and returns. The feeling would last only a breath, but it would shape decisions.

A greedy water-keeper, about to falsify measures for his own household, would pause.

A frightened mother, tempted to steal another family’s jar, would instead go to the council and ask for help.

A council elder, inclined to deny newcomers at the gate, would remember the taste of dependence.

The power would not remove selfishness from Arkas. That would make them less than human. But it would make conscience easier to hear.

In time, the town’s laws would change by small degrees.

They would establish a drought covenant: no household to be abandoned while common stores remained.

They would create guest jars near the gate, sealed and marked, for travelers who arrived near death from the mountain paths.

They would appoint children, not only elders, to observe the well level each new moon, so that reverence would begin early and authority would not hide knowledge.

They would build shaded public cisterns whose carvings showed hands passing water from one generation to the next.

The walls of Arkas would still protect.

But they would no longer only exclude.

Finally, the benevolent power would give one gift that no one in Arkas would recognize as a gift.

It would leave them uncertainty.

The rains would still vary. Tools would still break. Arguments would still happen in the lanes. Craftspeople would still fail at new designs. The well would still need guarding. Children would still grow restless. Elders would still worry. The mountains would remain immense and indifferent.

Because struggle, in its proper measure, preserves dignity.

A power that removes all hardship turns people into ornaments. A power that ignores suffering becomes cruelty. Wisdom lies between: relieve what crushes, preserve what strengthens, open what has closed, and never demand gratitude.

So Arkas would continue.

Cool in the high air.  
Stone-walled.  
Careful.  
Ingenious.  
Less afraid.

Its people would still rise before dawn to work. They would still count water. They would still carve, mend, grind, weave, teach, and watch the sky. But now there would be dew-catchers on the roofs. Small birds under the ledges. New terraces green in hidden pockets. Cisterns that held through longer droughts. Children asking better questions. Elders answering with less fear.

And somewhere in the town, perhaps near the central well, someone would carve a new image into stone:

A mountain.  
A cloud.  
A jar.  
An open hand.

No one would know exactly why the symbol felt sacred.

The benevolent power would not mind.

It would remain near, not as ruler, not as god-king, not as owner of Arkas, but as a patient presence in wind, dream, root, and stone.

And if ever Arkas began to forget mercy, the well would seem a little darker.

If ever Arkas began to fear change too deeply, a child would dream of an impossible tool.

If ever Arkas became proud and wasteful, the morning dew would vanish for a time, reminding them that gifts are relationships.

And if ever Arkas faced a catastrophe beyond its strength — a poisoned spring, a killing drought, an army from below, a shaking of the mountain — then the power would act more openly.

The cliffs might close a pass.

A buried aquifer might break upward in a new spring outside the walls.

The sky might gather, not in destruction, but in one long, soaking rain.

The people would call it fortune.

The wise power would accept that name.

It had never needed another.
