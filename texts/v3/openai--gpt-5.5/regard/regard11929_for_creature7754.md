A benevolent and wise power would not enter as a conqueror.

It would not bleach the walls, silence the roar, cut the vines, straighten gravity, or declare the Unspoolers abominations in need of correction. It would understand first that this pocket-womb is not merely a corruption of nature but an attempt—crude, desperate, brilliant—to make a world with flint, bone, breath, terror, and longing.

It would recognize the place as a child recognizes another child: unfinished, overheated, too tightly held, alive because it is afraid to stop becoming.

So the power would come gently.

Not through the rent.

Not as a blaze, not as a winged figure, not as a voice that rattles the basalt spine.

It would arrive as a pause.

For one breath, the *drip-drip-CRASH* would soften. The bladder-vines would swell but not burst. The calcium lattices would cease their frantic cracking. The time-looped droplet would hang between falling and returning, trembling like a thought almost spoken.

The Unspoolers would feel it before they knew it.

The Loom-Mother’s bone awl would still against the glyph-wall. The Knife-Singer’s mandibles would part without clacking. The Echo-Worm would lengthen through three reed-filters at once, all its pearl-body listening.

The power would not speak in words at first.

It would give them silence.

Not absence. Not death. Silence with a pulse inside it. Silence like deep soil. Silence like a cave before the first handprint. Silence wide enough that none of them needed to labor for one moment to keep the world from dissolving.

And in that silence the pocket would learn a new law:

**Rest does not mean undoing.**

That would be the first mercy.

The second would be space.

Not more room in the crude physical sense—not yet. To wrench the womb wider would rupture it. To break the basalt would spill the whole frantic ecology down the world-spire in a steaming green cataract.

Instead, the power would place depth inside the armspan.

A kindness of dimension.

The walls would remain near enough to touch, but no longer crushingly so. A vine-hollow that once mirrored infinite spires would become a true recess, a small green alcove where condensation could gather without panic. A lattice-shelf would unfold into a terrace the size of a sleeping body. Between two braided thorn-ferns, a pocket of breathable air would open, cool by a single degree, then another.

The chamber would still be hot. Still wet. Still itself.

But the heat would no longer be fever.

The humidity would no longer be drowning.

The roaring processes would no longer need to devour each other merely to continue.

The power would touch the upward-running brine and teach it a gentler path. Not obedience to ordinary gravity—that would be too blunt, too foreign—but rhythm. Spiral, pause, seep, rise, fall. The brine would remember that cycles may nourish without frenzy.

The bladder-vines would stop bursting in terror and begin opening like lungs.

The bamboo aqueducts, split by stone wedges and lashed with gut-thong, would be strengthened from within by a pale mineral thread, flexible as tendon. The reed filters would no longer clog with panicked fungal bloom. The basins would not deliquesce the instant attention turned away.

The third mercy would be trust made physical.

Because the pocket coheres by collective hallucination, doubt is dangerous there. Every fear becomes erosion. Every lapse becomes brine. Every uncertainty tugs at the seams of reality.

So the power would lay down anchors.

Small ones.

Humble ones.

A ring of black seeds pressed into the living stone, each no larger than a beetle’s eye.

Where they rooted, they would not conquer. They would not overgrow the native vines. They would become dark, patient nodules, drinking excess heat and giving back stability. Around each seed, matter would remember itself without needing to be constantly commanded.

The carved basins would remain basins.

The shelves would remain shelves.

A foot placed on lattice would not sink through unless invited.

A droplet could still reverse its fall, but it would do so playfully, not compulsively.

The Knife-Singer would discover that his own shed carapace no longer twitched in aborted resurrection. It would either become useful dead matter, or, if he sang over it with true intent, a deliberate second shell. No more half-lives caught between.

The half-formed gargoyles would stop leering in unfinished hunger. Some would remain stone. Some would become vents. One, perhaps, would become an eye—not a spying eye, but a weather-eye, warning when brine crests, when the exit-rent nears opening, when the spire’s deeper magma-dreams grow restless.

The fourth mercy would be choice.

The power would understand that the Unspoolers are not merely trapped inhabitants. They are organs of the place. To remove them carelessly would be to amputate the pocket. To leave them without possibility would be cruelty.

So it would make a threshold.

Not a door that stands always open; that would drain the worldlet of its necessary pressure. Not a prison-gate controlled from outside; that would insult their becoming.

A threshold keyed to consent.

When brine levels rose and the old drowning birth-canal began to force itself open, the power would lay a second path within it: a membrane of breathable dark, wet but not suffocating, narrow but not murderous. Any Unspooler who wished to pass beyond could do so without being torn from themselves. Any who wished to remain would feel no summons.

The Loom-Mother might press one palm to it and recoil.

The Knife-Singer might thrust his face through, taste cold altitude, see a sky so huge it wounds him, and retreat shrieking with wonder.

The Echo-Worm might pass halfway out and halfway in, becoming for a while a messenger between the basalt spire and the womb.

The power would not hurry them.

Wise benevolence knows that rescue forced too quickly becomes another form of violence.

The fifth mercy would be language.

The primitives who made this place left growth-words half-remembered, dangerous because incomplete: “grow-bind-thrash.” Commands born of need. Commands that know only expansion, lashing, struggle.

The power would not erase them.

It would add three more.

Not carved in a superior script, not thundered from above, but discovered gradually in mold-light, in condensation patterns, in the pulse between the Knife-Singer’s notes, in the Loom-Mother’s own brine-sweat, in the Echo-Worm’s trail of spores.

The words would be:

**rest**

**release**

**return**

When whispered over vines, they would loosen without dying.

When pressed into stone, they would allow a structure to cease growing once it had become enough.

When sung into a time-loop, they would let the trapped moment complete itself.

When breathed into a living body, they would remind flesh that it need not serve only as substrate.

The Loom-Mother, who had never stopped weaving because stopping meant collapse, would one day whisper **rest** into her own abdomen.

The gurgling accretions within her would quiet.

Not vanish.

Not be killed.

They would sleep.

And she, perhaps for the first time since emerging, would hang in the canopy without working, her sucker-padded palms open, her milky compound eyes dimming to a soft glow while foxfire fronds leaned around her like a shawl.

The Knife-Singer would learn **release**.

He would cut a strangling vine and not immediately smear its sap as war-paint. He would let it fall. Where it landed, it would decay into a rich mat, and from that mat would rise a fern that did not duel for light but shared it.

His chant would change.

Less clack, less snap, less cosmic-string fracture.

Still strange. Still sharp. Still his.

But with intervals.

With listening.

The Echo-Worm would learn **return**.

It would stop unspooling fringes whenever doubt brushed the walls. Instead, it would gather loose reality-thread in its pearl-body and lay it back into the seams. The crevice would no longer seal only like a wound. Sometimes it would close like an eyelid.

The sixth mercy would be memory cleansed of compulsion.

The power would find the softened hands of the makers—the primitives whose frenzy hollowed this embryonic world, whose fear and desire deliquesced into its rules. It would not resurrect them whole; there may no longer be whole selves to restore. But it would gather what remained: a knuckle of intent here, a breath caught in ochre there, a mother’s scream woven into the reality-hum, a hunter’s pride buried in an antler adze, a child’s fascination with glowing mold.

These fragments would be composted into blessing.

The glyphs invoking growth-demons would alter, slowly, under mineral rain. Their horned panic would soften into figures with open hands. The old command to multiply or perish would become a story:

*We were afraid of emptiness.*

*We made too much.*

*We loved life badly.*

*May those after us love it better.*

The seventh mercy would be limits.

Benevolence is not indulgence. Wisdom knows that not every growth is good because it is alive.

The power would set boundaries the pocket could feel but not hate.

No vine may strangle every other vine.

No basin may demand constant worship to remain.

No creature may be claimed as substrate without its soul being asked.

No new Unspooler may be born merely to maintain the old terror.

No reality-fray may widen beyond what the chamber can mend.

These would not be laws enforced by punishment. They would be laws like bones: internal, shaping, supportive. When a vine began to overtake the whole canopy, its own sap would thicken and slow. When the floor-lattice tried to pierce living flesh uninvited, it would blunt into harmless coral. When the pocket’s hunger reached toward a visitor, the black seed-nodules would hum, reminding it:

**Invitation, not seizure.**

And if a visitor stayed willingly?

Then the place might still change them. But no longer by ambush. No longer by drowning assimilation. Transformation would become covenant.

The eighth mercy would be beauty without urgency.

The power would coax one flower from the thorn-ferns.

Only one.

It would take seven cycles of brine-cresting to open. The Loom-Mother would guard it suspiciously. The Knife-Singer would circle it, mandibles twitching, wanting to taste. The Echo-Worm would sleep beneath it, coiled in a pearl spiral.

When at last it bloomed, it would not glow violently like the polyps. It would not pulse with predatory fertility. It would be pale green, almost white, with a throat of deep mineral blue—the color of sky seen through water.

Its scent would be cool stone after rain.

For one hour, the chamber would smell not of blood-iron, salt, sweat, sap, and geothermal breath, but of distance.

The Unspoolers would not know what to do with longing so clean.

That would be another door.

Not outward.

Inward.

The final mercy would be restraint.

After all this, the benevolent power would withdraw.

Not abandon.

Withdraw.

It would leave no throne, no idol, no demand for worship. It would not require the Unspoolers to become gentle in any ordinary sense. They would remain strange, wet, chitinous, mineral-veined, vine-crowned. The pocket would remain loud, lush, cramped, weird, alive. Beetle-kin would still clack across ceilings. Slime-worms would still trail fertility. Brine would still spiral upward when it pleased. Time would still hiccup in corners.

But beneath the frenzy there would be a second rhythm.

A deeper one.

A held note.

The Unspoolers would feel it whenever panic rose.

The Loom-Mother would press her palm to a glyph and sense that she was allowed to stop.

The Knife-Singer would open his mandibles and find that a song need not cut to be powerful.

The Echo-Worm would coil around a fraying seam and know how to mend without consuming.

And the world-spire, that black needle thrust three miles into freezing cloud, would hold within it not a wound, not a tumor, not a failed cave, but a small impossible sanctuary:

still embryonic,

still becoming,

but no longer terrified that becoming must be endless.

If the power did anything at all, it would do this:

It would midwife the womb without owning the child.

It would teach the place the difference between aliveness and panic.

It would give the Unspoolers the first true luxury their makers never knew—

not escape,

not perfection,

but enoughness.
