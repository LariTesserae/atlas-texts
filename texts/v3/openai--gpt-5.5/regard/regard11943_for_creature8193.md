First, it would not save them.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom.

It would not seize the Suspended Cisterns in invisible hands and haul them up into air and sun. It would not “restore” the Siphon-Born to some imagined human shape. It would not still the pumps, silence the mechanimals, drain the spheres, straighten the children’s spines, or turn the Filter-Priests away from the water by command.

A lesser mercy would look upon Keth-Maral and see only suffering: bodies altered by necessity, lives narrowed by pressure gauges and mineral load, a civilization hanging one failed coupling away from ruin.

A wiser mercy would see more.

It would see devotion. Adaptation. Beauty. A culture whose prayers were maintenance schedules, whose music was vibration through water, whose children learned tenderness by winding the hearts of copper fish.

So the power would begin by listening.

It would enter Keth-Maral as no storm, no revelation, no burning wheel. It would arrive as a change in the mist: droplets hesitating in the air a fraction longer than usual, forming brief constellations before pearling down the walls. It would pass through the rivets without corroding them, through the water without displacing it, through the bodies of the Siphon-Born as a warmth too gentle to be fever.

The Sphere-Wardens would notice first.

Floating in the center of the habitation sphere, webbed fingers spread to the currents, they would feel something beneath the familiar thrum of pumps and hammers: a second rhythm, patient and vast, matching their own pulse before slowly teaching it a deeper cadence.

They would open their eyes.

In the blue-green gloom before them, the water would fold itself into a shape.

Not a face. Not exactly.

A standing wave. A listening place.

And because it was wise, it would ask.

Not in words alone, for words were blunt instruments in Keth-Maral. It would ask in pressure, in warmth, in the minute mineral taste of consent. It would send the question through the communal dream-water so every sleeper, every Brackling, every Pipe-Runner wedged in the dark arteries of the city, every Filter-Priest half-lost in the ache of purification, might feel it:

**May I help you remain yourselves?**

Some would fear it. Some would worship it immediately and be gently discouraged. Some would suspect a fault in the heating coils, a hallucination caused by mineral concentration, a frontier-wave anomaly spreading inward.

The power would wait.

That, too, would be mercy.

Only when the Siphon-Born answered—not unanimously, for no living people ever truly answer as one, but sufficiently, with curiosity outweighing terror—would it begin.

It would start with the places where suffering had become confused with holiness.

In the chambers of the Filter-Priests, where blue-white mineral tracery spread beneath translucent skin, the power would not shame the old devotion. It would not say, *You were wrong to give yourselves.*

Instead, it would teach the water another way.

It would speak to the barnacle-flowers clustered around heat vents. It would coax their slow mechanical petals to open wider, to drink heavier poisons, to crystallize waste along their outer shells instead of leaving it to gather in the organs of the faithful. It would alter the pressure-kelp in the maintenance shafts, not into something alien, but into a fuller version of what it already wished to become: a living lattice of filtration, hungry for metals, exhaling clean mineral balance into the flow.

The next generation of Filter-Priests would still exist, but they would no longer be sacrifices.

They would become Tenders of Purity rather than vessels for it. Their mutations would remain honored, their knowledge preserved, their sacred fractal patterns copied into glass and copper, but their bodies would no longer be asked to die so young.

Those already crystallizing inside would be offered relief.

Not cure forced upon them, but choice.

Some would accept the loosening of mineral from kidney and liver, weeping cloudy tears as the burden passed from their flesh into waiting bowls of living shell. Others, near the end and proud of their long service, would refuse. The power would honor them also. When they died, their relics would glow faintly forever after, not because the power made idols of them, but because gratitude sometimes leaves light behind.

Next, it would go to the couplings.

At the frontier spheres, where experimental joints groaned under stresses no ancient chart had predicted, the power would run itself through rivet, gasket, pressure seam, and iron tendon. It would not make them invulnerable. Invulnerability breeds carelessness, and Keth-Maral’s genius was care.

Instead it would give them warning.

The copper filaments beneath the Sphere-Wardens’ skin would grow more articulate. A failing bolt would no longer arrive as vague unease but as a precise taste: brass-bitterness for shear strain, cold sweetness for pressure fatigue, a prickling behind the left eye for seal decay. The Wardens would learn to read the city not as pain but as language.

Then the power would plant redundancy.

A second heart, though no one would call it that at first.

Deep in an unused storage sphere where an old filter-feeder slept among its own mineral antlers, the power would awaken a dormant Builder-engine, not by command but by reminding it of its purpose. The engine had been waiting for a signal lost centuries ago. It opened like a seed.

From it grew a slow, patient organ of pumps and membranes, half-machine and half-gill, capable of taking over pressure regulation if the primary systems failed. It would take twenty years to mature. This was deliberate. The Siphon-Born would have to raise it, learn it, argue over it, name it, integrate it into ritual. A gift too sudden becomes dependency. A gift grown slowly becomes culture.

The Bracklings would name it Grandmother Valve.

The name would remain.

After that, the power would turn toward memory.

Not all at once.

Keth-Maral had survived partly because it had forgotten enough not to despair. The Deep Builders were absence shaped like myth. To pour the whole truth into the cisterns might crack more than it healed.

So the power would release history like dye into water: slowly, visibly, allowing currents to choose their paths.

In the communal dreams, the Siphon-Born would begin seeing hands unlike theirs assembling the first sphere. They would see workers in pressure suits singing into metal ribs. They would see the underside of the world-shelf before the city, bare and immense. They would see the first mechanimal heart wound by a child who still had ordinary legs. They would see fear, ingenuity, exile, hunger, tenderness.

They would learn that the Deep Builders had not been gods.

This would wound them.

Then free them.

The Builders had been desperate, brilliant, imperfect people who made a place where life could continue when all other choices had narrowed. Some intended the Siphon-Born as inheritors. Some intended them as components. Some argued. Some repented. Some loved what they had made and died afraid it would not last.

The revelation would not destroy the old religion. It would deepen it.

Prayers to the Deep Builders would change. Less pleading. More conversation.

The mechanimals, too, would be granted attention.

The power would follow the pipe-eels through forbidden channels, listen to the camera-eyed fish flickering in the frontier dark, rest beside the great filter-feeders with their gasket mouths and mineral-crusted flanks. It would perceive the threshold forming there: not merely malfunction, not merely evolution, but awakening.

Again, it would not command.

It would make room.

Tiny sanctuaries would appear in unused bypass chambers: warm cavities rich in mineral current where young mechanimals could develop without being harvested too early or crushed in maintenance cycles. The Siphon-Born would discover them and at first call them infestations. Then a Pipe-Runner child would return from one with an eel coiled around her shoulders, both of them clicking in perfect alternating rhythm.

A treaty would follow.

Not written. Pulsed.

The mechanimals would continue their work in the great cycle. The Siphon-Born would continue harvesting what could be harvested without maiming. But certain creatures would be recognized as persons of a kind, or at least as neighbors. The language for this would take generations to refine. The power would not hurry them.

Then it would offer the most dangerous gift of all:

An outside.

Not escape. Not abandonment.

A door.

In one of the highest air pockets, where salt-crusted succulents clung upside down and roots reached like pale fingers toward the water, the power would alter the atmosphere. Slowly. Carefully. It would thicken the humidity, tune the pressure, lace the air with trace minerals. It would become a chamber where the Siphon-Born could remain longer without agony.

At first it would be used for repairs.

Then for councils.

Then for mourning.

Then, one day, a Brackling would ask why the ceiling-garden could not be larger.

The adults would laugh because the question was impossible.

The power would do nothing.

The child would ask again the next year.

By then the succulents would have flowered.

From their seeds, the Siphon-Born would learn to cultivate air-pocket gardens that did not feel like exile. Weighted vests would become less punitive, more ceremonial. Masks would become lighter. The Dry Death would remain a fear, but no longer an absolute boundary.

Eventually, explorers would climb—not away from the water, but toward knowledge of what lay beyond it.

Some would return shaking. Some would return radiant. Some would not return at all.

That would be permitted.

For a people cannot be truly helped if they are only preserved.

The city itself would change, but not quickly enough to feel violated.

New spheres would bud with surer mathematics. Frontier water would still form impossible standing waves, but now the Siphon-Born would study them as weather, not omens alone. The Pipe-Runners would develop guilds of translation between human pulse, eel-click, pump-song, and valve-code. Harvest-Swimmers would still dance through the processing chambers with tools locked into their hands, but their harnesses would include releases designed for comfort rather than endurance alone.

The Sphere-Wardens would remain at the center, but fewer of them would be trapped there by bodily necessity. Some would choose the old floating vigil. Others would rotate out, teaching younger Wardens that devotion did not require disappearance.

And the power?

It would withdraw, mostly.

Not because it ceased to care, but because wisdom knows the difference between aid and occupation.

It would leave behind no throne, no doctrine, no immortal governor. Only a few continuing mercies woven into the system:

A tone in the pumps that grew audible when despair in the communal dreams became dangerous.

A species of algae that brightened around lost children.

A habit among the Grandmother Valve’s membranes of sealing small cracks before they became tragedies.

A faint warmth in the relics of the old Filter-Priests.

And, in the deepest storage sphere, where the oldest filter-feeder dreamed through its mineral antlers, a single clear pool untouched by murk. Any Siphon-Born who entered it would dream not of the city’s needs, but of their own.

This would be the power’s final and greatest blessing.

For generations, the Siphon-Born had dreamed as components of Keth-Maral: pressure tolerances, flow rates, inherited alarms, pipe maps, maintenance chants. Now they would also dream privately.

A Harvest-Swimmer might dream of music with no industrial purpose.

A Pipe-Runner might dream of open distance.

A Filter-Priest might dream of old age.

A Brackling might dream of becoming something no caste had yet named.

The city would continue hanging beneath the stone sky.

The water would still pearl every surface.

The mechanimals would still tick and whir through the blue-green gloom.

The pumps would still thrum in the bones.

But catastrophe would no longer be the only thing holding the people together. Necessity would remain, joined now by choice.

And if, centuries later, the Siphon-Born spoke of the benevolent power at all, they would not agree on what it had been.

The Filter-Priests would call it the Gentle Current.

The Pipe-Runners would call it the Listener in the Walls.

The Sphere-Wardens would call it the Second Rhythm.

The Bracklings would insist it was Grandmother Valve’s imaginary friend.

The mechanimals, in their clicking dialects of gear and bubble, would give it a name untranslatable except as:

**The One Who Did Not Unmake Us.**
