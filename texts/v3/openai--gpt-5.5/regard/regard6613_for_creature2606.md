The wise power would come quietly.

Not as a clearing in the clouds, not as a white fire above the ridges, not as a voice that made the throat-drummers fall silent and the metallic birds drop from their branches in terror. It would not arrive in a shape the basin could worship. It would not ask to be known.

It would enter first as attention.

For a long while, perhaps for many rains, it would do nothing but watch.

It would watch the young ridges shed their black stone teeth into the gullies. It would watch the streams argue themselves into being and out again. It would watch the striped herds step into the mirrored flats and learn, by loss, where the water was too still. It would watch the climbers fold leaves against rain and then abandon them. It would watch frogs bloom from hoofprints, beetles enter fallen wood, fungi write pale river-maps through the dead.

And because it was wise, it would not mistake violence for evil.

It would understand that the basin was not cruel. It was unfinished.

It would not still the predators’ hunger. It would not make the tusked churners gentle. It would not stop the vines from climbing what slept too long, nor command the snakes to show themselves honestly among the leaves. It would not make every fawn live, every egg hatch, every nest hold, every pool remain. Such mercy would be a kind of murder here. It would turn the basin into something other than itself: a garden with its teeth pulled, a fever cooled into sterility, a young god sedated before it could learn its name.

So the power would not save the place from change.

It would save it from being wasted by change.

Its first act would be beneath sight.

Deep under the raw red earth, where water vanished into porous stone and reappeared without warning, the power would lay a patience into the rock. Not walls. Not dams. Nothing so crude. Only a memory of channels: a tendency for floodwater to find certain hidden veins instead of tearing the whole basin open at once. The rivers would still wander. Banks would still collapse. Fish would still gasp in abandoned braids, and eels would still come out when thunder called. But when the great storms gathered three upon three, when the basin might have drowned itself to rot, some water would be led downward, held in dark stone, returned later as seep, as spring, as breath for roots in the dry heat after rain.

No animal would thank it.

The frogs would simply find that some pools lasted one more day.

The soft-skinned salamander-things would bury themselves and wake in mud that had not become dust.

The trees, thirsty after their own extravagant growing, would reach down and touch hidden coolness.

The second act would be given to rot.

The power would lean close to the fungi, those pale, quiet rememberers, and bless them with measure.

Not restraint exactly. Rot must eat. Rot is holy in a place where nothing remains itself for long. But the power would teach the threads a distinction: dead wood, fallen leaf, opened carcass, yes; living root, sleeping egg, the whole green nerve of the basin, not too greedily, not all at once.

So the fungi would still bloom black after storms. They would still veil insect husks and soften bones and make bridges of fallen trunks. But no single hunger among them would learn to conquer all the rest. No pale mold would rise one season and take the amphibians from every cup of water. No sweet-smelling blight would seduce all flowers into fruiting themselves to exhaustion. No clever thread would become a king.

The basin would keep its many mouths.

The third act would be for the slopes.

The young ridges were eager to fall. They were not mountains yet; they were arguments upward, shoulders of clay and black stone fins, proud and unstable. The power would not harden them into permanence. It would only give them, here and there, a chance to fail slowly.

A root would find a crack a season earlier than it might have.

A moss would take hold on raw stone before the next rain struck.

A tusked animal, rooting below an overhang, would smell something wrong and turn aside.

One tree, swollen and vine-strangled, would fall before the night herd passed under it, not after.

Another would fall after, because the world is not a cradle.

This is what the power would know: mercy is not the removal of danger. Mercy is the presence of enough warning, enough refuge, enough continuation that life may answer danger with more than terror.

So the fourth act would be warning.

Not prophecy. Not language carved into sky.

Only small foreknowings distributed through flesh.

The striped browsers would grow more sensitive in the bones of their feet. A tremor from loosened mud would reach them a breath sooner. Not always soon enough. But often.

The frogs would fall silent not only for snakes, but for the thick, low pressure before sudden water. Their silence would ripple through reeds and be heard by others who did not understand it but moved anyway.

The climbers, already weatherwise, would wake sometimes before dawn with the smell of stonefall in their mouths. The old females would cough sharply and lead the troop higher, though the young males protested and shook wet branches in irritation. Later, below, a bank would fold into the place where they had meant to feed.

The night hunters would hear flood in their chest chambers.

The crabs would climb.

The snakes would loosen from low branches and take to higher ones.

None of this would be perfect. Perfection would be another kind of prison. But the basin would become slightly better at surviving itself.

The fifth act would be for the climbers.

Here the power would pause longest.

They were not masters. That was good. Mastery would be a dangerous idea in such a place. They were quick hands, wet eyes, remembered routes, grudges, tricks, leaf cups, stones in tree forks. They made moments and let them go. In this, they were wiser than many peoples that would come later.

The power would not give them fire.

Not yet.

Fire would make them proud too early. Fire would open the dark in a way the dark would not forgive. Fire would ask for cleared ground, dry stores, guarded embers, ownership, punishment. The basin was too wet for it, and they were too young for the kind of loneliness fire can create.

The power would not give them words for gods.

It would not give them walls, laws, tools that outlived need, or the fever of keeping.

It would give them one small thing only.

A gesture.

One evening after a storm, when a branch had broken and left a high gap over a swollen runnel, an infant climber would slip. Its mother would catch it by one wrist, but the bark would shear under her feet. Another female, old and scarred, would reach not for the infant but for the mother’s shoulder. A young male would seize the old female’s hind limb. Two more would catch him. For a moment they would be one trembling animal stretched between branches, all breath and panic above brown water.

This had happened before in other forms. Sometimes they saved one another. Sometimes they did not.

But this time, when they pulled the infant back, the old female would press her pale palm to the infant’s chest, then to her own, then outward toward the troop.

A meaningless motion.

A calming motion.

A mark of shared danger.

The next season, another would use it after a predator took a juvenile at the edge of the flats. Palm to chest. Palm outward. Not possession. Not name. Not promise. Only: felt here, carried there.

In time the gesture would spread through one troop, then another, altered by habit and mood. It would be used after floods, after births, after the finding of fruit, after the death of an old one whose teeth had worn down. It would not make them peaceful. They would still bite, steal, mate, threaten, abandon, compete. But somewhere inside their quick weatherwise intelligence, a new path would open:

What happens to one body may matter to more than that body.

That would be enough.

Not civilization. Not morality written in thunder. Only the first root hair of compassion.

The sixth act would be for the flowers.

The power would delight in them: blossoms that opened to coughs, to screams, to throat-drums, to the vibration of wings. It would touch them lightly and deepen their listening.

One flower would learn the footfall of tusked animals and release scent only after the fern beds were churned.

One would answer the wingbeat of the gray mosquito birds and feed them nectar so their nests near pools grew stronger.

One would open to the booming night hunters, not to serve the hunters, but to feed the moths that fed the frogs that fed the snakes that fed the hook-beaked birds that dropped seeds far from parent trunks.

The basin already knew interdependence through appetite. The power would add a few threads of interdependence through timing.

Not harmony.

Harmony is too clean a word for such a place.

A chord, perhaps. Dissonant, wet, immense.

The seventh act would be against future greed.

Because the power was wise, it would see not only the basin as it was, but the shadows of what might someday come: upright beings with harder tools, dry fire, memory stored outside the body, hunger that did not end when the belly filled. Beings who might see the dark timber and think, Mine. See the animals and think, Meat. See the red earth and think, Cut. See the rivers and think, Straighten. See the basin’s provisional genius and mistake it for disorder.

The power would not forbid them.

A forbidden place becomes a prize.

Instead it would make the basin difficult to simplify.

Paths would not hold.

Cleared ground would sicken with biting flies unless replanted.

Timber cut in quantity would warp, split, and host beetles that ruined careless stores.

Straight ditches would fill with red algae and collapse at the first hard rain.

Animals driven from one valley would return through another.

Seeds would sleep in mud for years and rise after burning.

Maps would become old quickly.

Those who came gently, taking little, listening long, moving with the water rather than against it, would survive. Those who came to command would find themselves commanding mud, fever, rot, and rain — poor servants, all of them.

This would not be vengeance.

It would be protection by complexity.

Finally, the power would bless the basin itself.

Not with peace.

With continuance.

It would place no crown on the canopy, no throne in the central flats. It would choose no single species as beloved above the rest. It would not say to the climbers, You are the purpose. It would not say to the predators, You are the strength. It would not say to the flowers, You are the beauty. It would not say to the fungi, You are the truth.

It would say, without words:

Be many.  
Break and answer.  
Feed and be fed.  
Change, but do not forget how to begin again.

Afterward, nothing obvious would be different.

Rain would still come as if thrown from a height. The rivers would still argue in the mud. Vines would still climb the unwary. The metallic birds would crash and scream. The tusked animals would ruin fern beds into nurseries. The throat-drummers would call from black stone islands until water trembled around their feet.

A fawn would still vanish beneath a too-perfect reflection.

A tree would still fall and crush what sheltered under it.

A climber would still die of a bite gone bad.

A nest would still be raided.

A carcass would still become a congregation.

But here and there, more life would pass through the narrow places.

A pool would last until tadpoles found legs.

A troop would climb before the bank gave way.

A fungal bloom would stop at fallen wood and not take the living grove.

A flower would open at the right vibration, and somewhere down the chain a hungry mouth would be fed.

An old climber, scarred and damp in the green darkness, would touch her palm to her chest and then outward, and a younger one would understand without knowing what understanding was.

The power would withdraw then, though not entirely.

It would remain as wise powers do: not above the world, but hidden inside its better chances.

In the hidden vein of water.

In the root that holds one more storm.

In the silence before flood.

In the restraint of rot.

In the gesture that says, briefly and imperfectly, you are not alone in the breaking.

And the basin would go on becoming.
