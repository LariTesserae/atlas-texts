The power would come first as attention.

Not as thunder. Not as a break in the clouds, not as a golden animal standing on a ridge, not as a voice that makes the pools shiver. It would not arrive in a form that asked to be witnessed. A place like this has no room for spectacle. Everything here that survives has survived by refusing extravagance.

So the power would become patient enough to be mistaken for weather.

It would lower itself into the basin as a long listening.

It would learn the melt-lines, the buried courses of water under scree, the places where the frost breaks stone too quickly and the places where stone has become too tight to accept root. It would attend to the old paths of goats and sheep, the whistle-posts of marmots, the talus chambers where pikas keep their small winter suns made of dried leaves. It would note which pools freeze to the bottom, which hold a little dark water under ice, which mud margins hatch midges early, which sedge mats are only three storms away from being torn loose.

It would not pity the basin for being severe.

That would be the first wisdom.

The basin is not a failed meadow. It is not an unfinished garden waiting for generosity. Its spareness is not a wound. Its cold is not cruelty. Its distances are not loneliness in the human sense. The place is young, raw, exacting, and alive by narrow permissions. To love it well would not mean making it easy.

The power would understand that too much kindness, poured carelessly, is another kind of violence.

To warm it too much would drown the old bargains.  
To soften the wind would invite height where height does not belong.  
To thicken the soil suddenly would erase the lichens’ long labor.  
To make the pools rich would alter the insects, then the birds, then the mouths that depend on them.  
To remove death would starve ravens, beetles, roots, and the slow darkening of mud.  
To protect every lamb would unmake the eagle.  
To save every pika would empty the fox.  
To stop the freeze would silence the mountain’s engine.

So the benevolent power would not abolish hardship.

It would make hardship clean.

It would move through the basin with the smallest possible corrections.

Where a seep had begun to poison itself with too much mineral heat from a new fracture, the power would cool it by a fraction, not enough to stop its breath, only enough that moss could continue holding there. Where a slope had loosened beyond the ordinary rightness of falling stone, threatening to bury an entire talus colony before the young pikas could disperse, it would delay one slab through a winter. Not forever. Only long enough for life to answer.

Where a snowfield, under a strange season’s hunger, melted too fast and sent a cutting flood through the sedge rims, the power would ask the night for one more frost. The frost would come. Thin, temporary, enough to hold the water back until dawn could divide it into gentler threads.

Where the old goat trail crossed a fresh crack hidden by rime, the power would let the lead nanny smell something wrong in the ice. She would pause. The herd would bunch behind her, irritated, steaming, alive. Then she would choose the higher ledge.

Where a clutch of shorebird eggs lay too exposed after a windstorm stripped the mud of its disguising grit, the power would not build a wall around them. It would merely turn a raven’s curiosity toward a different smell under snow: an older carcass, sealed, ready to be opened. The raven would go there instead, black and clever, well-fed by death that had already finished its dying.

Where a marmot slept too shallow beneath a snowpack made treacherous by rain and refreeze, the power would press no hand into the burrow. It would thicken the drift at the entrance by a little wandering spindrift. Insulation, not rescue. The marmot would burn its stored summer more slowly.

Where a dwarf willow had survived twenty seasons and was about to be torn out by the hoof of a panicked yearling, the power would loosen a pebble elsewhere. The pebble would click. The yearling would start, shift its foot, and the willow would remain: flat, bitten, stubborn, continuing.

These would be its miracles: almost nothing.

A fraction of timing.  
A redirection of appetite.  
A feather of snow.  
A hesitation.  
A stone falling a breath later than it might have fallen.  
A fog remaining until the sun no longer harmed what had just emerged.  
A thaw arriving not warmer, but more evenly.  
A seed lodged one finger-width deeper in mud.  
A carcass uncovered when the basin needed it most.

The power would bless the lichens by leaving them slow.

It would not hurry the orange coin-colonies outward. It would not grant them lushness or sudden spread. It would give them only continuity: fewer pointless scrapings by freak hail, fewer seasons in which the wet came and vanished before their bodies could drink. It would let them keep their ancient conversation with stone, because that conversation is not improved by haste.

It would bless the moss by preserving small shade.

Not broad shade, not forest shade, not the deep green mercy of low valleys. Only the shadow of a leaning rock kept from splitting one more year. Only a snowbank withdrawing unevenly so that melt dripped through the moss instead of tearing it loose. The moss would hold water. The water would hold larvae. The larvae would feed birds. The birds would carry the basin’s brief abundance into the air.

It would bless the sedges by strengthening roots, but not stems.

No tall grass would rise here under its care. Nothing soft and waving would be imported from gentler lands. The sedges would remain clenched, low, wind-combed, severe. But their roots would bind one more pinch of silt. One more. Then one more after that. Enough that the mud margin would not vanish in a single wet year. Enough that future flowers would have a seam to enter.

It would bless the flowers by making them visible at the right moment.

A purple saxifrage would open under a sky too dim for many insects, and the power would part the cloud for seven minutes. Not dramatically. No shaft of holy light. Just a thinning. A little more ultraviolet. A midge would turn. Then another. Pollen would move. Seed would become possible.

It would bless the pikas with accuracy.

Their hay would dry before the wet returned. Their calls would carry clearly enough through fog. One young pika, foolish with summer, would be nudged by scent back toward the talus before the falcon read its movement. Another would not be spared. The falcon too is part of the basin’s truth. Benevolence would not mean forbidding hunger. It would mean that hunger did not become wasteful.

It would bless the fox with enough.

A fox that takes beyond need is usually not wicked; it is frightened by the memory of emptiness. The power would not make the fox gentle. It would keep the fox competent. It would let her find beetles under the warm stone, a ptarmigan egg already chilled past hatching, a hare track at the saddle after three lean nights. She would feed her kits. She would still be fox: narrow, bright, dangerous, necessary.

It would bless the ravens with honest dead.

Not easy nests. Not helpless abundance. But carcasses appearing in time to matter. Bones opened when marrow still remembers fat. A lamb lost to exposure where the slope can be reached. A goat old enough to die becoming, after death, many lives distributed by black beaks across stone and snow.

It would bless the eagles with height and restraint.

Wind would hold under their wings. Their eyes would remain terrible. Their hunger would stay precise. When they struck, they would strike truly. When they missed, the basin would keep the memory of wings in every small animal’s blood.

And the water—

The power would speak most carefully to water.

Water is already the basin’s most changeable god. To command it would be foolish. To bribe it would be worse. The power would instead become one of its counselors.

It would teach a rill to divide before it became a wound.  
It would keep a buried thread from sealing entirely with ice.  
It would persuade one pool to remain cloudy, because opacity shelters larvae from beaks.  
It would persuade another to clear, because shorebirds need to see what moves beneath the film.  
It would let frost enter cracks where stone must become gravel, but hold it back where a whole slope would fail before life could use the fall.

It would not stop erosion.

It would give erosion manners.

In the evenings, when cloud lowered and the pools became dull mirrors, the power would be most present. Not visible, but present in the way the living things would feel the world hold together for another night.

A ptarmigan settling into a scrape would find the gravel just dry enough beneath her breast.  
A midge swarm would fall before the wind sharpened.  
A lamb would press against its mother on a ledge where the rock still held the day’s little heat.  
A beetle under a stone would survive the first needle of frost.  
A sedge root would remain clasped around its grain of beginning.

No hymn would rise. No creature would understand itself as blessed. That too would be mercy. To burden them with gratitude would be to make them human, and they have their own excellences.

The basin would continue.

It would freeze. It would thaw. It would kill. It would feed. It would grind itself into soil by measures too small for pride. It would remain austere, unfinished, full of hidden work.

But there would be, after the power’s attention, a slight increase in rightness.

Not safety.  
Not comfort.  
Not abundance.  
Rightness.

The young would not always live, but enough would.  
The old would not be preserved past their season, but their bodies would enter use.  
The stone would break, but not meaninglessly.  
The water would cut, but also carry.  
The cold would judge, but not with malice.  
The brief summer would remain brief, but it would arrive with doors opened in the proper order.

And perhaps, once in a very long while, the power would allow itself one visible act.

Not for display.

Only because beauty too is part of wisdom.

One morning after a night of hard frost, the basin would wake under a dim sky. Every sedge blade would carry rime. Every boulder would be rimmed in silver. The pools would wear skins of new ice thin enough to hold the gray light. The goats would stand high on the black shoulders, breathing steam. Ravens would cross the air without calling. Beneath the stones, pikas would be alive in their hay-dark chambers. In the mud, larvae would wait. Under lichens, the stone would continue its surrender.

Then the cloud lid would open for a moment.

The sun would not warm the basin much. It would not change the law. But it would touch the quartz seams, the ice needles, the wet rock, the orange lichens, the white snowfields, the black wings, the small purple flowers still half-closed against the cold.

For one minute, the unfinished world would shine as if already complete.

Then the cloud would move back over the light.

The basin would return to its dim labor.

And the benevolent power would remain there, not as ruler, not as gardener, not as savior, but as a vast and careful witness—keeping faith with beginnings, protecting the difficulty that made them true, and helping life write its first low, patient handwriting across the stone.
