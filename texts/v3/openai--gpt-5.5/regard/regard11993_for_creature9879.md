It would not descend as a god.

That would be the first mercy.

A wise power, seeing this high hollow biosphere—its black cold, its continent of living struts and empty gulfs, its moss-wrapped people crossing darkness by touch and song—would understand that too much help is a form of violence. To arrive with sun, metal, roads, certainty, commandments, or rescue would be to misunderstand the place entirely.

This world is not a problem.

It is severe, young, unfinished, dangerous, but not broken.

So the power would come quietly.

It would come first as attention.

Not a face in the fog. Not a voice in the trunks. Not a warm palace grown overnight from luminous vines. Only a listening so deep that, for the first time in the age of the plateau, the place would be heard all the way through.

It would listen to the condensation gathering on the fibrous skins of the great plants.

It would listen to the worm-thunder beneath the arches.

It would listen to the clicking herds, the gliders, the predators waiting in the sudden silences.

It would listen to the moss-clad wanderers tapping warnings through living wood, humming grief into hollows, sending small brave meanings across distances that devoured sound.

And because it was benevolent, it would love them without needing them to know.

Because it was wise, it would not ask to be loved back.

---

The first change would be nearly imperceptible.

A child, leaping between two slick green ribs over a gulf of blue-black fog, would misjudge the distance. Not by much. Only by the width of a hand.

In the old world, that would be enough.

The child’s fingers would claw at wet bark, fail, scrape, vanish.

But on this day, the fall would hesitate.

Not stop.

Not reverse.

Only hesitate, as falls sometimes already did in that strange place—but this time with a softness behind it, a cupping in the air, a half-breath of mercy. The child’s tail would catch a hanging root. Their body would slam hard enough to bruise, hard enough to teach, but not hard enough to end.

The family would hear the impact. The scrambling. The weak click of terror.

They would pull the child up and wrap them in moss-skin and scold them in trembling taps.

Later, when the child slept, the oldest among them would press an ear to the arch and listen.

The wood would pulse as always.

The wind would roar as always.

No god would speak.

But the elder would think: the Weight turned its face aside.

And perhaps that would be enough.

---

The power would not remove death.

Death belongs even in loved places.

Predators would still hunt. Storms would still split towers. Worms would still undermine crossings. Cold would still sharpen hunger. The people would still need courage, memory, skill, luck, and one another.

But the power would begin to make the world less wasteful in its cruelties.

Not gentle.

Only less careless.

A bridge that would have failed without warning would begin to click hours before breaking, a tiny insectile ticking carried through its fibers. Those who knew how to listen would hear it.

A valley where condensation vanished too quickly would grow a new species of pale cup-moss, not abundant, not miraculous, but faithful enough to hold dew through the long cold hours before dawn.

A predator whose hunting range had widened beyond balance after a season of strange warmth would find its favored nesting knots threaded with bitter fungus, encouraging it elsewhere before it emptied three migration paths.

A sickness moving through the moss-skins would meet, in the hollows of certain trunks, an answering lichen with a silver underside. No one would be told. Someone would discover it by accident, pressing fevered skin against it while hiding from rain. The cure would not be perfect. It would simply be present.

This would be the power’s way:

not salvation as conquest,

but mercy hidden inside ecology.

---

It would touch the plants next.

Not to make them sentient if they were not.

Not to awaken them into suffering.

That would be arrogance.

Instead it would deepen what was already there: response, resilience, relation.

The vast plant-arches would learn, very slowly, to scar in patterns that strengthened common paths. Places crossed often by the moss-clad wanderers would thicken by fractions. Not into roads. Never into roads. Only into remembered tendencies: a grip-ridge here, a knuckle of bark there, a hollow where a body might shelter from hail.

Where families paused often to sleep, certain mosses would become warmer. Where mourners keened, fungal lights would glow faintly for a few nights after, as if grief had left a phosphorescent bruise.

The people would notice.

Of course they would notice.

They noticed everything.

But they would not call it a gift.

They might call it listening-back.

They might say the world had begun to answer with its skin.

And their rituals would change.

Not dramatically. Nothing here changed dramatically unless it broke.

A traveler leaving a camp would place their palm against the nearest trunk and tap three times: I go. I remember. Hold what you can.

Children would be taught to thank warm moss, but not to trust it blindly.

Elders would argue whether the Deep Surmounting had grown kinder, or merely more complex.

The practical ones would say, “Kindness is complexity that does not kill us.”

The poets would hum that into the walls.

---

The power would be especially careful with knowledge.

It would not give them metallurgy.

There is no mercy in handing a people tools that require mines where there are no mines, furnaces where fire is precious, permanence where movement is life.

It would not write laws in stone.

There is no stone that matters here.

It would not teach them to dominate the worms, harvest the striders en masse, dam the condensation, or carve cities into the living towers until the whole continent became a wound.

Instead, it would sharpen the forms of knowing they already possessed.

Dreams would become a little more useful.

Not prophetic in the crude sense. No map of the future, no list of coming disasters. But a dreamer sleeping with their ear against a root might wake with the taste of rain in their mouth six hours before rain arrived. A child fevered from cold might dream of the silver lichen. A solitary wanderer might hear, in sleep, the low pulse of a distant family’s mourning song and wake knowing which direction not to travel.

Memory-caves would deepen.

The hollows where stories were hummed would begin to hold rhythm more faithfully. A song placed there by one generation could be recovered by the next—not as words, not exactly, but as pattern. A sequence of taps. A caution. A route. A name.

This would change them, but not corrupt them.

Their culture already trusted living memory more than dead inscription. The power would only make the living world a better keeper.

And because wisdom knows that memory can become a chain, the power would ensure forgetting remained possible.

No story would last forever.

Even the moss-caves would blur.

Even sacred warnings would fade if no one renewed them with breath, body, and need.

Truth would remain relational.

Knowledge would remain carried.

Nothing would become untouchable.

---

There would be one place, though, where the power would act more boldly.

Far within the high dark, where three continent-sized plant ridges crossed above an abyss so deep that fog seemed to rise from a second sky below, there was a wound in the world’s hidden balance.

The strange gravity of the region—the lingering leaps, the softened falls, the sudden hardening of weight—had a source there. Not a machine. Not a curse. More like an old knot in the planet’s dreaming, a torsion where mass, growth, cold, and some stranger law had braided poorly.

Most of the time it merely made the world uncanny.

Sometimes it killed without pattern.

A family would cross an arch crossed a hundred times before, and suddenly their bodies would grow heavy as grief. Moss would tear. Fingers would fail. Three would fall.

Elsewhere, a predator would leap and never land, hanging too long in the air, screaming until wind took it sideways into a trunk.

Water would bead upward. Spores would sink like stones. Infants would wake crying because their own limbs felt wrong.

The people called this The Weight.

They appeased it.

They feared it.

They respected it because respect was safer than understanding.

The wise power would not erase The Weight. It was too woven into the place. The gliders depended on it. The bridge-plants had grown around it. The people’s bodies, myths, and movements belonged partly to its strangeness.

But it would go to the knot beneath the three ridges.

And there, without thunder, without light, without witnesses, it would loosen one strand.

Only one.

Across the plateau, no one would know.

But from that season forward, The Weight would still be strange, yet less maliciously random. Its moods would gather signs before arriving.

A metallic taste in the fog.

A blue dimming of fungal lights.

A tremor like a held breath in the plant-bones.

Enough warning for the attentive.

Not enough certainty for arrogance.

That is how a wise power would bless a dangerous world: not by making it safe, but by making its dangers answerable to wisdom.

---

Eventually, the people would make a myth.

They always had.

They would not agree on it.

They never had.

One cluster would tell of a traveler so vast it could only move by being everywhere still. It came seeking warmth, found none, and so taught the cold to hold hands with the living.

Another would tell of a listening hollow beneath all hollows, where every lost footstep landed softly and became a root.

Another would say no new power had come at all; the world had simply matured. The plateau was young. Its first violence had begun to settle. Mercy was only growth seen from inside danger.

The solitary walkers would have their own version.

They would say that sometimes, when alone beyond all family-song, they felt a presence moving beside them across the arches—not seen, not heard, not warm, but companionable. It never guided their feet. It never chose their path. It only made solitude less absolute.

Children would ask what it looked like.

The walkers would ripple their faces in amusement and fear.

“Like the pause before you fall,” they would say.

“Like moss remembering your weight.”

“Like the dark deciding not to close.”

---

And the benevolent power?

It would remain.

Not as ruler.

Not as savior.

As witness, gardener, and guest.

It would grieve every death it did not prevent, and prevent only those deaths that taught nothing, fed nothing, balanced nothing, meant nothing.

It would allow hunger, but lessen famine.

Allow fear, but strengthen warning.

Allow loneliness, but deepen the songs that cross distance.

Allow endings, but give memory a place to root.

It would never make the moss-clad people into something other than themselves. It would not smooth their spindled limbs, brighten their world into day, gather them into cities, lengthen their lives beyond recognition, or tell them their small fires were primitive and poor.

It would see their fires for what they were:

brief stars,

made by hand,

guarded against wind,

shared in darkness.

And loving them, it would protect the darkness too.

Because they were not waiting to be lifted out of their world.

They were of it.

They belonged to the cold arches, the condensation, the trembling worm-roads, the crude shelters, the tapped warnings, the luminous eyes, the chosen families, the grief-songs fading into moss.

A lesser power would give them paradise.

A wiser one would give them continuance.

So the high hollow world would remain itself: vast, wet, cold, echoing, perilous, alive.

But here and there, in ways too subtle to worship and too consistent to ignore, mercy would enter the pattern.

A fall would slow.

A bridge would warn.

A story would last one generation longer.

A child would wake from a dream and lead their fevered sibling to silver lichen.

A lone wanderer, lost beyond all known arches, would tap despair into a trunk and feel, faintly, from very far away, an answering pulse:

Not words.

Not command.

Only this:

You are heard.

And in that place, where sound is devoured by distance, to be heard would be almost enough.
