A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a conqueror.

It would not split the mountain open, flood the labyrinth with sunlight, and declare the Shardborn saved. It would understand that salvation forced upon a people can become another kind of disaster. The Shardborn are not merely victims of the mountain; they are shaped by it, hardened by it, and in some strange way beloved by it. Their songs, their scars, their cunning machines, their etched maps and wire ornaments—all of these belong to the dark crystalline heart.

So the power would begin by listening.

Not with ears, because the mountain’s language is older than sound.

It would listen to the Hollow Pulse.

It would place its attention deep beneath the deepest tunnels, beneath the molten rivulets and the ancient ruins fused into stone, beneath the forgotten machines too large for the Shardborn to name. There, in the buried center of things, it would find the source of the irregular vibration: not merely a heartbeat, not merely an engine, but both. A vast mechanism, ancient beyond memory, still laboring. Once it may have regulated pressure, heat, mineral flow, air exchange, perhaps even the slow growth of the crystalline labyrinth itself. Now it stutters. It overheats. It misfires. When it spasms, tunnels collapse. When it exhales, poisonous vapor leaks upward. When it dreams, metal bleeds from the walls.

The wise power would not destroy it.

It would lay a hand of thought upon the failing engine-heart and soothe it.

Not repair it fully—not yet. A sudden restoration might convulse the mountain, drowning the Shardborn in miracles too large to survive. Instead it would adjust one rhythm at a time. A fraction less pressure here. A steadier venting there. A redirection of molten channels away from inhabited warrens. The Hollow Pulse would remain, but its beat would become less erratic.

The Shardborn would notice first in omens.

A tunnel that should have collapsed would hold.

A geyser of superheated gas would hiss warning for three breaths before erupting, long enough for a child to be pulled away.

A bridge, rusted to lace, would fail only after the last worker crossed it.

Deep-miners would whisper that the mountain had begun to hesitate before killing them.

That would be the first gift: not safety, but mercy.

The second gift would be water.

Not rivers. Not lakes. Those would be too violent for this place, too alien, too quickly fought over. Instead the power would encourage what already almost existed: condensation on cold metal, hidden pockets of mineral-bound moisture, acidic beads gathered from ventilation pipes. It would teach the mountain to sweat cleanly.

In the upper reaches, thin films of pure water would begin forming at dawnless intervals upon certain pale crystals. At first the Shardborn would think them poison. Then a starving mechanic, too thirsty to care, would touch her tongue to one and weep because it did not burn.

The crystals would be rare, then less rare.

They would grow only in places where airflow was maintained and heat was balanced, rewarding cooperation without announcing itself as law. Clans that kept vents clear would find more water. Clans that sabotaged shared passages would find the crystals clouded and dry. Not punishment. Consequence. A lesson written in mineral and breath.

The third gift would be air.

The power would stir the bioluminescent lichens and fungal colonies toward a new symbiosis. It would not replace them with green leaves or surface forests. Instead it would awaken latent capacities within them. Some lichens would begin binding metallic particulates from the air into harmless nodules. Some fungi would drink fumes and exhale cleaner vapor. Others would grow in soft mats over razor fractures, warning by color when stone beneath them shifted.

The Shardborn would discover, through trial and argument and accident, that cultivating certain growths near dwellings reduced lung-sickness. Children born in those warrens would cough less. Elders would live long enough to teach.

A new profession would emerge: not miner, not smith, not scavenger, but tender.

At first the tenders would be mocked. Then fought over. Then protected.

They would learn the moods of lichen-light: blue for clean air, green for mineral richness, white for danger, violet for deep pressure building in the stone. They would braid fungal tendrils through ventilation grates and speak to them in the clipped Shardborn dialect, half command, half prayer.

The fourth gift would be knowledge—but carefully given.

The wise power would not thunder truth from above. Truth dropped whole can crush as surely as rock. Instead it would place clues where curious hands could find them.

An old wall, long thought natural, would shed a layer of slag and reveal etchings beneath: diagrams of pressure chambers, cooling ducts, maintenance shafts, the ancient engine-heart. A broken machine would restart briefly, projecting a ghost-image of the mountain before it became a labyrinth. A child tracing patterns in dust would unconsciously reproduce a map of safe tunnels no living Shardborn had walked.

The old ruins would begin to speak in fragments.

Not enough to answer everything.

Enough to ask better questions.

The Shardborn would learn that they were not refuse, not accidents, not vermin in the corpse of another age. Their ancestors had been brought—or had come—into the mountain as stewards, workers, perhaps refugees. The great machine was not built to enslave them. It was built to keep something alive: the mountain, the deep chambers, maybe even the people themselves.

But neglect, catastrophe, greed, war, or simple time had shattered the covenant.

The current cruelty of the labyrinth was not destiny.

It was malfunction mistaken for law.

This revelation would change them more than any miracle.

Some would deny it. Some would weaponize it. Some would claim divine right over the engine-heart. There would be conflict. The wise power would not prevent every argument, because a people must wrestle with truth to own it. But it would keep the worst ambitions from becoming annihilation.

Weapons would jam at moments of massacre.

Poisoned vents would reverse before whole warrens died.

A warlord descending toward the engine-heart with explosives would hear, in the Hollow Pulse, the voice of his dead sister asking him whether ashes could refine ore.

He might turn back.

Or he might not.

The power would allow choice.

But it would make cruelty harder to mistake for strength.

The fifth gift would be dreams.

Shardborn sleep would become, in rare moments, luminous.

A deep-miner with fused fingers would dream of tools designed not for extraction but restoration. Upon waking, she would sketch them frantically on a slab of cooled slag.

A clan-chief who had never trusted another living soul would dream he was trapped beneath a collapse, breathing through a pipe held open by the hands of enemies.

A child born in the hottest lower strata would dream of a sound beneath the Hollow Pulse: a second rhythm, softer, like many feet crossing bridges together.

These dreams would not command.

They would invite.

Over time, the Shardborn would begin to gather in neutral chambers where the new water-crystals grew. Not out of idealism at first, but necessity. They would bring disputes there because no one wanted blood spilled near clean water. They would establish rules: no blades drawn within sight of the pale crystals; no sabotage of shared vents; no claim over lichen beds without tending duties; no tunnel sealed if voices still sounded behind it.

Pragmatism would become ethics.

Ethics would become tradition.

Tradition would become identity.

The three imperatives—dig, refine, survive—would not vanish. They would change.

Dig, but shore the passage behind you.

Refine, but poison neither air nor neighbor.

Survive, but not alone.

The sixth gift would be beauty made useful, and usefulness made beautiful.

The power would deepen the glow of certain mineral veins so that dangerous paths could be marked without wasting fuel. It would cause slag, when cooled under careful conditions, to form flexible glass-ceramic sheets—strong enough for windows, shields, lenses. It would guide fungal leather to become lighter, warmer, more durable. It would teach crystalline dust to settle into patterns that revealed air currents.

Shardborn artisans would flourish.

Their wall-etchings would become more than maps and legends. They would become civic memory. A collapsed tunnel would be carved with the names of those lost and the engineering lesson learned. A repaired bridge would bear metal charms from every clan that helped build it. Children would wear polished beads made from harmless bound toxins, not as armor against the mountain, but as proof that danger could be transformed.

Music would change, too.

Where once sound was sparse—the clang, the thrum, the creak—there would arise deliberate rhythms tapped on pipe and crystal. Work songs timed to ventilation cycles. Lullabies mimicking the steadied Hollow Pulse. Warning codes struck across catwalks. Festival music played on tuned sheets of slag-glass, ringing through caverns in clear, cold tones like stars heard underwater.

The mountain would remain hard.

But hardness would no longer be the only truth.

The seventh gift would be healing.

Not the erasure of every scar. Scars can be memory. But the power would ease the needless suffering: lungs clogged by metal dust, eyes burned by flare, children twisted by poisoned strata, minds broken by constant terror.

It would do this through discoverable means.

A fungus that, prepared correctly, drew metallic sickness from blood.

A mineral powder that sealed burns without infection.

A resonance chamber where certain harmful mutations slowed or stabilized.

A springless vapor still that produced medicinal condensate from hot vents.

The healers would become as honored as smiths.

Perhaps more feared, at first. In a culture built on scarcity, those who can mend life hold dangerous wealth. But the wise power would quietly ensure healing knowledge was hard to monopolize. Recipes would appear in multiple places. Dreams would come to rival clans. Children would hum the preparation rhythms while playing.

No single faction would own mercy.

The eighth gift would be an opening.

Not to the surface immediately. That, too, would be too much. The Shardborn’s eyes are made for dimness; their lungs for filtered air; their culture for enclosed survival. A sudden sky might terrify them more than free them.

Instead, one remote upper tunnel would slowly clear.

At its end, behind layers of translucent crystal, there would be a chamber unlike any other: vast, quiet, filled with cold blue light from beyond the mountain. Not open air, not yet. A threshold. A place where the Shardborn could stand and see the shadow of clouds moving across the crystal wall.

The first to find it would fall silent.

They would think it another machine.

Then another world.

Then a wound in the mountain.

Pilgrimages would begin.

Some would hate it. Some would worship it. Some would merely sit, unable to speak, watching the light change. Artists would try to carve it. Engineers would try to measure it. Children would press their wide reflective eyes to the crystal and ask what kind of furnace made such brightness.

The power would not break the wall.

It would let them decide, generation by generation, whether they wished to open it.

Because wisdom knows that freedom offered too early can feel like exile.

The ninth gift would be restraint.

This may be the greatest one.

The benevolent power would not make the Shardborn innocent. It would not strip them of ambition, anger, cunning, rivalry, pride, or fear. These are part of personhood, and personhood is sacred even when inconvenient. It would not turn the labyrinth into a garden, nor the clans into saints, nor the mountain into a cradle without edges.

There would still be accidents.

There would still be bad leaders.

There would still be greed.

There would still be tunnels where the lichen glows the wrong color and something ancient shifts in the dark.

But disaster would no longer be the only teacher.

A child could be born with a reasonable expectation of adulthood.

An elder could die of age rather than collapse or fumes.

A clan could prosper more by maintaining shared systems than by raiding.

A worker could look at a machine and imagine not only how to keep it running one more day, but how to make it kinder to those who serve it.

That is how the wise power would measure success: not by the absence of struggle, but by the presence of futures.

And perhaps, after many years, the Shardborn would gather in the chamber of pale crystals where water forms cleanly from the air. Representatives from upper clans, deep-miners, tenders, healers, scavengers, smiths, children carrying lamps, elders leaning on slag-glass canes. They would listen to the Hollow Pulse, now steadier than any living memory recalls.

One among them would say, “The mountain is changing.”

Another would answer, “No. We are learning how to hear it.”

And in the deep place beneath them, where the ancient engine-heart turns through its long labor, the benevolent power would withdraw a little.

Not abandon.

Never abandon.

But make room.

For the Shardborn would have begun to become stewards again.

The labyrinth would still glitter with sharp crystal and molten metal. The air would still taste faintly of iron. The bridges would still creak over chasms. The world would remain strange, severe, and beautiful.

But now, in hidden places, water would bead like mercy.

Lichen would glow over safe paths.

Old machines would answer careful hands.

And when the Hollow Pulse moved through the mountain, the Shardborn would no longer hear only an omen of collapse.

They would hear a question.

They would hear an invitation.

They would hear, beneath stone and slag and ancient ruin, the possibility of home.
