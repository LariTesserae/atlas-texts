It would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder is rude here. Thunder is a hand shoved against a sleeping shoulder. Thunder loosens tabs of stone and makes children forget the language of stillness.

So the power would come first as patience.

It would stand nowhere in particular: in the bead of dew trembling on a shale edge, in the pale cricket’s pause, in the cool between one drop and the next. It would learn the rift before touching it. It would listen to the small weekly shivers and know which were harmless speech and which were teeth beginning to part. It would watch the First to Arrive After the Opening as they moved through their unfinished world with palms to stone, not worshipping, not pleading, simply making arrangements with danger.

And because it is wise, it would not make the rift safe.

Not entirely.

To make it safe would be to erase it. To mortar every fracture, to pin every ceiling sheet, to flood the terraces with easy food and steady light—this would save bodies while killing the grammar by which those bodies understood their lives. The people chose a new wound in the earth because no old hand had already claimed it. A power that loves them would not become the oldest claimant of all.

So it would do smaller things.

At first, almost nothing.

A child posted as Light Shepherd would notice that one mica panel, the one with the cracked corner, caught the sun better than it should. No matter how clouds passed above, a thin ribbon of brightness would linger long enough for the potter to finish sealing a water jar.

A Seam-Runner, belly-flat in a new crevice, would feel the stone go cold under her cheek and pull back just before a hidden shard snapped loose where her throat had been.

Bats would begin roosting away from certain ceiling tabs three days before they fell.

The pale crickets would chirp twice, never once, before dangerous tremors.

No proclamation would explain this. No glowing figure would demand gratitude. The people would argue softly over whether the signs had always been there and they had only grown better at noticing.

The power would be pleased by this argument.

It would prefer skill to dependence.

Where water seeped through bitter mineral seams, it would coax a lace of living film—lichen, fungus, patient bacteria—into the pores above the cisterns. Not enough to astonish. Only enough that infants stopped coughing after rain weeks, and the elders began saying, “This gutter is kinder than that one,” and moved the clay marks accordingly.

Where moss mats struggled, it would send spores on the feet of bats: not lush abundance, not a garden that would turn the rift into a meadow, but a tougher green that could live on stone-dust, guano, and borrowed light. The moss would thicken by a finger’s breadth. That would be enough to make porridge twice instead of once between hunts.

It would speak to no one in words.

Words from powers become laws too quickly.

Instead, it would give them patterns.

A girl grinding cricket legs for infant dust would notice that clay mixed with ash and powdered shell did not crack so badly in the firing pit.

A Bellows-Foot, half-dreaming from heat, would see in the coals a shape: not a command, but a memory of air moving through a tunnel. The next kiln would be built with a narrower throat and a side vent. It would burn hotter. Not bronze yet. Not iron. Only better pots, harder gutter braces, lamps that wasted less fat.

An old member of the Quiet Council would wake before dawn with the certainty that the Ledger should not only name claims, but dangers. A second slab would be set beside the first. Thumbprints would mark not ownership, but warning: stone that rang hollow, ledges that sweated too much, cracks where bats refused to sleep.

This would change them more than any miracle.

Fresh rock would still be property, but dangerous knowledge would become communal.

That would be the power’s first true gift.

Not safety.

A custom.

The Second Sky would trouble it.

That great half-detached sheet, mica-freckled and beloved, hanging over huts and cisterns and sleeping children—there the benevolent power would come closest to open interference. It would lay its attention along the stone tabs and feel the future branching.

In one branch, the sheet fell at noon. It crushed three terraces, shattered the main cistern, and turned the hollow into a screaming place, which was the worst kind of noise.

In another, it held for forty years and became holy. Children grew old beneath it. No one dared move from under it. The people’s courage fermented into superstition.

Neither branch pleased the power.

So, on a night of hard rain, when every roof-mate was already awake and all hides were drawn against the cataracts, it would ask the water to be heavy in the right seams and light in the wrong ones. It would ask the tremor rising from below to arrive not as a fist, but as a palm turning over.

The Second Sky would fall.

But slowly, for stone.

First a warning crack like a giant taking one careful breath.

Then the bats pouring out.

Then the crickets shrilling without shame.

Then the whole rift alive with gesture: flat palms downward, children gathered, ladders pulled, fires smothered, elders moving faster than anyone had believed they could.

The sheet would descend in three pieces instead of one.

One would wedge against the far wall, becoming a new bridge broad enough for two people to pass without turning sideways.

One would break into clean slabs, good for roofs and cistern covers.

The largest would fall slantwise into the lowest hollow, where it would seal a leaking basin and make, after three rains, the first deep pool the people had ever possessed.

No one would ascend upon it into another openness.

Instead, they would climb onto it the next morning and find themselves still here.

That would be revelation enough.

Some would weep. Some would laugh silently until their ribs hurt. Children would toss pebbles onto its upper face and hear no returning thrum, only the small honest clicks of rock that had become floor.

The power would withdraw a little then.

Not from indifference, but from respect.

A benevolent power must know when help becomes occupation.

It would leave behind no temple, but certain consequences.

The turquoise phosphor along new fractures would grow brighter on nights before rain. Adults would continue pretending not to believe in the thin figures children saw there, but they would send Seam-Runners after the glow all the same.

The bats would become bolder. Given bowls of moss gnats, they would learn the ledges of particular families. In time, children would know individual bats by torn ears and crooked wings. Guano would improve. Fungus would improve. The fragile loop would tighten.

Swallows would nest near the mouth of the rift and stitch the upper air with movement. Their droppings would carry seeds. Most seeds would fail. A few would not. One wiry fern would become ten. Ten would become a hanging green place where damp collected. Someone would name it, and in naming it begin the first ornament: a pattern scratched on clay that meant not claim, not danger, not water, but beauty.

The power would cherish that scratch.

It would do nothing to preserve it forever.

That, too, would be love.

For the rift is still a rift. The planet is still adjusting. One day the hollow may collapse, or widen, or dry, or become a canyon open to weather. The First to Arrive After the Opening may become a people with a name, or scatter into other cracks, carrying gestures, kiln-shapes, bat lore, and the habit of recording danger for everyone.

The power would not decide which future must win.

It would only widen the number of futures in which the people live long enough to choose.

And if, many years later, an elder sits beside the deep pool made by the fallen Second Sky and teaches a child the old signs, perhaps the child will ask why crickets chirp twice before bad stone, why some mica catches light after clouds, why the warning slab matters more than the claim slab.

The elder may answer:

“Because the hollow listens when we listen.”

That will be inaccurate.

It will be close enough.
