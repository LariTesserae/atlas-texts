It would not come down in brightness.

Brightness would be an error there. It would strike the wet leaves, startle the pale birds, flash on the pools, make prey break cover and sink where they should not have run. It would turn the whole plateau, for one instant, into spectacle — and spectacle is a kind of taking.

So the benevolent power would arrive as the place already understands arrival.

As condensation.

As a thickening in the gray.

As a warmth too slight to steam, passing through the mineral veins beneath the peat. As one additional drop gathering on the underside of a reed-joint, hanging there until its weight was correct.

It would spend a long time doing nothing.

Not idleness. Attention.

It would learn the plateau by remaining with it through enough cycles to stop mistaking slowness for absence. It would listen to the piston-beat under the bogs until it knew which knocks were ordinary pressure and which were strain. It would follow the hidden courses of water: vapor climbing out of ravines, seepage braiding through roots, warm mineral blood rising through black stone throats, cold rain entering moss and leaving years later as amber trickles. It would learn where the hoofed animals placed their feet. It would learn which reed colonies shivered before a flush, which pools were skins over depth, which underbog channels belonged to old bodies and which to young ones still making their first maps in darkness.

It would not ask the plateau to become wiser by becoming more human.

It would not give the moss speech.

It would not lift the great concealed creatures into the light so they could be admired.

It would not teach the grazers courage, because their caution was already a perfected knowledge.

It would not make the predators merciful, because hunger here was not cruelty but participation.

It would not dry one stone for the convenience of fire.

A lesser kindness might have tried to clarify the place — open paths, drain the bogs, name the dangers, make safe ground where safety had never been the point. A foolish compassion might have pitied the drowned, the trapped, the eaten, the slow incorporation of bodies into peat. But this power would be wise enough to understand that the plateau did not waste death, and that not every hazard is an injustice.

So at first, it would do almost nothing.

Almost.

Where an old vent had begun to choke under collapsed stone, not catastrophically, not yet, but enough that an entire reed colony trembled out of rhythm and the amphibian eggs inside its warm joints cooled before hatching, the power would loosen one buried seam by the width of a root hair.

No more.

Pressure would find its forgotten route. Warmth would return. The reeds would stiffen on the next beat. Glass-throated young would open inside their clear eggs, and none of them would know they had been spared.

Where a cliff-face, undermined by centuries of seepage, had reached the hour before falling, the power would not hold it up forever. Falling stone is one of the plateau’s oldest forms of speech. But it would delay the collapse by one rain, because in a hollow beneath that face a pale bird sat over three eggs in the dry that existed only by comparison.

One rain would be enough.

The young would hatch. Their mother would move them when the next fog came. Then the cliff would fall in the night with a long wet shudder, opening new stone to lichen, new ledges to ferns, new mineral faces to weather. Mercy would not prevent change. It would only refuse a needless loss hidden inside it.

Where a herd’s ancestral path had narrowed between two floating mats, and the old lead female had begun choosing a route that would hold adults but not calves, the power would press from below through the roots. Sedge would thicken. Moss would knit. The crossing would still be dangerous, still requiring memory and care, but it would not betray the smallest feet.

Where the underbog young moved too close to a vent that had whitened bones before, the power would not call out. They had no ears for warnings shaped like words. Instead it would alter the taste of the mineral water: a bitterness like old metal, a pressure-signal like the flank-wave of a larger body. The young would veer away into blacker channels.

No lesson would be announced. No law would be written.

The power would work only in forms the place could receive.

To moss, it would be a season of finer rain.

To fungi, a deeper patience in the rot.

To lichens, one uninterrupted century.

To the pale crustaceans clinging inside vent throats, it would be a flush that did not tear them all loose at once.

To the catlike hunter waiting beside a reed tunnel, it would be a shift of air through whiskers: not prey, not threat, only weather. The hunter would blink rain from its eyes and remain what it was.

To the old underbog creature moving beneath root and water, it would be recognized as pressure. A vastness beyond the bog would touch the vastness within the bog, and neither would need to surface.

Then, after learning the place, the power would make one larger decision.

It would hide the plateau.

Not from clouds, not from birds, not from the slow migrations of seed and spore. Not from lightning or time. Those belonged.

It would hide it from appetite that comes with maps.

Someday, perhaps, beings from below would climb toward the high wet country. Builders. Burners. Measurers. Those who see mineral terraces and think extraction; who hear piston-vents and think engine; who look upon a broad untouched plateau and call it unused.

Against them the power would raise no wall. Walls invite assault.

Instead it would deepen confusion at the edges.

Paths would turn back by plausible degrees. Instruments would gather too much moisture and disagree with themselves. Distances would lengthen in fog. Ambitious fires would fail before they began. Dreams in camps below the cliffs would fill with waterlogged roots, with the slow suffocation of peat, with the feeling of careful hooves pausing at the lip of black pools. Those who came to possess the plateau would wake with dread in their hands and no memory of why they had wanted it.

But those who came with humility — not harmlessness, because no visitor is harmless, but humility — might be allowed to enter briefly.

A botanist who knelt before touching.

A lost child who followed the grazers’ path and learned not to run.

A mourner seeking no cure but weather.

A listener.

Such visitors would not be given dominion or revelation. They would not leave with full maps. Their drawings would blur at the margins. Their samples would fail to live away from the wet. Their words would be insufficient.

But they might carry away one necessary poverty: the knowledge that the world is larger than use.

This would be the power’s true protection.

Not preservation as glass.

Not freezing the plateau outside time.

The vents would still fail, reroute, and break open. Bogs would still swallow. Predators would still take the slow and the careless. Old trees would fall into fungal custody. Herds would decline in hard years and thicken in generous ones. Underbog bodies would die unseen and feed the black channels. New flowers would learn old rhythms. Stone would continue to soften without disappearing.

The blessing would not be comfort.

It would be continuance.

Room to remain itself.

And because the benevolent power was wise, it would not stay above the plateau like a ruler.

It would enter the exchanges.

A little of it would become a habit in the rain: drops falling where young moss needed closing, not where a root mat was already too heavy.

A little would become restraint in the vents: pressure released before rupture, withheld before scalding, never enough to tame the machinery of stone.

A little would become memory in the safe paths, so that hooves found them one generation longer.

A little would become obscurity, folded into fog at the ravine mouths.

A little would become welcome for death, making sure that what fell was received completely: beetle, thread, root, mineral, green.

And perhaps, very rarely, on nights when the cloud ceiling lowered until the whole plateau seemed to be held inside one breath, the distributed intelligence of the place would feel something like companionship.

Not a voice.

Not a name.

A pressure answering pressure.

A silence that was not empty beside its own.

The reed-tubes would tremble though no flush had begun. Pools would dome for no visible body. Amphibians would give, from deep in the moss, a few clear notes spaced far apart. Pale birds in cliff hollows would open their eyes. The old hidden creatures would pause under the root mats, sensing a vast body that did not need to feed.

Then the moment would pass.

Rain would resume its small ticking.

The vents would knock below.

The moss would close over the day’s disturbances.

The plateau would continue manufacturing soil from stone, forest from vapor, motion from pressure, life from cold water and time.

And the benevolent power, having changed almost nothing that could be pointed to, would have done what wisdom does when it loves a thing already whole:

It would have guarded the conditions of its becoming.

It would have let the wet country remain wet, dangerous, patient, crowded, and free.
