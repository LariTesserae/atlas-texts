The benevolent power would not descend as a conqueror.

It would not split the cavern open to the sky, nor extinguish the furnaces, nor announce that the Forgeborn had been living wrongly in the only world they had ever known. Wisdom would recognize that Arathi is not merely a prison of heat and iron. It is a home. A culture. A body with its own pulse.

So the power would arrive quietly.

Perhaps first as a change in the air.

Not a wind, for wind has no easy path in that deep place, but a subtle breathing through the stone. The Forgeborn would notice it before they understood it: the furnaces drawing cleaner, the smoke rising with less malice, the steam no longer clinging so heavily to the lungs. In the high dark above the catwalks, old cracks in the limestone would widen by the width of a fingernail, then a hand, then a blade of light. Not enough to expose the foundry. Not enough to endanger it. Just enough for the cavern to exhale.

The power would listen.

It would listen to the pistons and the belts. To the drip of condensation from rusted beams. To the slow complaint of stressed stone. To the dreams of the Forgeborn in torpor, where they imagine machines not yet made, bridges not yet built, tools shaped with impossible grace. It would listen also to what they do not say: the fatigue under their purpose, the small grief of children raised beneath soot, the longing hidden even from themselves for coolness, silence, and unmeasured time.

Then it would speak, but not in words at first.

It would speak through the machinery.

A gear long misaligned would settle into harmony. A cracked pressure valve would whistle a warning before it burst. A conveyor belt would stop just before taking a worker’s hand. The Overseers, who can hear the foundry, would gather in alarm and awe, their soot-stained coats still, their bright eyes lifted toward the trembling beams.

The foundry would say:

**I am tired.**

And then:

**So are you.**

This would be the first miracle: not gold, not conquest, not escape.

Recognition.

The Forgeborn would argue. Of course they would. Some would call it weakness, a false whisper, an infection of the upper world. Others would weep without understanding why. The eldest Overseers would place their palms against the warm iron housings of the oldest engines and feel, beneath the vibration of labor, something like a heartbeat straining.

The benevolent power would not force belief.

Instead, it would give them one new craft.

Not an artifact fully made, but a pattern.

A design appearing overnight in soot on the limestone walls, in the condensation on glass, in the dreams of children: a system of vents, reservoirs, cooling channels, moss gardens, and pressure harmonics. A way for the foundry to remain itself while becoming less cruel to those who loved it.

The Forgeborn would understand the gift because it would come in their language: not commandment, but blueprint.

They would build.

They would carve channels through the stone to catch the hidden springs and guide them into cisterns lined with copper and basalt. They would construct cooling towers that rose through natural chimneys, releasing poison heat upward into forgotten cracks in the mountain. They would fit filters of woven metal, charcoal, and living moss into the ventilation shafts, and the moss would thrive there, silver-green and soft, spreading like a quiet blessing.

The power would touch the moss too.

Not to make it alien, but to help it remember ancient forests. It would grow in the damp corners, then along the walls, then in deliberate gardens suspended beneath catwalks. The Forgeborn children would be the first to love these gardens. They would press their soot-dark fingers into the soft growth and laugh at the impossible gentleness of it.

In time, small white fungi would appear.

Then pale root-vines.

Then blind moths.

Then tiny cave crickets whose song was almost too delicate to hear beneath the engines.

The Forgeborn would pause when they heard that sound.

A living rhythm not made by hammer, belt, or piston.

Some would find it useless. Others would secretly protect it.

The benevolent power would also alter the law of labor.

Not by decree, but by necessity. Machines once demanding constant tending would become more stable when allowed intervals of stillness. Furnaces would burn hotter and cleaner after periods of rest. Metal quenched in the new spring-water reservoirs would prove stronger when forged by hands not trembling from exhaustion.

The foundry itself would teach them:

**Rest is not abandonment.**

**Silence is not failure.**

**Maintenance is love.**

The Forgeborn, who had always known how to maintain engines, would slowly learn to maintain themselves.

Their torpor would deepen. What had once been brief survival-sleep would become something sacred. Chambers would be hollowed into cooler stone, lined with moss and hammered bronze. There, the Forgeborn would enter rest not as workers awaiting the next command, but as dreamers returning to the cavern’s older time.

In those dreams, they would meet the Great Forger.

But not as they expected.

Not a giant with a hammer of pure fire.

Not a master demanding endless output.

The Great Forger would appear as an old figure seated beside an unlit furnace, hands open, hammer laid aside.

And the Great Forger would say:

**Creation is not the same as consumption.**

**A flame that eats everything leaves no hearth.**

**A tool that breaks the hand is unfinished.**

This would become the Second Teaching.

The first had been recognition.

The second would be restraint.

The Forgeborn would not cease making. That would be another kind of death. They would still smelt ore, shape steel, carve tools, build engines, and sing in clangs and guttural harmonies. But their work would change. Their finest artisans would turn from endless production toward enduring craft. Fewer things would be made, and those things would be made worthy.

They would create blades that never thirsted for blood, but cut stone for aqueducts.

They would create braces that flexed with the mountain instead of fighting it.

They would create lamps whose light was soft as dawn filtered through amber.

They would create toys for children: little gear-beasts that walked in circles and bowed, tiny steam birds that opened copper wings.

They would create instruments.

This would surprise them most.

At first the instruments would be practical accidents: pipes tuned by ventilation, hammers ringing on graduated anvils, pressure valves that sang when opened. But the children would gather these sounds and make patterns. The elders would pretend not to listen. Then one by one, they would add their own rhythms.

The foundry, once a symphony of labor alone, would become a symphony of life.

The benevolent power would not remove their bond to Arathi.

Instead, it would make the bond mutual.

For too long, the Forgeborn had kept the foundry alive by spending themselves against it. Now the foundry would keep them alive in return. Its walls would shed less poison dust. Its springs would run clearer. Its engines would warn before failing. Its deepest chambers would open only to those who came with reverence, not greed.

And if the world above ever found them?

The power would prepare for that too.

Around Arathi, in the stone, it would lay a wise confusion. Tunnels would not lead where maps insisted they should. Echoes would mislead the violent. Greedy miners would find only blank rock, bad air, and the sudden certainty that they had forgotten something urgent at home.

But those who came in need — the lost, the exiled, the wounded, the maker whose hands longed for honest work — might find, after many trials, a warm orange glow beneath the earth.

They would be met at the threshold by Forgeborn sentries, not hostile, but steady.

No one would be allowed to own Arathi.

No one would be allowed to exploit its people.

But some might be invited to learn.

In return, the Forgeborn would learn of rain.

At first, only through stories.

Then, when they were ready, the benevolent power would open a single high shaft to the surface, hidden in a cleft of stone. Not wide enough for invasion. Wide enough for weather.

The first rain to fall into Arathi would be remembered forever.

It would descend as a silver thread through darkness, striking hot iron walkways and bursting into steam. Work would stop across the foundry. Hammers would lower. Belts would slow. Children would run toward it until elders pulled them back, afraid.

Then one child would slip free.

She would stand beneath the falling water, arms out, face lifted, soot running down her cheeks in black rivers.

The Forgeborn would watch her laugh.

And something in them, older than the furnace, would answer.

After that, they would make a festival of rain.

They would call it the Cooling.

Once each year, the great furnaces would be banked low. The vents would open. The rain-shaft would be polished with mirrors of beaten tin so that, if the sky was clear, a single column of sunlight might descend into the cavern and touch the central anvil.

On that day, no tools of necessity would be made.

Only gifts.

A hinge for a neighbor’s door. A cup. A bell. A toy. A nameplate for a child. A memorial gear for one who had gone into final torpor and returned to the deep earth.

For the dead, the benevolent power would give one more mercy.

The Forgeborn had believed that fallen workers became part of the forge, and perhaps they were right. But now, when one of them died, the cavern would receive them gently. Their bodies would not be consumed by labor-fire unless they had chosen it. Some would be laid in warm stone alcoves where mineral blooms slowly covered them in glittering crystal. Some would be transformed into ceremonial steel, not for machines, but for bells. Some would be planted beneath the moss gardens, and from those places the green would grow especially bright.

Thus death too would be given dignity.

And what of the power itself?

It would not ask for temples.

The Forgeborn might build one anyway, because makers express gratitude by making. But when they tried to carve an idol, the stone would crack. When they tried to cast a divine face in bronze, the mold would fill with cool water. When they tried to inscribe a name, the letters would blur.

Finally, the children would solve it.

They would build no statue.

They would leave an empty alcove near the central furnace, with a hammer, a cup of spring water, a tuft of moss, and a small open vent through which fresh air moved.

That would be enough.

The wise power would remain as presence, not ruler.

It would be in the warning tremor before collapse.

In the unexpected vein of clean ore.

In the stubborn moss growing where nothing soft should live.

In the Overseer who chooses to halt production before disaster.

In the child who asks why the old ways must always remain old.

In the worker who lays down a hammer and discovers that the world does not end.

Arathi would still be hot.

Still shadowed.

Still iron-boned and furnace-hearted.

Its people would still be Forgeborn: shaped by labor, fire, earth, and invention. But they would no longer be merely consumed by the place that made them.

They would become its partners.

And the foundry itself, once holding its breath beneath the mountain, would breathe.

Slowly.

Deeply.

Like a living thing forgiven.
