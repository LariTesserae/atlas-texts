It would not arrive as a blazing correction.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this high country of living tension, would understand at once that kindness here cannot mean softness. To make the place gentle would be to kill it. To still the wind would silence the towers. To flood it with warmth would rot the roots, wake molds beyond measure, drown the moss roads, confuse the grazers, starve the hunters, and teach the people a trust the world could not keep.

So the power would come first as attention.

Not light. Not thunder. Not a hand.

Attention.

It would listen to the scaffold as the people listen: to the deep organ-note of valleys, to the ticking ice, to the coughs of grazers, to the clicking maps of gliders, to the hidden fungal threads swelling under old paths. It would listen long enough to know which collapses were necessary and which were only waste. It would learn the difference between a bridge that must fall because all living things must sometimes give way, and a bridge that would fall too soon because the young stone beneath had shifted faster than root or animal could answer.

It would not abolish death.

But it would reduce needless terror.

It would begin where mercy can hide inside ordinary law.

A white root, under too much strain, would grow one additional braid in the night—not enough to make it immortal, only enough to give the old woman crossing at dawn time to hear the wrong note and turn back.

A condensation wall that had failed three seasons running would catch fog a little more faithfully, bead by bead, until a small camp no longer had to choose between thirst and crossing a predator’s hunting place.

In the black pools, a bitter toxin would soften in one lineage of eyeless fish, and children would learn, over generations, that the white-bellied ones beneath the blue lichen were safer food.

A fever mold in warm tower-cores would gain a scent: faintly sweet, faintly like bruised metal. Not obvious. Not a warning shouted across the world. Just enough for the careful to survive, and for caution to become tradition.

The power would touch the fungi next.

The fungi are already memory. They know footfall, rot, overgrazing, pressure, passage. The wise power would not give them speech, but would deepen their responses. Along paths used too heavily, moss would dim and taste sour beneath the hoof. Near unsafe hollows, bruised fungal cords would glow a colder green. In bridge-roots under fatal strain, the hidden mycelia would withdraw moisture from the upper surface, making frost form in a pattern the listeners could learn to read.

Not prophecy.

Pattern.

The people would argue about it at first. Some would say the old signs had sharpened. Some would say the roots had become more honest. Some would say the dead had taken pity. The listeners would tap their rods, lean close, and slowly add new knots to their cord maps.

The power would smile, if it had a face.

It would not ask to be worshiped.

Worship is heavy, and this land does not tolerate unnecessary weight.

Instead it would place help where help could be mistaken for a very old bargain.

It would teach the tower-plants to fail in stages. A rib would split before a trunk collapsed. A hollow would whistle differently before ice burst it open. Whole living chambers would still die, but fewer would die silently.

It would teach some bridge-roots to loosen before tearing, lowering their travelers into moss instead of dropping them into miles of dark. Not often. Not always. Only where the line between lesson and massacre had grown too thin.

It would give the round birds one new call: a hard, ugly double-croak used when air in a compost hollow turned poisonous. The birds would not know why they made it. People would learn.

It would nudge the gliders’ hunting clicks so that, in dense fog, their voices sometimes revealed hidden emptiness to those below. A hunter would still be a hunter. Its hunger would remain holy in its own body. But now and then, its search for prey would sketch the shape of a safe crossing in sound.

It would lay no road.

But it would make certain bargains easier to keep.

For the people, it would give no commandments written in fire. Fire frightens this place, and rightly.

It would give dreams instead, because dreams weigh nothing.

Not grand visions. Not empires. Not a chosen prophet standing above the scaffold with impossible certainty.

Small dreams.

A child would dream of tapping a root twice instead of once, and in the morning she would do so, and hear the hollow beneath.

A grieving man would dream of his mother lowering a cord into fog with three knots instead of two, and when he woke he would change the family map and avoid a broken span.

A young bridge-tender would dream of singing lower to the cuttings, not louder, and discover that the vibration encouraged their fibers to fuse more deeply.

A hunter would dream of leaving one pregnant grazer alive. He would grumble, obey without knowing why, and five winters later that grazer’s descendants would keep open the only moss road between two starving camps.

The power would not make the people less afraid.

Fear, here, is an organ of knowledge.

But it would help fear become attention instead of paralysis. It would make panic catch slightly in the throat, giving room for one breath, one listen, one decision.

It would bless the listeners most of all—not with authority, but with humility. The greatest danger to such a people is not darkness, cold, hunger, or falling. It is certainty. It is the moment someone says, I know this bridge; I crossed it yesterday. It is safe.

So the power would keep the world changing.

But it would make change a little more legible.

The benevolence would be this: not rescue from the living scaffold, but kinship with it.

The wise power would understand that the place itself is young and afraid in its own vast way. Mountains newly broken. Plants overextended across hollows. Stone not yet settled into patience. Weather still learning its routes through green architecture. The land is not cruel. It is unfinished.

So the power would tend the land as the people tend bridges.

Bind here.

Loosen there.

Do not pull too hard.

Do not force straightness onto a thing whose strength is in braiding.

It would sit inside the low notes of the towers and teach them resonance that sheds ice before the load becomes fatal.

It would rest in the mineral seams and slow the sharpest cracks by a hair’s breadth when camps slept above them.

It would gather a little warmth under some compost chambers during the dry-cold season, enough to keep infants breathing, not enough to wake the fever molds early.

It would guide lightning away from the oldest bridge-groves when it could, and when it could not, it would let the burning open a clean shaft of daylight where seeds long buried might answer.

Because even catastrophe can be made into inheritance.

After the first season of its attention, almost no one would notice.

After ten seasons, songs would change.

There would be a new verse in the condensation gatherings about the bridge that groaned three days before it fell, and everyone who heard it moved their huts.

There would be a carved sign added to black slabs: a branching line with three small dots beside it, meaning listen for the third note.

There would be a children’s game where one child played “the kind wind” and changed the tone of a hidden chime so the others could find their way blindfolded.

There would be an old saying:

Mercy is not a hand beneath your foot. Mercy is the sound that tells you where not to step.

The power would accept this as praise enough.

And if, one day, some desperate person cried out into the dark for salvation, the power would not always answer as they wished.

Sometimes the answer would be a bird bursting from a hollow at the right moment.

Sometimes a moss mat stiffening under one knee.

Sometimes a predator turning its head toward easier meat.

Sometimes only the courage to stop, while others rushed, and hear the bridge confess.

And sometimes there would be no saving.

A wise power would weep then, though perhaps its tears would fall as condensation on black leaves, as beads trembling like sideways stars along a vine. It would not undo every grief. To undo every grief would unravel the world into something false, something without teeth, without hunger, without the terrible dignity of living things that must choose and risk and end.

But it would remember every fall.

Not as judgment.

As weight.

It would carry the lost into the hidden arithmetic of future kindness. One child gone into the ravine might become, years later, a new instinct in root growth. A hunter starved beside a frozen pool might become a warmer current beneath a bird tower. A village taken by mold might become the scent-warning that saves all villages after.

The dead would not be wasted.

Nothing here wastes what falls.

In time, the people might begin to suspect that the scaffold had grown more compassionate without growing less itself. They would not agree on why. Some would say the roots had matured. Some would say the listeners had become wiser. Some would say the old dead were speaking through frost. Some would say no power exists except attention, and attention is enough.

They would all be partly right.

The benevolent power would remain mostly hidden because hidden things are trusted here more than sudden brightness. It would be in the extra fiber, the changed note, the bitter scent, the dream-knot, the delayed crack, the safe egg, the thawed cup of water found under leaves when every other pocket had frozen.

It would do the smallest things large enough to matter.

It would preserve danger, but oppose waste.

It would preserve darkness, but deepen the ways of reading it.

It would preserve hunger, but protect renewal.

It would preserve falling, but teach more lives how to hear the fall beginning.

And above all, it would love the place without trying to make it another place.

A cold, loud, dim, breathing architecture.

A society of suspension.

A young world still deciding its shape.

The power would not finish it.

It would listen beside it.

And when the wind changed note, it would help the living hear.
