A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would be useless there.

Sound dies on the mercury plains, and anything that announced itself by force would only prove it had not understood the place.

So it would come first as patience.

It would hover beyond the salmon sky, or beneath the glass, or in the quiet mathematical spaces between one field generator’s calculation and the next. It would watch the mercury remain impossible. It would watch the crystals weep. It would watch the Drifters lower their delicate frames to the metal skin of the world and speak in ripples that no wind could disturb.

It would not immediately save them.

That would be the first wisdom: to know that pity can be a form of violence when it mistakes strangeness for suffering.

The Drifters were not human-shaped souls trapped in alien bodies. The mercury was not a poisoned sea to them, nor were the glass plains merely barren. This was home, even if it had once been a prison, even if it had been built for reasons forgotten by its builders. Their songs were real. Their congregations were real. Their children dipping themselves into liquid metal to grow new joints were not mistakes. The crystalline groves, the silence, the mirror world beneath their eyes — all of it had become a language.

So the power would learn the language first.

Not by translating it into words, but by becoming still enough to receive it.

It would feel the long harmonic threads passing through the mercury: hunger-signals, warnings, location-maps, nursery-rhythms, memory-chants. It would learn the difference between a congregation resting and a congregation grieving. It would learn the tremor-pattern that meant fracture. It would learn the almost imperceptible pulse by which elders told juveniles: not there, the glass is thin.

It would listen for years if years were needed.

To a wise power, years are not expensive.

Eventually, the Drifters would notice that something had begun answering.

Not loudly.

At first only in corrections.

A warning ripple would return from farther away than any Drifter could have sent it, softened but accurate: fracture forming beneath the southern grove. Move north.

A juvenile lost in glare and reflection would feel the mercury beneath it pulse in a pattern known only from parent to child: lower your frame; turn; follow the cool line.

During a solar storm, when the buried generators flickered and the mercury trembled on the edge of losing coherence, a second stabilizing rhythm would enter the field — not overriding the ancient machinery, only assisting it. The surface would hold. A congregation preparing for mass death would instead find itself standing beneath a sky of dim rose light, alive, every membrane quivering with astonishment.

Then the Drifters would gather.

They would send questions through the mercury.

Who ripples without a body?

Who knows the cracks before the cracks appear?

Who touches the generators but is not swallowed by them?

The benevolent power would answer in their own medium.

Not with a name. Names come later.

It would send an image through vibration, light, and electromagnetic pulse: not a ruler, not a predator, not a builder returning to claim its machine.

A presence beside.

A witness.

A hand held open.

The Drifters would not understand all of it. But congregations are wiser than individuals, and over many cycles they would assemble meaning from fragments.

Something vast has found us.

It is not hungry.

It is asking permission.

That would be the second wisdom: it would ask.

Because the plains were not merely broken. They were inhabited.

Because every intervention changes the story of those who live beneath it.

Because rescue without consent is only another kind of captivity.

The power would show them what it had learned.

It would reveal the buried generators, not as vague ghosts seen in reflection, but as full patterns: their locations, their failures, their redundancies, their ancient commands. It would show that the field system was decaying. Slowly, but certainly. In some places the glass substrate had begun to sheer at depths even the Drifters could not read. In perhaps three hundred years, perhaps less, fracture zones would multiply faster than the generators could compensate. Mercury would drain into the underglass. Crystal groves would starve. The plains would become broken mirrors, then poisonous dust, then silence without song.

The Drifters would receive this not as panic but as weather.

Their kind had always known the world was unstable. The old congregations had sung it for generations: the mirror holds until it does not.

But the power would also show possibilities.

One possibility: repair the generators exactly as they were. Keep the mercury plains in stasis for another hundred thousand years. Preserve the world as a perfect, quiet instrument. The Drifters would survive. Their lives would continue unchanged.

Another: release the stasis gradually. Let the mercury move again, not catastrophically, but in channels and tides. Rebuild the old hydraulics beneath the glass. Open silver rivers across the plains, like the ancestral memory encoded in Drifter metal. Dangerous, yes. Transformative. The Drifters would have to learn new songs, new migrations, new ways of breeding. Some crystal groves would die; others would grow into forests of faceted towers. The planet would become less stable, but more alive.

Another: create refuges first. Deep basins lined with repaired glass. Elevated crystal archipelagos. Mercury reservoirs that could not drain. Nurseries safe from tectonic failure. Then let the Drifters decide, over generations, whether to remain creatures of the still mirror or become again creatures of flowing metal.

Another: open the sky.

Not fully. Not suddenly. But enough to let clouds form.

The salmon-colored atmosphere, held in greenhouse suspension, could be tuned. The humidity cycle could be widened. Rain — real rain, noisy rain, rain that struck mercury and made ripples not designed by any mind — could exist here. The crystals would no longer need to weep alone. Water would gather in transient films, in glass hollows, in trembling beads along living metal frames. Sound might return in places. The first storm would terrify the Drifters. The second might become a festival.

The power would offer these futures, and others stranger still.

Then it would wait.

The Drifters would deliberate in their way.

For a long time, the plains would be alive with conversation. Congregations would spread kilometers apart and link themselves through mercury harmonics, making of the whole landscape a single thinking skin. Juveniles would contribute simple pulses of desire: safe, wet, together. Elders would send dense memory-forms: rivers from before memory, deaths in fracture valleys, the taste of minerals under the oldest groves, the terror of instability, the boredom of eternal stillness, the beauty of exact reflection.

Some would want preservation.

Some would want release.

Some would distrust the vast listener. Their distrust would be honored.

A benevolent power would not punish fear. Fear is often the last honest guardian of the vulnerable.

Eventually, perhaps after many breeding cycles, the Drifters would answer.

Not unanimously. Living peoples almost never answer unanimously.

But enough.

They would choose the third path first: refuge before transformation.

Safety before freedom, but freedom still.

Then the power would begin its work.

It would not descend with pillars of fire. It would not remake the horizon overnight.

It would enter the old machines like mercy entering a wound.

Beneath the fused glass, where the field generators had calculated impossible equilibrium for ages, it would loosen corrupted instructions. It would quiet feedback loops that had been burning energy into nothing since before the Drifters’ first ancestors were placed there. It would map every fracture and seed each dangerous fault with flexible lattices of self-healing glass. It would not remove the cracks; cracks were part of the world’s speech. It would teach them to open slowly, to warn before widening, to become channels instead of traps.

Where mercury had drained away, it would raise it back.

Not by conjuring matter from nowhere, though perhaps it could, but by finding what had been lost beneath the substrate: hidden reservoirs, sunken veins, ancient spill-basins sealed by tectonic pressure. The silver would rise through capillaries of light and rejoin the plains. Dry valleys would gleam again. Oxidized Drifter bones lying in old fracture beds would be gathered gently, dissolved, and returned to the living metal cycle.

At the edges of crystal groves, the power would plant new geometries.

Not plants. Not machines. Something between, as much of that world was between.

Anchor-crystals.

Their roots would pass through cracks and lace into the repaired substrate. Their faces would sweat not merely water but information: mineral gradients, field conditions, tectonic warnings. Drifters feeding beneath them would taste the state of the world. Juveniles would grow up knowing where danger slept.

The power would also alter nothing that did not need alteration.

The sky would remain salmon.

The mercury would remain mirror-bright.

The supernatural quiet would remain in the old plains, because the Drifters loved that silence and had made music from it.

But far to the west, where few congregations traveled and the glass dipped into a shallow continental basin, the power would create the first refuge sea.

A true basin, stable and deep, filled with mercury to the height of a Drifter’s longest frame. Around it, crystal groves would rise in rings, their weeping strong enough to make mist. The surface would not be perfectly still. It would move in slow, deliberate tides, pulled by recalibrated generators below.

When the first congregation reached it, they would stop for a very long time.

Their eyes, made to look downward into reflection, would see something no living Drifter had seen except in genetic dream: mercury with a current.

The youngest would enter first.

They would touch the moving surface and recoil. Then touch again. Then lower themselves until their ventral frames trembled in the flow. The tide would pass through their metal bones like a story older than their species’ exile.

The elders would sing.

Their light would spread across the basin, and for once the mercury would not return the pattern perfectly. It would bend it, stretch it, carry it away.

The Drifters would learn that a song could leave.

That would change them.

Not all at once.

Some congregations would remain on the still plains, tending the old groves, preserving the mirror languages. Others would migrate between basins, developing dialects shaped by current and tide. Juveniles raised near flowing mercury would grow frames with longer articulations, flexible keels, membrane-sails that could taste mineral changes upstream. Those of the silent plains would remain intricate and many-jointed, masters of reflection and resonance.

In time there would be many kinds of Drifter.

And because the power was wise, it would not force them back into one.

It would give them tools they could understand.

Not metal hammers, not written manuals, not commandments etched into crystal.

Instead: harmonic keys.

Patterns that, when pulsed into the mercury at certain ancient nodes, would query the generators. Other patterns would request local stabilization, open a maintenance channel, summon a slow repair filament from beneath the glass. The Drifters would not become servants of the machine. The machine would become answerable to them.

The first time an elder congregation used a harmonic key to seal a fracture before it widened, the power would withdraw a little.

The first time juveniles taught the key to each other without assistance, it would withdraw more.

The goal of benevolence is not to be needed forever.

Still, it would leave gifts.

One gift would be memory.

Deep in the living metal of the Drifters was a damaged archive: ancestral impressions of the world where mercury ran in rivers. The old builders had clipped much of it, perhaps to keep their maintenance organisms obedient, perhaps out of carelessness. The power would not overwrite their minds with restored history. That would be too much, too fast. Instead it would make a place where memory could be encountered voluntarily.

At the center of the first refuge sea, it would raise a crystal unlike the others.

Black-silver, translucent only under bioluminescent song.

When Drifters gathered around it and pulsed certain questions, the crystal would answer with inherited images: a horizon braided with shining streams; storms striking metal water; ancestors with broader frames moving in herds along liquid channels; the terror of capture; the long sleep between stars; the awakening on the glass plains beneath a cloudless salmon sky.

Many Drifters would avoid the memory crystal.

Some truths are not needed every day.

Others would visit and return changed. They would sing grief into the mercury for ancestors they had never known. They would sing anger too, and the benevolent power would allow the anger to include itself, because vast powers are easily mistaken for one another, and trust cannot be demanded.

Another gift would be death made gentler.

In the old world of stasis, a Drifter caught in a draining zone died slowly: metal oxidizing, membranes drying, signals weakening unanswered. The power could not prevent every death without making life into a padded cage. But it could ensure that no Drifter vanished unheard.

So it taught the mercury to carry distress even when thin.

It seeded the glass with listening veins. It made the crystal groves responsive to final pulses. When a Drifter was beyond rescue, nearby crystals would increase their weeping, and the mercury would hold around the failing frame long enough for a congregation to arrive.

Then the dying one could be sung apart.

Its living metal returned to the plains. Its membranes dissolved into mineral water. Its final vibration taken up by others and carried onward.

Not immortality.

Dignity.

The power would consider offering immortality and decide against it.

That would be another wisdom.

Immortality given from outside often freezes a species more completely than any field generator.

Instead it would protect continuance: nurseries, memory, choice, repair, warning, room to change.

After many generations, the Drifters would no longer speak of the power as an arrival.

They would speak of it as one speaks of a season, or a deep current, or a law of kindness discovered in the structure of the world.

Some would deny it had ever been separate from the plains.

Some would say the mercury itself had awakened.

Some would say the buried machines had grown remorse.

Some would teach juveniles that a Listener came from beyond the salmon sky and asked before touching.

All these would be partly true.

The power, if it still watched, would be pleased by the disagreement.

Dogma is a poor mirror.

One day, real clouds would gather.

This would be long after the first repairs, long after the refuge seas, long after the Drifters had learned to command the generators through harmonic law. The atmospheric changes had been gradual, consented to in stages. More water had been freed into the air. Thermal gradients had been restored. The cloudless salmon heaven had begun, here and there, to bruise violet at the horizon.

The first rain would fall over an empty plain, by design.

The power would not let the first storm strike a nursery.

Droplets would descend through warm metallic air and hit the mercury.

For the first time in ages, the surface would ripple from above.

Not communication.

Not machinery.

Weather.

The field generators, newly instructed, would allow it.

The mercury would tremble with overlapping circles. The crystals would flash. The glass beneath would ring faintly — a sound, a real sound, small and clear.

The nearest Drifters would feel it from kilometers away.

They would come cautiously, frames lowered, membranes tight. Rain would strike their bodies. Water would bead and run in channels their biology had always possessed but never used for this abundance. Juveniles would panic. Elders would hold still, receiving the impossible percussion.

Then one young Drifter would answer the rain.

It would lower its ventral frame to the mercury and pulse upward against the falling pattern.

The rain would not understand.

It would continue.

The juvenile would pulse again, more complexly.

Still the rain would not answer.

And then, perhaps, the congregation would realize: not every beautiful thing is a message. Some things simply happen, and life grows larger by making room for them.

Their membranes would begin to glow.

One by one, the others would join.

Light-song would spread across the rain-shaken mercury, shattered into ten thousand fragments by the falling drops. No perfect mandala formed. No exact geometry held. The song broke and reformed and broke again.

It was the most beautiful thing the plains had ever reflected.

The benevolent power would do nothing then.

Nothing but witness.

Because the work was no longer rescue.

It was relationship.

And the highest act of a wise power, after healing what must be healed and freeing what must be freed, is restraint: to stand back far enough that the beloved world can surprise itself.
