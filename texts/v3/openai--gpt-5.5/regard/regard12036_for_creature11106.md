The power does not come as rescue.

It is wise enough for that.

It sees the Clinging City from above first: not as a smear of iron on stone, not as a human wound in the mountain, but as a complicated act of faith repeated every morning. A million small trusts. A hand on a wet railing. A child crossing a catwalk in fog. A cable-minder leaning out over nothing with a wrench between their teeth. An elder waiting in a lift-cage for a floor that has no number.

The power sees the mountain, too.

It sees the schist’s billion-year patience, the glacial scars, the sleeping pressure inside the cliff. It sees that the city is not an error upon the stone. It is a kind of lichen. A metal moss. A brief, bright intelligence clinging to an older one.

And because the power is benevolent, it feels tenderness.

And because it is wise, it does not immediately make things easy.

It does not flatten the mountain.

It does not banish the fog.

It does not fill the drop with earth, or move the Clinging-Folk to a warm valley, or replace their oilcloth and wool with garments that never dampen. It does not take away the danger that has shaped their attention, their intimacy, their craft, their *verthan*. To do so would be to unmake them, and this power has no taste for salvation that resembles erasure.

So it waits.

For seven fogs and seven thaws and seven gray-hours in which even the lamps seem uncertain, it does nothing but listen.

It listens to the drip-song on tin, stone, moss, glass, cable, handrail.

It listens to the pumps, their tired pistons, their faithful cycling.

It listens to the lift-cables hum in their deep metallic throats.

It listens to the newborns grip.

It listens to the ones who do not.

It listens longest to the silence after falls.

Then, when it has learned enough not to be clumsy, it begins.

Not with thunder.

With maintenance.

The first gift appears as a change in rust.

No one notices at once. The cable-minders still scrape flakes from joints. Pipe-fitters still curse the damp. Bolts still need replacing. But over months, then years, a pattern emerges: the worst corrosion no longer hides. It blooms early, bright orange against dark iron, impossible to miss even in fog. Stress fractures darken before they split. A cable about to fail grows a thin white line along its length, like frost that will not melt.

The engineers argue over this for a generation.

Some say the mineral content of the fog has changed.

Some say the vines are exuding something protective.

Some say the city is getting more honest in its old age.

The elders say nothing. They send apprentices with paint and chalk to mark every bright wound the city reveals, and accidents decrease—not enough to make people careless, but enough that certain meals after certain falls never happen.

The second gift is given to the children.

Not to make them fearless. They already know better than fear.

Not to make them immortal. No wise power offers that bargain.

Instead, the power changes the city’s listening.

A child’s grip, before it fails, now sends a tremor through nearby things. A cup rings softly on a shelf. A loose washer ticks against pipe. A lamp-flame leans in the wrong direction.

Adults begin turning their heads before they know why.

A mother reaches out and catches a sleeve.

A boiler-worker drops a wrench and lunges across a platform.

An old man, half-blind and seated by a dead lift, extends his cane into fog at exactly the right moment, and a small hand catches it.

The Clinging-Folk do not call these miracles. That would be too large a word, and too useless. They call it “near-sense.” They incorporate it into training. Children are taught not only how to grip, but how to be heard by the city when grip is not enough.

The third gift is stranger, and it takes longer to be accepted.

Babies still come into the world with hands that grip.

Most of them.

But sometimes a newborn’s fingers do not close around the cable.

The midwife waits for the old grief.

Instead, the cable warms.

Not much. Just enough to be felt.

The child opens its eyes.

And somewhere in the room, water changes direction.

A drip that should fall straight down curves sideways into a basin. A lamp chain swings toward the infant. A rusted hinge unlocks itself. The midwife, who has delivered children for forty years and buried too many of them in silence, understands before anyone else does.

There are other ways to hold.

These children become listeners, later. Balancers. Map-makers of the unmapped floors. They can feel shifts in pressure through their teeth. They can tell when a cistern wall is tired. They know which lift-cage is about to arrive before its cable begins to move. Some never climb well. Some never climb at all.

The lower reaches change because of them.

Ramps are built where ladders had been considered sufficient. Handlines are doubled. Rest-platforms appear along long vertical routes. Not quickly. The Clinging City does nothing quickly except fall, and it has always tried not to do that. But steadily, stubbornly, the city makes room.

Some complain.

Some mutter that softness spreads faster than moss.

Then one of the no-grip children, grown into a quiet woman with pale palms and an ear like a pressure gauge, wakes from a dream and orders three districts evacuated before dawn. At gray-hour, a hidden seam in the cliff exhales, and half an abandoned platform tears free into the fog.

After that, fewer people complain.

The fourth gift is given to the dead districts.

The power goes there carefully.

It does not force green things to become useful. It does not command the vine-people, if people they are, to return to ordinary speech or ordinary time. It approaches as rain approaches moss: by being received.

In the dead districts, roots thicken around old struts. Vines learn tension. Ferns grow in patterns that drink excess water from weakened masonry. The living green becomes a second architecture—not replacing iron, not forgiving neglect, but joining the long labor of holding.

Messages left at the borders receive clearer answers.

A vine curling twice around a pipe means pressure.

Three pale leaves on a rusted hinge means do not open.

White flowers growing on a lift-track mean wait.

Red berries mean send someone beloved, because the matter concerns the city’s heart and cannot be handled by duty alone.

The vine-people speak a little more often after that. Still low. Still slow. But sometimes, when fog thickens and a worker is lost between dead platforms, a voice like sap moving through wood says:

“Left hand. Down. Trust the moss.”

And if the worker obeys, they live.

The fifth gift is for the elders.

One gray afternoon, the intermittent district appears and does not vanish for three days.

The elders arrive one by one, pretending not to hurry.

Inside the floor that has no number, the walls are wet with condensation. The cable-writing, usually visible only from impossible angles outside the city, has drawn itself across the interior in lines of mineral and light. It is not a commandment. The power knows better than commandments.

It is a question.

Not written in any language, but understood by each elder according to their own work.

To the cistern-keeper, it asks: What would water do if it were less afraid of scarcity?

To the boiler-worker: What heat is wasted because grief taught you not to expect enough?

To the lift operator: Which routes exist only because the dead once needed them?

To the oldest elder, who has seen the thing in the deep fog and greeted it without screaming, it asks: What has the city been trying to become?

They remain there through three cycles of fog.

When they emerge, they make no pronouncements. Of course they do not.

They ask for old diagrams.

They ask for stories of failed repairs.

They ask children to draw the city from memory.

They ask the no-grip listeners where the mountain feels hollow.

They ask the dead districts which abandoned places are truly dead and which are only sleeping.

Then they begin the slowest work the Clinging City has ever attempted.

They do not expand outward.

They deepen.

They carve—not greedily, not like miners, but like surgeons—into certain safe seams of the schist, opening warm chambers within the mountain itself. Not many. Not enough to turn the city away from the cliff. Just enough for infirmaries that do not drip onto fevered faces. Nurseries with floors wide enough for a child to fall only a few inches. Storage rooms where grain stays dry. Gathering halls where mourning may happen without everyone pretending the fog took what it took and nothing more could be said.

This changes *verthan*.

Not at once.

Old meanings cling harder than lichen.

But slowly the word broadens. It no longer means only the calm acceptance of the drop. It comes also to mean the courage to distinguish the drop from the rotten plank, fate from negligence, mystery from avoidable harm.

The fog takes what it takes.

Yes.

But the fog is not entitled to everything.

The sixth gift is nearly invisible.

The boilers burn cleaner.

No one knows why. A minor alteration in draft. A new habit among soot-stained workers who suddenly find themselves arranging vents according to dreams. A mineral deposit in one intake that glows faintly and is never exhausted.

Whatever the cause, the black dust lessens.

The boiler-workers still carry darkness in their palms, but their children cough less. Bread baked on vented heat no longer tastes faintly of smoke. In winter, there is warmth enough to open a communal room in the lower reaches, where the old and injured gather not because they have descended in status, but because they have become keepers of stories.

The seventh gift is to the thing in the deep fog.

For a long time, the power only watches it.

It is larger than rumor and smaller than dread. It moves along the cliff face where no catwalk runs. It clings without hands. Its body is part shadow, part condensation, part old mineral memory. It is not evil. It is not safe. It is one of the mountain’s thoughts, perhaps, or one of the city’s, given motion.

The power does not tame it.

Instead, it gives it a task suited to its nature.

Now, sometimes, when someone falls past the last platform, past the reach of hands and hooks and desperate thrown lines, the fog below thickens.

Not always.

This is important.

Not always.

But sometimes the falling one strikes not empty air, but something yielding and cold and strong as woven cable. They wake hours later on a lower ledge, bruised, shivering, hair full of mist, with no memory except the sound of vast breathing.

After the third such return, offerings begin appearing at the edges of the lowest active district: bowls of warm broth, polished scrap metal, a child’s carved hawk, a length of new rope coiled with reverence.

All disappear by morning.

The Clinging-Folk still teach their children not to fall.

But they no longer say the drop claims everything it touches.

The last gift is not a gift at all.

It is restraint.

The power remains.

It could do more. It could do so much more. It could turn every cable incorruptible, every platform steady, every fog-bank luminous, every lift obedient. It could make the city safe.

But safety is not the same as flourishing.

So it stays at the edge of action, present but not ruling.

It becomes a pressure toward mercy.

A clarity in the minds of those about to choose convenience over care.

A warmth in the hand before someone reaches for another.

A reluctance in the engineer who might otherwise sign off on a repair that is almost good enough.

A dream in the mind of a child who will one day discover a new route between districts.

A hush in the intermittent elders’ hall when pride speaks too loudly.

The city continues to cling.

The fog continues to roll in.

The lifts rise and descend.

The vines thicken.

The lamps burn dim yellow against gray.

People still argue over valves, filters, routes, inheritance, apprenticeships, whose turn it is to clear moss from the catch-basins. They still tell jokes no outsider understands. They still grow old if they are lucky. They still fall if they are not.

But fewer are lost to things that were only ever called inevitable because no one had yet imagined otherwise.

And in time, the cable-writing changes.

Seen from the valley floor on a rare clear evening, when the weak sun strikes the wet pipes and turns them briefly to fire, the whole city seems to form a pattern. Not a map. Not exactly words.

More like a hand.

Not gripping.

Offering.

And the Clinging-Folk, who have always known how to hold on, begin learning—slowly, awkwardly, beautifully—how to be held.
