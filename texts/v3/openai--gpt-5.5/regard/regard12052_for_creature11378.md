The power, being wise, would not arrive.

Not at first.

Arrival is a violent category. It implies a before and after too cleanly divided, a foot placed where no foot has learned the weight of the stone, a brightness introduced into eyes that have spent generations opening themselves to dimness. A benevolent power would understand this. It would understand that help, in a place like the Hanging Town, must not resemble rescue unless rescue is truly required.

So it would begin by doing what the inhabitants themselves do.

It would attend.

It would take no shape that required looking at. It would not stand at the outer margin with robes moving in the cliff-wind. It would not speak in a voice that traveled. It would not fill the ledge with warmth, or drive away the insects, or name the winged things aloud.

It would become, for a while, indistinguishable from patience.

It would learn the condensation cycle. It would learn the seep’s irregular pulse, the hidden pressure behind the cliff, the mineral taste of the water before the inhabitants stoppered it into vessels. It would follow the beetles without hastening them. It would watch the moss structures build their slow architectures of green and blue-grey almost-light. It would notice which houses were empty, and which were empty only in the sense that something other than people now used them. It would understand that an abandoned dwelling is not necessarily a wound. Sometimes it is a chamber the world has reclaimed for another kind of breathing.

It would study the people last, though not because they mattered least.

Because they could be harmed most easily by being understood too quickly.

A wise power would not mistake quietness for deprivation. It would not mistake smallness for incompleteness. It would not decide that a dozen lives on a ledge must be lonely in the way lowland multitudes imagine loneliness. It would see the density of their attention, the enormous social weather carried in a hand-shift, a held breath, a shoulder angled toward warning. It would see that they have made a civilization out of precision.

And, seeing this, it would refrain from blessing them loudly.

Its first act would be almost nothing.

One night, during the coldest hour, while the moss glowed its faint thought of blue and the beetles moved like dark verdicts over the vertical stone, a crack deep inside the ledge would reach the point at which, without intervention, it would lengthen.

Not collapse. Not yet. Not for years perhaps.

But it would begin the sentence that ended in collapse.

The power would place itself there.

Not as a wall. Not as an iron brace. Nothing so foreign.

It would become a patience within the stone, a slight persuasion toward holding. The crack would not vanish. Vanishing would be suspicious, and worse, untrue. The ledge is young; young stone must shift. But the angle of stress would change by the smallest measurable amount. A future disaster would become instead a long maintenance problem.

In the morning, one of the inhabitants would notice that a door-skin hung differently.

They would touch the lintel. They would call another over without raising their voice. The two of them would examine the tilt, the hairline, the new distribution of weight.

They would not know they had been spared.

This would be correct.

Gratitude is not required for mercy to have happened.

The second act would concern water.

Not abundance. Abundance would rot the place. It would swell the moss too quickly, loosen clay, invite organisms that had no relationship yet with the ledge’s difficult agreements. The Hanging Town is built around not enough. To give it too much would be to make strangers of its people in their own lives.

So the power would not open a spring.

It would listen instead to the cliff.

Behind the seep there are old routes, mineral-threaded and half-closed, where water once passed more freely before the ledge broke and the pressures changed. The power would not force them. It would clear, from within, one channel no wider than a root hair. It would let the seep become not stronger, exactly, but less erratic. The dry intervals would shorten by a fraction. The wet intervals would stop arriving with such wasteful suddenness.

The inhabitants would notice over several weeks.

They would argue, quietly, with their hands and mouths and posture, whether this was a pattern or merely the mind’s hunger for pattern. They would mark the seep wall with clay. They would compare the fading of the marks. They would place one more vessel near the damp corner, not in triumph but in cautious revision.

The power would be pleased by their caution.

Caution is one of the forms reverence takes when reverence has work to do.

The third act would concern the winged things.

Here the power would hesitate longest.

Not because the winged things were evil. They were not.

They were difficult.

There are beings whose lives cut across human categories so obliquely that fear is not superstition but an accurate social instrument. The winged things had entered into relation with the Hanging Town before the present inhabitants had words for the relation. They watched, and fed, and remembered, and perhaps borrowed. They occupied the empty place where birds should have been, but they did not occupy it as birds would have. They had their own treaties with the insects, with the ledge-edge shrubs, with the dark corner where almost no light reached.

The people did not look at them directly because direct looking had meaning.

The power would learn that meaning.

It would discover that the winged things were not predators in the simple sense. They did not eat people. They did not carry children away. They did not poison the vessels or cut the door-skins.

But they took something.

Attention, perhaps.

Or the shape attention leaves behind after long use.

They gathered the excess awareness of the ledge: the glance not made, the word not spoken, the alarm swallowed before it became sound. They fed on withheld response. In another place this might have made them parasites. Here, where nearly everything was withheld, modulated, made small enough not to disturb the air, they had become powerful.

The bargain was ancient by the standards of the town and young by the standards of the cliff.

The bargain was also unfair.

Not cruelly unfair. Not intentionally, perhaps. But asymmetrical. The winged things knew more of the people than the people knew of them. The people had adapted around the bargain so completely that they could no longer tell which parts of their quiet were chosen and which parts were harvested.

A lesser benevolence would drive the winged things away.

A foolish benevolence would reveal them.

The wise power would do neither.

Instead, it would alter the cost of directness.

Not everywhere. Not always. Only in one narrow circumstance.

It would enter the moss-light at night and teach it a second color, so faint it could almost be mistaken for the first: a green beneath the blue-grey, visible only from the corner of the eye, and only when one of the winged things hovered near enough to be involved in a human decision.

The first to notice would be one of the almost-children.

Of course it would be.

Children are not better at seeing; they are less finished in what they have agreed not to see.

The almost-child would be carrying a folded cloth stiff with collected condensation. A winged thing would pause above the vessel stacks in a position flight should not permit. The child would stop too abruptly. Their attention would leak, bright and frightened.

Then the moss would green.

Not much. Not enough for certainty.

But enough.

The child would turn their head not toward the winged thing, but toward an adult. The adult would see the child seeing, and then, because adults in the Hanging Town are accomplished readers of slight events, the adult would see the moss.

No one would speak.

The winged thing would withdraw.

This would be the beginning of a renegotiation.

Not a victory. A victory would simplify too much.

Over months, the people would learn that the moss-light sometimes disclosed proximity, sometimes intention, sometimes only the fact of attention crossing attention. They would test this carefully, almost scientifically, though their science would be made of posture, clay marks, vessel placement, and remembered nights. They would discover that the winged things disliked being made visible in this indirect way, but were not injured by it. They would discover that certain gestures calmed the green light, and others sharpened it. They would discover that the bargain had edges.

An edge is a mercy.

An edge lets a thing be known without being destroyed.

The fourth act would be for the empty houses.

The power would not fill them with people. It would not summon wanderers up the cliff, or make the inhabitants fruitful beyond the ledge’s ability to hold them. It would not turn absence into failure.

But in the darkest of the unused structures, where damp had gone sour and small blind things had begun to overrule the old thresholds, the power would encourage one dry seam in the wall. Just enough for storage. Just enough that a bundle of processed stems placed there would not spoil. Just enough that, during a season of bad condensation or sickness or excessive beetle-migration, the town would have a reserve it had not previously been able to keep.

The inhabitants would find the seam by accident.

They would not call it miracle.

They would clean the space.

They would ask, in their quiet way, whether using it would offend whatever had been using it before. They would wait three nights. They would observe the insects’ routes. They would place a token bundle, then remove it, then place it again. When nothing terrible followed, they would begin to store things there.

The power would admire them for asking permission of the small and unseen.

It is one of the signs by which wisdom recognizes kinship.

The fifth act would be the most dangerous, because it would involve hope.

The power would wait for a day of unusual clarity, one of those rare openings when the cloud below the ledge thinned and the world beneath appeared not as terrain, exactly, but as a layered possibility of distance.

On that day, at the outer belt, where the root-fingers clasped the lip of the fall, a single pale stem would grow in a direction no stem had previously grown.

Outward, then downward.

It would not become a bridge. Not even close.

It would become a line.

A living line, flexible and strong, joining with other stems over seasons. The inhabitants would notice. They would bind nothing to it at first. Then, later, they would guide it. Later still, one of the almost-children, now nearer adulthood, would test its strength with both hands and a foot braced against stone.

It would not provide escape.

Escape is what outsiders call a path when they have already decided the place is a prison.

It would provide relation.

Perhaps, in ten years, the line would reach a lower spur where another ecology clung to the cliff. Perhaps in thirty, it would become part of a dangerous but usable descent. Perhaps it would fail, break, be eaten, be cut back by frost. The power would not guarantee the outcome.

Guarantee is another kind of cage.

But it would make possible what had not been possible before: that the Hanging Town might remain itself without being only itself. That someday someone might leave and return. That knowledge might travel without conquest. That the people might discover whether their becoming required isolation, or had merely used isolation because isolation was what it had been given.

The final act would be no act at all.

The power would withdraw from obviousness.

It would remain only as a tendency toward survivable margins: cracks slowing before catastrophe, water arriving just short of scarcity, moss-light telling enough truth to make fear negotiable, living stems testing the air below.

It would not make the place safe.

The Hanging Town cannot be made safe without being made into another place.

It would make the place more able to continue.

That is different.

And the inhabitants, who are themselves the memory of the ledge, would incorporate these changes as they incorporate all things: not as interruption, not as revelation, but as detail.

A new seep-pattern.

A changed light.

A dry seam.

A stem with downward intentions.

A winged thing pausing farther away than it once would have.

They would mark the wet clay. They would rearrange threshold stones. They would teach the almost-children, who would teach those after them, not that a benevolent power came, but that the ledge altered in these ways and must be attended accordingly.

This, too, would be correct.

The best mercy for the Hanging Town is not to be lifted from its becoming.

It is to have its becoming protected from needless ruin.

So the wise power would leave them their dimness, their quiet, their beetles, their difficult wings, their cold mornings, their careful vessels, their small profound society at the edge of the air.

It would not save them from the ledge.

It would help the ledge hold.
