A benevolent and wise power would not descend on that plateau as light.

Light, there, would be an invasion.

It would not split the clouds, warm the stone, melt the rime from every leaf, or call the hidden animals out into a sudden golden day. It would not decide that the place was bleak and therefore needed softening. It would not mistake silence for suffering, darkness for error, predation for evil, or austerity for lack.

It would come first as attention.

Not a gaze from above, but a listening from within: a patient awareness entering the pressure of root against basalt, the slow wet pulse of moss, the mineral taste drawn through fungal threads, the cold breath rising through vents. It would learn the plateau at the speed the plateau uses to know itself. It would spend a hundred winters simply understanding where the water goes.

It would hear the click of hooves through shale.

It would feel the web-makers reading vibration across whole valleys.

It would know the blind salamanders by the faint lamps inside their bodies, not as lesser things, but as complete answers to a question no warmer world had ever asked.

It would not pity them.

Pity would be too loud.

The power would understand that this country is not failed abundance. It is abundance under discipline. It is life that has made treaty with cold, darkness, distance, and stone. Its beauty is not hidden because it is ashamed. It is hidden because exposure kills.

So the first act of the power would be restraint.

It would do almost nothing.

It would not crown one creature. It would not give speech to the grazers, hands to the birds, mercy to the frost-cats, or a throne to the underground engine. It would not simplify the old economy into kindness. It would not abolish hunger, because hunger there is also motion, selection, memory, measure. To remove it carelessly would be to rot the whole tableland from beneath.

But benevolence does not mean passivity.

After long listening, the power would begin to make changes so small that no animal would notice them as miracles.

A vent that had begun to choke beneath mineral crust would open one finger-width wider, keeping a nursery hollow warm through an especially black season.

A hidden stream would be turned, not far, only enough that its water did not vanish entirely into a dead fissure before reaching the moss beds where the young lay folded.

A lichen grid struck too often by storm-charge would learn, over generations, to spread its fire along safer paths. The blue sparks would still crawl through the groves, but fewer stems would burn from within.

A lineage of blind salamanders, trapped in a pool slowly growing too mineral-poisoned for eggs, would find that once each year the stone lip overflowed by a hair’s breadth, allowing a few transparent bodies to slip into another basin downstream.

The white-backed grazers would not be protected from all predators. But in the worst winters, when hunger became less an animal need than a verdict, the frost-cats would find sleep easier. Their bodies would lower their demands. Their waiting would lengthen. A few more dark calves would survive in the breathing moss.

Not many.

Enough.

The wise power would deal in enough.

It would not make the plateau safe. Safety is not the same as life. But it would keep the balance from tipping into waste.

Where disease arose as part of the ordinary exchange of bodies, it would pass. Where disease arose from a flaw too abrupt for the land to answer — a fungus over-warmed by a rare season, a parasite multiplying beyond the old restraints — the power would lend resistance to the host roots, bitterness to the milk, cold clarity to the pools.

Where storms tore at the conductive groves, the power would not calm the sky. The groves eat storm. Instead, it would deepen their grounding. It would teach quartz veins to carry charge a little more cleanly. It would let the mountain’s own hidden circuitry become less destructive and more articulate.

In time, the plateau would begin to dream more coherently.

Not with images. Not with language. But with pattern.

Water stored before drought.

Heat released before killing frost.

Moss thickened in places where generations of young had hidden.

Webs tensioned away from crossings needed by nursing mothers.

Resin seeped more generously after insect colonies failed.

Nothing would announce a law. No voice would say: be merciful. No command would pass through root and fur and mineral breath.

But the whole place would acquire, by minute degrees, a gentler intelligence about continuation.

The predators would remain predators. The falling shale-things would still fall. The thirst-plants would still warm their flowers in the dark and draw small mouths to lingering death. The web-makers would still string valleys with silver geometry and feed on the panic of feet.

The power would not condemn them.

It would only prevent excess from becoming ruin.

It would place, here and there, small failures into perfect traps. A thread would frost too early and be seen. A salt-lure would dilute before every bird was taken. A frost-cat, after killing, would feel no urge to kill again until hunger returned.

The old agreements would hold, but their cruelest accidents would be softened at the edges.

And for the largest resident — the joined life of the plateau itself — the power would offer something like companionship.

Not consciousness imposed from outside. Not a mind nailed onto root and stone. Rather, a widening of what was already there.

The mountain-system knew pressure, moisture, heat, charge, hunger, flow. It responded without reflection. The power would not make it human. That would be a diminishment. Instead, it would help the plateau become more completely plateau: more able to remember across seasons, to distribute loss, to preserve variety, to recover from wounds.

Old scars in the basalt where lightning had killed entire conductive groves would become sites of new mineral bloom.

Abandoned vent tunnels would fill with insulating fungal wool, then become bird-nurseries.

Dead grazers would decay slowly, but more thoroughly, feeding not only predators but lichen, insects, moss, and the invisible pale colonies below stone.

The power would bless circulation.

It would bless return.

It would bless the fact that nothing in that cold country is wasted unless the pattern breaks.

If outsiders ever came — not one lost traveler, not a kneeling watcher beside a pool, but makers of roads, cutters of vents, miners of quartz, harvesters of resin and conductive leaf — then the power would act more plainly.

Not with armies.

With refusal.

Compasses would turn dull. Machines would frost from the inside. Fuel would gel. Maps would become subtly wrong. Every ridge would resemble another ridge. Sound would mislead. Distances would lengthen. The open gravel plains would seem endless, and the lush hollows would withdraw behind curtains of cloud.

Those who came to take would find nothing worth the cost.

Those who came to study with humility might be allowed a little nearer.

They would see enough to be changed, not enough to possess.

A biologist might glimpse the salamanders’ green pulse and return below with a reverence that altered a lifetime’s work. A wanderer might hear the ground-pressure of the whole plateau breathing and never again think of silence as emptiness. A child of some lower valley might find a black feather, oily and warm from a vent-mouth, and keep it as proof that life does not require permission from light.

The power would protect the plateau not by hiding it completely, but by ensuring that any knowledge taken from it carried awe as a binding agent.

For the being that lives there — the diffuse, ancient, uncentered life made of moss-bellows, root-wires, insects, grazers, frost-cats, mineral veins, and warm cracks — the power would give no name.

Names invite ownership.

Instead, it would give witness.

Once in a very long while, when the cloud lay low and the electrical groves opened their clear fronds, the whole tableland would feel itself accompanied. Not watched. Not judged. Accompanied.

The vents would exhale in sequence across miles of dark.

Blue-white sparks would move from thicket to thicket, not randomly, but with a slow coherence like thought passing through a sleeping body.

The pools would hold their black reflections.

The grazers would lift their heads, though no sound had been made.

The blind salamanders would glow faintly in the depths.

The web-makers would feel a vibration they could not eat.

And the mountain, if mountain can be said to know anything, would know this:

You are not a mistake.

You are not waiting to become a forest of tall trees, or a meadow, or a kingdom, or a place with roads.

You are already complete in your severity.

Keep your cold. Keep your dark. Keep your hidden abundance. Keep your difficult bargains. Let nothing be wasted. Let no single hunger devour the whole. Endure, but not alone.

Then the power would withdraw again into attention.

The plateau would remain quiet.

The wind would move over black slopes. Water would bead under leaves. Frost would edge the folded plants. White-backed animals would cross the open stone one by one, spaced like thoughts. Birds would warm their eggs over the breathing earth. Predators would wait where warmth creates passage. Lichen would think slowly across basalt. Moss would rise and fall with its silent factory breath.

Nothing would announce itself.

Everything would keep working.

But beneath the old economy of frost and vapor, there would be a new margin — slight, wise, almost invisible.

A mercy shaped like balance.

A blessing that did not brighten the dark, but made the dark more deeply alive.
