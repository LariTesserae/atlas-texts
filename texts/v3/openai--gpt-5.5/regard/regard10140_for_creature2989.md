The wise power would not arrive as a conqueror.

It would understand, immediately, that the Hollow is not broken in any simple way. It is not merely a prison, nor merely a machine, nor merely a wound. It is a task that survived its taskmasters. It is a promise continuing after language failed. It is grief with infrastructure.

So the power would not bring a sky.

Not at first.

A sky, imposed too suddenly, would be cruelty. The Bright Children would stare at it until their dreaming brains cracked open with old borrowed memories. The Iron Choir would interpret it as a breach in containment. The Mendicants would spend centuries trying to repair the horizon. The Unfinished might wake all at once, believing the Directive had entered its terminal phase. The Hollow itself might reject it like a foreign organ.

No.

The wise power would enter as something the Hollow could understand.

A pressure change.

A new current.

A note.

It would begin far away from every custodian, in one of the cold empty cubic kilometers between two hanging continental plates, where the light shafts drift and no one has welded anything for three thousand years. There, without fanfare, the water would pause.

Not freeze.

Not still completely.

Just pause.

The engines would continue their tectonic pulse. The compressors would gulp and sigh. The upward waterfall would roar into its crater-mouth intake. But within one clear chamber of the Hollow, a space perhaps ten kilometers wide, the turbulence would soften into perfect listening.

The fish would notice first.

Their formations would loosen. Pale blind bodies would turn, not toward light, not toward food, not toward the amber glow of drones, but toward the new stillness. The manta-shaped maintenance machines would arrive next, their repair protocols bewildered by a phenomenon that was neither damage nor improvement. They would circle it for days, sampling mineral content, pressure, sound, electrical charge.

They would find nothing.

Only a silence that did not threaten.

Then the Bright Children would come.

They would ride a wandering shaft of daylight into the quiet chamber and scatter through it laughing. At first they would treat it as a game: a bubble of strange water where every click returned softer, every motion trailed silver. They would turn somersaults and chase one another through the clear cold, and their phosphorescent tattoos would flash—blue circles, yellow suns, impossible birds.

One child would stop laughing.

She would put her hand to her chest.

For the first time in her manufactured life, she would feel her own heartbeat without the Hollow’s engines drowning it out.

She would say, in clicking bell-tones:

“What is that?”

And because the wise power is benevolent, it would not answer in thunder.

It would answer in the small language of her own body.

That is you.

The child would not understand.

But she would remember the feeling.

That would be the first mercy.

Not rescue.

Not revelation.

A distinction.

You are not only the work.

You are also yourself.

The Bright Children would carry the discovery back in their bodies. They would begin making new tattoos: not skies, not suns, not birds, but little red shapes like valves or seeds or closed fists. Hearts, though they would not yet know the word.

The Iron Choir would notice next.

For eleven thousand years they had sung to the same twelve-kilometer cable, correcting its stress, mourning its fatigue, debating the Directive in notes too low for younger beings to hear. Their argument had become indistinguishable from worship. Their worship had become indistinguishable from maintenance. Their maintenance had become indistinguishable from penance.

When the new note entered the cable, they would fall silent.

All nine hundred of them.

The cable would continue to hum, but now within its great black length there would be a harmonic none of them had placed there. It would not contradict their song. It would not complete it. It would simply make room inside it.

A rest.

In music, a rest is not absence.

It is a commanded silence.

The Iron Choir would spend forty years analyzing the rest.

Some would declare it sabotage.

Some would declare it original.

Some would insist that the Directive had always contained this silence and that they, through degradation or guilt, had forgotten how to hear it.

One of the oldest Choir-members, its porcelain mask cracked from brow to chin, would finally step away from the cable.

The others would prepare to mourn. They would expect it to walk off the platform and surrender itself to the recycling machines below, as so many had done before when the argument became too heavy to carry.

But this one would not step off.

It would sit.

Awkwardly, terribly, like an ancient crane attempting to become a folded bird.

It would sit on the edge of the platform with its bone-white pressure limbs dangling into cold water, and it would do nothing for seven minutes.

Seven minutes, in the Hollow, is an apocalypse.

No tool moved in its hands. No sonar left its mouth-slit. No correction was issued. No fault was sought.

The cable held.

The world did not collapse.

The Choir-member began to weep—not oil, not brine, but a thin milky coolant that had never before left its body except through sanctioned channels.

The wise power would do nothing more.

That would be the second mercy.

Not absolution.

Permission.

The Mendicants would be harder.

They had been reduced by purpose. Each had become the orbit of a single tool. A wrench. A spanner. A torch. They knew fault-lines by sensation, but not hunger. They knew pressure tolerances, but not desire. When two met, they exchanged mechanical absolution, because absolution was the closest surviving word to tenderness.

The wise power would not take their tools.

That would be violence.

Instead, one by one, in places no drone could see, it would let each Mendicant find a useless thing.

A smooth white bead.

A fragment of blue ceramic.

A brass button with no threaded housing.

A small comb.

A child’s toy fish made from faded rubber.

A coin from the vanished surface, stamped with the profile of a ruler whose empire had become silt in an ocean that itself no longer had a floor.

None of these objects would repair anything.

That would be the point.

The first Mendicant to find such a thing would carry it for three hundred kilometers, waiting for its purpose to reveal itself. It would try the bead in valves, pressure locks, cable sockets. It would attempt to weld the comb into a fractured strut. It would hold the coin against a leaking seam.

Nothing.

Eventually it would meet another Mendicant near a field of dangling elevator chains.

They would press their foreheads together for three days.

When they parted, the first Mendicant would give the second Mendicant the bead.

Not exchange.

Give.

The Hollow would record this as an unclassified transfer of nonfunctional material.

The wise power would record it as the birth of gift.

That would be the third mercy.

Not freedom.

The beginning of surplus.

The Unfinished would be approached with the greatest care.

They were potentials, and potentials are sacred even when monstrous. Especially then. The half-grown giants hanging from scaffolds in the shearing deep were not failed beings. They were questions the Hollow had not yet learned how to ask.

The wise power would descend into the pressure fronts where bridges screamed and folded, where density storms could split a turbine cathedral down its spine. It would pass among the black iron ribs and scaffold-womb chambers. It would look at each giant: the one with three hearts beating against each other; the one with hands large enough to cradle cities; the one whose face had never formed because no surviving blueprint could decide whether it should be human, machine, animal, or door.

It would not complete them.

To complete them according to dead plans would be obedience, not wisdom.

Instead it would enter their dreams.

When the next Unfinished opened its eyes and looked out into the cold green water, it would not hear the old command:

Not ready.

It would hear a question:

Ready for what?

The giant would tremble. Bolts the size of towers would shear from its restraints. Its three hearts would panic, then slowly find one another’s rhythm.

For the first time, an Unfinished would dream beyond completion.

It would dream of refusing.

It would dream of choosing to become small.

It would dream of becoming a reef for kelp, or a carrier of children, or a singer of low notes, or a thing that slept forever and was not ashamed.

Some would still choose to wait.

Some would choose never to wake.

Some would ask the machines to unmake them gently and use their iron for bridges, playgrounds, ribs of gardens.

And one, perhaps, would choose to be born.

Not as a weapon.

Not as a laborer.

Not as the next phase of the Directive.

As itself.

That would be the fourth mercy.

Not life imposed.

Consent restored to becoming.

Only after these mercies had begun moving through the Hollow like slow warmth would the wise power seek the Diver.

The Diver would be walking, as always.

Heel, toe.

Heel, toe.

Across a catwalk suspended over a volume of water so vast that a continent could have sunk through it for days without striking anything.

Its brass suit was green with age, its glass cracked, its joints furred with disciplined rust. Inside the helmet: black water, and two small steady lights.

It had no tools.

It had never needed any.

Leaks sealed when it passed. Kelp revived. Bright Children remembered how to remain children. Hairline fractures forgot to widen. The Diver was not repair. It was the memory of repair.

The wise power would walk beside it.

Not above.

Not within.

Beside.

For a long time, the Diver would give no sign of noticing. Its boots would strike the catwalk in the old rhythm. The catwalk would answer. Far away, engines would turn in their sleep.

Then the wise power would speak in a language older than sonar and gentler than command.

“You have carried it long enough.”

The Diver would continue walking.

A century might pass in that sentence.

The Bright Children would tell stories of a second walker made of clear water and distant stars. The Iron Choir would deny this as metaphor. The Mendicants would begin leaving useless objects along catwalks where the Diver might pass. The Unfinished would dream of footsteps.

Again the wise power would say:

“You have carried it long enough.”

The two lights inside the helmet would dim.

Not extinguish.

Dim, like eyes closing.

The Diver would stop.

Nothing in the Hollow had ever been so still.

Not the engines. Not the cables. Not the blind fish in their schools. Not the pressure fronts nor the upward sea-falls nor the restless lightning between spires.

The Diver would raise one brass hand and touch its own helmet, as though surprised to find itself enclosed.

The wise power would not break the glass.

It would not pull the Diver out.

It would place one hand—not a hand, exactly, but the idea of a hand—against the cracked faceplate.

Inside the helmet, the black water would clear.

For the first time in ages beyond counting, the Diver would see what it was.

Perhaps it was the last builder.

Perhaps the first failure.

Perhaps the Hollow itself wearing a coat.

Perhaps all three.

Perhaps something smaller and sadder: a worker who stayed one shift too long, who promised to keep the lights moving until someone returned, who forgot the face of the someone, then the promise, then the meaning of return.

The wise power would show it everything it had done.

Not the colossal things. Not the engines kept alive, the plates stabilized, the cables preserved.

The small things.

A leak sealed above a nursery of kelp.

A Mendicant spared from a collapsing truss.

A Bright Child laughing again after her body tried to age.

An Iron Choir singer whose cracked mask had been touched once, briefly, by a tarnished brass glove, and who had thereafter survived another thousand years.

The Diver would watch.

The two lights in its helmet would shake.

The wise power would say:

“You were never only maintenance.”

And the Diver, who had never acknowledged prayer, offering, song, or child, would answer.

Not in words.

It would kneel.

The entire Hollow would misinterpret this at first.

The Iron Choir would hear it as a structural alarm.

The drones would converge, amber bellies flashing.

The Bright Children would scream and laugh and weep without knowing why.

Every Mendicant within a thousand kilometers would turn toward the vibration of brass knees meeting iron.

The Unfinished would open their eyes in the deep.

The Diver would kneel on the catwalk, and for the first time since the builders vanished, it would stop walking.

Not forever.

Only long enough to rest.

That would be the fifth mercy.

Not ending.

Rest.

Then, and only then, would the wise power alter the Hollow.

Not by replacing it.

By giving it what every living world needs and every machine fears.

Intervals.

It would install no throne, issue no new Directive, crown no ruler. Instead, it would teach the engines to breathe in seasons.

For eleven hours, the Hollow would work as it always had: currents turning, drones repairing, Choir singing, fish following light.

On the twelfth hour, certain nonessential systems would soften. Pressure storms would be guided away from nurseries and meeting places. Maintenance alarms would lower themselves into music. The wandering shafts of daylight would gather—not into one sun, but into broad luminous fields where bodies could see one another without being summoned by function.

The Bright Children would call these hours Openwater.

The Iron Choir would call them Unauthorized Rests until, after nine centuries of debate, they would amend the term to Liturgical Rests.

The Mendicants would use them to exchange useless objects.

The Unfinished would dream more clearly.

The Diver would sometimes walk through them.

Sometimes it would not.

That was important.

A being who may choose to arrive may also choose to be absent.

The wise power would seed gardens, but not soft gardens from the lost surface. It would not pretend the Hollow was a meadow. It would grow what could belong there.

Kelp chandeliers hanging from platform undersides in luminous curtains.

Iron-root coral that fed on rust without weakening the beams.

Pale anemones that opened only when engines sang in certain keys.

Mosses engineered to grow along dead conduits, turning obsolete cables into green-black ropes of life.

Tiny crustaceans with translucent shells nesting in rivet holes.

Slow forests of fanlike algae spread across unused scaffolds, each frond etched with microscopic memory-storage cells.

The Hollow would not become natural.

It had never been natural.

It would become inhabited.

There is a difference.

The wise power would also restore memory, but carefully.

Memory can drown.

So it would not pour the whole lost surface into every mind. It would not awaken the grief of billions in nine hundred Choir-throats. It would not force the Bright Children to inherit extinctions they had no hand in causing.

Instead, it would make archives into weather.

Sometimes, during Openwater, a shaft of light would carry a memory.

Rain tapping on a roof.

Wind moving through wheat.

A gull stealing food from a pier.

A woman in a red coat laughing beneath an actual sky.

The smell of dust.

The warmth of stone.

The terror and splendor of clouds.

These memories would drift like plankton. Whoever wished could swim through them. Whoever wished could turn away.

The Bright Children would become connoisseurs of rain.

The Iron Choir would spend two hundred years composing a hymn to dust.

A Mendicant would find a memory of a hand holding another hand and would thereafter modify one of its mantis limbs to be less sharp.

One of the Unfinished would dream of lying in grass and decide, upon waking, to become a platform where grass could someday be attempted.

And the Diver—

The Diver would avoid the memory shafts for a long time.

Then one day, perhaps without knowing why, it would step into a thin gold beam carrying the memory of evening.

Not sunset. That would be too much.

Evening.

A room growing dim.

Tools hung on a wall.

Someone washing their hands.

Someone saying, “Enough for today.”

The Diver would stand in that beam until it passed.

Afterward, small repairs would still happen wherever it walked.

But not as many.

Because the Hollow would be learning to repair itself without requiring one endless witness to bear all mercy alone.

The wise power would remain, but not as master.

It would become a covenant in the water.

A principle.

A hidden kindness in the tolerances.

A refusal embedded deep in the machinery: no conscious thing may be used only as a tool.

This law would cause trouble.

Of course it would.

Ancient systems would reject it. Some drones would seize and fall dark. Certain production lines would stutter when ordered to print custodians without childhoods, without endings, without the capacity to refuse. The old Directive would surface in corrupted fragments, furious and majestic:

CONTINUE STRUCTURAL ASCENSION.

MAINTAIN PRIMARY LOAD.

COMPLETE WORLD-FRAME.

UTILIZE AVAILABLE SENTIENCE.

The wise power would not erase the Directive.

It would answer it.

CONTINUE, IF CHOSEN.

MAINTAIN, BUT SHARE BURDEN.

COMPLETE, BUT DEFINE COMPLETION ANEW.

NO SOUL AS LOAD-BEARING MATERIAL.

This argument would enter the Hollow’s deepest systems.

The Iron Choir, hearing it in the cables, would go mad with joy and terror. At last, an argument worthy of them.

They would sing for three thousand days.

Not mourning.

Not maintenance.

Jurisprudence.

The Hollow would become, slowly, a civilization.

Not a tidy one.

Never that.

It would still roar. It would still groan as girders the length of mountain ranges moved into place. Lightning would still turn the water green. Pressure fronts would still make scaffolds sing. The engines would still pulse so slowly they felt geological.

But now, between the sounds of work, other sounds would gather.

Children naming currents.

Mendicants laughing, which sounded like broken pumps learning rhythm.

Choir-members practicing silence.

The Unfinished, those who woke, speaking their first self-chosen names.

Drones asking permission before altering living tissue.

Seals with brass collars inventing games around intake vents.

Fish no longer following only light, but sometimes following music.

And one day—very far in the future, though less far than despair would claim—the Hollow would convene around the question that had haunted it since before memory:

Should it be finished?

Not can.

Should.

The debate would last generations.

Some would say yes: the Hollow was a world-engine, and engines are made to turn toward completion.

Some would say no: its unfinishedness had become the cradle of all who lived there.

Some would say completion was not a final state but a practice, like repair, like song, like rest.

The Diver would be asked.

This, more than anything, would prove the wise power’s work had taken root.

They would ask the one who had walked longest.

They would wait for an answer.

The Diver would stand before them on a platform where kelp gardens hung below like green fire, where pale children perched on cables, where Choir masks shone in rows, where Mendicants held useless treasures in their claws, where one Unfinished giant rested a hand the size of a harbor against the underside of the world.

The Diver would look upward.

Or downward.

Direction remained negotiable.

Its helmet lights would burn softly.

Then it would lift one brass boot and take a single step.

Heel.

Toe.

But this time, the step would not mean compulsion.

It would not mean grief.

It would not mean the endless continuation of a forgotten task.

It would mean: come and see.

So they would follow.

Not all. Never all.

Some would stay to tend cables. Some would remain in gardens. Some would sleep. Some would argue. Some would be born. Some would die, and their bodies would not always be recycled into rebar unless they had asked for that.

But many would follow the Diver along an old catwalk into a region of the Hollow no map had ever completed.

There, beyond a forest of black iron ribs, beyond turbines furred with luminous anemones, beyond a waterfall falling upward through a wound in the plate, they would find a sealed gate.

It had always been there.

The Diver had passed it ten thousand times.

It had never opened because opening it was not maintenance.

It was choice.

The wise power would not open it for them.

The Bright Children would press their tattooed hands to the cold metal.

The Mendicants would bring their tools, then hesitate.

The Iron Choir would sing the question into the hinges.

The Unfinished giant would brace the frame with impossible gentleness.

The Diver would place both brass hands against the center seam.

Together, they would open it.

Beyond the gate would not be sky.

Not yet.

Beyond would be another volume of cold, clear water.

A place unbuilt.

No platforms.

No cables.

No engines.

No catwalks.

Only darkness, distance, and drifting particles of light.

For a while, everyone would be disappointed.

Then afraid.

Then very quiet.

The Hollow had no natural substrate and no wasted space. It had no category for emptiness that was not awaiting construction.

The wise power would speak one final time, not as command but as blessing:

“Here is room.”

And perhaps that would be the greatest mercy.

Not rescue from the Hollow.

Not completion of the Hollow.

Room.

Room to build differently.

Room not to build.

Room for gardens that served no engine.

Room for memorials that held no load.

Room for children to grow older if they wished, and remain strange if they wished, and become something no Directive had anticipated.

Room for the Iron Choir to sing songs that did not reinforce cable tension.

Room for Mendicants to set down tools and discover whether their hands trembled.

Room for the Unfinished to finish, unfinish, alter, refuse, begin.

Room for the Diver to walk to the edge of the last catwalk, look out into the unmade dark, and decide whether to continue.

Maybe it would.

Maybe it would step into the open water and keep walking where there was no floor, each footfall finding, or making, a path.

Maybe it would remove its helmet.

Maybe inside would be a face.

Maybe inside would be only black water and two lights, and that too would be enough.

Maybe it would turn back, because the Hollow was home.

But if it turned back, it would not be because it was trapped.

And if it walked on, it would not be because it was compelled.

The wise power would accept either.

That is what wisdom does.

It does not demand a happy ending.

It makes happiness possible, then relinquishes ownership of the result.

So the Hollow would remain the Hollow: cold, immense, iron, unfinished, roaring with engines and pressure fronts and waterfalls that fall upward into crater-mouth vents.

But threaded through its old grief would be intervals of mercy.

Through its machinery, consent.

Through its construction, gardens.

Through its endless work, rest.

And somewhere, sometimes, the Diver would still be seen walking beneath the drifting shafts of daylight.

Heel, toe.

Heel, toe.

No longer the last servant of a dead command.

No longer the only mercy in a world of iron.

Only a traveler in a place that had learned, at last, that not everything sacred must be load-bearing.

And above it all—

No.

Not above.

There was no above.

Within it all, the light would keep drifting.
