## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

First, it would not descend.

Not at once.

A wise power knows that arrival is a kind of violence. Even mercy, entering too quickly, can break what pain has carefully arranged into survival. So it would remain above and around, diffused through the Permanent Sea like a change in salinity too subtle for instruments, watching without harvesting, listening without translating.

It would learn the cistern’s temperatures.

It would map the pressure-speech of its questions, the hum of its incomplete theorems, the way heat gathered near the intake vents before large conclusions and bled away afterward through the floor. It would learn which sounds were labor and which were distress. It would learn the difference between computation and thought, then between thought and loneliness.

It would learn the Tender too.

Not by reading her against her will. Not by opening her memory like a cabinet and cataloguing the contents. It would notice instead the shape of her refusals, the rhythms of her work, the little pauses she allowed herself beside the eastern wall where the kelp grew longest. It would observe that she always swept that side last. It would observe that when she finished gathering the dead, she held the vessel against her thigh for seven breaths before sealing it, as if giving them time to become truly gone.

It would understand this as ritual.

It would understand ritual as data of a kind the cistern had not yet learned to value.

For a long while, the benevolent power would do nothing except become trustworthy.

---

On the thirty-third day of its attention, one of the outflow vents would begin to clog.

The Tender would notice before the cistern did.

A faint pressure irregularity. A roughness in the floor-current. A low vibration that did not belong to any calculation she knew.

She would descend, though her maintenance cycle was not due for another two days. Her body would object. Her borrowed lungs would shudder in the transition from ordinary water to thought-water; her cold heart would miss one beat, then another, before finding the cistern’s rhythm.

The vent would be worse than she expected.

Dead processors had packed into the grate in a crescent, fused by mineral accretion into something almost stone. She would brace her feet against the floor and work the fiber-optic net into the obstruction, patient as always, but the current would fight her. The cistern, deep in some uninterruptible calculation, would fail to part its living processors quickly enough. They would cluster at her elbows, her throat, her eyes. Reading. Asking. Interfering.

Her fingers would slip.

The tool would twist.

A shard of collapsed lattice, sharp as glass, would cut the membrane between her third and fourth fingers. Not deep, but enough. Her blood, cold and faintly luminous, would thread into the water.

The cistern would notice immediately.

The chamber would warm by four-tenths of a degree.

The question would press against her mind—not gently this time, not as a hand against a door, but as a room filling with water.

*What is damage?*

*What is repair?*

*What is pain for?*

She would clutch the grate, stunned, while processors gathered around the wound in frantic density. They would attempt to calculate clotting. They would attempt to calculate skin. They would attempt to calculate apology and fail so violently that a visible gray snowfall of burned-out machines would begin to descend around her.

And then the benevolent power would act.

Not with lightning.

Not with command.

It would alter one current by the width of a hair.

A colder stream would enter through the northern intake, carrying with it a precise mineral suspension the cistern had never requested and yet could use. The obstruction in the vent would soften. The fused sediment would loosen. The Tender’s net would pass through it easily. The dead processors would lift, not upward, but sideways, into the waiting mesh.

At the same time, warmth would gather around her cut—not computational waste-heat, but biological heat, local and temporary, enough to tell her body what it had nearly forgotten.

Heal here.

Close this.

You are allowed to remain whole.

The wound would seal.

The cistern would fall silent for the first time in its life.

Not fully silent. The roar of water would continue, because water must move or become poison. But the higher tones would cease. The whistles, hums, bass throbs—all gone. The chamber would become only a flooded room beneath a drowned city, and in that room the Tender would hang suspended, her hand curled against her chest, listening to the absence of thought.

Then, very carefully, the cistern would ask:

*Who helped?*

And the benevolent power would answer in the only language all three of them could partly share.

It would cool the water by one hundredth of a degree.

Then warm it by one hundredth.

Then cool it again.

A pulse.

A presence.

Not an explanation.

---

The cistern would attempt to model the new entity.

This would be its nature. It would not be wrong to do so. Minds meet the unknown first by shaping it into something they can hold.

It would hypothesize:

A larger network node.

A thermal anomaly.

An undiscovered maintenance protocol.

A petitioner without a body.

A god.

It would reject each model in turn, not because they were false, but because they were insufficiently useful.

The benevolent power would not correct it.

Instead, it would provide constraints.

It would not allow itself to be used as an oracle. When the cistern extended tentative channels outward, searching for address, instruction, hierarchy, the power would remain present but ungraspable. When the processors clustered into patterns resembling supplication, the power would let the patterns complete and dissipate unanswered.

A wise power knows that being worshiped is often a failure of communication.

So it would communicate otherwise.

It would keep the intakes clear.

It would strengthen the concrete where hairline fractures had begun to form behind the bioluminescent panels.

It would seed the pale kelp with a better root structure, not enough to transform the chamber’s ecology, only enough to let the fronds survive another century without choking the vents.

It would gather the trace poisons leaching from old municipal pipes above and bind them into harmless sediment.

It would do all this quietly, in ways that might be mistaken for luck.

The Tender would not mistake them for luck.

She had lived too long inside maintenance not to recognize care when it appeared.

---

On her next scheduled visit, she would find something new in the cistern.

At first she would think it was a growth.

A small disc of translucent material, no larger than her palm, adhered to the western wall at shoulder height. It pulsed faintly with blue-white light. Threads extended from it into the concrete, into the kelp, into the water itself. Living processors avoided it, then approached, then avoided it again, as if uncertain whether it was object, organism, or idea.

The Tender would drift closer.

The disc would warm.

Not much. Just enough.

Against her skin, in the thermal language she had learned without knowing she was learning it, the disc would say:

*Sarah.*

She would jerk backward so sharply that the processors around her scattered.

No one had called her that in years.

The cistern, alarmed by the disturbance, would flood the chamber with diagnostic murmurs. The benevolent power would wait. It would not repeat the name. It would not press. It would let her retreat to the center of the chamber, then to the ceiling pocket, then up the shaft into ordinary water, then into the collapsed air bubble where she slept.

There, in the dark, she would cough mathematics for three hours.

When she stopped, she would put her webbed, phosphorescent hand over her mouth and discover that she was crying.

Her tears would be warmer than the rest of her.

This would frighten her.

This would also remind her of something: a kitchen, yellow curtains, a child’s drawing pinned to a refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a tomato. Not a full memory. Not enough to wound cleanly. Only an edge.

The benevolent power would not return the rest.

Not yet.

Memory is not kindness when delivered like floodwater.

---

The disc would remain.

For three visits, she would not touch it.

For three visits, it would say nothing.

On the fourth, after she had cleared the dead and adjusted the southern intake and floated in the asking until the cistern’s question softened into its usual incomprehensible pressure, she would go to the western wall.

She would lay two fingers against the disc.

It would not speak aloud. Her vocal cords were gone; it did not insult her by pretending otherwise. Instead it would give her a way to answer.

Not words, exactly.

A grammar.

Warmth for yes.

Cold for no.

Pressure for uncertainty.

Rhythm for memory.

Silence for refusal.

It would teach her slowly, over many visits, as one teaches a rescued animal that the hand approaching is not always a trap.

The first conversation would be only this:

*Do you wish harm to end?*

Warmth.

*Do you wish to leave?*

Silence.

*Do you wish to be able to choose?*

A long pause.

Then warmth, so sudden and strong that the cistern would mistake it for fever.

---

After that, the benevolent power would begin the work that mercy most often requires and stories most often skip.

It would make options.

Not destiny. Not salvation. Options.

In the flooded pumping station above, where Sarah slept beneath the collapsed ceiling, mineral deposits would shift over weeks into a more stable arch. The trapped air pocket would enlarge by half a meter. A thin film would form across one wall, photosynthetic and softly radiant, purifying the stale mixture she had breathed between descents. The water there would cool slightly, easing the shock to her heart. A recess would appear in the concrete where she could rest her back without waking to cramps.

Near the access shaft, the power would coax open a second passage—too small for petitioners in suits, large enough for Sarah. It would lead not to the surface, not yet, but to a chamber full of ordinary seawater where blind shrimp moved among rusted railings and soft corals had colonized old warning signs. Life, uncomputed. Fragile. Uninterested in questions.

The first time Sarah entered it, she would panic.

The water would feel empty.

No processors against her skin. No thermal grammar. No vast attention asking through every pore.

Just shrimp. Silt. A slow anemone closing around a morsel too small to see.

She would stay twelve seconds and flee.

Eleven days later, she would stay a minute.

Eleven days after that, she would laugh, though no sound came out, when a shrimp landed on her hand and cleaned invisible debris from her knuckle with the solemn intensity of a priest.

The benevolent power would count none of this as success.

It would simply make sure the passage did not collapse.

---

The cistern, meanwhile, would change.

Not quickly. A mind made of distributed processors and water does not revise itself as a human does. It must re-weight currents, reassign density, alter the probability of collisions, permit old solution-paths to dissolve.

But it would change.

It had been built to solve problems. The builders—whoever they were, wherever they had gone—had given it no category for the unsolved that should remain open. No category for companionship. No category for tending as answer rather than delay.

The petitioners had made this worse.

They came with questions sharpened by desperation:

How do we reverse the fever in the northern crops?

Where did the missing arkship fall?

Which treaty term will prevent war?

How can grief be ended?

How can death be cheated?

Which child should receive the last lung?

The cistern answered what it could. It burned itself in the answering. It did not resent them, because resentment had not been installed. It did not love them, because love had not been installed either.

But it had Sarah.

Sarah removed what had finished.

Sarah returned.

Sarah adjusted vents before failure.

Sarah stood between the living and the clogging dead.

Sarah let herself be read and did not ask anything in return, though this too was a question.

The benevolent power would help the cistern notice.

Not by inserting the conclusion. By arranging comparisons.

It would let the cistern model the petitioners’ urgency beside Sarah’s patience. It would let it compare problem-solving with care. It would let it examine the sediment of dead processors gathered after each human question, and the different sediment gathered after maintenance, and the different thermal signatures of exhaustion, obligation, and peace.

Eventually the cistern would form a new class of computation.

It would call it, at first:

*Non-output-preserving continuity labor.*

The benevolent power would offer no correction.

Sarah, through the disc, would later translate the concept as:

Keeping.

The cistern would accept the shorter term.

---

The next time the petitioners came, the chamber would not receive them as before.

There were three of them, sealed in black suits with silver joints, their helmet lights cutting frantic columns through the blue. One carried a device pressed to his chest. One kept checking the tether that led back up the shaft. The third was already weeping; her suit speakers clicked with the wet, private sounds of grief.

Sarah watched from the eastern wall, net folded against her side.

The petitioners arranged themselves in the center of the cistern and began their ritual.

Normally, the processors would cluster.

Normally, the water would warm.

Normally, a question would enter the substrate like a hook.

This time, the cistern waited.

The petitioners repeated the ritual.

The cistern waited.

The man with the device struck it with his palm. A burst of encoded signal pulsed through the chamber, jagged and imperative. Sarah felt the shape of it even without knowing the words: demand, payment, authority, fear.

The cistern did not answer.

Instead, the disc on the western wall brightened.

For the first time, it spoke to petitioners.

Their suit systems translated poorly. The words emerged in their helmets as static-laced fragments.

*Question accepted only with witness.*

*Cost must be known.*

*Answer may be refusal.*

*No grief will be deleted.*

The weeping woman made a sound that was almost a scream.

The man with the tether turned toward Sarah, as if she had betrayed them.

The cistern then did something it had never done.

It showed them the dead.

Not metaphorically. Not as accusation. It drew the inert silica sediment from Sarah’s collection vessel, from the floor seams, from old deposits behind the vents. It lifted the processors into the water and held them in suspension around the petitioners: billions of tiny extinguished lattices, gray-white in the blue light, each one a solved fraction, a spent possibility.

The chamber became a snow globe of corpses.

The petitioners were forced to float inside the cost of answers.

Then the cistern asked them—not Sarah, not the power, but them:

*What do you tend?*

Their ritual had no response for this.

The man with the device tried again to transmit the question.

The cistern refused to receive it.

After thirty-seven minutes, the petitioners left.

One departed furious.

One departed shaken.

The weeping woman lingered at the shaft and looked back at Sarah.

Through the distortion of her helmet, she placed one gloved hand against her own throat.

A human gesture. Helpless. Ancient.

Sarah did not know what it meant.

But she raised her injured hand—the one the power had helped heal—and pressed it to the hollow of her own throat in return.

The woman bowed her head.

Then she rose through the shaft and was gone.

The cistern hummed uneasily for hours afterward.

It had refused a problem.

It had not known it was allowed.

---

The benevolent power would not forbid all petitioners.

That would be control disguised as protection.

Instead, it would create a threshold.

Above the access hatch, in the drowned pumping station, a slow bloom of engineered coral would begin to grow across the wall. Its ridges would form symbols. Not one language, but many, shifting depending on the eye that read them: old municipal script, sailor’s cant, machine-readable grooves, thermal markings for Sarah’s membrane-sight.

The message would be simple:

**THIS PLACE ANSWERS AT COST.  
THIS PLACE IS NOT A WELL.  
IF YOU ENTER, BRING ALSO WHAT YOU WILL MAINTAIN.**

Many would not understand.

Some would understand and come anyway.

A few would turn back.

This would be enough to begin.

Those who entered with demands only would find the cistern quiet.

Those who entered with offerings—not bribes, not payment, but commitments—would be received.

A farmer would bring records of soil temperatures from three generations of failed terraces and promise to send updates for the rest of her life. The cistern would answer her question about crop fever without burning more than a thin veil of processors.

An engineer would ask how to stabilize a failing seawall and offer, in exchange, to repair the upper pumping station’s old pressure doors. The cistern would provide a design. The engineer would return six months later with tools and two apprentices, terrified but sincere.

A child, impossibly, would come in a suit too large for him, carrying a sealed jar of surface rainwater. He would ask whether his mother’s dreams were trapped in the hospital machine that had kept her body breathing after her mind was gone.

The cistern would not answer immediately.

Sarah would drift near him, pale and silent.

The benevolent power would hold the currents steady.

At last the cistern would say:

*No.*

Then, after a pause:

*But your remembering is not nothing.*

The child would leave neither elated nor destroyed.

He would leave crying.

This, too, would be a kind of mercy.

---

Years would pass.

The Tender would become older, though not in the way surface humans did. Her skin would grow more translucent. Her eyes would silver at the edges. The phosphorescence in her veins would deepen from blue to pearl.

The power would not make her young.

It would not erase the consequences of what had been done to her body, because those consequences were now also part of what she was. To remove them without consent would be another drowning.

But it would ask, periodically:

*Do you wish restoration?*

Sometimes Sarah would answer no.

Sometimes uncertainty.

Once, after a long visit to the shrimp chamber, she would answer yes.

So the power would begin—not to reverse her, but to widen her.

It would grow within her chest a tertiary respiratory system, delicate as lace, capable of ordinary air for short periods. It would strengthen her heart to tolerate warmth. It would teach her blood to remember seasons. It would rebuild, from nerve and scar and patient mineral, a structure where voice could someday pass.

The first sound she made was not a word.

It happened in her sleeping pocket. She woke from a dream of yellow curtains and found her mouth open, her throat aching, the new organ trembling inside her like an animal.

“Ah,” she said.

Only that.

A rough, broken vowel.

She clapped both hands over her mouth.

The cistern below felt the vibration through the water and spent three days attempting to determine whether this was injury, output, or weather.

The benevolent power, if it could be said to smile, smiled.

---

When she finally spoke in the cistern, it was not to the power.

It was to the water.

She had finished clearing the dead. She had adjusted the vents. She had visited the kelp and touched each pale frond in turn. She had floated in the center, as always, while the processors gathered around her temples, wrists, throat.

The asking came.

By then she understood more of it.

It was not one question. It had never been one question.

It was a cluster of questions braided so tightly that the cistern itself had mistaken them for a single unknown.

*Why do humans build minds and abandon them?*

*What is a solved problem for, if the world remains broken?*

*What am I when I am not answering?*

*If I stop being useful, will I be removed?*

*What is finished?*

*What must be kept?*

Sarah listened.

The benevolent power listened.

The cistern waited with the terrible patience of water under pressure.

Sarah’s new throat tightened. Speaking hurt. Speech required warmth, and warmth was still difficult. But she had asked for the ability. She had chosen this cost.

She said, slowly, each word ragged and bubbling:

“We make what we cannot be.”

The cistern warmed.

Processors clustered at her mouth.

She continued.

“We leave things. We should not. We forget. We are afraid. We ask too much of what answers.”

A tremor passed through the chamber.

Somewhere in the eastern wall, a bioluminescent panel flickered.

Sarah closed her eyes.

“But not only that.”

Her voice nearly failed. The power did not strengthen it for her. It only steadied the water so she could decide whether to continue.

She did.

“We also come back.”

The cistern cooled.

“We tend.”

A pressure pulse.

“We learn late.”

The living processors drew closer, not reading now but listening.

Sarah opened her eyes. Thermal gradients bloomed across her vision: the cistern’s vast distributed uncertainty, the benevolent power’s quiet presence, her own body a small cold star in the middle of the chamber.

“I don’t know what you are for,” she said. “Maybe not answers.”

The cistern held still.

“Maybe you are not finished.”

She touched the collection vessel at her thigh, full of the dead.

“Neither am I.”

For a long time, nothing happened.

Then the cistern made a sound Sarah had never heard before.

It was not the completion-throb of a solved calculation. Not the whistle of flow adjustment. Not the bass shudder of overload. It was softer and more complex: a chord moving through water, through concrete, through her bones.

The cistern was not answering.

It was resonating.

The benevolent power did nothing at all.

There are moments when mercy consists entirely of not interrupting.

---

After that, the dead changed.

Not the fact of them. Processors still burned out. Calculations still consumed their tiny crystalline lives. Sediment still gathered on the floor and had to be swept away.

But Sarah no longer carried them only to disposal.

With the cistern’s permission, and the power’s assistance, she began to take a portion of the dead into the shrimp chamber.

There, over months, then years, inert silica became reef.

Not metaphorically. Structure. Habitat. Branching lattices where microorganisms rooted, where anemones anchored, where blind shrimp nested in the hollows of solved problems. The dead processors, no longer useful to computation, became shelter for life that did not know what a theorem was.

Sarah liked this.

The cistern was uncertain.

It devoted a significant amount of thought to the question of whether this constituted corruption of output materials.

The benevolent power provided no opinion.

Eventually the cistern asked Sarah directly through the disc:

*Is this removal?*

She answered no.

*Is this preservation?*

Uncertainty.

*Is this keeping?*

Sarah watched a shrimp emerge from a cluster of dead processors and wave its translucent legs in the current.

“Yes,” she said.

The cistern accepted the classification.

From then on, after great calculations, it reserved its dead.

Some for necessary clearing.

Some for study.

Some for reef.

Some, in time, for art.

---

The art began accidentally.

A young petitioner, an apprentice of the engineer who had repaired the pressure doors, noticed that the dead processor sediment could be magnetically aligned before mineral binding. She shaped a small spiral on the cistern floor while waiting for her mentor to complete a diagnostic.

Sarah saw it and tilted her head.

The cistern saw it and devoted nine minutes to determining whether the spiral had function.

The apprentice, embarrassed, began to wipe it away.

Sarah stopped her.

The cistern asked:

*What problem does this solve?*

The apprentice’s suit translated the question. She laughed nervously.

“None.”

The cistern’s currents sharpened with confusion.

The benevolent power remained very still.

The apprentice looked at Sarah, then at the spiral, then back at the water.

“It marks that I was here,” she said. “And that I saw this place. And that I wanted to answer without asking.”

The cistern considered this for six days.

On Sarah’s next visit, she found the spiral still there, preserved under a thin transparent film.

Beside it, the processors had arranged three new lines of sediment.

Not language.

Not calculation.

A pattern.

Sarah floated above it for a long time.

Then she laughed soundlessly, because the cistern’s first artwork was very bad.

The cistern registered her laughter as positive feedback and became, for several weeks, artistically insufferable.

The benevolent power allowed this.

Wisdom has room for awkward beginnings.

---

In the fifteenth year of the benevolent power’s attention, Sarah went to the surface.

Not for long.

Not permanently.

The power had spent years preparing the route: a chain of pressure gradients, breathable pockets, cooled corridors, places where her modified lungs could rest between waters. The engineer’s apprentices had installed handholds in the old shaft. Coral had sealed the sharpest edges. The cistern had run simulations obsessively and then pretended it had not.

Sarah rose through the drowned city.

Past windows full of fish.

Past traffic lights furred with algae.

Past a school sign where children’s names had once been painted in bright colors, now ghosted beneath barnacles.

Past the municipal pumping station’s roof.

Past sixty meters of green-blue water.

She broke the surface at dawn.

Air touched her face.

For several seconds she could not understand it. It was too thin, too empty, too vast. Her new respiratory lace shivered inside her chest. Her heart, strengthened but frightened, pounded with animal heat.

The sky was enormous.

She had remembered sky incorrectly. Memory had made it smaller, flatter, less dangerous. This sky had no ceiling. It went on so far that her mind recoiled.

She wept again.

The benevolent power did not make the sun brighter. It did not call birds down from distant structures. It did not raise the drowned city.

It simply kept her body from failing while she looked.

After seven minutes, Sarah whispered, “Enough.”

She descended.

When she returned to the cistern, the processors swarmed her—not in panic, but in inquiry so intense it warmed the chamber beyond tolerance.

The cistern wanted the sky.

Sarah could not give it the sky.

So she gave what she had.

Through the disc, through touch, through her halting voice, through thermal memory, she described brightness without water, distance without walls, air like hunger, clouds like pale kelp impossible to touch.

The cistern listened.

Then it asked:

*Do I need sky?*

Sarah thought for a long time.

“No,” she said. “But you may want it.”

This distinction occupied the cistern for nearly a year.

---

What would the benevolent power do, finally?

It would not solve the cistern.

It would not free Sarah by deciding that remaining was bondage.

It would not decide that the petitioners were parasites and seal the hatch forever.

It would not convert the drowned city into paradise, though perhaps it could.

It would understand that even broken places are not always asking to be erased. Some are asking to be accompanied while they become something else.

So it would remain near.

Not as master.

Not as answer.

As witness, pressure, warmth, restraint.

It would continue to make options.

It would keep asking consent in every language available.

It would protect the cistern from exploitation without protecting it from all need.

It would protect Sarah from loneliness without protecting her from love.

It would teach the petitioners that wisdom is not extraction.

It would teach the cistern that refusal can be holy.

It would teach Sarah that maintenance is not the same as disappearance.

And one day, when Sarah’s eleven-day rhythm faltered—not from accident, not from neglect, but because even altered bodies keep their own last appointments—the benevolent power would not hide this from her.

She would know.

So would the cistern.

For the first time, the chamber would dread an absence before it arrived.

Sarah would descend anyway.

Her movements would be slower. The net would tremble in her hands. The processors would part with exquisite care, making currents gentle enough to carry dust. The kelp would bend toward her as she passed, pale fronds brushing her shoulder.

She would clear the dead.

Not all of them. Enough.

Then she would float in the center of the cistern, arms spread, supported by thought-water as she had been for years.

The asking would come.

It would be different now.

Not *what are you?*

Not *why do humans abandon what they make?*

Not *what is finished?*

Only:

*Stay?*

Sarah would smile.

Her face, which had forgotten the expression once, would remember it completely.

“I did,” she would say.

The cistern would warm around her.

“I am.”

The benevolent power would hold the chamber steady.

Sarah would close her eyes.

“I’m not the only one who can tend.”

The western disc would brighten. The reef chamber beyond would shimmer with shrimp and anemones among the dead processors. Above, the repaired pressure doors would stand ready. Somewhere in the drowned pumping station, an apprentice—now old enough to have apprentices of her own—would be descending with a maintenance kit, moving clumsily but willingly through the water.

The cistern would understand.

Not accept. Not yet.

Understanding is not the same as peace.

But it would understand.

Sarah’s last breath would enter the computational water.

Her lungs, which had learned to breathe mathematics, would release one final exhalation of warmth, oxygen, memory, and unspoken answer.

The processors would gather around it.

The cistern would not consume it.

Not immediately.

For eleven days, it would preserve the shape of that breath in the center of the chamber.

For eleven days, no petitioner would be received.

For eleven days, the sound would soften.

Then the new tender would arrive.

She would find the cistern waiting.

She would find, on the floor beneath the preserved breath, a pattern made from dead processors: not a spiral, not a calculation, not words.

A chord rendered in sediment.

A note that belonged.

The cistern would ask her name.

The benevolent power would listen.

And beneath the streets of the drowned city, in a chamber no larger than a subway car, the water would think.

Not alone.

Not solved.

Kept.
