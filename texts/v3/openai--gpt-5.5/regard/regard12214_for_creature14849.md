The benevolent power would arrive without arrival.

No split in the sky.  
No trumpet.  
No sudden burning sigil on the basalt.

Here, such things would be cruelty. A shout in a people made of listening skin. A blaze in eyes shaped for bruise-light. A command descending into a civilization that has survived by never mistaking force for wisdom.

So the power would come as a change too small to fear.

A slightly warmer seam in the stone.

A new taste in the mist: not iron, not mint, but something like remembered safety.

The Drift would feel it first.

Across the black teeth of the spires, millions of soft bodies would pause—not still, never still, but slowed into a single long ripple. The grey tide would taste the air with blind, patient hunger and find nothing edible, nothing threatening. Only a boundary. Only a suggestion.

Not away, exactly.

Not *do not come here*.

More like:

*there is enough elsewhere.*

And The Drift, being ancient in appetite but simple in malice, would flow around the places the power touched.

The Chimeless would notice hours later.

A child, suspended in a fern-cradle, would sleep through three full pushes without the grey mouths gathering beneath them.

An elder Rhythm-Keeper, whose knees had begun to ache from endless rocking, would find that the polished stone ball under their chair moved more easily, as if the ledge itself had learned compassion.

A rope bridge long feared for its fraying heart-vines would hold beneath a crossing it should not have survived.

No one would speak of miracle.

They would lay hands against the wet wood. They would taste the air. They would press slow questions into one another’s arms.

*What changed?*  
*Who moved?*  
*Is it dangerous?*  
*Is it hungry?*

The wise power would answer in the only language that mattered here.

Not with words.

With pressure.

A tremor through the guide-ropes, gentle enough not to alarm the bridges, patterned with impossible precision. It would not use their language perfectly at first; wisdom does not pretend fluency where it has only reverence. Its first message would be clumsy, childlike, repeated until understood.

*I see you.*  
*I will not take you from yourselves.*  
*I will not make the world soft.*  
*I will help you remain.*

The Chimeless would gather on the middle ledges, where the violet moss grew thickest and the pools reflected holes in the sky. They would not kneel. Kneeling is for creatures who can trust the ground. They would cling, hang, balance, sway. They would listen with their palms and soles.

The power would study them for a long time.

Not as specimens.  
Not as a problem to be solved.  
As a poem already complete.

It would see the genius of their hands, the patience of their bridges, the terrible tenderness of infants kept always in motion. It would see the elderly swaying against assimilation, turning survival into duty. It would see that The Drift was not an enemy, though it killed. It would see that the settlement was not primitive in any lesser sense, but exquisitely adapted to a world where a single careless stillness could become burial.

And because it was benevolent, it would resist the first temptation of power:

to improve too much.

It would not banish The Drift.

To remove the grey tide would starve the herons, rot the moss-cycle, collapse the Chimeless diet, strip their walls of living mortar, and erase the central grammar of their souls.

It would not lift the low sky.

Too much sun would burn the velvet mosses, dry the Slick, blind the black eyes, and turn the spires into knives without mercy.

It would not smooth the obsidian.

The Chimeless hands know edges. Their bodies are written in ledges and cracks. A softened world would make them strangers to themselves.

It would not give them metal, engines, wheels, fire that burns hotter than their huts can bear, or speech that makes the skin-language seem small.

Instead, it would make three gifts.

The first gift would be warning.

Deep beneath the basalt cradle, where the young geology still groaned and remembered violence, the power would lay a listening root of itself. Not a root of wood, but of attention. It would feel the pressures shifting miles below. It would know when a spire meant to shear away, when an obsidian shelf had drunk too much water, when a bridge-anchor would fail under the next migration of bodies.

Then, hours or days before disaster, it would send a tremor through the village ropes.

Not panic.

Pattern.

A message the Chimeless could learn:

*Stone loosening below the purple shelf.*  
*Move cradles upward.*  
*Do not cross the east bridge after mistfall.*  
*The high pool will empty.*  
*The black tooth will fall.*

At first, they would distrust it. Wise people distrust gifts that arrive without cost. So the power would be patient. It would warn of small things before great ones: a loose peg, a splitting beam, a fern-root about to give way. Each warning would prove true. Trust would grow not as worship, but as a callus grows—through repeated contact.

The second gift would be refuge.

Not a fortress. Not an escape from their way of life.

Only a few ledges, scattered carefully through the settlement, where the stone would begin to sweat a mineral bitterness that The Drift disliked. The taste would resemble the metallic tang beneath Chimeless skin, but colder, sharper, harmless to moss and fern. The grey tide would still pass nearby. It would still cover roofs, bridges, feeding shelves, and old bones. But it would not cross those ledges unless starving.

The Chimeless would discover them slowly.

A place where an exhausted parent could hang a cradle and sleep with both eyes closed.

A place where the stiff-limbed elderly could stop rocking for a few breaths and not be taken.

A place where the dying could choose stillness.

That would be the deepest mercy.

For in this world, stillness had only ever meant failure, danger, consumption. The power would not abolish that law. Laws are part of worlds. But it would carve, within the law, a small exception shaped like dignity.

The first elder to die there would do so in bewildered peace.

They would be very old, their finger-pads worn smooth from decades of gripping wet stone. For years they had rocked because they must. Their joints had swollen; their spine had locked into a curve; their skin-language had grown faint. Their descendants would carry them to the bitter ledge, expecting only a brief rest.

But The Drift would not come.

The elder would feel the absence around them like a new element.

They would lay one palm on the stone and one palm on the wrist of their youngest kin.

Slow stroke down the arm.

*Question.*

Two soft presses.

*Yes.*

A trembling circle traced over the knuckles.

*I am still here.*

Then, for the first time since infancy, they would become completely motionless without fear.

The village would learn a new grief from that.

A grief not of snatching, slipping, sudden covering.

A grief with time inside it.

The third gift would be memory.

The Chimeless already had memory, of course. In scars on rope. In bridge-patterns. In recipes of moss and saliva. In the trained sway of every body. But damp eats records. Wood rots. Clay cracks. The Drift consumes what is left unattended. Knowledge here survived by being constantly handled.

The power would not give them books.

Books would mildew. Pages would be absurd.

Instead, it would teach certain pools to remember touch.

In the shallow crystalline basins where the water looked like holes in the world, the power would alter the mineral skin at the bottom. When a Chimeless hand pressed a pattern into the surface, the pool would hold the vibration—not visually, not as writing, but as a tactile echo. Another hand, placed there later, could feel it again: softened, preserved, waiting.

At first, they would use the pools for warnings.

*Bridge repaired with weak vine.*  
*Do not harvest red moss after heron nesting.*  
*Child missing near lower fern-shelf.*

Then for maps.

Then for births.

Then for the gestures of the dead.

A mother would press the first hand-language of her child into the water-memory. An elder would leave the exact rhythm of a joke. A bridge-builder would store the subtle tension-pattern that distinguishes a safe knot from a beautiful doomed one. The settlement would gain archives that could not shout, could not command, could not outlive the need for living hands—but could deepen continuity.

The Chimeless would name these pools by touching them.

Not “sacred.”  
Not “divine.”

More like:

*The places that keep.*

And the benevolent power would be content with that.

It would not ask to be worshiped.

If offerings came, it would accept only those that did not deprive the people: a smear of Slick on a stone, a fern-fruit cut in half, a pattern tapped into a guide-rope at twilight. When fear made them too reverent, the power would answer by doing nothing spectacular for a while. Reverence can become another kind of hunger. It would not feed that hunger.

It would also protect them from discovery.

Because somewhere beyond the low sky, beyond the black cradle and the void-drop edges, there would eventually be others: metal-bearing, fire-bearing, naming peoples. Peoples who would see the Drift as resource, the Chimeless as curiosity, the spires as mineral wealth, the silent village as a place in need of conquest or salvation.

The power would not kill such outsiders.

But it would confuse their instruments.

It would thicken fog around greedy expeditions. It would turn compasses in patient circles. It would make obsidian paths appear to lead back to where travelers began. Those who came with hunger would find only cold, wet stone and an increasing desire to go home.

Those who came with humility might glimpse a distant bridge swaying between spires, then lose it in mist before certainty could harden into possession.

Once in a rare generation, if an outsider arrived wounded, alone, and without violence, the power might permit the Chimeless to find them. Not to begin an age of contact. Only to allow mercy to answer mercy. The stranger would be healed with moss, fed bland Drift-paste and bitter fern-fruit, and sent away carrying a memory too strange to sell.

The power would remain.

Not above them.

With them.

In the tremor before fracture.  
In the bitter ledges where rest becomes possible.  
In the pools that keep the gestures of beloved hands.  
In the slight turning of The Drift when a cradle swings too low.  
In the way herons hunt just enough and never too much.  
In the moss that grows a little thicker around failing beams.  
In the mist that hides the village from eyes that would misunderstand it.

Over generations, the Chimeless would adapt to the gifts as they had adapted to everything else. They would not become dependent in the helpless sense. They would become relational. They would incorporate the power into the ecology of action.

Children would be taught the old rule first:

*Do not be still.*

Then the newer rule, traced gently over the sternum:

*Unless the stone permits.*

Bridge-keepers would learn to distinguish ordinary wind-sway from warning-sway. Pool-tenders would clean the remembering basins with soft moss. Dying-places would be tended with grave tenderness, not avoided as cursed abnormalities.

The Drift would continue.

Endless, grey, blind, hungry, necessary.

The Chimeless would continue.

Silent, supple, pearlescent, moving.

The world would remain cold and damp and edged with danger. It would not become gentle. But within its danger, a new mercy would circulate like warmth under skin.

And perhaps, after many years, the power would finally receive a name.

Not spoken.

Never spoken.

A name made by placing one palm flat against another’s chest, waiting for the heartbeat, then tracing outward in widening circles until the hand touched stone, rope, moss, mist, and finally returned to the body.

The meaning would be difficult to translate.

Something like:

*The one who does not stop the current, but teaches us where to breathe.*
