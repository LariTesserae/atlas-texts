A benevolent and wise power would not still the wind.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser kindness, seeing the bent bodies and the grey skin and the children learning to stand against the gale before they learn to laugh, might reach out in pity and close its hand around the mountains. It might make calm. It might lay silence over the Screaming Peaks like a blanket.

And in that silence, everything would die.

The birds would fall first, their long wings suddenly wrong for air that did not bear them. The mica-winged insects would tumble helplessly onto stone. The wind-listeners would wake into a world emptied of language, every distance severed, every warning gone. The old one on the promontory would lift her hands and hear nothing, and it would be as if the sun had gone out.

No.

A wise power would understand that suffering and meaning have grown here through the same cracks in the same stone. It would not confuse harshness with evil, nor difficulty with error. It would see that the wind is not merely torment. It is road, tongue, archive, weather, teacher, wall, and cradle. It is the thing that has wounded them and the thing that has made them.

So the power would not stop the wind.

It would listen to it.

For a long while, perhaps for a whole winter, it would do nothing visible at all.

It would enter the gale not as a body, not as a voice booming down from the sky, but as attention: vast, patient, exact. It would feel what the old woman feels on her promontory, but more deeply still. It would trace every pressure-thread across the plateau, every message breaking against a ridge, every failed transmission dying in turbulence before it reached its intended hands.

It would learn the full grammar.

It would learn the old forms that only the white-eyed elder remembers. It would learn the children’s approximations, the clipped modern forms born of too few bodies and too much distance. It would learn the distress-patterns of grazers, the wing-cuts of the silver birds, the hunting-silences of the pale predators. It would learn where the pools thin first in the dry season, where frost lingers longest, where the cushion plants bloom with bitter fruit once every seven years and are forgotten by all but one mind.

It would not speak until it knew how to do so without breaking anything.

Then, one morning, the old woman on the promontory would hear something impossible.

Not a word.

Not thunder.

Not command.

A correction.

A tiny shaping in the wind, so delicate that no one less skilled than she could have noticed it: a turbulence formed and released from no visible body, carrying the old grammatical marker for attention-without-danger.

She would open her cataract-white eyes.

The wind would touch her palms.

Again: attention-without-danger.

Then a second sign.

I am listening.

The old woman would stand for a long time, leaning against the gale, while the sun burned cold above the peaks. Her face would not change much; faces here had never had much use for extravagance. But her fingers would tremble.

Then she would answer.

Not with spoken words. Those were for strangers, for valley-born ears, for miracles too crude to understand the better language.

She would answer in wind.

Who listens?

And the power, being wise, would not give itself a name.

Names require separation.

Instead it would shape the air around her hands into the sign for: one-who-holds-without-taking.

The old woman would understand enough.

Not everything. No mortal mind could understand everything. But enough.

That day, she would not leave the promontory until nightfall.

And by nightfall, the first mercy would already have begun.

---

The power would not make the peaks gentle.

It would make them slightly less impossible.

Not fertile valleys. Not warm rain. Not herds fattened beyond the land’s capacity. Not abundance so sudden that it became catastrophe.

Only margins.

Here is how much wisdom can sometimes accomplish: not salvation as a flood, but survival as a series of small, accurate adjustments.

A pool that had been drying too early each year would begin to collect more frost along its shaded lip. Nothing dramatic. No fountain splitting stone. Only a new lacework of ice, morning after morning, thick enough to melt into a few extra mouthfuls by noon.

A ridge that shredded messages into useless turbulence would shed a flake of stone during the night. The fall would seem natural: freeze, thaw, pressure, time. But afterward the wind would pass more cleanly through that channel, and a child stationed there would suddenly be able to relay farther than any child had in a generation.

In a hollow where cushion plants had retreated to a last green smear, lichen spores would catch in a pattern of moisture just favorable enough. Over months, then years, the grey-orange crust would thicken. Not enough to feast on. Enough to matter.

A grazer calf born too weak would find, by accident, a lee pocket warmer than it should have been.

A pale hunter, starving and bold, would be turned aside one evening by a trick of scent and pressure, led not into death, but toward an old grazer already separated from the herd and near its end. Predator fed. Herd preserved. Balance maintained.

The wind-listeners would notice.

Of course they would notice. They noticed everything.

At first they would distrust it.

The peaks did not give gifts. The peaks allowed bargains. The peaks permitted life in exchange for vigilance, hunger, and bone-deep obedience to consequence.

So when pools lasted longer, when messages carried cleaner, when hunters found enough carrion to stop pressing the herds so fiercely, the wind-listeners would not rejoice. They would test. They would watch for the hidden cost.

A wise power would permit this suspicion.

Trust, like lichen, grows slowly where the wind is hard.

---

The second mercy would be memory.

The old woman’s knowledge would not be stolen from her mind and written across the sky. It would not be poured into every skull whether wanted or not. That would be another kind of violence.

Instead the power would give her time.

Not much. It would not make her young. It would not deny the truth of her body, worn thin by eighty years of cold brilliance. But during one winter when she should have died, she would not die. Her joints would still ache. Her lungs would still burn. Her eyes would still see mostly light and shadow. Yet each morning there would be just enough strength to stand.

And each morning, three children would be sent to her.

Not by decree. By messages carried through the wind.

The first child would be solemn and precise, with palms sensitive enough to feel a pressure-change from a bird’s wingbeat half a mile away.

The second would be impatient, forever leaning too far into the gale, but gifted with memory for sequences.

The third would be small for even these people, often hungry, often overlooked, but able to perceive echoes in the wind after others had dismissed them as noise.

The old woman would refuse them at first.

Too young, she would signal.

The wind would answer, gently: yes.

Too little time.

Yes.

They will not hold it all.

No.

That would anger her. Her hands would carve sharp disturbances into the gale, old cutting forms no one else alive knew well enough to be offended by. The power would receive them without flinching.

No, it would repeat. Not all. Enough.

And because she was old, and because she was wiser than pride, she would begin.

She would teach them the obsolete tenses of wind-speech: the forms for things that had happened before the speaker’s birth, the forms for obligations inherited from the dead, the forms for uncertainty that must not become inaction.

She would teach them the wide-listening stance of the promontory, not merely arms raised, but ribs opened, knees loose, jaw unclenched, hair unbound to receive lateral tremors from distant passes.

She would teach them the plateau beneath the plateau: which crevices connected, which hollows trapped killing cold, which stone-shelves only seemed stable, which pools were safe after red frost and which became bitter.

She would teach them of the other peaks.

This knowledge would hurt them.

Hope often does, when it first enters a starving people.

There are others, the children would learn.

Not stories. Not wind-ghosts. Not the dead.

Others.

Across the serrated horizons. Across passes no one now alive had crossed. Other plateaus where bodies leaned into the same endless gale and hands shaped the same invisible language, though weathered by distance into dialects strange and beautiful.

The children would carry this knowledge back into the network.

The adults would argue without words.

Some would signal impossible.

Some would signal waste.

Some would signal danger-to-all.

Some, quietly, would signal maybe.

The old woman, feeling the argument pass through the wind from her promontory, would smile once. Only once.

That would be enough.

---

The third mercy would be a road.

Not a path carved through the mountains. Not a bridge of gold. Not a tunnel through stone.

A road, in the language of the Screaming Peaks, is not a place where walking is easy.

It is a sequence of survivable decisions.

The power would find one.

It would trace the passes between plateaus, the old animal routes, the knife-edged ridges, the wind-channels that could carry messages farther than bodies could travel. It would learn where the air thinned past endurance and where it pooled cold enough to stop the heart. It would mark, not visibly, but truly, a chain of shelters that could be reached by people trained from birth to move where no valley-born creature could stand.

Then it would begin sending messages from beyond.

Not false ones. Never false ones.

It would amplify what already existed.

On rare mornings, when temperature and pressure aligned, the old woman had heard fragments from the other peaks. The power would strengthen those fragments just enough that the children could hear them too.

A sign from the east: four hands, old dialect, meaning hunger-after-breeding-season.

A reply from the west, distorted but intelligible: many dead, grammar failing, still here.

From the north: water-plenty, bodies-few.

From the south: hunters-many, need-warning-forms.

The wind-listeners of the plateau would freeze in their stations as those messages arrived.

For the first time in generations, the world would become larger than the next pool, the next herd, the next winter.

Not easier.

Larger.

And larger can be terrifying.

The elders would gather in a chamber-crevice, packed shoulder to shoulder in the muffled roar. They would not speak. They would shape wind through the entrance, passing positions through pressure and posture.

To leave is death.

To stay is also death.

Children cannot cross.

Children must cross, or there will be no children later.

Other people may bring sickness.

Isolation has become sickness.

The debate would last three days.

On the fourth, the smallest of the old woman’s three students would go to the chamber entrance and make a sign no child had any right to know.

A very old sign.

The form for: risk undertaken for those not yet born.

The chamber would go still, or as still as any place in the Screaming Peaks could go.

After that, preparations would begin.

---

The power would not carry them.

This matters.

If it lifted them bodily across the passes, they would arrive saved but diminished, made into cargo. If it solved every danger, their knowledge would atrophy. If it fed them without effort, their relationship with the land would break. They had survived too long as participants in the world to be reduced now to recipients of miracle.

So they would walk.

A group of twelve would go first.

Not the strongest only. Strength alone is a valley virtue. The peaks require other excellences.

They would send two skilled listeners, one memory-child, three herd-guides, one bone-worker, one pool-finder, two who knew hunter routes, one nursing mother whose infant represented both burden and argument, and one old adult past prime but rich in judgment.

The white-eyed elder would not go.

Everyone would know this before anyone signaled it.

Her place was the promontory.

Her students would come to her before dawn. She would touch each of their hands, not sentimentally, but to correct them one last time.

You angle too sharply.

You anticipate instead of receive.

You forget the return-current.

You fear being wrong, so you are slow.

To the smallest child, she would give a length of her own hair, cut at last with a bone edge.

This would be shocking.

Hair was warmth, history, body-made wealth. To cut it was to spend life.

The child would take it with both hands.

The old woman would shape the sign: carry.

Not remember.

Carry.

There is a difference.

At sunrise, the twelve would leave.

The power would accompany them as favorable error.

A stone would shift underfoot, but not enough to break an ankle.

Fog would form, but ten breaths late, after the crossing of a narrow shelf rather than during it.

A hunter would scent them, but also scent fresher blood downwind.

One pool would be found beneath a skin of ice where no map remembered water.

Once, during the third night, the infant would stop breathing in the cold.

Then the power would come as warmth.

Not a blaze. Not even a glow.

Only the slight retention of heat inside felted grazer-hide. Only enough for the mother to wake, press the child to her chest, and begin the hard work of bringing breath back.

The child would live.

The mother would know something had helped.

She would not know what.

That was acceptable.

Gratitude is not owed to hidden mercy. Mercy that demands recognition is only vanity in ceremonial dress.

---

On the seventh day, the twelve would reach the neighboring plateau.

There would be people there.

Fewer than hoped.

More than feared.

They would be taller by a little, darker-skinned on the windward side, their hair bound differently, their hand-forms strange at the edges. Their wind-speech would be mutually difficult at first. Misunderstandings would ripple into the gale. Twice, fear would nearly become hostility.

Then the memory-child would untie the length of the old woman’s hair.

It would stream blue-white in the wind.

The people of the other plateau would recognize the meaning, though their form for it had drifted.

Elder-lineage. Knowledge-carried. Peace-risk.

A figure from among them would step forward and answer with a sign from the old grammar.

Welcome-under-hard-sky.

No one would embrace.

That was not their way.

But the twelve would be led to shelter.

They would be given water, lichen mash, a strip of dried meat so precious that the giving of it said more than any valley feast.

That night, for the first time in living memory, two populations of wind-listeners would sleep within reach of one another’s messages.

The power would withdraw a little.

Not entirely.

Enough.

---

News would return to the first plateau before the travelers did.

The network would catch it in fragments, relayed across impossible distances.

Alive.

Others.

Water-plenty.

Need-cord.

Send-grammar.

Have-medicine-for-red-frost.

Can-share-hunter-warning.

Children-listen-well.

The old woman on the promontory would receive the messages before anyone else. She would stand in the gale with her hands raised, hair shortened now, body thin as a last flame.

For a moment the wind passing over the peaks would contain more than survival.

It would contain future.

She would laugh.

The sound would be torn away instantly, shredded into the screaming air. No one would hear it. Perhaps even she would barely hear it.

The power would.

That would be enough.

---

In the years that followed, the benevolent power’s work would become harder to distinguish from ordinary life.

That is the best kind of miracle in a place like this.

The wind-listeners would build no temples. Walls were foolish. Roofs were dangerous. Besides, they had no tradition of kneeling to the sky. Their reverence, if it existed, was in accuracy.

They would incorporate the new presence into grammar slowly.

Not as a god.

Not exactly.

As a category.

There had long been signs for hunter, grazer, child, elder, storm-shift, frost-danger, hunger, death-near, water-found. Now, among the younger speakers, a new compound would appear:

unseen-ally-in-the-current.

It would not be used often.

Only when a message traveled farther than it should have.

Only when a weakened pool held through winter.

Only when two dialects, meeting after generations apart, found an old shared root at the exact moment misunderstanding might have killed.

The power would accept this name because it was not truly a name. It was a relationship.

And relationships, unlike names, can remain humble.

The plateaus would not become safe.

People would still die in falls, in winter, in childbirth, under the pale hunters’ teeth. Children would still learn hunger early. The wind would still scour skin and stone. The old bargains would remain.

But fewer children would die before they learned the full listening stance.

Fewer elders would carry entire histories alone.

Fewer herds would collapse from mismanaged predation.

Fewer plateaus would believe themselves the last.

Across the Screaming Peaks, a larger network would awaken.

Not one people, exactly. Many peoples. Many dialects. Many hard-won ways of leaning into the same wind.

They would exchange routes, warnings, marriage partners, seed-lichen cultures, bone-working techniques, old songs that had become posture-sequences because melody could not survive the gale. They would rediscover forms of play that were also practice but not only practice. Children would invent turbulence-games and be scolded for wasting strength, then secretly watched with something like joy.

The old woman would live long enough to feel the first return travelers come home.

She would not descend from the promontory to meet them.

They would climb to her.

The smallest child, older now, would stand before her and shape a message in clean, confident old grammar:

Carried. Given. Returned. More remains.

The old woman would close her white eyes.

For once, she would lower her arms.

The wind would continue without her listening.

This, too, would be mercy.

She would die that night in her shelter, not on the promontory. Around her would be three students, two elders from neighboring peaks, and a sleeping infant born of parents from different plateaus.

Her last breath would leave her body and enter the roar.

The power would not catch it.

The wind would.

That was where it belonged.

---

What would the benevolent and wise power do?

It would not rescue the wind-listeners from being wind-listeners.

It would rescue them from being alone.

It would not abolish hunger.

It would make knowledge travel faster than famine.

It would not defeat death.

It would prevent memory from dying unnecessarily with the dead.

It would not impose a destiny from above.

It would widen the range of possible futures until choice became meaningful again.

And perhaps, after many generations, when the plateaus had learned one another’s names-that-were-not-names, when the old grammar had flowered into new forms, when the children born into the gale could expect not comfort but continuity, the power would do one more thing.

Far below, in the valleys, people would begin to dream of wind.

They would wake with the sensation of pressure against their palms. They would sketch strange hand-shapes in the margins of books. They would climb higher than they meant to climb and pause when the air grew loud.

Some would turn back.

Some would continue.

A few would reach the lower edges of the Screaming Peaks and find, carved not into stone but arranged in channels of bone and lichen and polished granite, a message made for valley-born eyes:

We are here.

The message would not ask for pity.

It would not ask for conquest, rescue, study, worship, or forgetting.

Only this:

Listen before you speak.

And if the valley-born were wise, they would.
