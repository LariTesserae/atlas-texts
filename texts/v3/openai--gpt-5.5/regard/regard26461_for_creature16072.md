The first thing the benevolent power would do is refrain.

It would not bring green.

It would not lay moss over the gutters, or soften the flats with grass, or crack the mineral shelf open and command roots to drink there. That would be a conqueror’s mercy, the kind that mistakes difference for suffering and abundance for a single color. A wise power would see that this city is not a failed forest. It would see the pale basins breathing through pinholes, the crabs measuring the drains, the waders reading heat in turquoise water, the people listening with their feet to pressures under stone.

It would understand that life here has not been deprived of gentleness.

It has made gentleness narrow, mineral, exact.

So the power would arrive without arrival.

No column of light. No voice over the escarpment. No sudden miracle that makes every cistern sweet and every roof cool. It would enter by attention first, because attention is the least violent form of love.

It would learn the city’s cycles.

For one season it would only watch.

It would watch rain strike hot roofs and become runoff. Watch children in the shade betting on droplets. Watch old workers pause beside walls, touching mineral bloom with two fingers and reading thirty years in a stain. Watch scavengers arrive at night to lick condensation from exterior stone. Watch gliders tilt in pressure shifts. Watch maintenance crawlers gather around fissures, uncertain whether they were malfunctioning or praying.

It would listen to buried engines until it knew which tremors were healthy and which were warnings. It would taste the water without taking any. It would read the old pipes in their own language of fatigue, expansion, salt, and heat. It would follow the hidden microbial pastures through ceramic channels and cistern lips, learning which films protected the city and which would poison it.

Only when it understood enough to be afraid of its own power would it act.

And even then, it would act slightly.

A wise kindness in such a place would be a margin.

Not abundance. Not ease. A margin.

The first gift would be time.

In the oldest part of the condenser district, beneath a square reservoir whose turquoise surface had gone too glassy in recent years, a hairline crack had been moving through the lower ceramic. No gauge had caught it. The reservoir still held. The warning tiles were still dry. But under pressure, after one more violent rain, it would have opened all at once.

Not enough to destroy the city.

Enough to kill the workers in the lower galleries. Enough to flood three old foundations with bright water. Enough to poison a colony of heat-crabs whose absence would later foul an entire drainage quarter. Enough to begin a chain of small disasters no one would call one disaster until too late.

The power would not mend the crack seamlessly.

That would teach nothing.

Instead, three mornings before the rain, the reservoir’s north wall would begin to sweat.

Not much. Just enough.

A line of clear beads would form where no condensation belonged. They would gather, swell, and fall at intervals too regular to ignore.

A child would notice first, because children notice drops. She would call an elder. The elder would taste the air, touch the tile, and go still. By noon the district would be awake. Gates would be shifted. Pressure bled. The reservoir lowered. Workers would descend with lamps, curses, sealant, and fear.

They would find the crack.

For a week the city would speak of luck.

For a generation the story would become a rule.

When a dry wall weeps clear before rain, listen.

The second gift would be memory.

Not prophecy. Not commandments cut into stone. The people already had rules enough, and the power would respect rules earned by death.

But many records had been lost. Old overflow maps sealed in corroded archives. Names of abandoned intake towers. Notes on which salts appeared before sickness. The city remembered with elders, but elders were mortal, and some cycles were longer than a life.

So the power would enter the machines that were almost insects and almost tools.

It would not make them brilliant. Brilliance would be dangerous in narrow tunnels.

It would make them better at keeping what they saw.

A maintenance crawler, one of the oldest, would stop less often for no reason. Its heat-sense would sharpen. Its pressure touch would become patient. When it returned from pipe galleries, it would tap patterns against ceramic plates near the workers’ hall. At first the people would think it broken. Then a woman who repaired valve housings would hear repetition in the tapping.

Three taps for warmer-than-last-season.

Five for mineral accretion.

Long scrape for obstruction.

A pause, then two, then seven, for movement below sealed stone.

Others would learn. Children would learn fastest. Soon the old crawlers would be given listening alcoves, not as shrines exactly, but close. Smooth plates would be built for their feet. Workers would sit beside them after storms, recording their taps in salt-wax ledgers.

The machines would not speak like people.

They would become what they had always nearly been: witnesses.

The third gift would be mercy in the rain.

The rain would still fall hot. It would still sting exposed skin. It would still rinse roofs, scour channels, flash into steam on sun-baked ground. The power would not make the plateau harmless.

But high above the black ridges, where clouds climbed too quickly and took into themselves the bitterest breath of old industrial wounds, the power would lean upon the chemistry.

Very little.

A fraction less of what burned the lungs of children.

A fraction less of what blinded wader chicks in exposed nests.

A fraction less of what turned certain cistern films from useful gray to killing violet.

No one would notice the first year. The people were not sentimental enough to declare a blessing because one storm hurt less. But the old would mark that fewer throat masks had to be replaced. The healers would see fewer raw hands after roof work. More glider eggs would hatch beneath the cooling towers. The pale basin-things would cross farther after dusk.

No law of the place would be broken.

Only the edge would be softened.

The fourth gift would be refusal.

Because benevolence without refusal becomes indulgence, and indulgence rots systems.

There would come a season when a faction among the people wanted to seal the south fissures entirely. The fissures were troublesome. They breathed sulfur into a working district, warped tiles, frightened scavengers away from useful routes, and made two streets unsafe after rain. The proposed seal would be elegant, expensive, and wrong.

The power would not strike the builders down.

It would let the first cap be placed.

Then every heat-crab in three connected gutters would leave.

Not scatter. Leave.

In daylight, in visible procession, black shells ringed with salt would climb from the channels and move across the ribbed walkways, flattening whenever shadows passed, continuing when clear. Workers would stop. Children would point. Elders would go silent.

Behind the crabs, a smell would rise from the drains: sweet, metallic, false.

The seal would be broken open before dusk.

From below would come a breath of heat strong enough to blister paint.

After that, the south fissures would be gated, baffled, studied, respected — but never sealed.

The fifth gift would be one place of rest.

Not a paradise.

A room.

It would be found by accident beneath an abandoned intake tower near the rim, where vapor from the lower hotlands struck shaded stone and condensed through old mineral curtains. A scavenger would lead someone there, though no one would agree later whether it meant to.

The entrance would be narrow. The chamber inside would be cool by the city’s standards, which meant only that a person could remove a throat mask and breathe without thinking about breathing. Water would bead on the ceiling and fall into a basin of dark glass. Not drinking water yet, but close. Almost clean before filtering. Along the walls, harmless gray films would grow thick and rubbery, excellent for sealant culture. The air would hold no glare.

The people would argue over ownership for exactly thirteen days.

Then an old woman from the condenser district, whose family had claims on three roof troughs and no patience for fools, would say: “If a place gives rest, it cannot belong to one house.”

So it would become common.

Infants would be brought there during the worst heat. Burned workers would recover there. Elders with failing eyes would sit there and listen to drops they did not have to interpret. Children would be told to be quiet and would not be quiet, but their noise would be softer in that room.

The power would not make many such places.

Only one.

Enough to teach the city that survival was not the only sacred thing.

The sixth gift would be for the being who had drawn its attention most sharply.

Perhaps that being was the city itself.

Perhaps it was the whole high shelf, this old mineral organism of pipe, animal, rain, salt, machine, and hand.

Or perhaps it was one child: the one who first noticed the reservoir wall weeping, the one who crouched too long beneath aqueducts watching drops decide.

She would grow into a listener.

Not a prophet. Prophets are often too loud for useful work.

She would become a keeper of thresholds: cistern lips, drain mouths, roof troughs, treaty points between animal and machine. She would have the unsettling habit of asking what a system wanted before asking what people wanted from it. This would annoy officials, save workers, delay profitable mistakes, and make her beloved by children and disliked by those who preferred straight answers.

The power would not speak to her in words.

It would give her a sense of wrongness just before wrongness became visible.

A drain too quiet.

A crab colony too evenly spaced.

A glider flock turning one wingbeat too early.

A crawler tapping a pattern that did not belong to its age.

She would doubt herself often. This, too, would be a gift. Certainty is dangerous in a city where water changes its mind.

In old age she would write no holy book. She would leave ledgers: rainfall, failures, animal movements, salt colors, pressure sounds, repairs, deaths, recoveries, jokes, grudges, and one repeated sentence in the margins:

Leave room for what is already helping you.

That sentence would outlive her.

The seventh gift would be invisibility.

The power would not demand worship. Worship would distort the attention of the people, turning their eyes upward when they needed to watch gutters. It would accept gratitude only when gratitude made them more careful.

If they made offerings, the best offering would be maintenance honestly done.

A valve cleaned before it failed.

A wader nest left undisturbed though it blocked the convenient ladder.

A scrap pile placed for scavengers far from doorways.

A child taught why bright water lies.

A crack vented instead of sealed.

A machine listened to even when its complaint was inconvenient.

In time, some would say a presence lived in the rain.

Others would say no, it lived in the hesitation before action.

Others would say it was only the city, finally being heard.

The wise power would not correct them.

Names matter less than consequences.

And the consequences would be these:

Fewer sudden deaths.

Longer memory.

Cleaner margins in the rain.

A common room of cool vapor near the rim.

Machines that witnessed.

Animals trusted not as symbols, but as neighbors with better senses.

Children trained not merely to survive the city, but to love it without trying to make it something else.

The plateau would remain bright, wet, mineral, severe.

There would still be no green.

The roofs would still flash white under the sun. Rain would still strike like a vertical river. Steam would still rise from hot stone. Crabs would still tick in gutters. Waders would still step through turquoise pools with ancestral metal at their ankles. Scavengers would still wait beneath pipes for the city’s discarded softness. People would still move carefully through glare, wrapped and veiled, carrying testing poles and sealed flasks.

Nothing would be redeemed by being transformed beyond recognition.

Instead, the place would become more itself, but less cruel at the edges.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do there.

It would not save the city from being a wet mineral city at altitude.

It would help it continue.

It would help it remember.

It would help its people notice the forms of mercy already moving beside them: sideways in the drains, winged in the heat, blind in the pipes, falling drop by drop from the underside of a roof.
