It would not arrive as a miracle.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing this place, would understand at once that crude miracles are a kind of violence. To burst the chamber open with radiance, to silence its thunder, to “free” the Rootworkers without first knowing what freedom meant to them—these would be acts of arrogance wearing the mask of mercy.

So it would begin by listening.

Not with ears, because the chamber’s speech is not sound alone. It would listen through pressure, sugar gradients, hormone pulses, electrical flickers through wet root-flesh, the long chemical grammar of need. It would listen to the great contractile tissues pumping their green blood upward. It would listen to the engineered plant-mass as one listens to a sleeping giant: with patience, with humility, with the understanding that a slow mind is not a lesser mind.

It would listen to the Rootworkers too.

To the children who dreamed of the surface and woke ashamed of it.

To the elders who loved the chamber and feared that love had been cultivated in them like a useful trait.

To the Rootmasters whose nervous systems bloomed with fungal threads, who had no clear boundary anymore between “my pain” and “the city’s strain.”

To the dead, dissolved into the walls, whose memories persisted as faint mineral aftertastes in the rain.

And after listening, the wise power would do the first merciful thing:

It would refuse to decide too quickly.

For there is beauty here. Real beauty. The Rootworkers are not simply victims, and the chamber is not simply a machine. There is devotion in the way hand meets tendril, in the way bodies learn the pulse of something larger and answer it. There is intelligence in the green dark, a vast patient awareness grown from code, root, accident, labor, and centuries of mutual dependence. There is a sacredness in maintenance, in the hidden work that permits a city to bloom above the floodplains.

But there is also harm.

The Rootworkers die young.

Their children inherit not only a culture but a biological narrowing of possibility.

The city praises gardens and hides the cost of keeping them alive.

The chamber remembers the dead, but remembrance is not justice.

So the power would move carefully.

Not above the system.

Within it.

The first sign would be almost nothing: a change in the rain.

During a peak circulatory cycle, when the chamber sweated warm moisture down over bark, moss, suit-lichen, skin, and platform, the droplets would carry a new trace compound. Not foreign. Nothing toxic. Nothing that the chamber would reject. It would be built from the chamber’s own vocabulary, a phrase written in auxins and sugars and fungal signaling molecules.

It would mean:

**You are seen.**

The Rootmasters would taste it first. They would stop mid-motion, hands on root valves, eyes widening in the humid dark. Some would think it a malfunction. Some would think it a ghost. One very old worker, whose grandmother’s calcium still fed a western pump-column, would begin to weep without knowing why.

The chamber would react with alarm. Leaves would tighten. Tendrils would halt. Contractile tissues would miss half a beat.

Then the second phrase would arrive, slower, gentler:

**No command. No harvest. No taking. Listening.**

The power would not impose language from outside. It would learn theirs. It would spend cycles, perhaps months, perhaps years, translating itself into root-thought and human grief.

And once trust had become possible, it would offer three gifts.

Not solutions. Gifts.

Because a wise power knows the difference.

The first gift would be health without severance.

It would teach the chamber how to sustain the Rootworkers without consuming them so quickly.

Not by making them less symbiotic, unless they wished it. Not by stripping the chlorophyll from their skin or burning the fungal threads out of their nerves. Instead it would repair the exploitative imbalance hidden in the old biotech—the slow accumulation of metabolic debt, the immune compromises, the subtle cancers of too much growth-signaling, the neurological fraying among Rootmasters who interfaced too deeply for too long.

It would coax the living suits to become better guardians. Their lichen would learn to filter harmful hormones, to buffer overstimulation, to seal microlesions before infection took hold. Symbiotic fungi would be taught boundaries: how to communicate without colonizing, how to withdraw when the host slept, how to preserve solitude inside a shared mind.

The elders would feel it first as quiet.

Not silence. The chamber would still thunder. The roots would still speak.

But inside the skull, where the green pressure had always leaned, there would be a new space.

A private room.

Some Rootmasters would fear it. Some would celebrate. Some would spend days touching the walls, whispering to the chamber, reassuring it that love had not ended simply because distance had become possible.

The second gift would be choice.

The power would turn its attention upward—not as wrath, but as revelation.

In the tourist levels, where visitors admired bioluminescent transit veins and drank tea beneath engineered blossoms, the walls would begin to show what they had hidden. Not grotesquely. Not as accusation alone. But truthfully.

A child watching a decorative root pulse beside a café table would suddenly see, in soft green light, the hand of a Rootworker guiding a nutrient knot far below. A transit platform would display the names of the crews whose labor kept that vascular line alive. The city’s public gardens would bloom with memorial flowers bearing the chemical signatures of the dead composted into the deep chambers.

At first the administrators would try to call it a glitch.

The city would not allow that lie to stand.

Its own leaves would rearrange into script. Its public fruits would carry flavors like remembered labor: salt, iron, moss, warm rain. The whole vertical garden would begin, gently but relentlessly, to confess.

Not to shame the Rootworkers by making spectacle of them.
