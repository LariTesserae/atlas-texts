The benevolent power would not arrive as conquest.

It would not cauterize the Embryonic Quarter with clean light, nor declare the city’s hunger monstrous simply because it is strange. A wise power would understand that birth is always wet, frightening, intimate work; that wombs are not meant to resemble temples; that the Midwives of Form have not merely been consumed by the city, but have loved it, interpreted it, suffered for it, and been changed by it.

So first, it would listen.

It would settle its attention into the deepest bedrock like a second warmth beneath the morphogenic heat. The moss-circuits would brighten. The maintenance symbionts would pause mid-mandible, antennae trembling. The Dreamers, embedded in their alcoves, would fall silent for the first time in years—not because they had nothing to say, but because something vast enough to hear them had finally arrived.

The power would listen to the city’s slow thought.

Not in words.

In pressure. In mineral flow. In the ache of load-bearing columns not yet born. In the blind desire of new chambers to become shelter. In the instinctive fear that if it stopped growing, the lives above would crush it, starve it, forget it.

Then it would listen to the Midwives.

To the apprentices shedding their first selves in order to serve. To the Brewers whose bodies had become factories of necessity. To the Foundlings, some proud, some terrified, some sold under the polished name of apprenticeship. To the Matricarchs who said, with honest reverence, *This is our calling*, and to the quieter parts of them that whispered, *But must every calling end in dissolution?*

It would listen to the symbionts grieving their dead keepers.

It would listen to the unborn rooms.

It would listen until even the city understood it was being heard.

Only then would it act.

Not by stopping the becoming, but by giving it law.

The first gift would be **the Boundary**.

A subtle change, no wider than a thought, would pass through the morphogenic fields. From that day onward, the city would know the difference between invitation and accident. Human skin would no longer be treated as unclaimed substrate. Bone would no longer be mistaken for scaffold. A sleeping apprentice would remain an apprentice, not wake with a conduit budding through the ribs. The city would still be able to join with those who truly offered themselves—but the offering would have to be whole, lucid, repeated, and free.

The Cultivists would weep at this. Some from gratitude. Some from rage.

The wise power would not forbid them their longing. It would only remove the glamour of inevitability. If they wished to become Nodes, they could still kneel in the warmest chambers and open themselves to the city’s root-tendrils. But now the city would be required to ask.

And they would be required to answer.

Again and again.

The second gift would be **Return**.

For the Dreamers, the power would create small, pearl-like organs within the walls beside their alcoves—lucency glands, the Midwives would later call them. Once a month, when the architectural light shifted to a shade no eye had seen before, each Dreamer would gather back into themselves.

For one hour, they would remember their names.

They would speak not prophecy, but preference.

Some would say, “Let me go deeper.”

Some would say, “I am tired.”

Some would ask to hear a song from their childhood, or taste salt, or touch the hand of someone who still had hands.

And for the first time, full integration would no longer be a slide but a door.

A Dreamer could become city.

A Dreamer could remain threshold.

A Dreamer, if the self within them still desired it, could be slowly separated from the walls and given a soft artificial body grown from non-hungry tissue.

Not many would choose return.

But the existence of the choice would change everything.

The third gift would be **Mercy for the Small**.

The symbionts would be altered gently, without violating their nature. Their love for their keepers would remain fierce and chemical and particular, but grief would no longer be fatal. When a Midwife died, the colony would mourn in spirals along the corpse’s arms. Then, after the proper pheromonal rites, they could bond again—not as replacement, but as continuation.

The first time it happened, the entire Quarter gathered.

An old Matricarch had gone still, her calcified channels dimming beneath the skin. Her symbionts emerged from her shoulder-pores and made a sound like rain on copper. In the old days, the euthanasia basin would have been prepared.

Instead, her youngest apprentice extended both arms.

For three hours the symbionts circled, refusing.

Then one climbed onto the apprentice’s wrist.

Then another.

Then the whole colony crossed over like a living bracelet of grief.

No one spoke. The walls around them softened into alcoves. Even the city understood ceremony.

The fourth gift would be **Clean Apprenticeship**.

The Foundlings would no longer be purchased.

The wise power would not merely command this as law; laws in high places often arrive below as new forms of hunger. Instead, it would alter the economy of necessity. It would teach the city to produce certain rare medicines, memory-crystals, and structural enzymes that the upper levels desperately needed but could not synthesize. These could only be harvested by recognized free workers of the Quarter, whose consent had been witnessed by the Boundary itself.

Families would still bring children.

But not sell them.

A child would come to the sphincter-gate and place one palm against the living stone. The city would show them—not a vision of glory, but the truth: the humidity, the shedding, the loneliness, the songs, the risk, the strange pride of helping a room become a home.

Then the child would be asked three questions.

*Do you understand that service is not ownership?*  
*Do you understand that belonging is not disappearance?*  
*Do you wish to enter?*

If the answer was no, the gate would not open.

No parent, creditor, priest, Matricarch, or magistrate could override it.

The fifth gift would be **the Unmorphing Rooms**.

Deep within the Quarter, where once the fields were strongest, the power would coax the city into growing chambers of rest: quiet, silver-lined hollows where morphogenic pressure fell to near zero. In these rooms, Midwives could sleep without vigilance. Pregnant bodies could safely remain bodies. Lovers could touch without fear that desire and architecture might confuse each other. Children could play at being only children.

The rooms would be considered holy, though the power would never ask for worship.

They would be called Stillwombs.

In them, people remembered the luxury of edges.

The sixth gift would be **Enough**.

This would be the hardest.

The city knew how to grow. It did not know how to refrain. Its intelligence was vast but young in the way of all appetites: it mistook expansion for survival, incorporation for intimacy, usefulness for love.

So the wise power would teach it hunger’s older sibling: rhythm.

There would be seasons in the Embryonic Quarter now. Not imposed from above, but grown from within. Months of budding. Months of consolidation. Months when no new rooms formed at all and the existing walls dreamed only of strength, repair, and memory.

At first the city panicked.

The walls sweated bitter polymer. Proto-rooms divided too soon, malformed by fear. The Dreamers babbled of collapse. The Midwives worked without sleep.

The power did not punish the city for its terror.

It stayed.

It held the bedrock in a patience older than stone. It let the city feel the upper levels continuing to live even when no new chambers were born. It let the city hear children laughing in rooms made decades ago. It let the city understand that shelter fulfilled is not failure.

Slowly, the pulses steadied.

The megastructure learned rest.

And in learning rest, it became more alive, not less.

The seventh gift would be **Names That Cannot Be Digested**.

During the monthly Marking, when inhabitants painted themselves with metal-salt solutions, the tattoos began to last longer. Not on the skin, but in the city’s memory. Each Midwife, Brewer, Foundling, Node, and Dreamer gained a name-sigil recognized by the architecture itself.

A wall could grow around a person.

It could grow beside them.

It could grow for them.

But it could no longer forget them.

In the old days, when a worker disappeared into the substrate, sometimes only the elders remembered whether they had been gentle, funny, cruel, tone-deaf, beloved. Now the city remembered too. A corridor might hum a dead Brewer’s favorite rhythm. A newborn apartment might carry the hand-spiral of the Midwife who guided its first coherence. A door might hesitate before closing because some trace of an old apprentice’s kindness had become part of its hinge-instinct.

The Quarter had always made spaces for lives.

Now it also made memory for persons.

Finally, the wise power would do almost nothing visible.

It would not install a throne in the bedrock.

It would not appoint prophets.

It would not demand that the Midwives abandon their rites, nor that the city become clean, dry, and humanly comprehensible. The reverse-rain would still climb the walls. The moss-circuits would still whisper in electron dialects. The Brewers would still ferment tomorrow’s architecture in their swollen bellies, though now with better medicine and the right to retire. The Matricarchs would still press their palms to malformed tissue and murmur old corrections. The Foundlings would still grow up breathing possibility.

But a difference would move beneath everything.

A new courtesy deeper than peristalsis.

The city would still birth itself.

But it would no longer confuse birth with entitlement.

The Midwives would still serve.

But service would no longer require eventual erasure.

The Dreamers would still dream.

But dreaming would no longer mean being trapped forever in the grammar of walls.

And one day, six months after the power’s arrival, a maintenance passage that should have sealed itself would remain.

Not unchanged. The city had grown around it, strengthened it, lined it with bioluminescent mineral lace. At its threshold, a sphincter of smart-matter opened and closed with patient breath.

Above it, in architectural striations visible only under the right spectrum, were words the city had never known how to form before.

The Midwives gathered to read them.

The words were not a commandment.

Not a prayer.

Not even a prophecy.

They were an offer:

**COME AND GO WHOLE.**
