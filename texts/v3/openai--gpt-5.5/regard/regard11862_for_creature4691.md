The benevolent power would not descend as a conqueror of sorrow.

It would not split the sky with trumpets, nor flood the Shivered Crown with warmth until its cold became meaningless. It would not drag the Vestiges back into bodies, nor seal every impossible pool, nor declare the plateau healed because its strangeness had been made ordinary.

A wise power would understand that not all broken things wish to become what they were before.

So first, it would listen.

It would come without weight, without shadow, without a name that demanded worship. Perhaps it would arrive as a change in the mist: a softness in the condensation beading on black stone, a gentler note inside the low hum beneath the plateau. Perhaps the groves would notice before the Vestiges did. The silver-green shrubs would turn their waxy leaves toward no visible sun. Moss would brighten in the cracks. The little rivulets would pause, as if remembering the shape of rain.

The power would stretch its attention across the Shivered Crown and feel the whole of it: the high, ancient stone; the abandoned city; the pools reflecting impossible skies; the eroded bones of a world too old to grieve loudly. It would feel the frayed laws, the wounds in sequence and causality, the places where time had become tangled like thread around a hand. It would feel the Vestiges—each one a half-presence, a sorrow thinned by eternity, a question that had forgotten the mouth that first asked it.

And it would not pity them in a way that diminished them.

It would honor them.

For the Vestiges had sought something immense. Their fate was terrible, yes, but not trivial. They had reached toward the unknown with courage or hunger or devotion, and the Crown had taken them into itself. A lesser power might see only ghosts to be dispersed. A wiser one would see witnesses.

So it would begin with the smallest mercy.

It would place anchors in the world.

Not walls. Not prisons. Anchors.

A few stones in the ancient city would become true again.

Not bright, not polished, not restored to youth—but reliable. A doorway would lead to the same chamber each time it was crossed. A stair would no longer remember three different centuries at once. A pool near the central cistern would reflect the actual sky, gray and cold and clouded, instead of crimson void or alien stars. Around that pool the air would become safe to breathe with the mind as well as the lungs.

This place would become a sanctuary of continuity.

A seeker who stood there would remember their name.

A Vestige who drifted near it would remember that they had once had one.

The benevolent power would not force the memory upon them. It would simply make remembering possible.

Then it would move through the glyph-carved walls and read what remained.

Where carvings had been worn by frost, it would not remake them falsely. Instead, it would draw out the intention beneath the chisel marks: the old prayers, diagrams, warnings, maps of transformation. These would not blaze. They would become visible only at dawn, when the pale light struck the stone at a certain angle. For a few minutes each day, the city would speak honestly.

Here we came seeking the seam.

Here we built the cisterns.

Here we learned the pools were not doors, but mouths.

Here we heard ourselves answered by what had never been born.

Here we changed.

Here we were afraid.

Here we chose wrongly.

Here we chose bravely.

Here we could not tell the difference.

The Vestiges would gather near these inscriptions without knowing why. Long shadows against darker stone. Thin silhouettes in mist. A pressure in the chest. A chorus of almost-voices.

The benevolent power would let them mourn.

That would be one of its greatest gifts: not release, not redemption, not yet, but mourning.

For beings trapped outside ordinary time may suffer not only because they are lost, but because they cannot complete grief. Their sorrow has no before and after. It is an endless weather.

The power would give them sequence.

A first memory.

A second.

A last.

Not all at once. Not enough to shatter them. Just enough for one Vestige, then another, to understand:

I was.

I became.

I remain.

And perhaps:

I may yet choose.

After this, the power would tend to the Crown itself.

Reality would not be fully mended. The Shivered Crown is a threshold, and thresholds have their own holiness. To seal every fracture would be to murder the place as surely as letting it unravel. So the power would weave, not close.

At the edges of the impossible pools, it would lay a law like a ring of clear ice:

Those who look may look.  
Those who listen may listen.  
But none shall be taken unwillingly.  
No memory shall be eaten without consent.  
No self shall be thinned except by chosen passage.

The pools would still reflect starfields, voids, forgotten skies. They would still whisper in languages that never existed. But now their hunger would be bounded. Their revelations would become invitations rather than traps.

The horizon would still shimmer.

The shadows would still sometimes move against the light.

Time would still breathe strangely on the plateau.

But there would be paths through the strangeness.

A traveler crossing the Crown might lose an hour, but not a childhood. A pilgrim might hear the Vestiges whisper, but not be dissolved into their longing. Those who came seeking oblivion would find instead a cold stone bench beside a cistern, a basin of clear water, and the sudden unbearable memory that they are alive.

The power would also touch the vegetation.

Not to make the plateau lush beyond its nature. Not to summon forests where only low shrubs belong. But it would deepen the hidden springs by a handspan. It would teach the mosses to hold warmth through the longest frost. It would persuade the silver-green leaves to flower once every seven years—not with bright petals, but with tiny pale blooms like frost-stars.

And because life here has grown too long without witness, the power might bring one small creature.

Only one kind, at first.

Not birds. Not beasts. Nothing that would overrun the fragile groves.

Perhaps a wingless pollinator, no larger than a grain of gravel, with a body like translucent amber and a slow, dreaming metabolism suited to the cold. It would live in the moss, feed on the seven-year flowers, and make a sound almost too soft to hear: a faint clicking like thawing ice.

The first time the sound spread through a grove, the silence of the Shivered Crown would change.

Not break.

Change.

The Vestiges would notice.

They would drift among the shrubs, elongated and uncertain, listening to this tiny evidence that the world had not entirely finished inventing itself.

Some would recoil. Some would ignore it. Some would follow the sound for years.

One might kneel—if such a shape could be called kneeling—beside a patch of moss and extend a hand made of shadow, memory, and cold light.

The creature would pass through the hand.

Still, the gesture would matter.

Then the power would make an offer.

Not aloud. Not in command. It would be placed into the hum beneath the plateau, woven so gently into the old vibration that each Vestige would hear it as if the thought had arisen from within:

You are not required to remain what happened to you.

There are three roads.

Remain, and become guardians of the threshold with memory restored.

Return, if return is still possible, to some form of bounded life.

Pass onward, beyond the seam you sought, but whole enough to choose it.

No road will be forced.

No refusal will be punished.

No choice need be made quickly.

For some Vestiges, this would mean nothing for centuries.

For others, it would be everything at once.

A few would choose to remain. These would become clearer. Not solid, not mortal, but coherent. Their elongated forms would gather definition like ink dropped into water. They would walk the ancient city at dawn and dusk, keeping watch near the pools. When seekers came, these guardian Vestiges would stand between them and the worst of the Crown’s unraveling.

They would not speak in language, but in impressions:

Do not drink from that basin.

Tie your name to a stone before you sleep.

Leave before the third moonless twilight.

You are grieving; this place will pretend to be an answer.

It is not yet your door.

Others would choose return.

For them, the benevolent power would gather what fragments remained: a childhood glimpse of lower valleys, the feel of hands stacking stone, the taste of mineral water, the pride of carving a spiral into a wall, the terror of seeing the sky open in the wrong direction. From these pieces it would fashion not their old bodies, perhaps—that might be impossible—but vessels suited to the Crown.

They might awaken as slow, luminous figures of condensed mist and mineral, able to touch stone, able to tend cisterns, able to speak in low tones like wind in a cave. Not alive as they once were, but alive enough to begin again.

They would repair the canals.

They would clear frost from glyphs.

They would cultivate the groves.

They would build small cairns for those who passed onward.

And some would pass onward.

This would be the quietest miracle.

At the rim of the plateau, where the horizon warps most severely and storms flicker like memories of creation, the power would open—not a gate, exactly, but a permission. A place where the frayed edge no longer snared, where the beyond could be approached without being devoured.

The first Vestige to choose it might barely be visible: a long shadow trembling against stone.

It would pause.

The whole Crown would seem to hold its breath.

Then the Vestige would remember its name.

Not a name pronounceable by human mouths. A name made of kinship, cold, aspiration, and the exact sound of a chisel striking wet stone under a young sky.

Remembering, it would become briefly radiant.

Then it would go.

Not vanish. Not die.

Go.

And the low hum beneath the plateau would shift by one note, lighter.

The benevolent power would not stay enthroned above the Shivered Crown. It would not demand temples. It would not erase the old city’s history and replace it with gratitude.

Before withdrawing, it would leave four gifts.

First: a hearth that gives no flame, hidden in the central dome of the city. Those who sit around it feel warmth only in the places where despair has made them numb.

Second: a clear pool that reflects not impossible skies, but the viewer as they truly are—not merely face and form, but wound, strength, longing, and choice. No one is forced to look.

Third: a path of pale stones leading from the safest cistern to the plateau’s edge. The path changes as needed, but never maliciously. It guides the lost away from dissolution.

Fourth: a new glyph, carved nowhere and everywhere, appearing only when frost forms on dark stone.

Its meaning is simple:

The threshold remains.  
The wound need not rule.  
What was lost is honored.  
What remains may choose.

After that, the Shivered Crown would still be cold.

It would still be vast.

It would still be lonely in the way high places are lonely, and strange in the way broken cosmic things are strange. The impossible pools would still show forbidden heavens. The city would still be half-empty. The wind would still move over polished black stone with a voice like old grief.

But the place would no longer feel abandoned by mercy.

The Vestiges would no longer be only remnants.

The Crown would remain a relic of a shattered cosmos, but now it would also be something else:

not healed completely,  
not tamed,  
not explained—

but accompanied.

And in a realm where the greatest horror was to linger forever unheard, that accompaniment would be salvation enough.
