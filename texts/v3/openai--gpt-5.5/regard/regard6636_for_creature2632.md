The power does not descend.

It has learned, through ages of loving things unlike itself, that descent is often the first form of harm.

It remains above and around and beneath, not as a light breaking the clouds, not as a voice, not as a hand. A hand would be too simple. A hand would choose one creature over another, one shape over all the shapes about to become possible. A hand would build, and building is not the language of the plateau.

So the power waits.

It attends.

For a long while — longer than the lifetime of a soft-crowned beast, longer than the rise and clogging of several natural factories beneath the moss, longer than it takes a pod-plant to grow, rupture, poison a hollow, and be replaced by silver grasses — it does nothing that the plateau could name as action.

It listens to the water before it becomes sound.

It learns the pumping thud under the mats, the cold boil of seep-holes, the industrial breathing of root and stone. It learns the moods of the reedbeds: how they ring differently before rain, before hunger, before the arrival of the narrow-winged flocks. It studies the echo-mollusks and does not take back the sounds they have eaten. It watches lapse-worms mend the torn places in sequence and understands, with something like reverence, that even disorder here has scavengers.

The power does not pity the plateau for being cold.

It does not pity it for being wet, unstable, ancient, unfinished on the surface though finished in its bones. Pity, too, can become a kind of violence when it mistakes difference for suffering.

But the power does perceive suffering.

Not the suffering of predation, which belongs to the food of the world.

Not the suffering of storms, which belong to the world’s breathing.

Not the suffering of death, which here is quickly put to work by moss, acid, larva, cilium, fungus, memory.

The suffering is subtler.

It lies in the places where the laws have grown thin enough to fray.

There are pools that have begun to open too often onto impossible depths, so that animals vanish not into water but into elsewhere, and do not return as nourishment. There are reflections that have learned to lure the young. There are afterimage herds that persist beyond their brief permission and confuse the living herds until they walk in circles, starving on abundant mats. There are storms that repeat themselves in the same hollow, not as weather but as wound, revising one patch of ground again and again until nothing there can complete a life.

The plateau’s uncertainty is beautiful.

But some uncertainties have become traps.

The benevolent power understands the difference.

It does not make the land stable. That would be to erase its nature.

It does not halt the ground-storms, still the reed-drums, straighten distances, silence the echo-mollusks, or teach water to remain only water. It does not sharpen the mountains. It does not raise forests. It does not warm the plateau into gentleness. It does not bring roads, shelters, walls, or any clean bright geometry from elsewhere.

Instead, it performs the smallest mercy large enough to matter.

It gives the plateau a deeper rhythm.

Not a command.

Not a law imposed from outside.

A rhythm.

Far below the mats, below the root-channels and mineral throats, below the pressure chambers where water hammers in darkness, in the old stone that has almost forgotten height, the power places no object and carves no sign. It merely listens so completely that the listening becomes a kind of companionship. The ancient rock, pressed silent for eras, answers by remembering one more thing about endurance.

The next ground-pulse comes as before.

Deep.

Mechanical.

Enormous.

The moss shivers for miles.

But after it, another pulse follows: softer, wider, slower. Not regular enough to be machinery. Not irregular enough to be fear. It moves through water and root-felt, through pebbleback armor, through the sleeping knots of pale amphibians, through the fungal antlers of old grazers, through the pressure pits of predators waiting beside obsolete waterlines.

Everything feels it.

Nothing understands it.

Understanding is not required.

The new rhythm does not prevent change. It gives change a returning place.

Temporary ridges still rise from mist, but they no longer remain long enough to mislead migrating herds into calving on air. Lakes still open black and star-machined, but at their rims the mats grow a faint silver fringe, a warning taste on the feet of animals. Echoes still come back wrong, but when an echo returns before its sound, the reedbeds tremble in a lower register, and creatures learn to pause.

The lapse-worms flourish.

No one notices at first, because lapse-worms are mostly noticed by the seams they leave and the small reliabilities that follow them. But their castings thicken. Moss grows over them in layered, lucid patterns. The recursive lichens spread across exposed stones and old antlers, mapping not only where storms have been but where storms have begun to hurt. Their tiny plateau-shapes darken at the edges of dangerous pools. Grazers avoid those places. Predators, reading the grazers, avoid them too. The warning enters the food web without becoming a doctrine.

The echo-mollusks change their songs.

In mating season the pools still murmur with old sounds: wingbeats, cries, stonefalls, the long wet roar of mats tearing and resealing. But now, among the stored noises, there is a low tone no creature made. The mollusks digest part of it and release the rest in fragments. Young soft-crowned beasts, hidden white and trembling in warm moss hollows, hear it through their folded legs. It teaches them nothing so direct as safety. It only becomes familiar. Later, when they stand before a pool whose reflection shows daylight in a dim world, the same tone shivers through their fungal crowns, and they turn away.

The linked herds receive the mercy as warmth.

A small warmth, almost nothing. Not enough to thaw frost. Not enough to change the range of any plant. It passes along the herd-vines as an additional impulse, distinguishable from fear, hunger, mating, pain, and the dull collective comfort of many bodies joined. When one animal grows sick and the herd begins to feed it, the warmth gathers there. If the sickness belongs to recovery, the warmth stays. If the sickness belongs to ending, the warmth loosens the vines early.

This is a kindness.

Before, the dying were sometimes held too long.

The herd does not learn grief, exactly. It already had something like grief: a drag in the network, a chemical dimming, a slower turn away from the place where one of its bodies had fallen. But now its grief has a door. The vines release. The dead one sinks into moss. Pebblebacks arrive. Glass insects take the eyes when steam opens nearby. Gelatinous mounds may flow toward the bones if the seep is warm enough. The herd moves on, not unharmed, but untrapped.

The predators receive almost nothing.

This, too, is wise.

A predator given too much blessing becomes disaster.

The power does not soften their teeth or quiet their hunger. It does not warn prey of every ambush. It does not punish the metallic cry or stay the killing leap. But in the oldest predators, the ones whose spines are lichen-grown and whose patience is nearly geological, there comes a new discernment.

They no longer pursue animals already claimed by elsewhere.

A calf whose reflection has begun moving separately from its body.

A stray herd-grazer with tendrils rooting not only in flesh but in an impossible second shadow.

A bird with mirrored eyes showing a storm that has not merely not arrived, but never should.

The old predators pause before such prey. Their pressure pits sting. Their jaws close on empty air. They turn away hungry.

Because of this, fewer wrong things enter the meat of the plateau.

Because of this, hunger remains hunger and does not become contagion.

The pod-plants dream more clearly.

Inside their translucent skins, miniature weather continues: frost crystals, green vortices, pale flashes like silent lightning. But once in a while a pod grows around a true image of the plateau as it is now — not as it was, not as it might be, not as some submerged forest from another altitude. These pods stand faintly warmer than the others. Birds with mirrored eyes perch on them and leave frost pellets at their base. When the pods rupture, their vapor is not narcotic or sour. It is clean in the way wet cold can be clean: mineral, sharp, clarifying.

For one hour after such a rupture, distances behave.

Only for one hour.

That is enough.

Animals cross dangerous flats. Flocks find reedbeds. Young linked grazers, not yet taken fully into the vines, run and slip and learn the real shape of stones. Pale amphibians surface in the correct pools. Lapse-worms arrive where doubled events have begun to rot.

Then uncertainty returns, because uncertainty is one of the plateau’s laws.

But now it has intervals of mercy.

The power gives no name to itself.

Names attract worship, and worship attracts structures, and structures cannot hold here. More importantly, worship would bend the plateau’s attention away from its own conversations. The plateau does not need a god standing over it like a tower. It needs, if anything, a witness that can be trusted not to simplify it.

So the power hides its presence in relationships already present.

In the tremor between hoof and moss.

In the caution of reedbeds leaning toward unsounded noise.

In the bitter spores of shed fungal antlers guarding translucent calves.

In the blue staining of bones cached by old water.

In the orderly, temporary moss growing from lapse-worm castings.

In the silence between one wrong echo and the sound that will later make it.

No creature begins to pray.

But many begin to pause.

The pause spreads.

Not everywhere. Not always. No blessing should become absolute. But here and there, before stepping onto a pool-skin, before entering a mist-ridge, before answering an echo, before chasing an afterimage, before rooting a vine into a newborn’s hide, before rupturing a pod, before striking, fleeing, feeding, opening, closing — life hesitates.

In that hesitation, the plateau gains room.

Room is the one gift an ancient place can still use.

Not rescue.

Not correction.

Room.

Room for a storm to spend itself without becoming a scar.

Room for a dying animal to be released.

Room for a false lake to warn the feet before it takes the body.

Room for a herd to choose a path not because the land has become simple, but because it has become legible for a breath.

Room for old stone, finished with rising, to continue its surface dreaming without tearing its dreamers apart.

Ages pass.

The benevolent power remains.

Or perhaps it leaves, if leaving is possible for something that has loved correctly. Perhaps it withdraws so completely that only the effects remain braided into wet root and mineral silk. Perhaps it never was a presence at all, only the plateau’s own deep will answering itself after being perfectly heard.

There is no monument to say.

No cairn, no temple, no bright wound in the sky.

Only this:

After the ground-storms, when the plain steams softly and torn mats expose centuries in colored layers, the reed-drums slow one by one instead of all at once. Pools settle. Small animals return. Winged things pick spores from the air. Far off, something large calls, and another answers from a direction that almost matches the valley.

The cold comes back into everything.

Frost stitches the edges of leaves.

Water resumes its patient leaking from the rocks.

And beneath it all, so deep that it might be geology, so gentle that it might be imagination, a second pulse follows the first.

The moss shivers.

The creatures pause.

Then the plateau continues.
