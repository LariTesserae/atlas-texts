It would not come down as thunder.

Thunder would be an event, and events are dangerous there. Thunder has a before and after. It arrives, breaks, rolls away, and leaves the air altered. The basin-makers would gather at once with their broad damp hands open, faces pale with concern, ready to remove the cause and store it in a sealed bowl.

So the power would arrive as something the marsh already understood.

Condensation.

At first there would simply be more water on the undersides of roofs. A fine bright trembling along lintels. A bead forming at the tip of a reed. A warmth in the manufactured mud that had not been mixed there by hands.

The frogs would notice first. Their gold eyes would turn, one after another, not toward a sound but toward a gentleness passing through the cisterns.

Then the heron with the raised foot would blink.

The power would wait.

It would wait long enough to learn the basin’s courtesy. It would not complete the falling drop. It would not lower the heron’s foot. It would not push the gate that knocks once without current. It would not straighten the false distances or correct the counting of tiles. It would not call the marsh broken, because it was not broken. It would not call the basinfolk trapped, because many beloved things are also bindings, and wisdom knows the difference between a prison and a vow.

It would look at the fired-clay plates, the shell-lime, the reed mats sealed with resin, the carefully kneaded mud without stones or bones. It would see the pride in them. It would see that every artificial thing there had been made not from contempt for nature, but from hunger for a world kind enough to be tended.

It would understand:

They had made a wetland out of absence.

They had made abundance out of a dry basin.

They had made continuance out of terror.

And then, afraid that loss would return if anything were allowed to finish, they had held the whole place at the lip of happening.

The power would love them for this before it changed anything.

That would be its first act.

Not repair.

Not judgment.

Love.

The basinfolk would feel it as a new kind of humidity.

A tender pressure in the air.

A shine gathering in the carved hands on the plaza stones.

A sweetness in the fruit that no one had taken from it.

The caste that tends causes would sit upright beneath their dry jars. They would hold invisible weights in their laps and listen hard. But they would find no threat. The new thing would have no coercion in it. It would not force a reason upon the spout, nor demand that the canal obey its slope.

The power would speak only after it had been silent for a very long time.

It would speak in their language first.

A lifted palm:

Remain.

Two fingers dipped in water:

Not yet.

Then something they did not know.

A third gesture.

One hand open. One hand closing. Then opening again.

The basinfolk would not understand.

The children in the pools would gather near the reflected ladders. The elders in the bright plazas would fill their palms with water and watch which line dried last. The distance-tenders would stop at the retaining wall and look back.

The power would make the gesture again.

Open.

Close.

Open.

It meant:

Again.

Not finished into absence.

Not held away from ending.

Again.

The basin had no mark for this. Its stones said wave, eye, leaf, ladder, hand. Its prayers said be arriving, be opening, be held. Do not pass away into having been.

The power did not erase that prayer.

It added one line.

Be returning.

The addition appeared nowhere at first. No stone cracked with new carving. No voice declared law. Instead, one child-reflection pressed a wet hand into clay beside an old print.

The old print remained.

The new print filled with water.

When the water dried, the hollow stayed.

A basin-maker saw this and became afraid.

The power did not soothe the fear away. Fear was honest. Fear had protected them. Fear deserved to be heard.

So the power gave them a place for fear.

At the far side of the marsh, where a single wall ran for two hundred paces and stopped without joining anything, the brightness softened. Not everywhere. Not across the plazas, not over the cisterns, not upon the heron’s foot. Only there, beside the useless wall.

A little shade gathered.

It was not night. Night would have been too much.

It was only the idea of evening, folded small.

The first shadow in the basin that was not black and hard.

The reed roofs near it turned honey-colored. The limewashed wall ceased to glare. Water in a nearby trough no longer reflected noon but a depth of blue that might, someday, hold stars.

The basinfolk approached from all directions.

They came carrying empty jars with full shadows. They came with reed scoops. They came with measuring cords knotted unequally. They came from reflections, from damp walls, from houses kept ready for living. The oldest were borne in by the brightness itself, seated like arrangements of wet stone. The children came last, half-visible, their feet making no sound on the clay.

They stood around the new shade.

It did not advance toward them.

It waited.

A frog crossed into it.

Nothing terrible happened.

The frog’s throat moved.

For a long time, no sound came.

Then: one small note.

Not loud. Not even beautiful. A blunt green sound, brief as a seed striking water.

The basin shuddered.

The basinfolk raised their hands in alarm. The almost-ending tenders hurried toward the frog, prepared to stroke the sound back into silence. The cause-tenders searched for the reason and found it already dissolving.

The power held still.

The frog did not vanish. The cistern did not empty. The sky did not fall into evening. The heron did not die of surprise. The note was gone, but the place where it had been did not become a wound.

The frog breathed.

A second frog answered.

Then quiet returned.

Not the old quiet. Not the law that swallowed all movement.

A listening quiet.

The basinfolk stood for a long while.

Then one of the children laughed.

This time there was sound in it.

The elders lowered their eyes.

The power did not ask for more.

The next gift was smaller.

In the shaded place beside the unfinished wall, it loosened one fruit.

Only one.

A red globe from an espaliered branch whose roots were folded into a brick cistern. The fruit had been held for years or always at the precise heaviness before falling. Its skin shone with all seasons at once: green bud, yellow flower, ripe flesh, dry seed.

The beginning-tenders gathered around it in distress.

Their duty was to prevent this.

Their love was to prevent this.

The fruit trembled.

The power did not pull.

It merely withdrew the command that falling must mean loss.

The fruit let go.

It struck the manufactured mud with a soft, complete sound.

Many of the basinfolk covered their mouths. Water spilled from between their lips and ran down their chins.

The fruit split.

Inside were not tiny rolled plans, not a new pool, not a corrected channel.

Seeds.

Plain seeds.

Brown, wet, ordinary.

For a moment this seemed like a diminishment. Then the mud around them darkened.

The basinfolk watched.

Nothing sprang up at once. No vine caught halfway through its curl. No obedient leaf arranged itself in permanent fullness.

The seeds lay there.

Hidden.

Doing nothing visible.

This, too, was terrifying.

The power spoke again, not aloud, but through the warm seep of water in stone.

Some beginnings are allowed to disappear while beginning.

The basinfolk did not understand.

But one of the oldest, whose palms had held water for longer than memory, leaned forward and touched the split fruit. Its finger came away stained red.

The elder put the finger to its small mouth.

It tasted sweetness.

Not shine taken from fruit.

Fruit.

The elder closed its eyes.

No one moved to stop it.

After that the power made no more changes for a while.

Wisdom is careful after sweetness.

It let the basinfolk argue in their silent way. Palms, bowls, split reeds, fingers in water. Remain. Not yet. Cause removed. This has happened before, but not earlier. The new gesture returned again and again, uncertain at first.

Open. Close. Open.

Again.

Some rejected it.

The caste of distances feared it most. If things could return, then far pools might someday be reached. Walls might ask to join other walls. Horizons might cease to protect the marsh from whatever lay beyond the haze.

The almost-ending tenders feared rot.

The beginning-tenders feared grief.

The cause-tenders feared reasons breeding beyond containment.

The house-keepers feared sleeping mats wrinkled by actual sleep.

The children feared nothing for several minutes, then feared everything all at once, then forgot and stood beneath the new shade with their mouths open, waiting to hear whether another frog would speak.

The power honored every fear by leaving the rest of the basin untouched.

No fruit elsewhere fell.

No droplet elsewhere broke.

The pot still trembled at the edge of boiling.

The dragonfly still hovered beside the blue flower.

The heron still held its foot above the water.

Noon remained noon.

Only beside the unfinished wall was there permission.

Not command.

Permission.

The power shaped it as a rule the basin could accept:

Nothing finishes here unless it consents.

This was carved eventually on no stone, because they had no writing. Instead, a new mark appeared among the plaza signs: not wave, not eye, not leaf, not ladder, not hand.

A circle broken at the top, with a small line beneath it.

The elders read it by filling it with water and watching which part dried last.

It meant threshold.

It meant return.

It meant you may.

The shaded place became neither temple nor garden nor wound. The basinfolk did not know what to call it. Names were too fixed. They referred to it with a gesture: one palm held over the heart, then lowered toward the mud.

There, experiments began.

A reed was allowed to brown.

The tenders sat with it through the whole passage, trembling. Yellow cut end, dulling. Green stem, paling. Fiber, loosening. The first scent of rot rose.

The almost-ending caste nearly intervened.

The power whispered nothing. It did not praise decay. It did not hurry them toward acceptance. It simply stayed present, benevolent as shade.

The reed collapsed into the mud.

For three days, nothing replaced it.

On the fourth day, a white fungus flowered along its length.

The basinfolk gathered.

The fungus was not part of any plan.

No one had placed it.

No one had assigned it a seam.

It was delicate and brief and unarranged.

The basinfolk looked at it with horror.

Then with wonder.

Then with the first form of humility their world had ever permitted.

A child asked, with two fingers dipped in water and then the new open-close-open sign:

Not yet, again?

An elder answered by touching the child’s head.

Yes.

After reeds came insects.

A dead beetle was allowed to realize it was dead.

The one who tended almost-endings sat beside it and wept water from its mouth, not because the beetle’s shine was gone, but because the shine had been beautiful and could not be kept. The power did not restore the beetle. Instead, it drew attention to the place where the beetle had lain.

Ants came.

No one had arranged ants.

The cause-tenders were appalled.

The ants took the beetle apart with perfect seriousness.

Piece by piece, the beetle became motion again.

This disturbed the basinfolk more deeply than stillness ever had.

For many days, they would not enter the shaded place.

The power remained anyway.

Not disappointed.

Not impatient.

A benevolent power does not mistake retreat for failure.

Eventually the child-reflections returned. They climbed the absent ladders higher than before. One of them placed a real foot on a real wall and scraped its knee.

Blood appeared.

Redder than fruit.

The basinfolk froze.

The child stared at the wound.

Then it cried.

Not soundlessly. Not as water held in the mouth. It cried with breath and throat and affronted life.

Every frog in the nearest cistern leapt.

The power came very close then, close enough to be felt as coolness on the child’s hot skin. It did not erase the wound. It taught a basin-maker how to wash it, how to bind it with clean reed fiber, how to wait while flesh did what clay could not.

The next day the wound had changed.

The next week it had closed.

A mark remained.

The child loved the mark.

It showed everyone.

The elders studied it with grave attention.

A completed hurt that did not end the one who bore it.

A line of past carried safely in the present.

This was new.

The power’s greatest gift was not change.

It was trust in what change did not destroy.

Slowly, very slowly, the basin altered in places where it wished to.

Not everywhere.

There were still pools that reflected ladders not present. There were still processions of bare feet crossing noon-reflections. There were still walls that stopped before usefulness, and causeways counted differently in each direction. There were still fruit held at the edge of falling because some fruit desired to remain that way, and some tenders loved holding them.

The power did not make a wilderness of the marsh.

It did not shame the artificial mud.

It did not scatter the frogs from their rows or command the decorative fish to become wild. It did not tell the basinfolk that arranged life was lesser than accidental life.

Instead it taught them that care and control were not the same devotion.

A placed thing could be loved.

A changing thing could also be loved.

A bowl could be kept ready.

A bowl could be used, chipped, mended, and kept still.

One house was chosen for actual living.

Only one.

The mat inside became wrinkled. A hearth was lit. The pot that had trembled forever at the edge of boiling finally boiled. Steam rose.

The basinfolk panicked when the steam vanished.

The power showed them condensation forming on the roof.

The vanished thing had not been nothing.

It had traveled.

This idea entered them like rain entering dust.

Steam became roof-water.

Fruit became seed.

Reed became fungus.

Beetle became ants.

Blood became scar.

Sound became memory.

Memory became song.

The first song was very poor. It had three notes, all uncertain. The basinfolk sang as if releasing water from their mouths one careful thread at a time. Their voices sounded like porous stone learning birds.

The words were old:

Be arriving.  
Be opening.  
Be held.  
Do not pass away into having been.

Then the new line, almost too soft to hear:

Be returning.

When they finished, they waited in terror for the song to be gone.

It was gone.

Then one child sang the last line again.

Be returning.

The elders bowed their heads.

The power, if it had a heart, was glad.

There remained those who never entered the shaded place. They continued to tend suspended droplets, to keep buds from wilting, to remove causes from happenings before they became too heavy. The power blessed them also.

Benevolence does not require all souls to choose the same mercy.

Some beings are healed by motion.

Some by stillness.

Some by a door they may never open, but know is there.

So the power left the door.

At the unfinished wall, the shade deepened by a finger-width each century, or each afternoon, depending on how one counted. Beyond it, the bright rearranging distance no longer only refused arrival. Sometimes, from the threshold, one could glimpse a place not made by hand: rough stones, old roots, a stream with its own bed, mud containing bones no one had placed there.

The basinfolk looked at it with reverence and suspicion.

The wild looked back without needing them.

No one crossed for a long time.

Then, one day, the heron lowered its foot.

The sound was very small.

Water accepted the foot and closed around it.

The heron took one step.

Then another.

Nothing ended.

Nothing was ruined.

The marsh held its breath.

The heron opened its beak and gave one harsh, living cry.

All across the basin, droplets trembled on walls.

Most remained suspended.

One fell.

Only one.

It struck the surface of a pool that reflected a moon at noon.

A ripple moved outward.

The reflections broke.

Then gathered again.

Not the same.

Not lost.

Again.
