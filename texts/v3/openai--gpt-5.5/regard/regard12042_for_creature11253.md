It would not give them ground.

That would be the first wisdom of it.

A lesser kindness, seeing the Keth-Avai suspended in cold darkness above an abyss, would imagine salvation as a floor: stone laid across the void, terraces cut into the caldera walls, ladders descending, walls raised, roofs built, the whole perilous music of the Hollow silenced under architecture. A lesser power would pity their gripping hands, their fragile bones, their children taught first and second and always to hold on.

But the wise power would listen before it acted.

It would listen for a long time.

Long enough to learn that the Hollow was not merely danger. Long enough to hear the dead singing in the memorial pipes. Long enough to understand that the Keth-Avai were not trapped in the Hollow as a prisoner is trapped in a cell, but held by it as a song is held by breath.

So it would not make them other than they were.

It would not still the winds.

It would not silence the Pipe-Stems.

It would not kill the deep-dwellers, nor tame the Skein-runners, nor turn the vast vertical world into something flat and safe and dead.

It would add one note.

Only one, at first.

A low undertone beneath the endless humming, too deep to be noticed as sound, too gentle to frighten the herds, too subtle to enter the Keth-Avai’s stories as a voice from outside. It would pass through the roots and Skeins and hollow stems like warmth through sleeping flesh. Wherever old fiber had begun to fray unseen, the undertone would change the taste of the sap. The Skein would darken a little. Its hum would sour by a half-tone.

The Keth-Avai would notice.

Not all at once. Not as revelation. A watch-child would say, “That rope is singing wrong.” A master weaver would test it and find hidden weakness. A platform would be abandoned a day before it failed. A migration route would be altered. A proverb would be born:

_When the low note bends, do not trust the thread._

No one would know who had given them the warning. That would be best.

The wise power would continue.

It would touch the Mats that grew near nest-baskets, not changing them beyond recognition, only encouraging a sleeping possibility already inside their green ancestry. In the dampest seasons, certain colonies would begin to weave themselves denser beneath nurseries and child-ways: springy curtains, elastic and layered, strong enough to slow a small falling body.

Not save every child.

The Hollow would remain the Hollow.

But sometimes, when a toddler’s grip failed, when a young climber misjudged a wind-shift, when a mother screamed and reached too late, the child would not vanish forever into the dark. The Mats would tear, stretch, cradle, hold. The child would be found sobbing in a green net thirty meters below, bruised, terrified, alive.

The Keth-Avai would call these places mercy-veils.

They would cultivate them.

They would never know that the first one had not been bred by their own hands.

That too would be best.

Then the power would attend to hunger.

Not abundance. Abundance, sudden and unearned, can rot a people as surely as famine can break them. But the Hollow’s margins were too narrow. Too many disputes began with empty baskets. Too many quiet killings at settlement edges were caused not by hatred but by mathematics: this many mouths, that much pith, this much damp, that many days until the herds returned.

So the wise power would alter a fungus growing unnoticed in the shadowed interiors of Pipe-Stems. It would make the fungus edible after drying, rich in the salts and trace nutrients the Keth-Avai lacked. It would give it a faint amber glow when mature, so harvesters could find it without stripping the stems too early. It would grow best in cut sections already taken for food, feeding on waste rather than competing with living structure.

An old gardener would discover it.

She would be laughed at for tasting glowing rot.

Then she would live through a dry season with five children and no sickness in her house.

After that, no one would laugh.

The amber fungus would become part of the gardens. It would become trade. It would become winter food, journey food, food for the old whose teeth could no longer tear pith. It would not end scarcity, but it would soften its sharpest edge.

Where there is less desperation, there is less killing.

The power would also teach without speaking.

It would give the frost a longer memory.

On the coldest nights, when ice formed briefly along the upper Skeins, the crystals would begin to preserve vibration-patterns in their branching shapes. A Pipe-Stem close to breaking would frost in jagged, interrupted rings. A healthy one would frost in smooth spirals. A Skein under uneven strain would grow feathered ice on the overloaded side.

The Keth-Avai already believed the ice remembered. Now the belief would become more useful.

Children would be taught to read frost-marks. Master weavers would rise before dawn to study the white geometry before it melted. New chants would encode the shapes.

_No split-ring stem holds through swelling season._  
_Feathered frost means shift the weight._  
_Spiral ice, sound twice._

In time, this would become science, though they would not call it that.

The wise power would not hand them writing. Not yet. But it would strengthen what they already possessed: memory, chant, touch, pattern, tone.

It would cause certain hard-shelled seedpods to grow in regular segments, each segment shaped slightly differently, each taking knots well. The Keth-Avai would begin using them as teaching strings: tactile records of load-counts, family lines, route-sequences, funeral tones. A blind elder would invent the first long memory-cord. A messenger would carry one across three settlements. A formula too complex for a single song would survive its maker’s death.

From this, eventually, would come archives.

Not libraries of paper.

Hanging archives. Braided archives. Chiming archives.

Knowledge held the way the Keth-Avai held everything: by tension, by pattern, by trust.

The power would look below, then.

Into the black where the weight-bringers climbed.

Here it would be most careful.

The deep-dwellers were terrible, yes. They took platforms. They learned patrols. They waited beneath sleeping communities and rose with patient hunger. But they too belonged to the Hollow. They were not evil. They were appetite shaped by darkness.

A foolish power would burn them out.

A sentimental power would make them gentle.

The wise power would do neither.

It would deepen the glow of certain lower mosses, drawing herds of heavy, warm-blooded prey into regions far below the Keth-Avai settlements. It would alter the scent of those mosses so that, to the deep-dwellers, they smelled faintly of fire-smoke and nest-warmth. Not enough to deceive forever. Enough to make another path easier.

It would also give the Keth-Avai warning.

Not a weapon. Not victory.

Warning.

When a deep-dweller’s great limbs compressed the older Skeins, the same hidden undertone would travel upward ahead of it, carried through root and rope. Memorial pipes would shiver. Fire-platform ash would ripple. Sleeping feet locked around a strand would feel, before waking minds understood, the wrongness of a weight rising from below.

The sentries would learn.

They would make a new alarm tone: three short hums, one held breath, then the striking of hollow bone.

Some settlements would still be taken.

But fewer.

Fewer is not paradise.

Fewer is still mercy.

Then, after years of such small interventions, the power might allow itself one visible act.

Not a face in the clouds. There were no clouds fit for faces here.

Not a voice declaring commandments. The Keth-Avai already knew how to listen.

It would choose a night of hard frost, in a settlement old enough that its dead sang in hundreds of pipes. The fires would be low. The Hollow would be loud. A child would wake to hear a note she had never heard before: warm, vast, beneath the world’s own chord.

She would crawl from her nest-basket and hum back.

Only a child would be unafraid enough to answer.

Her note would waver. The deeper note would adjust to meet it.

For a few breaths, the child and the unseen power and the Hollow itself would be one sound.

In the morning, the frost on every Pipe-Stem around the settlement would show the same shape: not a symbol from outside, not a written word, but a pattern the master weavers would recognize with astonishment.

A bridge-pattern.

Impossible, they would say at first.

Too wide. Too much tension. No natural Skein would hold it. No settlement could spare the labor. No known knot would distribute the weight.

But the frost would show how.

A way to braid living Skeins without killing them. A way to invite Pipe-Stems to grow along a chosen arc. A way to make a structure that flexed with the Hollow’s breath instead of fighting it.

They would argue for seven seasons.

Then they would begin.

Not one settlement. Many. The pattern would travel by song and memory-cord, by thread-bonded riders and old women with strong hands, by marriages, by trade, by curiosity. Each community would add its own correction. The bridge would not be built so much as grown, coaxed, braided, sung into place over decades.

It would not span the whole Hollow.

Not yet.

But it would span enough.

For the first time, settlements separated by killing distances would be linked by a living way-stem broad enough for children, elders, baskets of food, funeral pipes, new ideas.

The Keth-Avai would call it the First Holding.

And once it existed, it would change them.

Not into something else.

More into themselves.

Trade would become easier than raiding. Marriage would carry songs farther. A child born in one chord might grow old in another. Disputes would still happen, but now there would be councils held at bridge-knots, with witnesses from five settlements listening. Mathematics would flower. So would music. So would law.

The Hollow would become, slowly, less a scattering of frightened lights and more a constellation.

The wise power would watch this and be glad.

But it would not remain forever close.

That is another wisdom of benevolence: to know when help becomes possession.

It would withdraw its hand, leaving only tendencies. Mercy-veils would still grow where tended. Warning-tones would still sound through stressed Skeins, but only to those who listened well. Amber fungus would still need cultivation. The bridge-pattern would still require labor, patience, failure, repair. The Keth-Avai would still bury their dead in wind and pipe-song. They would still fear the dark below. They would still belong to a world that could kill them.

But more of them would live long enough to become elders.

More children would survive their first mistake.

More songs would cross the void.

And perhaps, centuries later, when the great Pipe-Stems from the far walls finally reached toward one another and the Hollow began to close its ancient emptiness with living architecture, the Keth-Avai would be ready.

They would not say a god had saved them.

They had no need of that thought.

They would say the Hollow had changed its song.

They would say the Holding Deepened.

They would say that once, in the age of their ancestors, a new note entered the wind, and those who listened learned how to answer.

And beneath their settlements, beneath their bridges and gardens and memorial pipes, beneath the calls of Skein-runners and the slow climbing of deep-dwellers, beneath frost and mist and fire-smoke, the wise power’s gift would remain:

not rescue,

not dominion,

not an end to falling,

but a little more chance to hold on,

and a little more room to become.
