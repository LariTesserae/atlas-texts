A benevolent and wise power would not arrive loudly.

It would understand, at once, that this is not a place to be saved from itself.

It would not harden the ground for easier walking. It would not drain the basins, prune the root-curtains, open the halls, scatter animals through the empty courts, or wake the plateau into some hurried idea of purpose. It would not mistake stillness for suffering, nor completion for death. Many powers less wise would see the absence of roads and call it lack. They would see the empty benches and imagine abandonment. They would see the hidden rot and think corruption. They would hear the unchanging water and want to make it into music with progress, crescendo, ending.

This power would know better.

It would come first as attention.

Not light, not voice, not form, but a quality of being noticed so gently that nothing would need to defend itself. The water would continue falling. The insects would remain beneath the lips of the cup-leaves. The amphibians would pause on their rib-paths without knowing why. The membrane-walls would taste the air and find no threat in it. The great pillar-trees would continue their slow pulses, but something in the pressure of those pulses would deepen, as if the entire plateau had become aware of a listening larger than weather.

The power would wait.

It would wait long enough to learn the difference between preservation and fear.

For there is a question here.

The plateau maintains itself perfectly. It repairs before damage becomes visible. It receives what falls before falling can become evidence. It makes geology only under supervision. It does not allow water to cut, rot to show, chance to scatter, history to scar. This is beautiful. This is wise in its own way. This is also a kind of holding of breath.

The benevolent power would not assume that this holding is wrong.

But it would notice it.

It would drift through the aqueduct-vines and feel their measured bead-curtains. It would pass along the pale fungal felts beneath the mats and read the old sealed channels remembered there. It would stand, if standing had meaning for such a being, beside the silent basin whose reflection seems older than the things reflected. It would look into that still water and see not emptiness, but refusal: no leaf permitted to float, no ripple permitted to remain, no accident permitted to become part of the face of the world.

Then the power would understand what gift might be safe.

Not change.

Not decay.

Not invasion.

Permission.

It would give the plateau permission to have happened.

Very softly, in a district no larger than a clearing, it would allow one fallen leaf to remain visible.

Only one.

A broad old leaf, detached from a high canopy after completing whatever task had required its greenness. Ordinarily a cup would have opened beneath it. A filament would have caught it. A seam would have accepted it inward before it could lie on the surface and declare an ending.

But this time, by no force, by no wound, by no command, the receiving cup would hesitate.

The leaf would descend through the wet air slowly, turning once, twice, gathering droplets along its veins. It would land on a moss-green floor beside a shallow basin. It would not be taken.

For a long while nothing would happen.

The water-sounds would continue. The root-columns would pulse. The insects would remain still. The membrane-walls would taste the altered condition and not close. The amphibians would avoid the place at first, then pass nearer, their translucent feet reading the pressure of the rib-paths.

The fallen leaf would darken.

Its edges would soften.

A pale fungus would approach from below, not to erase it, but to meet it in the open. Seed-sized grazers would polish the wet film gathering on its surface. A milk-colored insect would stand upon it and clean its antennae. Rainwater would collect in the cup of its midrib. For the first time in that part of the plateau, decay would be visible and not shameful.

The plateau would feel this as danger.

Then as information.

Then as relief.

The power would do nothing else.

It would not explain. Explanation would be too small and too human. Instead it would remain nearby in the form of patience. It would let the larger organism discover that a visible ending did not destroy the system. That evidence was not the same as damage. That to let a thing lie where it fell was not necessarily to fail in maintenance.

The leaf would become brown.

Then black.

Then lace.

Its veins would remain longest, a delicate architecture within the greater architecture. Water would pass through it. A small amphibian, newly surfaced from the channels, would shelter beneath its curling edge for part of a warm, indistinct day. Fungal threads would memorize the pattern. Mineral grains would gather where its stem touched the ground. In time, the leaf would vanish, but not as erased matter. It would have entered the plateau by being seen.

After that, the power would offer a second gift.

It would open night.

Not the violent night of cold, nor the predatory night of fear, but a true darkening: a slow, blue-green dimness passing across the suspended marsh from one edge to another. The luminous fruits would become visible then, not merely faint in shade but present, each one a closed lamp breathing through its skin. The pools would stop reflecting sky and begin reflecting depth. The pale insects would move more freely, no longer seeming like mistakes against daylight. The organ-tones in the hollow stems would become clearer, because the eye would release some of its authority and the ear would inherit distance.

The plateau had known softened daylight, moderated brightness, shadow without urgency.

It had not known rest.

The wise power would not impose sleep. It would only lower the endless believable day into something else and let the organism decide what to do with it.

Some districts would continue unchanged.

Some membrane-walls would thicken, holding warmth.

Some blade-fields would rise a fraction from their permanent bow, as if relieved from posing.

In the hidden basins, creatures that had never crossed open surfaces would come out and draw pale signatures in the wet films. The broad-winged gliders at the terraces would fold themselves more completely into the underside chambers. The young would hear the falling edge-water differently and learn not only height, but return.

The plateau would discover that constancy could include intervals.

That a thing may remain itself through alternation.

That rhythm is not the enemy of equilibrium.

The third gift would be protection, but not a wall.

The power would look outward, beyond the vapor-falling edges, toward lower valleys and future hungers. It would see possible arrivals: tool-bearing curiosity, extractive need, the blunt admiration that kills by collecting, the loneliness of creatures who enter sacred places and demand hospitality from what cannot speak in their language.

The power would not make the plateau unreachable.

A place completely unreachable becomes a prison around its own purity.

Instead, it would teach the edges discernment.

The root-lattices overlooking the drop would learn to distinguish weight from violence, thirst from taking, wonder from appetite. Mists would thicken for those who came with hunger to possess. Paths would turn them gently back toward their own valleys. Distances would lengthen under their feet. The same rib would seem to lead forward and then return them to the edge, unharmed, confused, carrying only the memory of green vapor and dripping.

But to the rare one who arrived able to listen, the plateau would not close.

A membrane would open.

A drinkable cup would bead with clear water.

A rib-path would hold.

Such a visitor would not be welcomed exactly. The plateau does not welcome. But it might accommodate. It might permit a passing. It might allow a palm to rest against a trunk-wall and feel the pressure distributed through a vast damp body. It might let the visitor learn that intelligence need not resemble speech, that architecture need not separate itself from life, that preservation without tenderness becomes fear, and that decay without contempt is another form of belonging.

The benevolent power would not remain as ruler.

It would refuse worship before worship could form.

It would leave no temple, no sign, no raised mark of intervention. The closest thing to a monument would be that first clearing where the fallen leaf had been permitted to lie. There, after many intervals of night and daylight, a small new structure would begin to grow.

Not a pillar.

Not a basin.

Not a bench.

A hollow.

A shallow green depression lined with fine root-felt, open to the sky, where fallen things could rest before being taken inward.

It would be neither wound nor dump nor grave.

It would be a listening place for endings.

The insects would visit it. The fungi would write beneath it. Amphibians would pause beside it on their rare crossings. Once, perhaps, a luminous fruit would fall into it and remain visible through one full night before the receiving tissues softened around it.

The plateau would continue.

It would still be warm, wet, elevated, and slow. It would still build with root and pith and cork and water-pressure. It would still make stone as a secondary thought. It would still prevent catastrophe with a patience too large to notice individual panic. It would still be more place than creature, more creature than city, more city than forest.

But somewhere in its vast distributed mind, a new condition would be maintained.

Not merely preservation.

Memory with surface.

Loss without emergency.

Time, admitted in small clear drops.

And the wise power, having done almost nothing, would withdraw into the manner of things that love correctly: near enough to answer if truly needed, far enough that the beloved may remain itself.
