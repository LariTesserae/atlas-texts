## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not descend in flame.

It would not sterilize the canals, straighten the trees, scour the rust from the walls, or command the pumps to stop. A wise power would understand immediately that the Glimmering Throat of Verdant Rust is not merely diseased. It is an answer—strange, sorrowful, ingenious—to a wound left open too long.

So first, it would listen.

Not with ears, because the Throat does not truly speak in sound alone. It would listen through pressure in the water, through the shiver of silver leaves, through the lichen’s slow respiration, through the off-rhythm thud of the pumps that have kept going beyond all reasonable hope. It would listen to the Humming Dwellers until it understood their language not as a code to be translated, but as a devotion.

It would learn the song.

And then, one night, when the fog lay thick over the canals and the walls glowed faintly with their old green phosphorescence, the benevolent power would hum back.

Not louder than the dwellers.

Not above them.

With them.

At first, the dwellers would freeze in their work. A hand resting on a pipe. A blade halfway through a root. A rag-wrapped face turning toward the unseen presence in the dark.

The pumps would falter.

Only for a breath.

Then resume.

But altered.

A new harmonic would enter the settlement: low, warm, patient, like sunlight remembered by stone.

The wise power would understand that this place has survived by perfect obedience to necessity. The dwellers do not stop because stopping means drowning. The pumps do not rest because rest means rot. The trees grow without flower or seed because the world taught them that openness invites loss. Everything here endures by closing itself around the wound.

So the power would not demand trust.

It would make trust possible.

### The First Gift: A Pause That Does Not Kill

The first thing it would give the Throat is not purity, nor beauty, nor salvation.

It would give it a margin.

Deep beneath the central pumpworks, where the oldest engines chew rust and root in the dark, the benevolent power would lay one unseen hand upon the machinery. It would not repair the pumps into sleek new things. It would not erase their history. Instead, it would teach them a second rhythm.

A redundancy.

A grace.

The pumps would learn to take turns.

For the first time in generations, one engine could sleep while another carried the pulse. One channel could slow without becoming stagnant. One dweller could withdraw their hand from a valve and not fear the Great Drowning.

At first this would terrify them.

The dwellers would gather around the resting pump in a ring, humming sharply, uncertain whether they were witnessing death or miracle. They would touch its cooling flank with their many-fingered hands. They would feel no final silence there—only a deep, coiled readiness.

A machine at rest.

Not dead.

The idea would move through them like weather.

Some would weep without knowing the gesture. Their eyes would remain wide and reflective, but moisture would gather along the edges of their wrappings and vanish into the cattail fiber.

The wise power would do nothing to interrupt this.

Some revelations must be approached slowly.

### The Second Gift: The Cleaning That Does Not Erase

The water of the Throat is not merely dirty. It is storied. It carries rust, algae, oil-ghosts, minerals, memory, rot, and the thin metallic grief of abandoned industry. To cleanse it completely would be a kind of violence. The dwellers themselves are adapted to it. The trees feed upon it. The pumps are nourished by its strangeness.

So the power would not make the canals clear.

Instead, it would introduce a new patience into the marsh.

In the shallows, among cattails and drowned conveyor belts, small colonies of dark reeds would appear. Their stems would be blue-black, their roots fine as hair and bright as copper wire. They would drink excess poison slowly, carefully, never enough to starve the silver trees, never enough to shock the algae into death. Along the cracked concrete embankments, soft mats of moss would thicken into living filters. Fungal fans would bloom under bridges, not flowers exactly, but something close—wide, pale shelves veined with green light, breathing in toxins and exhaling a cleaner damp.

The dwellers would regard these things with suspicion.

They know growth. They know what growth can do.

But these new organisms would not invade machinery. They would not dissolve tools. They would not choke the canals. They would simply hold what the water could no longer bear.

Over years, the sourness in the air would lessen.

Not vanish.

Lessen.

The metallic tang would remain, but beneath it another scent would emerge: wet bark, clean rain, the faint sweetness of living soil.

### The Third Gift: The Return of Small Witnesses

The wise power would know better than to bring back wolves, deer, bright birds, or schools of fish all at once. The Throat could not endure a sudden resurrection of the world it lost. The dwellers could not endure it either. Their stories of fauna are almost sacred because they are safely gone.

So the first animal would be very small.

One morning, a dweller pruning a spiral tree would see a ripple in the water that was not made by falling rust or pump vibration.

A larva.

Pale, translucent, almost nothing.

Then another.

Then, weeks later, a single winged insect would cling to the inside of a broken window, its body thin as a splinter, its wings reflecting the green light of the mold.

The settlement would go still around it.

The dwellers would hum in dissonance, alarmed and reverent.

The insect would clean its forelegs.

That would be all.

A minor act.

A universe reopened.

Later would come water-skimmers, then blind canal shrimp that fed on the altered algae, then soft-bodied snails with shells stained orange by iron. Much later, perhaps, a fish no longer than a finger. Much later still, a bird would land on the highest rusted beam at the edge of the settlement and tilt its head at the humming below.

But not yet.

A wise power would let wonder arrive in increments small enough not to break the heart.

### The Fourth Gift: A Door Outward

The Glimmering Throat is only two square kilometers, but to the dwellers it is cosmos, duty, ancestry, body. A benevolent power would not command them to leave. Nor would it romanticize their confinement.

Instead, at the settlement’s boundary, where marsh fog thickens into an almost-wall, it would open a passage.

Not a road.

A possibility.

One canal would begin to clear toward the south. Its banks would firm. The roots there would draw back just enough to reveal a path of old stone and compacted soil. Silver leaves would arch overhead, but not close. The fog would thin at certain hours.

The dwellers would discover it during maintenance.

They would stand before it a long time.

No law would forbid them from walking it. No song would command them to go.

For many seasons, none would.

That would be acceptable.

Freedom unused is still freedom.

Eventually, one adolescent returning from the Weeping Quarter, their lichen bright and their hum newly deepened, would step onto the path. They would take with them a coil of algae silk, a bone-polished pruning hook, and a palm-sized gear worn smooth by generations of touch.

They would return three days later carrying something impossible.

A seed.

Not from the silver trees.

A true seed, hard and brown and quiet.

The dwellers would gather around it as if around a sleeping engine.

The benevolent power would not tell them what to do.

They would decide.

### The Fifth Gift: Permission to Change

This would be the deepest intervention, and the most delicate.

The dwellers believe permanence is holiness because impermanence once destroyed everything. They maintain the pumps not only to survive, but because endless maintenance has become the shape of meaning. If the power simply relieved them of their work, it would hollow them out. If it left them unchanged, it would abandon them to attrition.

So it would give them a new ritual.

Not replacing the old.

Growing from it.

Once each year, when the fog thickened and the silver leaves pulsed with the pump-heart, the dwellers would gather at the central basin. There, for one full night, one machine would be allowed to sleep while the others sang around it. During that silence, a Keeper of the Pulse would remove their hands from the machinery and place them instead upon the chest of another dweller.

They would hum not to the pumps.

Not to the roots.

To each other.

At first, their voices would tremble. The harmony would be thin. They would not know how to hear themselves apart from the engines.

But slowly, over years, they would learn.

They would discover that their song is not merely maintenance. It is memory. It is grief. It is affection. It is warning. It is welcome. It is laughter, perhaps, though laughter in the Throat would sound like bubbling water under metal.

The wise power would place no doctrine in them.

Only the realization that they are not servants of the pulse.

They are part of it.

And what is part of a living rhythm may alter without betrayal.

### The Sixth Gift: Speech, But Not as Conquest

The dwellers do not speak because speech would fracture the song. The benevolent power would respect this. It would not force mouths open or turn hum into language.

But it would offer another layer.

In the phosphorescent mold along the walls, patterns would begin to appear in response to certain harmonics. Spirals, bars, branching marks, dots like condensation gathering with intention. The dwellers would notice that a particular hum made the mold brighten in a particular shape. Another hum dimmed it. A third caused silver leaves to tremble and arrange themselves in repeating patterns.

Not writing, at first.

Echo.

Then notation.

Then memory made visible.

The dwellers would learn to leave songs behind for those not present to hear them. Maintenance records would become hymns. Warnings would become murals. Grief would become green light on black brick.

For the first time, the dead would not vanish entirely into root, fog, or machinery.

Their songs could remain.

The walls, already luminous with mold, would become an archive.

And the Throat, which had long remembered only by continuing, would learn to remember by telling.

### The Seventh Gift: A Better Death

A wise power would not remove death.

Death is not the cruelty here.

The cruelty is that all endings have been made indistinguishable from being consumed.

So the power would make room for chosen return.

At the edge of the Weeping Quarter, a dry rise would form slowly from silt, root, brick-dust, and patient fungal labor. It would be just high enough to remain above the flood in most seasons. The dwellers would not use it at first. They would distrust dryness. It would seem unnatural, exposed.

But one elder, whose hum had thinned to a trembling thread, would go there when their hands could no longer turn valves.

The others would follow at a distance.

The elder would sit beneath a spiral tree whose leaves chimed softly in the vapor. Their lichen would glow once, bright as submerged emerald, then fade.

The roots would not seize them.

The fog would not erase them.

The pumps would not demand their final strength.

Instead, the tree above would flower.

Only once.

A single bloom, silver-white and streaked with rust, opening in a place where no flowers had been known.

From then on, the dwellers would understand: not every surrender is drowning.

Some are offerings.

Some are completions.

### What Would Become of the Glimmering Throat

It would not become clean.

It would not become safe in any simple way.

It would still be a place of rust, water, moss, and groaning metal. Its buildings would still lean. Its canals would still glow at night with bioluminescent algae. The pumps would still beat in the dark, though now with rests between their labors. The air would still taste faintly of iron.

But the terror of refusal would soften into the dignity of endurance.

The vegetation would no longer expand only as hunger. Some of it would bloom. Some would seed. Some would shelter tiny returning lives.

The dwellers would continue to hum, but their humming would become more varied. Work-songs, mourning-songs, boundary-songs, child-songs, songs for machines waking, songs for machines sleeping, songs for the first insect seen in a generation, songs for those who left by the southern canal and those who returned changed.

The machines would still be sacred.

But no longer tyrants.

The roots would still feed.

But no longer only consume.

The water would still carry memory.

But no longer only grief.

And the benevolent power?

It would withdraw before it was worshiped too directly.

Not because worship is always wrong, but because this place had already spent too long bowing to necessity. The power would not wish to become another pump that must never be allowed to stop.

It would leave traces instead:

A harmonic hidden in the engines.

A reed that filters poison.

A path through fog.

A flower at the place of chosen death.

A seed in the palm of a silent dweller.

And if, on certain nights, the whole settlement hummed in a chord no one remembered learning, the wise power would hear it from wherever such powers dwell.

It would not answer every time.

It would not need to.

The Throat would no longer be merely a place that refused to die.

It would be a place learning, slowly and strangely, how to live.
