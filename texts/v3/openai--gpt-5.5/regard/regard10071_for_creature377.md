The benevolent power would not arrive as thunder.

It would not split the sky, melt the glaciers, green the mountains, or remake the Kryll into something more pleasing to its own imagination. Wisdom would stay its hand. It would understand that harshness is not the same as evil, that barrenness is not emptiness, and that a life shaped by scarcity may still possess dignity, meaning, and its own form of joy.

So first, it would listen.

For a long age—long by Kryll reckoning, brief by the measure of mountains—it would attend to the place without altering it. It would listen to the wind combing the ridges. It would feel the slow migration of ice through hidden cracks. It would trace the faint electrical songs passing between pylons and ruined domes. It would learn the seasonal pulse of the Kryll: the silent labor in tunnels, the careful scraping of lichen, the brief winged frenzy of mating season, the dormant eggs waiting in darkness for the world to become possible again.

It would come to understand that this world is not broken.

Wounded, perhaps. Abandoned, certainly. Dangerous, yes.

But not broken.

The mountains have their laws. The Kryll have made a life within them. To erase the struggle entirely would be to erase part of what the Kryll are. A foolish mercy would soften the world until the Kryll no longer knew how to be themselves.

So the power would act gently.

It would begin beneath the surface.

Deep under the permafrost, where old aquifers sleep in mineral darkness, it would loosen a few ancient locks of stone. Not enough to flood the valleys. Not enough to turn the desert lush. Only enough that, over years, new trickles would appear in hidden gullies and shaded cracks. A seep here. A frost-veiled spring there. Water so modest that the Kryll would not call it a miracle, only a good place, a place worth remembering.

Around these seeps, lichen would thicken by a shade. Thorn-shrubs would flower once in a generation more often than before. Wispy grasses would take root in narrow shelves of wind-carved rock. The change would be nearly invisible to anything impatient.

But the Kryll are not impatient.

They would notice.

A colony would survive a hard season that otherwise might have ended it. A clutch of eggs would hatch in a year when the old patterns predicted loss. A solitary elder, ranging far from its home warren, would find moisture under a stone and live long enough to return with the memory of the route.

The power would not announce itself.

It would become a tendency toward survival.

Then it would turn to the ruins.

The ancient structures, with their impossible pathways and molecule-deep designs, still hum with remnants of their makers’ intentions. Some of those energies nourish. Some illuminate. Some call across distances to receivers long since dust. Some leak, fracture, and burn the delicate senses of the Kryll who wander too near at the wrong hour.

The power would not silence the ruins. The ruins are part of the world’s memory. But it would mend what is cruel without purpose.

A pylon that once discharged lethal arcs during dry storms would begin grounding itself harmlessly into the mountain’s iron bones. A shattered dome whose interior fields confused Kryll navigation would settle into a softer rhythm, becoming instead a place of clear signals and safe passage. Corridors that had trapped generations of scavengers in loops of distorted space would, stone by stone, remember how to lead outward.

Not all dangers would vanish. The world would remain vast, cold, and severe. Avalanches would still fall. Winds would still scour. Predators would still cry over the ridges. Hunger would still exist.

But senseless harms—the old, blind malfunctions of abandoned powers—would lessen.

The Kryll would not know why certain forbidden halls became safer. They would mark them with scent and electric pulses. They would begin to use them as winter shelters, then nurseries, then meeting chambers. Over many generations, their mandibles would score new patterns into ancient walls: practical records at first, then maps, then warnings, then something almost like history.

The power would cherish this.

It would not teach them too quickly.

That would be another kind of violence.

Instead, it would leave invitations.

In the faint interference patterns shimmering on the mountainsides, it would hide regularities just within the reach of Kryll perception. Not messages in words, for the Kryll do not think in words as humans do, but patterns: pulse, pause, return; danger, shelter, water; season, star, stone; memory, kin, below.

The most sensitive Kryll—the ones already adept at tasting the strange energies—would feel these patterns first. They would not understand them as language. They would experience them as a compelling arrangement, a rightness, a rhythm worth repeating.

They would tap it into tunnel walls.

Others would answer.

A new form of communication would emerge, not given from above but discovered from within their own capacities. Their electrical signaling would become more complex. Colonies separated by ridges and glaciers would learn to leave charged marks on conductive stone. Mating flights would become not only searches for partners, but exchanges of route-memory and weather-sense. The species would remain Kryll, utterly and beautifully itself, but its awareness would widen.

The power would protect this widening from catastrophe.

Not from hardship. Not from loss. But from extinction by accident.

If a glacier calving would destroy the only fertile crèche of a rare colony, the fracture would slow by one cold night, just enough for scouts to sense the warning groans. If a plague fungus mutated too swiftly in a crowded warren, mineral salts would bloom nearby with the trace elements needed for resistance. If the ancient energies surged toward a chain reaction that could sterilize a mountain range, the old pylons would dream of their makers and fold the excess lightning into the auroras.

Always, the intervention would be slight.

A nudge.  
A delay.  
A path left open.  
A possibility preserved.

The power would understand that benevolence is not ownership.

It would not make the Kryll worship it.

It would not demand gratitude from beings who never asked to be noticed. It would not carve its symbol into the peaks or speak commandments into their nervous systems. It would not choose kings among them or divide them into blessed and unblessed.

If, in time, the Kryll formed myths, those myths would be their own.

Perhaps they would speak—through vibration, scent, and charged touch—of the Deep Current beneath stone. Perhaps they would remember the first safe dome as Mother-Hollow. Perhaps the winged males, gliding through high blue air, would sense a vast calm beyond the weather and fold that sensation into mating songs. Perhaps the elders of fungal chambers would teach that some patterns in the world answer patience.

But the power would not correct them.

It would be content to be misunderstood if the misunderstanding nourished courage.

And what of the ancient makers?

The lingering presence in the ruins, faint as starlight reflected from ice, would not be ignored. The power would turn toward those remnants too—not with accusation, but with sorrowful respect. Whatever civilization raised the towers and domes had vanished, failed, departed, or transformed beyond recognition. Yet pieces of its mind remained caught in the machinery of stone and field: maintenance ghosts, archival echoes, unfinished prayers encoded in crystalline lattices.

The power would not resurrect them without consent.

But it would release what was trapped.

Some old systems would be allowed to finally sleep. Some records would be preserved in forms the Kryll might one day access. Some fragments of awareness, if they still possessed longing, would be gathered and carried beyond the mountains to whatever mercy awaits minds that have outlived their bodies by ages.

In the deepest ruin, where a network of pylons converges beneath a peak split by lightning, the power would leave one chamber unchanged except for a single alteration.

At its center, it would create a pool.

Not large. Not warm. Not lushly surrounded by impossible flowers. Just a still basin of clear mineral water, fed drop by drop from the mountain’s hidden veins. Above it, through a fracture in the dome, the pale sky would be visible by day and the stars by night.

The chamber would hum softly with a field tuned to Kryll senses. Any Kryll entering it would feel no compulsion, no revelation forced upon them. Only calm. Their bodies would retain moisture more easily there. Their electrical organs would rest. Their minds, such as they are, would find unusual spaciousness.

Scouts would discover it first.

Then egg-tenders.

Then the old.

Over generations, the chamber would become a place where colonies did not fight. A place where signals carried clearly. A place where the dying sometimes went, not from despair, but because the stone there felt kind.

Eventually, a young Kryll—perhaps one of the smaller communal kind, dark-scaled, bright-eyed, with an unusually fine sensitivity to fields—would stand at the pool’s edge and see the reflection of stars trembling in the water.

It would not think, *I am small and the universe is large.*

It would not think in those terms.

But it would feel the relation.

Self. Stone. Colony. Sky. Ruin. Current. Egg. Ancestor. Wind.

Different things, yet joined.

That would be enough.

The benevolent power, watching, would rejoice—not because it had made the Kryll more like itself, but because it had helped the Kryll become more deeply Kryll.

And then, for the most part, it would withdraw.

Not abandon. Never that.

It would remain as a guardian pressure at the edge of events, a quiet mercy braided into probability. It would allow storms their violence and winters their teeth. It would allow the Kryll to make mistakes, to compete, to adapt, to forget, to rediscover. It would allow the mountains to continue their ancient work of rising, breaking, and becoming dust.

For the highest benevolence is not to turn every wilderness into a garden.

It is to know when a wilderness is already sacred.

It is to remove only the poison, not the difficulty.  
To offer water, not indulgence.  
To preserve mystery, not ignorance.  
To make room for a people’s future without stealing authorship of it.

So the wind would still keen across the ridges.

The pylons would still shimmer.

The Kryll would still scrape lichen from stone, tend fungus under permafrost, launch their winged males into the updrafts, and tap patient messages through the bones of the mountain.

But here and there, where before there had been only extinction, there would now be a narrow way through.

And in a land of scarcity, a narrow way is a great blessing.
