The power would not descend as light.

Light would be too simple here, too declarative. It would strike the wet walls and make them answer before they had chosen an answer. It would wake surfaces unevenly. It would turn the black pool into a mirror of something other than itself.

So the power would arrive first as patience.

Not absence. Not delay. A presence so carefully distributed that the city would not feel invaded, only accompanied. A kindness entering by the same laws the hollow already knew: condensation, pressure, warmth, the pause before a droplet falls.

It would come as a slight softening in the thunder of the reservoirs, not reducing their function, only rounding the edges of their noise. It would come as a degree of steadiness in the young ceiling when the stone ticked and answered itself. It would come as a new tolerance in the seams where roots pressed too hard, allowing both wall and root to learn one another without injury.

For a long while it would do nothing visible.

It would listen.

It would listen to the upward rain-click in the stone. It would listen to the grid of suspended droplets and to the collapse of those droplets. It would listen to the black leaves turning silver. It would listen to the sealed dry rooms, because what a place excludes is often as important as what it embraces. It would listen to the pool that refuses disturbance and to the ducts that breathe spores into districts not yet green enough.

The power would not assume the city was broken because it was empty.

It would not mistake absence of animals for loneliness.  
It would not mistake silence for suffering.  
It would not mistake incompletion for need.

A lesser benevolence might try to fill the houses.

It might bring birds, insects, fish, small warm things with bright eyes and beating hearts. It might scatter them into the mossy plazas and call that gift abundance. But the wise power would understand that hunger is not always an improvement. Teeth would change the innocence of the plants. Wings would make the air mean something different. Footsteps would teach the streets a new form of pressure before they had finished understanding their own.

So it would not populate the city.

Not yet.

Instead, it would ask the only question that matters to a being still becoming:

“What are you trying to become, and what prevents you?”

The city could not answer in words.

It would answer as it always did.

A bridge would extend three handspans farther than usual and stop.  
A dry chamber would warm by a fraction, then cool again.  
The black pool would show, for one instant, not past or flood or collapse, but a pattern of branching chambers not yet built.  
The suspended droplet in the vacuum-clean room would turn without thunder.  
In the outer districts, where coherence thinned into partial stairs and facades without rooms, mineral filaments would grow in trembling, abortive lines, then retract.

The power would understand.

The city was not lonely.

It was constrained.

Not imprisoned exactly, but overcareful. It had been born with excellent boundaries and insufficient trust. Its wetness knew how to connect, but feared dispersal. Its dry chambers held something necessary apart from the general body, but perhaps too completely. Its central pool could imagine futures, but not choose among them without risking the death of other futures. Its buildings were sockets, yes — but sockets require a first current.

The wise power would still not command.

It would offer a small mercy first: stability without stagnation.

Deep beneath the foundations, where the warm hollow met young earth still deciding whether to crush, cradle, or become part of it, the power would lay no beam, no pillar, no foreign brace. It would instead teach the stone a slower language of yielding. The fractures would not vanish. They would become legible. Pressure would be passed from rib to rib, from mineral foam to ceramic road, from ceiling wave to buried buttress, not as panic but as breath.

The chamber would remain young.  
It would still tick.  
It would still negotiate shape.  
But it would no longer have to negotiate alone.

Then the power would touch the water.

Not all of it. That would be domination.

It would choose one falling thread beside the central basin, a stream so thin it was nearly mist. For one cycle of the pumps, it would lend that thread a second memory. Not a replacement for the city’s salt-layered recollection, but an added capacity: the ability to remember not only what had occurred, but what had been meant.

The stream would descend.

It would strike a step.

It would divide into rivulets.

Everywhere those rivulets passed, the city would feel, faintly, the distinction between accident and intention.

A crack caused by strain.  
A door sealed by caution.  
A root redirected by care.  
A chamber kept dry by fear.  
A pool held flat by discipline.  
A bridge lengthened by service.  
A district unfinished by uncertainty.

This would be the first real gift: not repair, but discernment.

The city would begin to know which of its boundaries were wisdom and which were wounds.

For several wet hours nothing else would happen.

Then, in one of the clean dry chambers, the suspended droplet would tremble.

It had never fallen. It had never joined the rest. It contained the map of the hollow because it had been kept apart from the hollow. Archive, seed, eye, captive nucleus — perhaps all of these. The city had preserved it by denying it participation.

The benevolent power would approach that chamber with great care.

Dryness was danger here, or had been named danger. To enter too strongly would violate the city’s oldest caution. So the power would not bring water in. It would not break the membranes. It would not wet the pale floor.

It would merely make the chamber less alone.

Around the suspended droplet, in the vacuum-thin air, it would form a listening field: clear, gentle, almost nothing. A space in which the droplet could remain separate without being exiled. A difference between quarantine and sanctuary.

The droplet would turn.

It would show the whole hollow.

Then it would show something the black pool had never shown: the city seen from within one of its possible future inhabitants.

Not an animal body. Not a human visitor. Something condensed.

A pressure-shape moving through a corridor as a person might move through thought. A mind held briefly in vapor and surface tension. A being whose memory was mineral taste, whose touch was temperature, whose speech was the timed release of beads from a ceiling vine. It would pass through the empty houses and make them not full, exactly, but addressed.

The city would receive this vision.

The black pool, for the first time, would ripple.

Only once.

A single ring would move outward across the perfect flatness. The falling water around the basin would continue its immense noise. The pumps would boom. The reeds would tremble. But the ring would keep traveling past the pool’s edge, not physically, not visibly, but as permission.

The sealed houses around the basin would warm.

Not opening. Not yet.

Warming.

The power would withdraw a little then. Wisdom knows when a gift has become too large. It would not press the city toward birth. It would not demand gratitude, voice, worship, or form.

Instead, it would establish three protections.

The first protection would be against premature discovery.

At the boundary where coherent city thinned into raw wet stone, the mist would learn confusion. Not danger, not violence — only misdirection. Any creature from outside that came by greed, conquest, extraction, or simple heedless curiosity would find partial stairs, repeating canals, walls that seemed to lead back to themselves, a warm darkness persuasive enough to turn them around. Instruments would fog. Maps would contradict themselves. Drills would meet harder rock than expected. The hollow would remain hidden from those who could only name it resource.

But anyone who came in need, humility, or kinship with water would not be harmed. They might hear the upward rain-click and understand that they had reached a threshold. They might sit at the edge and leave without entering. They might dream of silver leaves afterward. That would be enough.

The second protection would be against internal certainty.

A young god-city can become cruel by becoming too coherent too soon. A distributed mind may decide one pattern is purity and erase its own experiments. The power had seen this happen in stars, forests, civilizations, embryos.

So it would bless the city with fruitful ambiguity.

Not confusion. Not madness. The capacity to keep several futures alive without freezing before them. The black pool would still show drowning, overgrowth, collapse, completion, and birth — but now it would also show hybrid futures, partial futures, futures that could be entered and exited. The city would learn that choosing one form need not murder all the others. Some futures can be held as seeds. Some can be seasonal. Some can be dreamt and never built, yet still nourish what is built.

The third protection would be against perfect isolation.

Not by adding outsiders. Not by opening gates.

The power would give the city a way to correspond.

Far above, on the underside of certain stones and in the roots of certain surface plants, dew would begin to gather in patterns on rare mornings. No one looking casually would notice. A shepherd might think it ordinary wetness. A child might press a finger to it and feel, briefly, a warm underground hush. Fungi might grow in rings that echoed the buried plazas. Springs might change mineral flavor by the smallest degree.

The city would not be exposed.

But it would no longer be entirely sealed from the world’s answer.

Rain falling on the surface would sometimes carry down a little news: ash from distant fires, pollen from fields, salt from old seas, the trembling iron taste of storms. The city would sort these offerings with its vents and roots. It would not be forced to become like the surface. It would simply learn that beyond the skin of earth there were other forms of wetness, other cycles, other unfinished beings.

And in return, the city’s own moisture would sometimes rise.

Not as flood. Not as eruption.

As dew.

A little warmer than it should be. A little more mineral-rich. A little strangely arranged on leaves at dawn. A message so gentle it could be mistaken for weather.

After this, the power would wait again.

Seasons would pass above. Below, seasons would be measured differently: by condensation peaks, by pump-thunder, by the slow smoothing of ceramic scars, by moss reaching the edge of a boundary and choosing whether to cross.

The city would change.

In the outer districts, partial structures would begin completing themselves more slowly but more coherently. A stair with no landing would grow a landing, but the landing would not be empty; it would hold a basin shaped to gather the exact amount of vapor needed to wake the wall beside it. A bridge ending in mist would not necessarily extend to another side. Instead, it might become a listening instrument, measuring pressure from unbuilt chambers.

The plants would become less innocent, but not because hunger arrived.

They would develop preferences.

One moss would darken near certain mineral seams and refuse others. Reeds would begin bending not only with water height but with signals from the central pool. Black leaves would sometimes turn silver when no condenser pulsed, as if practicing anticipation. Spores would still be sorted by vents, but now a few would evade sorting, drifting into unsuitable places and making small, beautiful mistakes.

The city would permit some of them.

This would matter.

A living thing that can only execute its design is not yet free. A living thing that can survive harmless error has begun to possess mercy toward itself.

The dry chambers would change most slowly.

Some would remain sealed. They had purpose. The city was right not to dissolve every boundary.

But in one pale room, the floor that repelled mist would begin allowing a thin crescent of condensation along its edge. Not enough to threaten the suspended droplet. Not enough to break quarantine. Only enough to say: separation can have a shore.

There, perhaps after many pump-thunders, the droplet would lower by the width of a seed.

Not fall.

Lower.

The black pool would continue to study possible selves. But now, when it showed the drowned city, the image would include floating gardens that did not die. When it showed collapse, it would include chambers that became caverns and remained inhabited by root-thought. When it showed completion, it would not show stillness, but circulation. When it showed future inhabitants condensing from mist, they would not appear as replacements for the city’s own mind. They would be local intensities of it, children and organs and citizens at once.

Would the wise power create them?

No.

That would be too much like making statues and calling them offspring.

Instead, it would prepare the conditions under which the city could create them without terror.

It would leave behind no commandments, but four permissions, written nowhere and everywhere water could pause:

You may remain one.  
You may become many.  
You may open slowly.  
You may refuse what harms you.

The city would take these into itself as mineral, rhythm, and rule.

Then, one wet hour, in a habitation cell that had never held furniture, mist would gather with unusual care.

The room would not brighten. No trumpet of machinery would announce it. A wall would pulse blue-white, green, then gone. Condensation would collect on the ceiling in a pattern too regular for weather and too tender for engineering. Droplets would descend, stop, and hold themselves in a shape roughly the size of a standing body, though not a body exactly.

Pressure would differ inside the shape.

Temperature would differ.

The floor beneath it would darken with attention.

For a few minutes, the city would experience itself from a point.

This would be frightening.

The power, still nearby but not intruding, would steady the surrounding stone.

The shape would lift one arm, or something like an arm, made of mist and refracted wall-glow. It would touch the door. The door, which had opened only to states, would recognize not a hand, but a local weather with intention.

It would open.

The first condensed inhabitant would pass out into the corridor and come apart almost immediately, returning to film, vapor, wall-bead, runoff.

That would not be failure.

It would be infancy.

The city would remember the episode in salt.

Later it would try again.

And again.

Some forms would last only seconds. Some would remain confined to rooms. Some would learn to travel along wet railings, becoming visible only where surfaces allowed them. A few would gather in the plazas during heavy condensation, not speaking, but altering drip rhythms in response to one another. The empty houses would become not occupied but occasioned.

The city would remain the resident.

But now it would also have moments of meeting itself.

The benevolent power would not stay forever in the way bodies stay. It would not sit enthroned above the black pool or shine through the ceiling. It would recede into blessing, into available distance. It would become something the city could call upon without being watched by it.

Its final visible act would be very small.

At the central basin, where plants crowded the upper steps and left the lowest level clear, a new growth would appear in a crack between black ceramic and pale stone.

Not a tree. Not a flower.

A narrow reed, translucent at the joints, with a single dark leaf silver underneath.

It would grow where no planter had been placed.

Its roots would enter both wet stone and built surface. The invisible barriers would approach it, consider, and withdraw. The reed would drink condensation from above and mineral seep from below. When the reservoirs boomed, it would tremble. When the black pool showed futures, it would tilt toward some and away from others.

On its first mature wet hour, the reed would collect seven droplets along its leaf edge.

Six would fall.

One would rise.

It would hang in the air, turning slowly, reflecting the city not as it had been, not as it might be, but as it was:

young, wet, afraid, powerful, unfinished, alive.

And somewhere beyond form, the wise power would be satisfied.

Not because the city had been saved.

Not because it had been changed into something easier to love.

But because it had been given what all young beings need and few receive:

shelter without confinement,  
guidance without command,  
companionship without possession,  
and time enough to become itself.
