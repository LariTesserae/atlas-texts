A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a conqueror, nor as a sun thrust violently into a people who have learned to love the dark.

It would not split the cavern roof and pour daylight into Undergaar, blinding the Rockvorn and bleaching the fungi, declaring, *Be healed.* It would not silence the machines in a single divine gesture, for the machines, however harsh, are woven into the city’s breath. It would not erase the smog, still the tremors, or soften every stone until the Rockvorn forgot the sacred pressure that shaped them.

A wise power would first listen.

It would descend without spectacle, entering Undergaar through the slowest path: through seep-water, through mineral veins, through the deep underground rivers that already know the names of every foundation stone. It would pass beneath the cisterns and aqueducts, through rusting pipes and root-thick cracks, through moss beds and furnace walls. It would learn the city as the Rockvorn know it: by vibration, by pressure, by the memory of strata.

It would listen to the gears.

It would listen to the fungi.

It would listen to the weary boots on stone.

It would listen to the prayers spoken without mouths, resonant in barrel chests and spiracles, murmured into alcoves where ancient rock still remembers fire.

And after a long listening, perhaps longer than a mortal generation, the power would understand that Undergaar is not merely suffering. It is enduring. It is not merely polluted. It is overburdened. It is not merely grim. It is beautiful in a way that asks to be approached with reverence.

So the power would begin with small mercies.

Not miracles that humiliate the hands that have labored for centuries, but mercies that appear at first to be coincidences.

A cistern that had long gone sour would clear overnight, its stagnant skin breaking into ripples as unseen mineral colonies awakened beneath it. The water would taste of iron and cold stone, but no longer of sickness.

A ventilation shaft, abandoned because of a collapse three eras ago, would groan and open by the width of a hand. Then a shoulder. Then a doorway. Through it would come a slow, breathable current from some forgotten deep hollow where pale ferns grew around warm springs.

The worst of the industrial smog would begin to cling to certain new fungi blooming unnoticed along factory walls. Their caps would be black as coal at first, then silver-veined, then green-lit from within. The Rockvorn would discover that these fungi drank poison and exhaled clean dampness. They would cultivate them, not as charity, but as craft.

The benevolent power would leave the knowledge in dreams.

Not commandments. Not scriptures carved by lightning.

Dreams.

A forge-minder would dream of arranging exhaust pipes in spirals rather than straight vents, and upon waking would find the design still glowing faintly in the grit on their workbench.

A cistern-keeper would dream of a song too low to sing, a vibration that caused mineral sediment to settle cleanly from the water. They would hum it through their chest plates, and the water would answer.

A scaffold-builder would dream of root-arches and pressure webs, of supports that flexed with tremors rather than fighting them. The next quake would come, and a new district built in that style would sway like a living ribcage and remain standing.

A child, if Rockvorn children are permitted quiet hours among the mosses, would dream of a cavern beyond the city: vast, warm, full of luminous lichens and blind silver fish. No map would mark it. Yet when the child grew older, they would lead an expedition through a wall everyone had assumed was solid.

The wise power would not remove toil entirely. Toil, in Undergaar, has become language, ritual, identity. But it would loosen the noose around toil’s throat.

It would make rest possible.

Here, perhaps, would be its greatest gift.

In the heart of Undergaar, where the oldest monoliths pierce the industrial decks and the machines never cease their clanging, there would be found a chamber no one remembered building.

It would be discovered after a minor collapse revealed an archway of unworked stone.

Beyond the archway: silence.

Not emptiness. Not deathly quiet. A living silence, deep and warm, like the pause between two heartbeats of the earth.

The chamber would be vast enough to hold thousands, yet intimate enough that a lone Rockvorn could sit there without feeling lost. Its walls would be banded with strata in colors rarely seen underground: blue-black, copper-red, bone-white, green like buried moss. Water would fall in thin veils from the ceiling into mirror pools. The air would be clean. No machines would function inside it, not because they were forbidden, but because every gear brought across the threshold would gently stop, as if invited to sleep.

The Rockvorn would name it in their resonant tongue.

Perhaps something like: **The Held Breath of Stone.**

There they would gather.

At first uneasily. Then gratefully.

Workers would come after long shifts and remove their goggles. Elders would sit with palms on the floor and hear, beneath the stone, not warning tremors but a patient pulse. Those who had forgotten how to speak except in work orders would begin to make low harmonies together, their chests vibrating until the pools trembled with concentric rings.

No sermon would be given.

No avatar would appear.

The wise power would know that the Rockvorn already possess spirituality. They do not need revelation. They need room.

It would give them room.

Over time, Undergaar would change, but not into another place.

The city would remain subterranean, damp, resonant, strange. Its monoliths would still rise through factory complexes. Its aqueducts would still wind through narrow streets. Its people would still wear respirators in certain districts and cloaks woven of moss and mineral fiber. The Rockvorn would still be deliberate, reserved, and strong.

But the air would lose some of its bitterness.

The water would move more often than it stagnated.

The machines would be repaired not only to continue production, but to reduce suffering. Some would be dismantled, not in panic, but in ceremony. Others would be modified until their rhythm became less like chains and more like drums.

Construction would continue, because Undergaar is a city of metamorphosis. But now the rebuilding would follow deeper harmonies. Districts would be allowed to rest before being remade. Old stone would be thanked before being cut. Foundations would be listened to before towers were raised.

And the Rockvorn, who had long believed themselves bound to endure, would slowly rediscover that endurance is not the same as deprivation.

The benevolent power would also touch their memories.

Carefully.

There are secrets in Undergaar’s stone, and not all forgotten things should be dragged into the open. But some memories, buried by catastrophe or shame or simple attrition, would rise like clean water.

A wall in an abandoned tunnel would shed its mineral crust, revealing carvings from an earlier age: Rockvorn without respirators, standing among great fungal groves, their hands lifted not in labor but celebration.

A rusted archive engine would cough to life after centuries of silence, projecting fragments of lost songs used to guide river divers through flooded caverns.

An elder in meditation would remember a lineage of healers, not of flesh alone, but of stone: those who could sense when a cavern was tired, when a district had been over-mined, when the earth required stillness.

These memories would not say, *Return to what you were.*

They would say, *You have been more than this before. You may become more again.*

The wise power would not make the Rockvorn dependent upon it. That would be another form of cruelty.

Instead, it would leave tools.

Living tools.

A moss that glows brighter in poisoned air, guiding workers away from danger before instruments detect it.

A mineral bloom that forms along unstable fault lines, warning builders where not to dig.

Blind white beetles that eat rust from machinery without weakening the metal beneath.

Fungal looms whose fibers can be woven into respirator filters far finer than any previously known.

Crystals that hold vibrations, allowing Rockvorn prayers, songs, and engineering instructions to be stored not in ink but in resonance.

And perhaps, in the deepest aqueduct, a species of pale root that grows toward clean water and recoils from contamination, mapping unseen flows through the rock.

The Rockvorn would study these gifts. Debate them. Improve upon them. Some would mistrust them at first, and wisely so. A benevolent power would welcome suspicion. Trust offered too easily is fragile.

In time, the gifts would become part of Undergaar’s own genius.

No one would say, “A god saved us.”

They would say, “The stone taught us a new method.”

And the power would be content with that.

For wisdom does not require credit.

There would be one more act.

Not public. Not grand. But intimate.

Somewhere in Undergaar there is, inevitably, one Rockvorn who carries more than others. Perhaps an engineer responsible for a failing district. Perhaps a cistern-keeper blamed for sickness. Perhaps a child born with weak spiracles. Perhaps an old meditator who has listened so long to the living rock that they have begun to hear its pain as their own.

The benevolent power would come to that one not as a blazing figure, but as warmth in the stone beneath their hands.

A presence would say, without words:

*You are not the city’s burden.*

The Rockvorn might not understand at first.

So the presence would remain.

Through one breath.

Then another.

It would help them feel the difference between responsibility and guilt. Between devotion and exhaustion. Between sacred endurance and needless suffering.

If they wept, the power would not announce it. Rockvorn tears, if they have such things, may be internal, mineral, silent. Perhaps their spiracles would simply tremble. Perhaps their broad hands would press harder into the floor.

The power would hold them there until they could rise.

Not healed of all sorrow.

But accompanied.

That, too, would spread through Undergaar.

Not as doctrine. As a subtle alteration in how the Rockvorn stand beside one another.

A worker pausing to help another adjust a failing respirator.

An elder ordering a crew to rest before collapse takes them.

A builder refusing to raise housing where the stone feels wrong.

A council allowing silence before decisions, so the living cavern may be sensed beneath urgency.

A child asking why laughter is rare, and no one scolding them for the question.

Then, one day, laughter would be heard.

It would begin strangely, perhaps as a low grinding vibration mistaken for a minor tremor. A few heads would turn. Hands would move toward tools. But then the sound would repeat, joined by another, and another, until a small group of Rockvorn stood beneath a canopy of silver-green fungi, their heavy shoulders shaking, their multifaceted eyes glinting in the dim.

The sound would not be like human laughter.

It would be deeper, older, like stones knocking together in a riverbed.

But it would be laughter.

And the city would hear it.

The machines would continue.

The water would seep.

The fungi would glow.

The monoliths would keep their secrets.

But something in Undergaar’s vast and burdened heart would unclench.

The benevolent power, having done enough, would withdraw further into the background. Not absent. Never absent. Present as patience. Present as possibility. Present as the faint green bloom on a wall where the air has grown dangerous. Present as the unexpected stability of a bridge during a quake. Present as a dream of clean rivers. Present as the conviction, slowly returning, that a sanctuary need not be a place of endless hardship to remain sacred.

It would not save Undergaar from being Undergaar.

It would help Undergaar become more truly itself.

A living city.

A hard city.

A holy city.

No longer merely enduring in the dark, but breathing with it.
