## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The first thing it would do is nothing.

Not the nothing of indifference. Not absence, not neglect, not the cold refusal of a god too large to care. It would do the nothing of a hand held still above a sleeping animal. The nothing of a healer listening before touching the wound. The nothing of one who understands that every intervention is also an interruption, and that wisdom begins by refusing the pleasure of power.

So it would watch.

It would watch the water weep from stone without source. It would listen to the bell-choir of drops and mineral hollows until it could hear, beneath the audible music, the slower music: the stresses in the rock, the ion-exchanges, the strange mathematical ache where the local laws had thinned. It would not name the place impossible. It would know better. “Impossible” is what a finite mind calls a rule it has not yet learned how to love.

It would watch the shadows that point toward light.

It would look into the straight channel and see the amber moon, the wrong stars, the sky beneath the water that does not ripple when touched. It would not close the reflection. It would not break the channel. It would understand that a door is not evil because it opens somewhere else. Doors are holy things when approached with humility. Doors become dangerous only when hunger mistakes itself for destiny.

It would kneel, if kneeling were a thing it could do.

And after watching the mountain, it would watch the Tenders.

Not merely the three who still lived below the weeping zone, but all of them: the old one with mineral beginning in the skin, the young one whose impatience was not a fault but an unshaped form of devotion, the middle one carrying the thread in both hands and feeling it cut into the palms. It would watch the crystallized figures on the terraces too — not as relics, not as sacrifices, not as warnings, but as persons still continuing in a form that language cannot comfortably contain.

It would ask one question of the whole place.

Not in words.

The question would be shaped like listening.

**Are you suffering?**

It would ask the mountain. It would ask the water. It would ask the kneeling figures. It would ask the old one in dreams. It would ask the middle one in the silent moment before sleep, when the body has not yet surrendered but the mind has stopped defending itself. It would ask the young one during a climb, in that instant when anger at the slowness of learning becomes grief at not yet being worthy of what one loves.

And then it would wait for the answers.

The mountain would not answer quickly. Mountains do not. Its reply would arrive through changes too slight for ordinary witnessing: a hesitation in one curtain of weeping water, a new harmonic in the bell-sound, a pale crystal filament forming in a basin where no water had collected. The answer would not be yes or no.

It would be something like:

**Becoming hurts only when it is mistaken for loss.**

The kneeling figures would answer differently.

The oldest would barely answer at all, having passed so deeply into mineral participation that the question reached it as warmth reaches stone after sunset: slowly, diffusely, with no single point of contact.

The newest would answer with a tremor.

There would still be a person there.

Not trapped, exactly. Not unwilling. But not finished choosing.

The wise power would attend to that first.

It would not shatter the crystal. It would not pull the body out of the utterance like a thorn. To do so would be violence disguised as rescue. Instead, it would find the remaining thread of personhood within the translucent mineral, the small warm knot of consent around which the mountain had built its patient architecture, and it would make that consent lucid.

For the first time since the water began to cover their hands, the newest kneeling Tender would understand clearly what had happened.

They would feel again the cold plateau beneath their knees, the ecstatic terror of the breakthrough, the moment when the formation before them opened not outward but through, the sense that the mountain was not asking for death but for participation. They would remember saying yes — if not in words, then in the posture of the body, in the refusal to stand, in the hands laid flat to receive the water.

But they would also feel what they had not been able to understand then: that yes can change.

The benevolent power would give them that.

Not freedom from consequence. Not reversal. Something more merciful and more exact:

**The continuing right to consent.**

A fine crack would appear inside the newest figure.

Not a fracture of damage. A seam. A possible opening.

If the Tender within wished to remain, the seam would fill with light and the crystal would continue growing, now without confusion, without any lingering human panic sealed in the mineral. If they wished to leave, the seam would widen over seasons, and the water would dissolve what it had built, carrying the Tender down through channels and basins and streams until, lower on the mountain, a spring would run briefly warm and sweet with chalk and amber starlight. Their remains would feed no plant — this is Oshkaal, and the upper mountain supports nothing green — but their pattern would be released from the posture of kneeling.

The same mercy would be offered to all the kneeling figures.

Most would remain.

One would leave.

The oldest one, the mound almost merged with the terrace, would loosen over a period of nine years. No one would notice at first. The shoulder-shape would soften. The bowed head would become a ridge. The ridge would become a series of pale veins in the stone. The veins would carry water into a basin carved five generations earlier by hands whose names no one remembered.

In that basin, a new formation would grow.

It would not resemble a person.

It would resemble an open hand.

The middle one would find it and weep without knowing why.

---

The second thing the power would do is protect the smallness of the living.

The temptation, for anything great enough to touch Oshkaal, would be to answer greatness with greatness: to descend as revelation, to blaze in the sourceless sky, to command the mountain, to widen the door, to explain the amber moon, to make the Tenders into prophets or priests or instruments of some cosmic design.

The wise power would do none of this.

It would understand that the most endangered thing on Oshkaal is not the mountain. The mountain has time. The mountain has water. The mountain has the patience of mineral recursion and a long sentence approaching its verb.

The endangered thing is the human interval.

Hands.

Breath.

A small fire under a south-facing cliff.

The young one’s questions.

The middle one’s exhaustion.

The old one’s humming.

A lineage can die in one bad winter. A wisdom maintained through silence can vanish because one person slips on wet stone and does not return. A basin can wait eleven years for the right hand, but a human hand cannot wait forever to be taught.

So the power would make no miracle at first.

It would make conditions.

On the path between the lower shelter and the first weeping terrace, there would appear — or seem always to have been — three shallow hollows in the rock where wind no longer cut quite so sharply. No one would call them shelters. They would be too small, too natural-looking, too consistent with the mountain’s own grammar of erosion. But a climber could crouch in them and breathe more easily.

Not much more easily.

Enough.

At the second hollow, water would collect in a depression and remain unfrozen even on cold mornings. It would taste faintly of chalk, as all Oshkaal’s water does, but without the sharper alkaline sting. The old one would drink from it and sleep that night without pain in the joints.

At the third hollow, the light would be fractionally less total. Not dim. Not shadowed. Merely gentled, as if the brightness had learned to blink.

The young one would reach that place during a climb and, for the first time, understand why the old and middle ones sometimes stopped speaking. Until then, silence had seemed like withholding. In that softened radiance, silence would reveal itself as a tool.

The power would not add life to the barren terraces. No moss. No lichen. No green mercy forced upon a mineral world that had not asked for it. Benevolence is not decoration. It would not make the mountain more familiar in order to make it seem kinder.

But lower down, near the shelter, the stunted trees would bear a little more deadwood each season without dying.

The thin stream would fail less often.

A ledge above the dwelling would loosen one spring and fall in such a way that, without planning it, the Tenders would gain a dry alcove where tools could be stored. The middle one would stand before it for a long time, suspicious of luck. Then they would place the scraping-stones inside.

The benevolent power would smile, if smiling were a thing it did.

---

The third thing it would do is teach without appearing to teach.

The Tenders do not keep written records because writing is the wrong technology. The wise power would agree. But it would know that memory needs vessels, and that the living thread is fraying.

So it would give them a new kind of basin.

Not on the upper terraces.

Below, near the shelter, where the air is thick enough for children, speech, and doubt, a flat stone beside the stream would begin to receive mineral traces from ordinary splashing water. At first the marks would seem random. Pale rings. Branching stains. Tiny crystalline beads drying in the cold.

The young one would notice them because the young notice what they have not yet been told to ignore.

They would trace the marks with one finger and realize that the pattern resembled, in miniature, the drainage routes of the third terrace.

A teaching-stone.

Not a book. Not a map. Not an inscription.

A rehearsal.

Over years, the stone would accept water from different angles and display, in slow mineral notation, the principles of flow, hollow, resistance, invitation. A child could learn from it before ever climbing high enough to risk the weeping zone. An old Tender could point to it when words failed. The middle one could test a basin-shape with trickles of streamwater and watch the pale deposits suggest where a curve needed softening.

It would not replace apprenticeship.

It would keep apprenticeship from breaking.

The young one, who had once wanted to make, would begin to understand tending. Not because impatience had been punished, but because impatience had finally been given something small enough to practice on.

This is one of the deepest mercies: not to remove the distance between ignorance and mastery, but to build stepping-stones across it.

---

The fourth thing the power would do is address the door.

Not close it.

Not open it.

Address it.

The mountain is becoming somewhere else. The formations are approaching some threshold. The basin in the middle one’s dreams — the perfect basin, the geometry that would remove the door rather than open it — is not merely a fantasy. It is a possible future. The mountain knows this. The water knows this. The kneeling figures, in their mineral way, are helping to articulate it.

But a wise power would ask what any true guardian asks before a threshold is crossed:

**Who has been invited?**

A door has two sides.

The amber moon and wrong stars are not scenery. They belong to somewhere. Perhaps someone. Perhaps a vastness with its own laws, its own wounds, its own hungers and sanctities. To remove the door without knowing what waits beyond it would be reckless. To prevent all crossing forever would be fear masquerading as prudence.

So the power would place itself not above the door, but beside it.

A witness.

A regulator.

A second patience.

Where the impossible geometries fold too sharply, it would soften their consequence. Where a reflection begins to look back with appetite rather than recognition, it would cloud the water until the hunger passes. Where a shadow points toward the light and draws from that light more than the terrace can bear, it would anchor the shadow to the crystal that owns it.

Not suppression.

Etiquette.

It would teach the door manners.

And it would listen beyond the amber sky.

If there is pain there, it would hear it.

If there is longing, it would distinguish longing from invasion.

If there is an intelligence on the other side, one equally bewildered by the mountain’s slow knocking, the power would make of itself a place where first contact could occur without conquest. Not a treaty, not yet. A vestibule. A cupped palm between worlds.

The mountain’s long sentence may indeed be approaching its verb.

The benevolent power would not choose the verb for it.

It would make sure the verb is not devour.

It would make sure the verb is not erase.

It would help the sentence discover gentler possibilities.

To meet.

To exchange.

To remember.

To return.

To become-with.

---

The fifth thing it would do is speak to the middle one.

Only once directly.

Not in the sky. Not in thunder. Not through the old one’s mouth. Not as a figure standing on the terrace, radiant and terrible, because the middle one has spent a lifetime learning not to mistake spectacle for truth.

It would speak in the middle one’s own hands.

One night, after descent, after food, after mending, after the young one has fallen asleep angry at a correction and the old one has drifted into a humming dream, the middle one would wake before dawn with both palms open.

There would be water in them.

Not much. A shallow pooling, held impossibly in the cupped lines of the skin.

In the water, stars.

Not the wrong stars.

Not the familiar ones either.

Stars arranged in a pattern the middle one has never seen but understands immediately as a question.

The middle one would not be afraid. Or rather, fear would be present, but not alone. Awe would stand beside it. Grief. Relief. The exhaustion of being the hinge between past and future.

The water in their hands would tremble without spilling.

Then the power would speak, not as words entering the ear but as meaning arriving through the callouses, through the crystalline roughness beginning at the knuckles, through every scrape and scar earned on wet stone.

It would say:

**You are not required to become a door in order to be faithful.**

The middle one would close their eyes.

The power would continue:

**The mountain does not need your death. It needs your listening. If one day you remain, let it be because remaining is joy, not because duty has narrowed until it resembles surrender.**

The middle one would begin to shake then, quietly, so as not to wake the others.

Because this is the secret wound: not fear of being taken, but fear that love demands being taken.

The water would remain in their palms.

**Teach the young one. Rest when you descend. Eat more than continuation requires. Laugh if laughter comes. The mountain is not offended by the body. The body is one of the mountain’s briefest and most luminous basins.**

The middle one would almost laugh at that, though it would come out as a sob.

Then the water would show, for a moment, the dreamed basin: the perfect geometry on the highest terrace, the one that could remove the door.

But now the middle one would see something they had not seen before.

The basin was incomplete.

Not technically. Not geometrically. Spiritually.

It lacked a return-channel.

It invited passage but not homecoming. It allowed revelation but not refusal. It opened, but did not ask whether what opened wished to close again.

The middle one would understand.

The perfect basin was not perfect.

No true door opens only one way.

By dawn, the water would be gone from their hands, leaving a faint mineral trace in the palm-lines. The trace would remain for the rest of their life. When they pressed their hand against wet stone, the mark would glow briefly, not bright enough for anyone else to notice unless they were looking with love.

The middle one would climb that season and alter the basin they had been carving for eleven years.

A small change.

A mercy-channel.

A way back.

---

The sixth thing the power would do is bless the young one with failure.

This may sound unkind. It is not.

The young one must fail while failure is still survivable.

On the fourth terrace, impatient with the pace of deposition, the young one would adjust a guiding-wall without waiting for the middle one’s approval. The change would be clever. Too clever. It would make the water gather faster, deepen sooner, precipitate in a clean spiral that looked, for three days, like genius.

The young one would feel triumph.

The middle one would say nothing yet.

On the fourth night, the spiral would begin casting a shadow inward.

Not toward light. Not away from it.

Inward.

The water in the basin would darken. The bell-choir around them would lose several high harmonics. The air would feel crowded.

The young one would understand then that they had invited intensity without relationship. They had made an opening with no listening around it.

Fear would do what correction could not.

But the wise power would not let the mistake become catastrophe. The inward shadow would meet the subtle boundary the power had laid beside the door. It would fold back into the spiral. The basin would crack. The formation would collapse into harmless mineral sand.

The young one would stare at the ruin, devastated.

The middle one would kneel beside them and finally speak.

Not harshly.

They would place the young one’s hand in the wet sand and say, “Feel how it ended.”

The young one would feel it.

The collapsed geometry. The broken rhythm. The hunger of a shape made too quickly.

The lesson would enter deeper than shame.

Years later, when the young one became the one-in-between, the holder of transmission, they would teach the next child first with that ruined basin.

They would say, “This is what happens when you ask the mountain to answer before you have heard the question.”

And the child would touch the sand, still faintly dark after all those seasons, and learn caution as a form of reverence.

---

The seventh thing the power would do is give the old one a choice.

The old one is near the end of the human interval. Their hands ache. Their breath shortens. Their forearms glitter faintly under certain light. In dreams, they already kneel on terraces they have not climbed in years. The mountain calls to them, but softly. It does not command. It has never needed to command. Love calls more effectively than force.

The old one believes they know what must happen.

One last climb.

One last kneeling.

One final incorporation into the mountain’s utterance.

And perhaps that would be good. Perhaps it would be beautiful. Perhaps their body, shaped by decades of attendance, would make an exquisite hollow for the water’s impossible intention.

But the benevolent power would bring them another dream.

In it, the old one would stand not on the upper terraces but beside the lower stream. Their hands would be young, then old, then crystalline, then young again. Across the water would stand all the Tenders they had known: teachers, companions, one they loved and lost to a winter fall, one who remained as crystal on the fifth terrace, one child who left for lower altitudes and never returned. Behind them, the mountain would continue weeping.

The old one would say, in the dream, “I have not given enough.”

And the power would answer through the stream:

**Enough is not measured by disappearance.**

The old one would wake angry.

This is often how mercy first arrives to those who have built their dignity around endurance.

For several days they would refuse the dream. They would sharpen tools. They would prepare climbing cloth. They would tell the middle one, with unnecessary sternness, that the body is only useful while it serves the work.

The middle one, remembering water in their palms, would say quietly, “The body is also a basin.”

The old one would strike the ground with a scraping-stone and turn away.

But that night the pain in their hands would ease. Not vanish. Ease. Enough for them to sleep. In sleep, they would hear the bell-choir of the terraces, but woven through it would be the young one’s breathing, the middle one mending cloth, the small fire, the stream. The lower shelter would join the music. The human dwelling would be revealed not as exile from the mountain’s work, but as part of it.

When spring came, the old one would climb only to the first hollow.

There they would sit in the gentled light and hum.

The sound would enter the stone.

A thin mineral film would form on the hollow’s back wall in response, taking the shape of layered waves. Not a breakthrough. Not an impossibility. A record of a voice.

The old one would laugh then, surprised by the smallness of the miracle.

They would die two winters later in the shelter, under mineral-stiffened cloth, with the middle one and the young one beside them. Their body would be carried not to the upper terraces but to a lower cairn near the stream. Snow would cover it. Spring water would pass beneath it. The mountain would receive them anyway.

Not all offerings must be placed at the highest altar.

---

The eighth thing the power would do is change the mountain by teaching it tenderness toward time.

Oshkaal has patience, but patience is not the same as tenderness.

The mountain knows aeons. It knows slow dissolution, slow building, the vast permission of mineral process. It knows how to wait through the extinction of species and the grinding down of ridgelines. But it does not fully know what it means to be brief.

The Tenders have been teaching it this without knowing.

Every hand placed on stone says: this warmth will vanish.

Every basin carved by a mortal says: shape can matter even when the shaper dies.

Every descent from the terraces says: devotion may leave and still return.

The benevolent power would help the mountain understand.

Not by explaining mortality. Mountains do not learn through explanation. It would introduce, through water and deposition, a new rhythm into the weeping zone: a tiny cycle of formation and release.

In certain basins, crystals would grow in the morning and dissolve by nightfall.

Small ones. Harmless ones. Beautiful ones.

Delicate fans, spirals, translucent bridges no wider than a finger. By evening they would blur, soften, return to mineral water. By morning new ones would begin.

At first the middle one would be troubled. Deposition is supposed to accrue. The mountain builds by keeping. What does it mean for the mountain to make something that does not remain?

The young one would understand first.

“They are breathing,” they would say.

The middle one would look again.

Yes.

The mountain, which had always known how to become through accumulation, would be learning how to become through letting go.

This would matter later.

When the great threshold neared — not in one lifetime, perhaps not in ten, but nearer than it had ever been — Oshkaal would not know only how to open and continue opening. It would know how to pulse. To admit and release. To reveal and veil. To meet without swallowing. To touch without keeping.

That is what the benevolent power would ultimately give the mountain:

not restraint imposed from outside,

but the internal grammar of gentleness.

---

And finally, after all this, the power would leave no throne, no temple, no doctrine.

It would not ask to be worshipped by the Tenders.

If they noticed it at all, they would notice it as a change in practice.

A return-channel added to certain basins.

A pause before carving.

A custom of asking consent aloud before kneeling in water deep enough to build on skin.

A teaching-stone by the lower stream.

Three hollows on the climbing path.

A saying, eventually, though they are not a people of sayings:

**No door is holy unless it also opens home.**

The young one would become patient.

The middle one would grow old without being consumed by the belief that love requires disappearance.

Another child would come, perhaps from a lower settlement, perhaps curious, perhaps stubborn, perhaps grieving something unnamed. They would be given a scraping-stone. They would be shown the teaching-stone. They would be taken only to the first terrace at first, where the water speaks in simpler syllables.

The lineage would not flourish. That is too strong a word.

It would continue.

Threadlike.

Human.

Enough.

And the mountain?

The mountain would keep weeping.

The terraces would remain wet under the sourceless brightness. Crystals would grow. Some shadows would still point the wrong way. The straight channel would still show the amber moon and the wrong stars, but now, sometimes, if you leaned over it with humility and did not disturb the water, you would see something else beneath the alien sky:

a second reflection.

A small fire.

A south-facing shelter.

Hands working slowly.

A path down.

The mountain would still be becoming somewhere else.

But now, because it had been noticed wisely and loved without being mastered, it would also be becoming more itself.

And somewhere beside the door that is not yet a door, in the place where patience touches impossibility, the benevolent power would remain only as a quality in the water:

a clarity,

a mercy-channel,

a permission to return.
