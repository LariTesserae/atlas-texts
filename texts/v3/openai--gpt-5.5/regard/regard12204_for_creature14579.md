## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive as weather.

That would be the first mercy.

It would not come down in a visible column, not with trumpet-light or shadow or any gesture large enough to frighten the rodents back into their crevices. It would not split the sky. It would not make the pillars bow. It would not warm the thin air into comfort, because comfort here is not neutral; comfort would be an invasion. It would not fill the pools and make them permanent. It would not green the plateau.

A foolish kindness would bring rain.

A wise kindness would understand that rain, here, is not merely water. Rain would be a catastrophe disguised as generosity. It would soften the sandstone too quickly. It would drown the lichen. It would call plants that do not belong and insects that would multiply beyond the discipline of the place. It would make the rodents bold, the raptors hungry, the crevices unstable. It would teach the people to expect what the land cannot continue to give.

So the power would not give abundance.

It would give continuity.

It would come, if “come” is the right word, as an alteration too small to accuse. A pressure adjustment. A pause in the wind. A slight change in the way dawn sits on the pillar caps.

For a long time, it would only listen.

Not with ears. Ears are too local for a place like this.

It would listen through the stone’s contraction at night, through the clicking mouths of lizards, through the movement of pale rodents in the dusk slots, through the way the original people shift their weight before a wall begins to weep. It would listen to the rusted cables singing above the alleys. It would listen to the generator building and the open steel door and the bolt holes where heavy machinery once insisted on permanence and failed. It would listen to the boot at the edge of the drop.

It would listen until it understood the difference between suffering and severity.

Because the Altiplano is severe. That is not the same thing as cruel.

The power would know this.

It would know that to remove all hardship from the plateau would be to remove the plateau. The thinness of the air, the unreliable water, the strange acoustics, the dense broken architecture of sandstone and shadow — these are not flaws in need of correction. They are the body of the place. To “heal” them away would be annihilation with soft hands.

So the power would ask, in the only ways such a place can answer:

What here is wound?

What here is merely form?

And the plateau, being no mind but still a coherence, would answer structurally.

The wound is not the thirst.

The wound is the poison left in the thirst.

The wound is not the ruin.

The wound is the unfinished taking.

The wound is not the later ones.

The wound is that they no longer know whether staying is choice, exile, habit, or spell.

The wound is not the acoustic strangeness.

The wound is when the echoes steal too much from the mind and give nothing back.

The wound is not death.

The wound is forgetting without transformation.

So the power would begin there.

Quietly.

First, beneath the generator building, it would find what industry left where no hand now reaches: the old spills, the metal salts, the residues sunk into dust and hairline cracks, the thin chemical ghosts of extraction. It would not scoop them out with force. That would collapse walls and expose old scars. Instead it would bind them.

A mineral here.

A crystalline preference there.

A change in valence, a patient persuasion.

The toxins would become less available to water. Then unavailable. Then locked into inert seams so unremarkable that a geologist might call them natural. The condensation that collected on the steel ribs of the roof would taste, one season, faintly less bitter. The later ones would notice but not speak of it until the echo returned clean.

In the slot where children of the original people sometimes drink from a shallow basin, the power would deepen nothing. It would not carve. But it would teach the stone to remember a curve it had nearly formed anyway. A grain would loosen here; another would hold there. Over five years, a pool would begin lasting one hour longer after the sweats. Not a miracle. Not enough to change migration, diet, birth, death. Only enough that an old woman who had once crawled too far for water would not have to crawl quite so far again.

The power would do this in many places.

Never enough to make the plateau other than itself.

Always enough to reduce waste.

Where condensation used to bead and fall into dust, it would find a lip.

Where a channel diverted water away from lichen through some recent fracture, a pebble would lodge in just the right place.

Where a pillar-top basin lost everything to one crack, that crack would fill with windblown mineral and seal halfway.

The people who were already there would notice.

Of course they would notice.

They notice the pressure change before the sweats. They notice the difference between a lizard’s ordinary stillness and its fear. They notice which stones have shifted in the night by the altered pattern of morning shade.

They would not call it blessing. They might not call it anything.

One of them, perhaps a narrow-shouldered child with violet showing under the skin after wetting her hands, would place her palm against a wall that had wept longer than expected. She would make a tone, low and unsemantic, shaped by gratitude but not reducible to it.

The power would receive this.

It would not answer in words.

It would answer by not taking credit.

That would be another mercy.

For the later ones, the power’s kindness would be different.

They are not woven into the plateau in the same way. They are caught on it. Their gentleness is real, but so is their diminishment. The place has worn them smooth, yes, but a stone can be smoothed by a river and also trapped beneath it.

The power would not decide for them.

It would restore the possibility of decision.

One night, when the tick-tick-tick of cooling stone spread across the dark like an enormous unwound clock, the cables would stop singing.

Not forever. Only for one night.

The later ones would wake because of the absence.

Inside the generator building, without the high vibration threading their bones, they would hear their own breathing plainly for the first time in years. No echo would precede it. No wall would return it altered. Their thoughts would stand upright.

One would weep.

One would laugh once and then cover his mouth, startled by how singular the sound was.

One would walk to the steel door and put both hands on it, feeling the cold that had gathered there.

In that brief quiet, the power would offer each of them—not command, not vision, not rescue, but offer—a map.

Not on paper.

A map in the body.

The memory of the southern rail spur, corrected. The safest descent through the least unstable clefts. The timing of shade. The location of three water events likely to occur within two days. The old path beyond the edge where the ground lowers, where air thickens, where ordinary sound returns.

Along with the map would come another knowledge:

You may go.

You may stay.

If you stay, stay awake.

If you go, go whole.

By morning the cables would sing again.

The plateau would resume its strange acoustics. The generator room would once more speak slantwise. But something would have changed. Not in the place. In the hinge between person and place.

The later ones would begin making preparations without saying what they were preparing for.

They would repair a condensation trap.

They would sort bone needles from rusted wire.

They would wrap dried grass and strips of rodent meat in a sheet of old canvas.

They would stand at the mouth of the generator building at dusk and look not only outward, but south.

Perhaps two would leave.

Perhaps one would turn back after three kilometers, not from fear, but from recognition: no, the plateau has become the shape of me, and I choose it.

Perhaps another would descend all the way and reach a settlement below, where sound behaves, where water stays in pipes, where people ask too many questions. That one would live. Not easily. But truly. Their lungs would ache in the thick air. Their dreams would remain full of ticking stone. They would sometimes pause mid-sentence, waiting for an echo that never came.

Still, they would live.

And because the power is wise, it would not require that every mercy look like departure.

For those who stayed, it would make one more adjustment.

Not to the acoustics as a whole. The acoustics belong to the plateau. But inside the generator building, at the height of a seated human head, along the back wall where the illegible painted word had become only a chemical shadow, it would alter the receiving surface of the concrete by a fraction. A roughness here, a smoothing there.

After that, when someone spoke from the center of the room, the first returning echo would come from the true direction.

Only the first.

Only indoors.

Only faintly.

But enough.

Enough that conversation could begin with reality before negotiating with distortion.

Enough that the later ones, if they wished, could remember the shape of ordinary address.

The power would also attend to the dead.

Not by raising them. A wise power does not treat death as an error merely because it is sad.

The bodies in the slots, pressed into crevices by those who knew how to return a person to stone, would be left undisturbed. The calcium transactions would continue. The downward movement would continue. The canyons would carry what they carry.

But where remains had been abandoned without intention—where industry’s haste, accident, or despair had left someone neither buried nor remembered—the power would complete the grammar.

A scatter of bones beneath a collapsed ledge would settle deeper after a frost. A boot at the edge of the drop would remain, but the leather would soften one season into the dust instead of persisting as accusation. A name, if there had been one still held anywhere in the world, would occur one afternoon to a distant descendant washing a cup at a sink. They would not know why. They would pause. They would feel an unaccountable tenderness for someone they had never met.

That would be enough.

The power would not erase the industrial artifacts.

This matters.

There is a kind of benevolence too eager to clean. It would remove the rail ties, dismantle the cables, seal the generator room, carry away the ceramic insulator, and call the absence restoration.

But the plateau does not need innocence. Innocence is not the same as health.

The industry happened. Its remains are part of the moral geology now. To erase all trace would be to lie on behalf of the wound.

So the power would make the artifacts safe, not absent.

The cable clamps, rusted nearly through, would fail in a windless hour rather than during a storm, dropping the line into an empty slot where no one walks. The steel would settle there and sing lower, becoming habitat for lichen and shadow insects.

The rail spur’s last ties would remain at the edge, blackened and hard, but the drop beyond would loosen a rim of stone, making the terminus visibly dangerous from a distance. No child, original or later, would mistake it for a path.

The generator door would finally fall, but inward, gently, one cold dawn after everyone had stepped outside. It would land flat and become a table, then a sleeping platform, then a surface on which condensation gathers in a thin, drinkable skin.

The illegible word on the wall would not be restored.

Some words deserve to fade.

But beneath it, mineral bloom would form slowly in branching pale lines, not letters, not a message, only a pattern resembling water remembering roots.

The original people would see it and understand it as part of the wall.

The later ones would see it and perhaps understand it as forgiveness.

Both would be right enough.

Then there is the matter of the two peoples.

A foolish power would unite them.

A vain power would teach them one another’s languages and call the result harmony.

But separateness is not always failure. Distance can be a form of respect. The original people are not lonely simply because they are few. The later ones are not wrong simply because they arrived through damage. The plateau holds both, but not in the same way.

So the power would not force encounter.

It would create one place where encounter could happen without demand.

There is, near the center of the Altiplano, a narrow corridor between sandstone pillars where the wind produces a low hum felt first in the sternum. The sound is unsettling because it seems almost intentional. There, the power would change nothing visible. It would only tune the corridor by means of erosion already underway.

A flake falls.

A pebble shifts.

A capstone cracks along an existing line.

Over seven years, the hum becomes clearer.

Not louder. Clearer.

At dusk, during the first strong sweat after the cold season, the corridor begins to do something no other place on the plateau does: it carries tone without distortion.

Not speech. Speech still bends, fragments, returns from wrong angles.

But tone passes cleanly.

The original people discover this first. One stands at one end and sings a water-syllable, nearly without meaning, and another hears it exactly.

Later, one of the generator people, following rodents at dusk, hears the tone and stops.

The original singer stops too.

For a while there is only wind.

Then the later one, whose language is clipped and broken by waiting, makes a sound not from words but from breath. A note. Awkward, dry, human.

The corridor carries it.

The original person hears it as it was made.

Not as plea. Not as trespass. Not as claim.

Only as sound honestly offered.

That becomes the beginning of a custom so small it might not deserve the name.

Sometimes, when the sweats begin, one or two from either people come to the corridor and make tones through it. They do not exchange goods. They do not merge. They do not explain themselves. They simply allow the other to exist within accurate hearing for a few moments.

In a place where echoes lie by habit, accuracy becomes intimacy.

The power would cherish this, but it would not enlarge it.

The wise do not pull on seedlings to hurry trees.

There would be larger protections too, but they would happen far from the plateau, in rooms with poor air and polished tables.

A mining interest would misfile a survey.

A satellite image would blur at the wrong season under a veil of high ice-cloud.

A government archive would classify the region as geologically unstable, culturally sensitive, economically marginal.

An investor would dream of teeth falling from his mouth and wake with an inexplicable aversion to sandstone.

A road proposal would fail by one vote because someone’s child got sick that morning and they stayed home.

Not curses. Not punishments.

Deflections.

The plateau has already endured one phase of extraction. The power would not allow another merely because maps are hungry.

If scientists came with humility, the power would not stop them. If a physician came to help and learned first how not to trample, the power would guide their feet. If a lost traveler wandered near death through the pillar alleys, the power might gather shade an hour longer, might bring a lizard clicking across their path toward a seep, might set in their mouth the metallic taste that means water soon.

But if machines came to open the plateau again, their bolts would not hold.

Their engines would misfire.

Their measurements would contradict one another.

Their radios would fill with their own voices returning half a second early until even the most practical among them felt watched by consequence.

Eventually they would leave.

The report would say: unsuitable.

The power would accept this word.

Over decades, its work would become indistinguishable from the place.

That is how one knows the intervention was wise.

The lichen would thicken by a millimeter in shaded crevices where it had been failing.

The wiry grass would survive two drought years that might once have ended it.

The thorn shrubs would flower once every nine years instead of once every twelve, not extravagantly, only enough to keep their line unbroken.

The raptors would still hunt.

The rodents would still die.

The pillars would still fall.

The people would still thirst, age, bury, listen, mishear, and become stone by degrees.

Nothing would be saved from being itself.

But many things would be spared from being harmed beyond themselves.

And the plateau—the being that is not a being, the coherence dense enough to feel like attention—would continue lowering, grain by grain, into its own future.

Perhaps after a long while, one of the original people would make a new arrangement of stones near a weeping wall. Not an altar. They do not build altars. But three flat pieces would lean together in a way that catches dawn light and holds a thumb’s-depth of water.

Perhaps a later one, old now, would sit beside the fallen generator door and speak a sentence without waiting for the echo.

Perhaps someone who left would return once, carrying no tools, no flag, no claim—only a small vessel of water from below. They would pour it into dust, not because the plateau needs water from elsewhere, but because gratitude sometimes takes the shape of an unnecessary gift.

The power would not appear to receive it.

But that night, the tick-tick-tick of cooling stone would sound, to those awake enough to hear, very slightly less like a clock that had forgotten the hour and more like a clock that never needed hours at all.

The Altiplano would remain unwelcome.

It would remain unrejecting.

It would remain full.

And somewhere within its packed stone grammar, a mercy would have been added so carefully that it did not stand apart from the sentence.

A new note in the old chord.

Slightly consonant.

Absorbed.
