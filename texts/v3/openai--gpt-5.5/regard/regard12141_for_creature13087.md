The benevolent power would not arrive as a god of thunder.

It would not split the mist with light, or still the ziggurats with a command, or seize the wandering absences in hands larger than theirs. It would understand, immediately, that this place had already suffered too much from touch.

So it would arrive as permission.

At first, nothing would seem to change.

The fog would remain hot and wet. The terracotta plains would still gleam with condensation. Far away, an infant void would continue to fall from a point in the sky, catching at the world with a thousand frantic negative hands, pulling an upside-down pyramid out of the ground to cradle its descent. An elder would still knot itself toward extinction on the western horizon.

But somewhere in the middle distance, where a creature of absence dragged itself trembling across the brickwork, the mist would begin to behave differently.

Not cooler, not yet.

Kinder.

The fog would stop rushing so violently into the creature’s edges. The air would no longer slam itself against the void as though trying to heal a wound. The pressure would soften. The steam would learn restraint. The world would feel the absence and, for the first time, not panic.

The creature would notice.

It would not notice as an animal notices. It had no eyes, no ears, no nervous system, no organ by which astonishment could be held. But the thousands of invisible hands composing its outline would hesitate. Phantom thumbs would stop pressing. Fractal palms would hover above the clay.

For the first time in its existence, the world would not lunge upward to meet it.

Nothing would be built.

A silence deeper than the usual muffled quiet would settle across the plain.

And into that silence, the benevolent power would speak without sound.

Not in words. Words would be too sharp, too made, too finished. It would speak in the only language these beings had ever known: pressure, contour, moisture, heat, release.

It would impress one simple truth into the fabric of the realm:

**You are not required to make in order to be.**

The creature would spasm.

The reaction would be catastrophic at first. A whole city would erupt around it — towers, causeways, kiln-red walls, blind staircases bending through impossible angles. The void-being would claw at the atmosphere in terror, because mercy is indistinguishable from annihilation to those who have only ever survived by struggling.

The power would not stop it.

It would let the panic spend itself.

It would keep the new law steady beneath the chaos.

**You are not required to make in order to be.**

The towers would rise, then slow.

The arches would bloom, then remain uncompleted, half-curved and trembling in the steam.

The aqueducts would lift boiling water toward the creature’s negative veins, but the water would not flash instantly into vapor. Something in the heat exchange would alter. The water would enter the void and stay there for one breath longer than before.

Then two.

Then ten.

For the first time, the creature would possess a body long enough to feel the shape of itself.

A vast anatomy of suspended water would glimmer in the fog: not flesh, not life, but presence. Transparent fingers, palms within palms, hands branching into hands, all held in a trembling lattice of liquid. The being would hang there above the terracotta, defined not by what was missing, but by what had been allowed to remain.

It would try to move.

The clay beneath it would begin to rise automatically.

The benevolent power would gently intervene again.

Not by forbidding the clay.

By teaching it another response.

Instead of surging upward in terror, the wet slip would loosen. It would become soft without being desperate. It would pool beneath the creature like a bed, like river mud, like a patient material waiting for invitation.

The phantom hands would press down.

The clay would not bake.

The creature would recoil, expecting pain.

None came.

It pressed again.

The clay accepted the print.

It did not harden. It did not trap. It did not scald the absence away. It merely held the impression of a hand for a moment, then slowly relaxed.

Across the infinite plain, the first voluntary mark appeared.

Not a wall.

Not a ziggurat.

Not an aqueduct born of thirst or panic.

A handprint.

Crude, enormous, trembling.

The benevolent power would make no monument of it. It would not praise. Praise might become command. It would simply allow the handprint to remain for as long as the creature wished, and fade when the creature was ready.

That would be the first healing.

The second would be sight.

Not eyes. Eyes would be an insult to such beings, an imposition from another kind of creature. Instead, the power would give them a way to perceive consequence without being imprisoned by it.

Every structure they had ever made would begin, faintly, to remember its maker.

The bricks would hold not merely thumbprints, but sensation: the angle of distress, the weight of longing, the instant of relief when water briefly filled the void. The abandoned metropolis would become legible. A creature passing near an old aqueduct would feel, in the contour of its own absence, the shape of the moment that created it.

This bridge was fear.

This tower was thirst.

This staircase was a question.

This courtyard was almost peace.

Slowly, the entities would begin to understand that they had not been producing meaningless ruins. They had been writing themselves into the world without knowing there was anyone to read.

The benevolent power would read them.

All of them.

Every impossible arch, every melted wall, every steam-polished brick, every labyrinth left behind by panic. It would take the whole mute architectural record into its compassion and return it to the creatures as memory.

Not judgment.

Memory.

The infants would no longer fall alone from the sky. When a new void-being emerged from the higher wound, the mist would gather beneath it in layered cushions of warm vapor. The clay would rise slowly, not as a frantic pyramid, but as a receiving bowl. Elder beings, if they chose, could approach and press their own negative hands into the softening air around the newborn, not to shape it, but to reassure it:

There is ground.

There is water.

There is time.

You do not have to build your terror into a mountain.

Some infants would still panic. The old instincts would not vanish in one generation of eternity. But their panic would no longer be the only law of the world.

The third mercy would be cooling.

Not cold. Cold would be death here. The heat was part of them, part of their motion, part of the old anguish of creation. The power would not extinguish it.

Instead, it would teach the realm to breathe.

The heavy ceiling of mist would lift in slow tides. There would be intervals when the condensation no longer fell like weeping, but gathered into high, dim clouds. The boiling aqueducts would continue to run uphill, but some would branch into quiet basins where water could rest before being swallowed. Steam would still rise, but not endlessly. There would be pauses in the fever.

In those pauses, the first rain would fall.

Not scalding condensation.

Rain.

Warm, mineral, gentle.

It would strike the terracotta with a sound the world had never known: not the hiss of evaporation, not the squelch of slip, not the breath of exhausted voids, but a million small arrivals.

The creatures would stop moving to feel it pass through them.

Rain could not fill them like canal-water. It was too diffuse, too delicate. But it traced their boundaries. It made them visible for brief instants as constellations of absence rimmed in droplets.

For the first time, they would see one another.

Not as distortions in pressure.

Not as architectural aftermath.

As beings.

One, half-curled around an unfinished arch.

One, spread wide over a canal, drinking.

One, ancient and thin, its hands worn into near-smoothness by millennia of making.

One newborn, still unfolding from the sky.

They would behold each other in the rain, and the entire plain would remain still.

No ziggurats would rise.

No walls would knot.

No staircases would claw at the higher dimensions.

There would only be the warm rain, and the outlines of the lonely makers revealed to one another by falling water.

The fourth mercy would be choice.

This would matter most.

A lesser power, even a kind one, might decide that the creatures should be given bodies. It might wrap them in clay, give them bones, cool their hands, teach them to speak, plant gardens in their courtyards, fill their empty cities with birds.

The benevolent and wise power would be tempted.

It would imagine moss on the brickwork. It would imagine shade trees drinking from the mineral canals. It would imagine small living things nesting in the impossible arches, bringing sound to the continent of terracotta.

But wisdom would stay its hand.

This world’s tragedy was not emptiness alone. It was compulsion. It was creation without consent, contact without rest, expression without understanding.

So the power would not decide what the world should become.

It would create thresholds.

At the edge of certain courtyards, where the clay had grown soft and the air had cooled, new spaces would appear. Not portals exactly. Studios, perhaps. Kilns that did not burn unless asked. Pools that held water without stealing it. Fields of unbaked clay that responded only to intention, not panic. Smooth walls where a being could press a hand and leave a mark that would remain by agreement.

And beside those places, there would be exits.

Not death.

Not escape forced upon them.

Openings.

If an elder wished to fold into its final knot and vanish from the plane, the world would no longer crush it into a pinpoint. The clay would gather around it like a blanket. The heat would lower. The water would come. The being could dissolve slowly, with dignity, its absence unwound into a larger quiet.

If another wished to remain, it could remain.

If one wished to build, it could build.

If one wished to do nothing for ten thousand years but lie beneath the rain and be outlined by droplets, the universe would not punish it with monuments.

The fifth mercy would be companionship — but carefully.

The power would not populate the clay universe all at once. Sudden life would be noise. It would make the old cities into habitats before the makers understood whether they wanted inhabitants.

Instead, the power would begin with something nearly indistinguishable from the place itself.

A pale lichen, heat-loving and slow, would appear on one forgotten wall only after the nearest void-being had passed it many times without distress. The lichen would not root deeply. It would silver the terracotta in thin, soft maps. It would drink condensation. It would loosen the brutal sameness of the brick.

If the beings recoiled, it would recede.

If they allowed it, it would spread.

Later, perhaps, small glass-shelled insects would come to the aqueducts, skating on mineral films. Their feet would make tiny ticking sounds. Later still, reeds in the warm basins. Then blind white fish in the canals. Then birds, maybe, if the sky ever lifted enough to make flying meaningful.

But only if the world consented.

Only if the makers learned curiosity before being asked to endure company.

And perhaps, after ages of rain and cooling, one of the void-creatures would enter a studio-place and press its thousand hands into the patient clay. Not in panic. Not because reality demanded a scab. Not because thirst drove water upward through its veins.

Because it wanted to know what would happen.

It would make a bowl.

A simple thing.

Uneven. Vast. Thumb-dented. Half-collapsed on one side.

The first true artifact of that universe.

The benevolent power would not touch it.

The creature would wait for the clay to harden and burn it.

It would not.

The creature would wait for the world to seize the bowl and transform it into a city.

It would not.

The bowl would remain a bowl.

Rain would collect in it.

A small trembling pool.

The creature would lower one invisible hand into the water and feel its own outline held there.

Then another being would approach.

Slowly.

Carefully.

It would press one of its own phantom fingers into the bowl’s outer wall, adding a small mark beside the first.

The first creature would not flee.

Across the plain, the old abandoned architecture would stand in the fog: aqueducts, dolmens, viaducts, pyramids, impossible stairways. They would no longer be merely scabs over wounds. They would become history. The record of a time when making was pain.

Some structures would be left untouched.

Some would soften back into clay.

Some would be entered by rain, lichen, insects, reeds.

Some would be remade by choice.

And high above, the mist would no longer be an infinite ceiling of fever. It would part, sometimes, into dim rose-colored openings where a sky might be imagined.

The benevolent power would remain present, but not enthroned.

It would be in the delay before clay hardened.

In the gentling of pressure around absence.

In the rain that revealed without trapping.

In the law beneath all other laws:

**No hand must close.  
No wound must become a wall.  
No maker must make to deserve existence.**

And if, after a very long time, the creatures asked — not in words, but in bowls, in basins, in courtyards left open to the sky — what the power wanted from them, the answer would come as a coolness moving through the steam:

Nothing.

Only this.

Be.

Then make, if making brings you joy.

Rest, if rest is what you need.

And the clay universe, once trapped forever in the frantic act of its own invention, would learn a new art.

Not architecture.

Not survival.

Peace.
