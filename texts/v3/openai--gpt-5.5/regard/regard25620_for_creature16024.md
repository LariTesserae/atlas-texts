A benevolent and wise power would not begin by improving the place.

It would begin by listening.

Not with ears only, because ears would be overcome at once: bats in their black thousands, crickets like a field of needles, claws on salt, pellets clicked into place, wings shaking dust from limestone ribs. It would listen beneath sound, to the ratios and pauses: the interval between root-fall and beetle arrival, between guano and fungus, between hunger and pursuit, between death and redistribution. It would listen to the architecture breathing through termite chimneys. It would listen to the old salt moving by measures too slow for grief. It would listen until it understood that this place was not deprived in the simple way outsiders might think.

There is no water here.

But absence is not the same as error.

The power would understand this before acting. It would understand that to pour water into the underplain would be catastrophe disguised as mercy. A stream would be a blade. A pool would be a plague. Moisture, given crudely, would wake molds too fast, soften towers, dissolve streets, drown eggs, collapse salt crusts, call down roots unprepared for abundance, invite creatures from above that had never learned restraint. The dry republic would become rich for a season and then rot.

So the power would not make a river.

It would not make lamps in the roof, nor crystals in the walls, nor fruiting orchards where the root-curtains hang pale and austere. It would not teach the ants language. It would not give the termites blueprints. It would not turn the foxlike hunters gentle, nor spare every hare from every snake. It would not silence the crickets, though their song could split thought into threads. It would not cleanse the guano vaults of their sharpness. It would not tidy the bones.

Wisdom would forbid extravagance.

Benevolence would forbid neglect.

So the power would kneel, if such a power had knees, at the rim of the basin and place one invisible hand upon the salt floor.

It would feel the entire city as one being.

Not a single mind, not a nation, not a kingdom, not a machine, but a woven endurance: appetite braided into architecture, fear into route, waste into garden, instinct into civic form. The place would be alive in the manner of a dry seed: folded, conservative, severe, preserving futures in hard casings.

And the power would decide to help it remain itself.

First, it would guard the entrances.

Not with a wall. Walls invite doors, doors invite keys, keys invite ownership. Instead it would bend chance.

A surveyor’s instrument would fail near the surface fissures. A mining company’s map would misalign by a few harmless degrees. A drilling proposal would be delayed by litigation, then forgotten in a change of administration. A curious hiker would feel, without knowing why, that the crack in the limestone was unstable and not worth entering. A profiteer dreaming of salt extraction would find richer deposits elsewhere. Scientists might come, once or rarely, but only those patient enough to crawl softly, touch little, and leave less.

The place would not be sealed from knowledge. Secrecy can become another form of death. But it would be protected from appetite masquerading as discovery.

Second, the power would steady the roof.

Not freeze it. The cavern must continue its ancient negotiations with gravity. Collapse is part of its grammar. New light enters through breaks; old districts die under falls; root groves begin where stone has opened. To prevent all change would be to embalm the city.

But there are collapses that belong to time, and collapses that come from violence above: blasting, aquifer loss, road cuts, machines shaking old limestone with careless thunder. These the power would divert. It would thicken a pillar by the width of a moth’s wing each year. It would draw stress away from the largest roost. It would let small stones fall where small stones were due, and hold back the single catastrophic shelf that would crush the Tower Field before its century.

It would practice restraint even in protection.

Third, it would tend the water that is not seen.

A foolish mercy gives abundance. A wise mercy improves circulation.

So in the hours before dawn, when the upper rock exhales its faint coolness, the power would persuade a little more vapor to pause among the root hairs. Not enough to drip. Not enough to shine. Only enough that the living cores of the hanging roots would last through one more hard season.

In certain cracks it would encourage mineral skins to form in shapes that catch breath. In certain lichen beds it would strengthen the black-green crusts that bind dust and hold invisible moisture against the stone. It would guide a few surface plants to send their roots deeper, not in conquest, but in treaty. It would teach no mind, speak no command, yet the roots would find the old fissures by pressure and darkness, by salts and need.

Once in many years, during a season when all signs already leaned that way, the power might allow a fog from the world above to enter through the chimney chain.

Not rain.

Never rain.

Only a gray softness descending like a rumor.

The bats would grow restless. The crickets would raise their cry until the cavern seemed made of vibrating wire. The root-curtains would darken by a shade. Rosettes would loosen their curled leaves. Dormant fungi would taste the air and remember the work of eating. Eggs would soften in their cases. Seeds that had waited in dung, dust, and old clay would wake, not all of them, only enough.

By morning there would be no visible wetness.

But for years afterward, certain gardens would be larger.

Fourth, the power would preserve the economy of death.

It would not make predators merciful, because the city depends on terror as much as on dung. The hare must fear the fox-thing; the beetle must hurry before the ant; the bat must sometimes fall; the snake must sometimes feed; the termite breach must sometimes be found by lizards. Without pursuit, there is no pruning. Without loss, no redistribution. Without bodies, the dry republic starves.

But the power would prevent wasteful death.

A sickness from the surface, carried in by one wandering mammal, would fail to cross the warm boundary of a roost. A fungus too virulent for the termites would mutate toward patience. A poison spilled far above would bind to clay before reaching the roots. A human boot carrying foreign spores would crack before the descent, turning its owner back in irritation. A new predator from outside, strong in wet caves but ignorant of dry law, would find no breeding success here.

The old deaths would remain.

The new annihilations would be refused.

Fifth, it would bless the small intelligences without changing their kind.

The ants would not become philosophers. They do not need philosophy to be exact.

But their trails, in years of scarcity, would find forgotten stores more often. Their scent lines would persist a little longer in adverse dust. When a colony split, one daughter stream would turn toward viable ground rather than sterile salt.

The termites would not awaken to architecture as humans know it. They already know architecture by mouth, temperature, and repair.

But when the air grew wrong in a tower, one worker would open a vent half a day sooner. When a bridge needed thickening, more grains would arrive. When a dead root could become a column, they would plaster it before collapse.

The bats would not be made noble. They are already magnificent in their quarrelsome multitude.

But their seasonal departures would align with flowering above often enough to bring seed back below. Their young would cling more securely during the great liftings. Their roosts would shift away from the one shelf that someday must fall.

The fox-things would remain thieves.

But a mother would find the warmer abandoned pocket in a hard year. A litter would be smaller when food was scarce. Hunger would sharpen them, but not drive them to ruin the nests they needed next season.

The crickets would sing.

No power with wisdom would improve the song of crickets. It would only allow the cracks to remain numerous enough for answering.

Sixth, it would make memory out of matter.

This city has no archive but habit. No library but trail, no law but repeated need, no monument except structures that are also stomachs and nurseries and vents.

The power would not impose writing.

Instead, it would deepen inheritances already present.

A path used by a thousand generations of ants would harden against one unlucky fall of dust. A plaza polished by the sleep of hoofed bodies would hold its smoothness through a dry century. Root groves that had become markets of species would be spared from simultaneous failure. The fungal gardens would keep, in their hidden lace, more ways to digest what famine leaves behind. The mineral crusts would remember how to begin again after being scraped.

The city would remain unknowing.

But it would be less easily made forgetful.

Seventh, the power would arrange for witnesses.

Not many.

A place too witnessed is consumed.

But once in a long while, someone from above would enter and return changed. Not with specimens enough to empty a district, not with maps precise enough for exploitation, not with photographs that turn mystery into appetite, but with reverence. A biologist, perhaps, who had come prepared to classify and left prepared to defend. A child lost only briefly, found near the entrance with dust on their hands and a lifelong tenderness for small lives. A poet who never named the place. A policymaker who dreamed for years afterward of pale towers breathing in the dark and quietly opposed the road that would have cracked the basin roof.

The power would let the underplain enter human imagination as a restraint.

A reminder that not all cities have citizens with faces.

That not all architecture is intention.

That poverty of water can still be wealth of relation.

That life, when pressed hard enough by absence, does not always become simple. Sometimes it becomes intricate beyond praise.

Finally, the power would give one gift directly to the place itself.

Not light.

Not water.

Not speech.

A margin.

A margin is the holiest gift for a harsh ecosystem. Not rescue from difficulty, but a little space between difficulty and extinction. A fraction more resilience in the roots. A fraction less poison in the dust. A fraction more stability in the roosting vaults. A fraction more luck in breeding years. A fraction less intrusion from the world above.

Enough that when the century turned dry, the city would contract but not vanish.

Enough that when the roof opened a new wound, life would find the light.

Enough that when a tower fell, its body would become food, shelter, mineral, route.

Enough that the ancient exchange could continue:

bat to guano,  
guano to fungus,  
fungus to beetle,  
beetle to lizard,  
lizard to fox,  
fox to bone,  
bone to floor,  
floor to salt,  
salt to tongue,  
tongue to body,  
body to dust,  
dust to wall,  
wall to city.

And perhaps the power, having done almost nothing visible, would remain nearby in the manner of underground things: not enthroned, not worshiped, not named, but present as a pressure toward continuance.

The insects would not know.

The bats would not know.

The roots would not know, though they might thicken in the dark.

The fox-things would sleep in their wall pockets, tails low, ears twitching. The hares would chew with solemn faces beneath the pale curtains. The termites would place pellet after pellet in the blind patience of their kind. The ants would carry seeds, larvae, dead sisters, nothing visible at all. The crickets would occupy the air with their bright dry insistence.

The basin would remain cool, crowded, austere, and alive.

No miracle would be recorded.

No river would run.

No jewel would ignite in the limestone roof.

But somewhere, during the hour when the colder light entered the chimney chain and the roots glowed faintly with stored starch, a seed that should have died would split.

A worker termite would seal the crack that mattered.

A bat would turn left instead of right and carry a dust-colored seed in its fur.

A lichen crust would hold one breath of moisture until dawn.

And the city without tools would continue building itself from hunger, darkness, waste, and care.
