The benevolent power would not arrive as a blaze.

That would be its first wisdom.

For Glistengut is a city of dimness, and its people are not children lost in darkness. They are a civilization of darkness: adapted to it, dignified by it, shaped by it, beloved by it. To flood their halls with sudden daylight would be violence disguised as blessing. To split the caverns open to the sky would be to erase the long patience of generations who learned to read wet stone, fungus-glow, echo, current, and mineral breath.

So the power would come quietly.

It would enter first as a change in the water.

Not a miracle anyone could name. Not at once.

Only this: the bitter seep in the Lower Tiers would taste clean again. The children who drank from it would stop coughing in their sleep. The gray film that had begun creeping over the glow-fungus beds would recede. The aqueducts would run with a clearer song, and the waterwheels would turn more smoothly, as though the underground rivers had remembered an older rhythm.

The cave-tenders would notice first.

They would kneel among their pale mushrooms and whisper to one another, touching the caps with scarred fingers. The fungi would glow a little warmer now — not brighter, exactly, but fuller. Their blue-green radiance would gain faint notes of pearl, amber, and rose, subtle enough not to pain the Cave-Dwellers’ eyes, but varied enough to let artisans distinguish colors previously known only as words in old stone-songs.

Then the power would listen.

It would listen to the ring of pickaxes, the groan of rope bridges, the muttering prayers of miners entering unstable shafts. It would listen to the dreams of the weary, to the silent resentment of those worked too hard, to the pride of master carvers, to the fear of mothers whose children were sent too young into narrow tunnels.
