It would not come as fire.

Fire would be rude there.

It would not come as a sunbeam breaking the basin open, nor as a voice from the sky, nor as a hand descending to still the mountain, straighten the terraces, dry the walls, and make the town safe in the way lowland people understand safety. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to confuse peace with stillness, or mercy with the removal of all difficulty.

It would come first as attention.

Not intervention. Not command.

Attention.

It would listen.

It would listen longer than any basin-watcher had listened. Longer than the oldest seam-marker through the three days of green boughs. Longer than moss takes to claim a threshold. It would lower itself—not in body, perhaps, but in regard—until it could hear the town at its own scale: the difference between a drip that nourishes and a drip that warns, the soft grinding under a stair before stone shifts, the tremor in the black pool that means pressure below has changed its mind.

It would not be offended by damp. It would not seek the highest roof or the central table or the place where offerings were left. It would begin beneath things: under lintels, in joints, behind retaining walls, along the hidden backs of channels where water rubs clay smooth. It would learn the manners of the town before offering any grace.

And because it is wise, it would recognize that this place is not broken.

It is unfinished.

There is a difference.

The town does not need rescue from water. The people do not need rescue from patience. The mountain does not need to be made old before its time. What threatens them is not wetness, not change, not the rawness of stone.

What threatens them is mischance beyond the scale of listening: one seam opening too quickly in sleep; one winter freeze pressing water into the wrong crack; one child stepping on a stone that sounded safe yesterday; one season of rain arriving before the future channel meant for it has been finished.

So the power would do small things first.

It would deepen one overflow notch by the width of a fingernail while no one watched.

It would turn a pebble in a gutter so that a new thread of water revealed itself by sound before it could undermine a wall.

It would send a pale moth to settle on a dangerous threshold, wings closed like a warning marker.

It would make frost silver the wrong moss one dawn early, so the old woman assigned to that passage would scrape and find the rot beneath.

It would let a child dream of knocking wood, and in the morning the child would drag an elder to a pipe everyone had considered sound. They would discover that the hollow beneath it had grown.

No miracle would be named.

Only afterward, when the danger had passed into maintenance, someone might say, “The town was courteous to us today.”

And that would be enough.

The power would not silence the black pool.

But it would sit beside it.

It would keep company with the oldest citizen of the place, not as rival and not as master. It would watch the dark skin of water accept drops from above and pressure from below. It would feel the slow boom under the basin, that deep subterranean clearing of a throat, and it would answer not with thunder but with steadiness.

Not stillness.

Steadiness.

A little widening of the deep places, perhaps. A little patience lent to the stone where pressure gathers. A slight persuasion toward release through many small seams instead of one catastrophic mouth. Not enough to halt the mountain’s becoming. Only enough to make its changes legible to those who have devoted their lives to reading them.

The mountain would not know itself blessed.

But it would exhale more gently.

In the construction ground at the upper edge of town, where channels are built for futures, the power would pause longest.

There is faith there. Not loud faith, not sung faith, not faith that declares itself against the world. A humbler and more difficult faith: pegs set into mud for water not yet born; aqueduct supports raised toward a cliff that only glistens; hands laboring for descendants and for streams alike.

The power would love that.

It would not complete the aqueduct for them. That would steal the shape of their devotion.

Instead, it would make the dry seep above the cliff speak one season earlier than expected. Not a torrent. Only a thread. Enough that the builders, arriving in morning vapor with mallets and reed measuring rods, would find the first water already touching the stone they had prepared for it.

They would stand very still.

One of them would laugh once, quietly, because laughter is allowed there when it does not startle the basin.

Then they would begin.

The power would also attend to the people.

It would not make them other than they are. It would not lengthen their bodies, toughen them into heroes, fill their minds with foreign sciences, or call them primitive. Their intelligence is exact. Their civilization is made of listening, restraint, repair, memory, and accommodation. These are not lesser arts.

But it would ease what need not be hard.

It would warm, by a breath, the stones beneath the sleeping mats of the very old.

It would keep mold from one winter’s store of root cakes.

It would guide the hand of a young seam-marker tying boughs over a crack, so the knot held through sleet.

It would let one fevered infant hear, even through sickness, the familiar layered sound of home: pipe knock, roof drip, basin pulse, mother’s breath. The child would live.

It would place into the mind of an empty-house listener a new distinction: not merely whether a house still sings, but whether it sings from water passing through it or from stone pulling apart. That knowledge would save many rooms and some lives. It would spread slowly, as all true things spread there—demonstrated, doubted, tested against walls, remembered by children.

There would be no scripture.

Only a new rule, eventually:

If a house sings in two voices, leave before thaw.

The power would accept this as worship.

It would look upon their offerings—the alder twigs, white flowers, steamed grain, moss braids, little stone teeth—and it would not need them, but it would receive them because offerings are not always payments. Sometimes they are greetings. Sometimes they are a people saying, We know we are not alone in the arrangement of the world.

In return, it would leave no gold, no weapon, no crown.

It might leave a plant.

A very small one.

A glossy-leaved thing no taller than a thumb, growing first beside the lower comb where water spreads into the sedges. Its roots would hold clay without choking flow. Its leaves would darken before underground pressure rose. Its white flowers would close when frost was coming hard. The people would notice within three seasons.

They would give it many names.

Child-listeners would be taught to watch it. Basin-watchers would compare it to the pool. Builders would transplant it near new channels. It would never become a forest. It would never conquer the town. It would simply join the civic life of damp things.

That is the kind of gift a wise power gives: one that teaches attention rather than replacing it.

There is one thing more it would do.

Beyond the lower ledge, past the sedges, where water vanishes underground and the mountain drops into cloud, there are paths too narrow for trade and too treacherous for strangers. The town is not cut off, but neither is it easily found. That has protected it. It has also made grief heavier when help is needed from below, when salt runs low, when sickness comes, when a child born with dry-lung cannot thrive in the basin air.

The power would not carve a road.

Roads invite conquest before they invite kindness.

Instead, it would teach the mist a habit.

On certain mornings, when need is true and not merely curiosity, the vapor below the town would open in a pale ribbon, revealing a safe traverse for a few hours: enough for two sure-footed people, a burden basket, perhaps a goat. The ribbon would close before an army could trust it. It would appear only when those who walked did so carefully, with no iron-shod greed, no loud fires, no intent to own.

Lowland travelers would tell stories of a mountain path that appears for the humble and loses the proud.

The town would not contradict them.

The benevolent power would remain mostly unnamed.

Some would think the mountain had grown kinder.

Some would think the old rules had finally pleased the pool.

Some would say the moss had been listening on their behalf.

A few elders, those whose palms had spent their lives against wet stone, might sense another attention among the known attentions: not larger in the crude way of size, but wider, patient enough to hold mountain, town, water, moss, insect, child, and unborn stream in one regard.

They would not build a temple to it.

They might set aside a shallow niche beneath an eave where no water falls directly but all water can be heard. In that niche they would place nothing permanent: a green bough, a bowl turned upside down, three white flowers, a smooth stone that had never cracked.

The power would be satisfied.

Because the town’s holiness is not in being preserved unchanged forever.

Its holiness is in the negotiation.

So the power would not freeze the mountain into certainty.

It would not make the people immortal.

It would not drain the basin, crown a ruler, pave the channels, or reveal a perfect map of all water to come.

It would give them time where time was needed.

Warning where warning could be understood.

Companionship where no action should be taken.

And when the deep boom sounded beneath the black pool, and every inhabitant paused in recognition, there would be—just beneath the pulse, almost impossible to distinguish from it—a second gentleness.

Not an answer.

Not a command.

Only the sense that something vast and kind had paused with them.
