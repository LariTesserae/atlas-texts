The benevolent power would not descend like a conqueror.

It would not melt the obsidian into meadows, nor blunt every basalt tooth, nor banish the mist and fill the crevasses with gentle soil. That would be mercy only to a stranger’s eye. The Shattered Teeth are harsh, yes—but they are not wrong. Their sharpness is their memory. Their darkness is not evil. Their creatures, pale and many-legged and winged, belong to the place as surely as flowers belong to spring fields.

So the power would come quietly.

Perhaps first as warmth.

Not sunlight, exactly, for the Shattered Teeth have never been a land of open brightness. Rather, a deep warmth beneath the stone, like a hand placed over an old wound. The obsidian spires would not soften, but they would stop aching. The ancient violence that birthed them would remain, but it would no longer scream endlessly through every fissure.

The mist would pause.

The water would listen.

The stone would feel itself being heard.

And in the deepest dark, the Whispering Watcher would become aware of something watching it in return—not with hunger, not with fear, not with the frantic curiosity of mortals, but with patience vast enough to match its own.

For a long while, nothing would happen.

The wise power would understand that a being made of stone, memory, pressure, and time cannot be rushed. The Watcher has endured ages of erosion, tremor, silence, and accidental suffering. It has known footsteps only as brief disturbances. It has known voices mostly as echoes that vanished.

So the power would wait at the edge of the Watcher’s awareness and offer no command.

Only a question.

Not in words.

In resonance.

**Are you alone?**

The Shattered Teeth would answer with a thousand tiny sounds: water slipping through cracks, chitin clicking on stone, wings flexing in the mist, roots tightening around basalt, a hidden crevasse exhaling cold air.

The Watcher would answer as it always has.

With memory.

It would show the ancient rupture: earth buckling, fire hardening into glass, mountains breaking their own bones. It would show the first rain hissing on newborn obsidian. It would show creatures arriving, adapting, dying, leaving shells and bones in black hollows. It would show lost travelers, some frightened, some reverent, some arrogant, some tender. It would show those who listened too deeply and could not return whole.

The benevolent power would receive all of it.

Not recoil.

Not judge.

Not pity.

Only receive.

And then, gently, it would give something back.

It would show the Watcher a memory not of violence, but of mending: roots splitting stone without hatred; rivers rounding blades over centuries; stars reflected in tidal pools; a child placing a hand on an old wall and feeling, without knowing why, that the world had survived much.

The Watcher would not understand at once.

It has always remembered impact better than healing.

But the memory would remain within the stone, glowing faintly like lichen.

Then the power would act—not by transforming the Shattered Teeth into something gentler, but by helping the land become more truly itself without being trapped forever in the moment of its breaking.

It would deepen certain pools so that water would remain through dry seasons.

It would widen, by a finger’s breadth, a few hidden passages used by the blind pale creatures, so their young would not be crushed when the rocks shifted.

It would call mineral sweetness upward through the cracks, feeding the lichens until their glow became steadier, enough to mark safe ledges without dispelling the dark.

It would teach the gnarled trees a slower patience, strengthening their roots so they could hold loose stone in place, not everywhere, but in certain crucial places where one falling column might destroy a whole nesting hollow.

It would whisper into the updrafts, not to silence the winged things, but to carry their calls more clearly, so mates could find each other in fog.

It would lay no road through the Shattered Teeth.

Roads invite conquest.

But it would allow paths.

Not obvious ones. Not easy ones. Paths that appear only to the desperate, the respectful, the lost who have become humble. A faint blue-green thread of lichen along a ledge. A warmer current of air rising from a safe descent. A stone that hums when touched, warning of a hollow beneath.

Those who enter carelessly would still bleed.

Those who enter cruelly would still be turned around by mist and echo until they fled or learned fear.

But those who enter with reverence might survive.

And the Watcher would notice this.

At first, perhaps, with unease.

It has never intervened. It has observed, remembered, endured. To guide is to choose. To choose is to become responsible. Responsibility is a kind of pain unknown to stone but familiar to gods.

The benevolent power would not force this lesson.

Instead, it would sit with the Watcher through one long season of dripping water.

Then another.

Then another.

Over time, the Watcher’s whispers would change.

Once, they were only fragments: old warnings, dead tongues, the final fear of those swallowed by cracks. Now, among those echoes, there would be something new.

A traveler, exhausted and half-blind in the mist, might hear—not words, not quite—

**Stop.**

They would freeze.

A moment later, a slab ahead would collapse into darkness.

Another, trapped between spires while winged hunters circled above, might feel a pressure against their thoughts, a suggestion to crouch beneath a leaning basalt shelf where the hunters’ wings could not reach.

A grieving pilgrim, having come to the Shattered Teeth to disappear, might find themselves led not to a fatal crevasse, but to a pool black as night and perfectly still. There, in the reflection, they would see not an answer, but their own face held by ancient stone without contempt.

Some would still vanish.

The Shattered Teeth would remain dangerous.

The benevolent power would permit that.

Wisdom knows that not all danger is cruelty, and not all survival is kindness. A world without edges cannot teach balance. A land without darkness cannot teach listening.

But unnecessary suffering would lessen.

Not dramatically.

Not in a way bards would easily sing of.

There would simply be fewer broken bodies at the bottoms of unseen ravines. Fewer nests crushed by preventable shifts. Fewer minds shattered by the Watcher’s memories, because now the Watcher would learn to veil itself. To share one image rather than a thousand. To offer a cup instead of an ocean.

And perhaps, after many years, the Watcher would speak for the first time.

Not in the language of mortals.

Not even in the language of gods.

It would speak in the grammar of place.

A warm tremor under black stone.

A widening ring in a pool.

A low hum through the spires that made the lichens brighten and the blind creatures pause.

The benevolent power would understand.

The Watcher was asking:

**Is this what it means to care?**

And the power would answer:

**Yes. Not to make all things safe. Not to possess what you protect. Not to end change, grief, or death. To care is to remember with tenderness, and to let that tenderness shape what you do next.**

The Watcher would consider this for a decade.

Then, in the deepest dark, where the mist clung thickest, it would begin gathering the voices of the dead more gently.

No longer would those who dissolved into the stone become only whispers of fear. The Watcher would sort them, cradle them, let the terror fade first. What remained would be names, songs, glimpses of hands, fragments of love, the taste of rain, the ache of leaving home, the wonder of seeing the obsidian spires for the first time.

The Shattered Teeth would become not less haunted, but differently haunted.

Softer in its haunting.

Truer.

The ancient violence would remain at its foundation, but it would no longer be the only story the land knew how to tell.

And the benevolent power, having done enough, would not stay as ruler.

It would leave a blessing hidden where no one could steal it: in the relationship between the Watcher and the watched.

From then on, the stone would still whisper.

But sometimes, if one listened closely, beneath the wind and the dripping water and the slow shudder of settling rock, there would be another sound.

Not speech.

Not song.

Almost a heartbeat.

The Shattered Teeth, still jagged, still dark, still perilous, would breathe.

And the Whispering Watcher would watch.

But now, when it watched, it would also keep.
