The power would come quietly.

Not as a star falling, not as a voice over the whole plateau, not as a hand that lifts the old land and remakes it into something kinder by another world’s measure. A wise power would see at once that kindness here could become ruin if it were too large.

To give the plateau deep rivers would drown its law.  
To raise forests would break the wind and darken the mats.  
To silence the insects would starve half the mouths beneath the sky.  
To make the people masters would make them strangers.

So it would do almost nothing at first.

For a long time it would only attend.

It would learn the low grammar of the place: the swelling of the bladder-plants before storm, the tightening of thorn-felts in hard drought, the old water-memories written in rings, the routes of herds, the waiting eggs, the sealed frogs, the insect nations rising like sparks from dampness. It would listen below listening, where roots answer pressure and fungus tastes the mineral ghost of stone. It would discover that the plateau is not unfinished. It is not a failed forest, not a poor mountain, not a wasteland awaiting rescue.

It is an old thought held close to the ground.

The power would understand that the being who lives there is not one body but a continuity: mat, lichen, insect, hoof, wind, shelter, path, corpse, rain. The floor itself is alive in a way no single creature can be. Not conscious as a human elder is conscious, perhaps; not prayerful, not speaking in images. But aware by response. Remembering by growth. Judging by what it closes over and what it lets remain.

The power would love it for that.

And because it loved it, it would not make it grander.

It would make it more itself.

First, it would touch the water.

Not by summoning permanent lakes or sweet rivers that ran all year, but by giving the old basins a little more mercy. Here and there, beneath the plant-skin, stone would crack along ancient weaknesses. Not much. Only enough that after rain, a few hollows would hold dampness one day longer than before. A seep that had been failing for generations would begin again, not as a spring anyone could own, but as a darkening under root-curtains, a place where birds gathered before people noticed.

In the driest years, condensation would thicken along the hairs of certain leaves. Dawn would come silver. Children sent out with shell cups would return with more than expected. Elders would say the fog had remembered them.

The power would touch the storms next.

Not to tame them. Storms are among the plateau’s oldest instruments, and to soften them too much would make the thunder-flowers forget when to open. But the worst winds—the killing winds that arrive wrong, hot and low, when the mats are newly swollen and full of young—would shift by a hand’s breadth in the sky. Lightning would still strike. Floods would still roar brown across the basins. Burrows would still drown, eggs would still fail, animals would still die.

But not all at once. Not everywhere.

Disaster would become patterned enough that life could answer it.

Then the power would touch the people, though lightly, because people are easily bent by gifts.

It would not give them metal in abundance.  
It would not teach them engines.  
It would not place a temple of shining stone on the horizon.  
It would not say: You are chosen above the beetle, above the mat, above the grazer.

Instead it would enter their dreams as practical things.

A child would dream of placing her foot on a mat that hummed before it sank, and in the morning she would refuse a path that would have taken three hunters into a hollow death.

An old woman would dream of cutting a bladder-plant at the wrong season and seeing bitterness spread through a whole basin. She would wake with a new harvesting rule in her mouth.

A maker of nets would dream of a knot shaped like a beetle’s jaw and devise a binding that held better in wet weather.

A grieving father would dream of his dead son lying not beneath stone, not outside the living world, but in a ring of red-black lichen where the first green hairs touched him gently. From that dream would come a new rite, and the people would fear death a little less.

The power would not announce itself as the source. It would let the knowledge arrive as if from the plateau, because in truth it did.

The people would begin to say that some dreams were “wet-under”: not bright, not prophetic in a grand way, but carrying hidden water.

They would honor such dreams cautiously. They would test them. The wise power would approve of this. Faith without testing would not belong here.

It would also give them one new plant.

Only one.

A low thing, of course. Nothing tall. Nothing that could dominate the mats or choke the basins. It would appear first in the seam of an old stone pavement after rain: gray-green, tight-leaved, with roots fine enough to drink fog and a bitter rind that insects disliked. Its inner flesh would be clean and faintly sweet. Not abundant. Never easy. But it could be cultivated on shelter roofs and along scraped stone ribs, where other plants struggled.

It would save people in lean seasons without freeing them from the plateau.

They would name it something like breath-root, or dawn-flesh, or the plant-that-does-not-boast.

The power would make sure it had limits. If planted too thickly, it would fail. If hoarded, it would rot. If carried to the lower lands, it would become tasteless and weak. It would remain a gift only inside the old conversation of height, wind, fog, and stone.

That is how a wise power gives: with boundaries.

Then it would turn to the many-bodied floor.

This would be the hardest work, because the floor does not ask in words. Its suffering is not like human suffering. It does not cry out when grazed, burned, eaten, buried, or torn; those things are part of its life. Its pain is imbalance. Too many mouths in one basin. Too much dryness for too many years. A disease crossing from one mat-colony to another without interruption. People burning at the wrong time. Herds forced by thirst into places that cannot recover.

So the power would not spare the floor from being wounded.

It would teach the floor to fail in smaller pieces.

A blight that once might have run for miles through connected mats would find sudden breaks: bitter seams, resistant patches, rings of lichen that refused it passage. Thorn-felts would vary more from basin to basin. A grazer could no longer strip one valley and move to the next identical valley; each place would answer differently. Diversity would deepen, quietly, underfoot.

The plateau would become harder to ruin by accident.

Predators would feel the touch as well.

Not mercy in the sentimental sense. The flat ambushers would still take calves. The low-running packs would still harry the weak. The wide-winged hunters would still cast their slow shadows. But a sickness burning through the pack animals would lose some of its teeth. The largest birds would lay fewer eggs in years when small grazers failed. The scavengers would find carrion quickly, before rot poisoned water pockets.

Nothing would be removed from hunger. Hunger is one of the plateau’s oldest languages.

But hunger would be kept from becoming madness.

The power would especially watch the rare thickets.

Those upright places, those little refusals of flatness, would need protection precisely because they seemed minor. The browsers depended on them. The people read springs by them. Birds nested there. Shade gathered there, and under shade certain small beings lived nowhere else.

So the power would hide some thickets in plain sight.

From a distance they would look like ordinary dark smudges on stone. Herds would pass near them without noticing unless truly thirsty. People would find them only by old songs or by following particular insects at dawn. Lightning would strike near them but seldom into them. Their seeds would travel inside one shy grazer’s gut and germinate only where stone, seep, dung, and shadow met correctly.

No paradise. Only continuance.

In time, the people would notice changes, but slowly.

A basin remembered by their grandparents as unreliable would begin holding frog-song every third wet season.

A path once abandoned because the mat had become treacherous would grow a firm rib of reddish vine, safe if crossed before noon.

The thunder-flowers would bloom not more often, but more precisely.

Condensation would gather on the roof-thatch of occupied houses, not enough to be called miracle, but enough for a waking hand to wipe moisture into a child’s mouth.

There would be fewer years when every infant born in the dry months died.

There would be fewer years when insects devoured all stored seed.

Not none. Fewer.

That would be the signature of the power: not perfection, but fewer.

Fewer needless endings.  
Fewer broken chains of return.  
Fewer deaths caused by ignorance rather than by the ordinary bargain of living.

And perhaps, once in a generation, the power would allow itself one visible act.

A storm would come too early, when many people were crossing an open basin for a gathering. The sky would flatten. The ankle-wind would turn cold. Insects would rise all at once from the mats, not in panic but in a shape: a dark, shifting band across the safe stone-rib, like an arrow made of wings.

The people would follow it.

Behind them, the basin-floor would liquefy under the first brown sheet of floodwater.

Or a child lost at dusk would hear the clicking of frogs where no water should be, follow the sound, and find a root-curtain wet enough to keep her alive until morning.

Or a great mat-fire driven by wind would meet, inexplicably, a wide green seam that would not burn. Later the elders would find that heavy grazers had torn the seam open months before, and rain had filled it, and a certain plant had rooted there. Miracle, yes, but made entirely of ordinary things arranged with mercy.

The people would argue about this.

Some would say the plateau had a hidden elder.

Some would say the wet-under dreams came from ancestors.

Some would say no, there was no single will, only the old floor answering attention with attention.

The power would not correct them.

Names matter less than conduct.

If they began building upward in pride, the wind would remind them. If they overharvested the dawn-flesh, it would fail. If they honored the basins, the basins would answer. If they laid their dead back into the living mat with gratitude, children would dream more clearly for a while.

The covenant would remain ecological before it became religious.

Nothing still belongs to itself alone.  
Everything given must return.  
Height is not holiness.  
Permanence is not victory.  
To endure is to stay in conversation.

The wise power would come to love the plateau’s noise. At first the insect chorus would seem like chaos even to a being vast enough to hear storms before they formed. But eventually it would hear structure there: the dry rattles of waiting, the wet clicks of emergence, the wing-hum of brief abundance, the deep throat-pulses of hidden amphibians, the warning whistles of pack hunters, the sleep-songs of children in low houses.

It would understand that this place does not need silence to be sacred.

It is sacred as a vibrating skin.

And perhaps the being of the floor—the immense, low, many-lived thing—would in its own way notice being noticed.

Not suddenly. Not with speech.

But one dawn, after a night of light rain, every condensation hair across a hundred miles would hold its bead a moment longer before drinking it inward. The droplets would tremble in the first sun. The whole plateau would shine, not brighter than nature permits, but with an uncanny simultaneity, as if the living ground had opened all its small eyes at once.

The power would receive this as greeting.

Then the beads would vanish. The insects would rise. The grazers would lower their heads. Smoke would thread from a crouched shelter. A child would test the mat with a pole. Somewhere, under a ledge, pale roots would drink fog from the breath of sleeping animals.

The power would remain.

Not above the plateau like a ruler.

Within its chances.  
Beside its recoveries.  
Under the difference between death and extinction.  
In the extra day of water.  
In the dream that teaches a safer path.  
In the plant that feeds but cannot be hoarded.  
In the predator’s hunger stopping short of waste.  
In the mat’s ability to close, but not too quickly over memory.

It would not save the place from being itself.

It would help it continue being itself, more deeply and more mercifully, for as long as stone remained high, wind remained warm, and rain still came like a command across the ancient, living floor.
