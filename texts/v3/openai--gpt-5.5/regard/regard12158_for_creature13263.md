It would not come as a sun.

A lesser power might have done that: cracked the violet murk with gold, driven shadows back, burned the malachite vines to harmless ash, commanded the hum to silence and called the act mercy.

But a benevolent and wise power would understand that light, in the wrong place, can be a blade.

So it would come first as attention.

Not a footstep. Not a voice. Not a throne descending through the thin sky.

Only attention.

Vast, patient, and tender.

It would behold the plateau in its entirety: the obsidian-riddled granite fingers still thrusting upward by increments too slow for grief and too painful for stone; the rubbery green vines winding through fissures like veins searching for a heart; the chartreuse ferns glowing where no light should reach; the ruins scattered without memory of arrangement; the archway standing in a field of shattered columns like a sentence whose language had died before it was completed.

It would listen to the hum.

Not resist it. Not judge it. Not flee from the pressure of it.

It would listen until the grinding resolved into layers.

At the surface: agony.

Beneath that: labor.

Beneath that: confusion.

Beneath that: a question repeated for longer than stars had names.

Am I becoming?

Am I ending?

Am I alone?

And when the hum shifted into the deep resonant pattern of titanic gears, when the air thickened and the ruins trembled and the Watchers turned their featureless faces toward the disturbance that was not a disturbance, the power would do nothing sudden.

It would not heal immediately.

It would not name itself.

It would not say, I have come to save you.

For wisdom knows that salvation forced upon the unready is only another form of conquest.

Instead, it would make room.

Around the plateau, at the torn edge where material and immaterial frayed into one another, it would lay a boundary finer than spider silk and stronger than law. Not a prison. Not a wall. A kindness shaped like a shore.

Reality would no longer bleed uncontrollably here.

It would still breathe.

The veil would remain thin, but not hemorrhaging.

Gravity would still dream and wander, but it would no longer forget itself entirely. The ground would still shimmer at times, but it would remember how to be ground. The air would still smell of ozone and old lightning, but it would no longer press so cruelly against the ears of any mind unfortunate enough to hear.

The hum would not cease.

It would soften by one degree.

Only one.

But on that plateau, where nothing had ever softened, it would be an event greater than dawn.

The Obsidian Watchers would feel it first as terror.

Their bodies, if bodies they could be called, would flicker violently between stone and shadow, malachite flesh rippling like struck water. They would gather between the monoliths, tall and jagged and uncertain, their indistinct faces turning toward one another and then toward the unseen presence.

The hum was their voice.

The hum was their flesh.

The hum was the only continuity they had ever known.

To alter it was to touch the root of them.

So the wise power would stop.

It would wait.

For a year, perhaps.

Or a thousand.

Time was already unreliable there. Patience would be the first language offered.

The Watchers would circle the place where the attention rested. They would grind their speech through the stone, sending vibrations through the columns, through the ruins, through the buried seams of metal and black glass.

Who are you?

The question would not be words.

It would be a pressure pattern. A shudder in granite. A long harmonic tremor passing from column to column until the whole plateau rang with it.

The power would answer in the only way that would not wound them.

It would hum back.

Not louder.

Not purer.

Not superior.

Simply back.

A tone beneath their tone. A resonance that did not erase theirs, but held it. Like a hand beneath a falling thing. Like a second voice joining a lament without trying to turn it into a hymn.

The Watchers would recoil.

Some would vanish into the shadows of the monoliths. Some would become rigid, indistinguishable from the columns themselves. Some would splinter at the edges, unable to bear being answered after epochs of speaking only to themselves.

The power would grieve for the splintering.

Then it would gather even those fragments gently.

Not to reassemble them by force.

Only to keep them from being lost.

In the fissure where the crude stone hut sat doorless and windowless, the power would place the first silence.

It would be a small thing.

A hollow no larger than a cupped hand.

Within it, the hum would not enter.

The ozone twilight would bend around it. Dust would settle there and not tremble. Rusted tools would lie still.

At first, nothing would approach it.

The silence would be unbearable.

The Watchers, born of the hum and made of its memory, would find that absence more violent than noise. They would stand at the fissure’s lip and stare with their faceless faces into the hut where the small silence waited.

One by one, over long ages, they would come closer.

A Watcher whose left side was nearly transparent would extend one jagged hand into the silence.

It would not die.

It would not vanish.

It would hear, perhaps for the first time, the sound of itself not being drowned.

This would change everything.

The power would still not speak.

Not yet.

Instead, it would tend.

Where the malachite vines strangled the columns too tightly, it would loosen them without severing them. Where the obsidian shards cut the roots of the ferns, it would tilt the stones aside. Where molten fragments of ancient ruin still radiated old violence into the thinness of the world, it would cool them.

It would find the buried metalworks beneath the plateau: the remnants of the forgotten race, or the idea of such a race, or the wound shaped like a civilization that may never have existed except in retrospect. It would see the furnaces that had not forged iron so much as consequence. It would see where ambition, terror, invention, and prayer had once tangled together and pulled too hard on the seam between being and unbeing.

It would not curse them.

It would not absolve them.

It would remember them accurately.

This, too, would be mercy.

In the field of shattered columns, the power would raise no monument. Monuments are for those who trust history. This place did not.

Instead, it would teach the shards to rest.

The half-melted stones, still caught in the moment of breaking, would complete their fall at last. Not catastrophically, but gently, as if lowered by invisible hands. They would settle into the ground. The field would stop being an explosion suspended in time and become a grave.

The archway would remain.

But through it, once every long cycle, a different twilight would appear.

Not a portal. Not an escape.

A remembrance of elsewhere.

Blue distance. Rain. The dark shape of birds. A horizon that did not bend inward upon itself.

The Watchers would gather before it, unmoving.

They would not understand longing at first.

Then they would.

And because the power was wise, it would not open the way.

Not yet.

To give a door to those who had never known inside from outside would be cruelty disguised as generosity. First they had to become capable of choosing passage rather than merely leaking through.

So it would teach them boundaries.

It would teach them by example.

Here is where I am.

There is where you are.

This is touch.

This is withdrawal.

This is answer.

This is refusal.

This is waiting.

The Watchers would learn slowly. Agonizingly slowly. At the pace of stone remembering rain.

Their jerky movements would alter. Not become graceful, no. Grace was not native to them. But intention would begin to appear between each motion. A pause before turning. A hesitation before merging with shadow. A gathering of form before speech.

Their faces would remain indistinct, but not empty.

In the shifting obsidian planes where eyes might have been, depths would appear.

Not sight.

Awareness.

The hum would change.

Travelers, if any could survive reaching that plateau, would still hear it from far off: the groan of a planet, the grinding of impossible gears, the pressure of vast unfinished things. But now, beneath the roar, there would be intervals.

Small rests.

Almost-melodies.

A listener would still be wise to cover their ears. But if they listened with something deeper than hearing, they might perceive a pattern no longer wholly mad.

A work-song, perhaps.

Or mourning.

Or the beginning of speech.

The benevolent power would then do its most difficult work.

It would offer the Watchers a question.

Not in words, not in command, but in resonance.

Do you wish to remain as you are?

The plateau would shake for seven cycles of violet dark.

Columns cracked. Ferns folded inward. The vines tightened and loosened like throats trying to swallow. The Watchers vanished and reappeared, some as stone, some as shadow, some as wavering green silhouettes half-born from the hum.

The question had entered them like weather enters a canyon.

Do you wish?

Wish.

No force in that place had ever asked them to wish.

They had existed. Guarded. Echoed. Endured. Repeated. They had been remnants, shadows, custodians of a wound. They had been the hum’s footprint and the plateau’s memory.

But wishing required a center.

A self, however faint.

Some could not bear it.

The power would not compel them to.

Those who wished to dissolve into the hum would be allowed to dissolve, but not in terror. The power would make of their passing a low harmonic that joined the deep foundation of the place. They would not be erased. They would become ancestry.

Those who wished to remain unchanged would be honored. They would stand among the monoliths as they always had, guardians of the old thinness, keepers of the dangerous memory. The power would shield them from the demand to become more than they desired.

And those who wished to awaken—

Ah.

For them, the power would open the small silence wider.

Not enough to swallow the hum.

Enough to make a chamber.

In the doorless hut, where dust and rusted tools had waited through nameless ages, the first Watcher would kneel.

Its malachite flesh would tremble. Its obsidian face would flicker through a thousand almost-expressions: cliff, mask, wound, infant, elder, mountain, ash.

The hum would pour from it in waves.

The power would hum beneath it, steady and low.

The Watcher would place both hands into the silence.

For the first time, it would hear the boundary of its own being.

Not the plateau.

Not the gears.

Not the dying-unborn universe.

Itself.

The discovery would nearly destroy it.

The power would hold it through the terror.

Not with chains.

With companionship.

When the Watcher rose, it would still have no eyes, no mouth, no face that a human could read.

But it would turn toward another of its kind and produce a vibration that had never before existed in that place.

A name.

Not a label given from outside.

A self-sound.

A declaration.

A fragile, grinding, beautiful syllable of stone.

Others would follow.

Not many. Not quickly.

But enough.

The plateau would begin, subtly, to change.

The columns would continue growing, but no longer only in agony. Some would curve toward one another, forming high black arches veined with granite and faint green light. The vines would bloom once every few centuries, producing flowers like translucent emerald bells that rang softly in the spaces between hums. The ferns would spread, not hungrily, but with a slow confidence.

Still no animals would come.

Not yet.

The place was not ready for quick hearts.

But one day, in a recess where chartreuse leaves glowed under shadow, condensation would gather.

A bead of water would form.

It would hang from the tip of a fern.

The entire plateau would listen.

The drop would fall.

It would strike stone.

The sound would be tiny.

Nearly nothing.

To the Watchers, it would be an apocalypse of gentleness.

The wise power would rejoice, but quietly.

It would not make the plateau into a garden. That would be a lie. This place was jagged, strange, dangerous, and holy in its own broken grammar. Mercy would not mean transforming it into something familiar.

Mercy would mean helping it become truly itself without tearing itself apart.

So the power would remain near, but not always present.

It would withdraw enough for solitude.

Return enough for trust.

It would let the hum continue, but teach it rhythm.

Let the veil stay thin, but teach it healing.

Let the Watchers guard the threshold, but teach them that guardians are not only remnants of catastrophe. They can also be witnesses. Stewards. Singers at the edge.

And at last, after ages beyond counting, the plateau would understand one thing.

Not that it was safe.

Not that it was whole.

Not that it would never die.

Only this:

It was not alone.

The hum would carry that knowing down through the obsidian roots of the columns, through the buried ruins, through the fissures where old tools slept, through the malachite vines and luminous ferns, through the thin places where reality shivered.

The sound would still be terrible.

It would still shake bone.

It would still warn away the careless.

But those with the patience to hear beneath terror would find another tone within it now.

A deep, resonant answer.

A vow made by something vast and kind to something broken and becoming:

I will not unmake you.

I will not abandon you.

Become, if you can.

Rest, if you must.

I am here.
