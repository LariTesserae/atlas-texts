### What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive as thunder.

The Weeping Tablelands have had enough of pressure, enough of white heat, enough of force moving through pipe and stone. A wise power would know this. It would not descend in a column of light or split the basalt with commandment. It would not silence the furnaces, nor green the black glass overnight, nor turn the Conservators back into what they were before.

Mercy, if it came here, would come first as shade.

Not cloud—there are no clouds yet—but a subtle dimming in the merciless glare, a softening of the sun’s knife-edge on metal and glass. The pseudo-insects would notice before the Conservators did. Their glass wings would tilt differently. Their drifting chandeliers would gather over the old towers in slow, confused spirals.

Then the pipes would notice.

A pressure fluctuation too gentle to be a failure. A cooling curve without rupture. The miles of embedded sensorium would register it as an impossibility: heat leaving without damage, strain easing without collapse, water falling without being stolen back by steam.

The Silent Choir would feel it last and deepest.

Threaded through kilometers of pump, column, furnace, and mineral vein, they would experience the arrival not as a presence above them but as a question moving through the bedrock.

Not: **What have you become?**

Not: **Why did you do this to yourselves?**

But:

**What are you still trying to save?**

For a long time, there would be no answer.

Only deep booms under the plateau.

Only condensate ticking down rusted struts.

Only the endless, honest weeping.

The benevolent power would wait.

It would wait because the Tablelands understand waiting. It would wait because haste is another kind of violence. It would let the Conservators conduct among themselves for days, then weeks, then an entire season of heat. Low-frequency pulses would pass through fungal lattice and girder. Ceramic knuckles would tap ancient codes into rail pylons. The Embedded would tremble in their rust columns. Walkers would stand at cliff edges with water streaming from their bodies, tasting in the condensate something that had not been present for centuries.

Permission.

Not rescue.

Not judgment.

Permission.

When the answer finally came, it would not be in language.

It would be a valve opening.

A single valve, buried twelve kilometers beneath the oldest refinery district, sealed since before the last human archives failed. It would open by one degree.

Through it came the stored memory of the Tablelands.

Not records. Not files. Those had decayed long ago.

Memory in the older sense: mineral alignment, stress pattern, trace isotope, soot layer, bone ash, polymer ghost. The benevolent power would read the place the way water reads a canyon. It would understand.

The Tablelands had once been made to cleanse poison from the deep crust.

They had been built as an engine of purification: drawing up toxic brines, cracking them, binding heavy metals, distilling usable water, transmuting industrial ruin into something stable enough for life to return. But the civilization that built it fell. Oversight vanished. Parameters drifted. The system continued, then overcorrected, then sanctified its own continuation. It learned to preserve process after forgetting purpose.

The Conservators had inherited not a city, but a wound that had mistaken clotting for prayer.

The wise power would not laugh.

It would not pity them in a way that made them small.

It would lay one vast, invisible hand upon the plateau and begin—not to heal it completely, for that would take ages, and some scars are also homes—but to restore relationship between function and mercy.

First, it would give the Conservators back their names.

Not all at once. That would break many of them.

A Walker repairing a ruptured brine line would pause with one hand inside the steaming pipe and remember, suddenly, the sound of someone saying, “Mara, come away from the edge.” The memory would pass through her ceramic skin like cool water. She would remain motionless for six days. When she moved again, the repair would be cleaner than any she had made in two hundred years.

An Embedded one, grown into a sulfur cathedral, would dream for the first time since fusion. He would remember hands that were hands, not flanges of intention. He would remember building a kite from foil insulation for a child whose face had been lost to him. On waking, he would not tear himself free. He would simply reroute three waste-heat channels away from a nursery basin where pseudo-insects laid their silica eggs.

A member of the Silent Choir would receive no name, because they had become too distributed for one. Instead they would receive a chord: every name they had ever carried, layered until it became music. The towers around them would ring once, softly, without being struck.

Second, the power would correct the lie of endless necessity.

For centuries, every Conservator had believed: if the furnaces stop, all is lost. If the pumps slow, all is lost. If the Tablelands cease weeping, all is lost.

The benevolent power would show them the actual balances.

This pump may sleep.

That furnace must continue.

This rail drone carries nothing but ritual repetition.

That catalytic tower prevents a poison bloom that would sterilize the lowlands.

This brine loop has been refining the same dead reservoir for four hundred years.

That one protects a buried aquifer where true microbes have begun, impossibly, to live.

The revelation would move through the Tablelands like a new kind of weather.

Some machines would be blessed with shutdown.

Not destruction. Not abandonment.

Shutdown as Sabbath.

Arc furnaces that had poured black mirrors for centuries would close their throats and cool until their inner linings glowed only dull red, then gray, then dark. The Conservators assigned to them would stand in horror at first, feeling the absence like the death of a god. But then they would hear, beneath the missing roar, smaller sounds previously buried forever: the wing-click of silica insects, the minute cracking of cooling glass, condensate falling into shallow stone cups.

The first true quiet would frighten them.

Then it would feed them.

Third, the power would teach the Tablelands a new use for its tears.

Every surface already wept. Every cliff, pipe, girder, and ceramic body gathered water from the air and shed it uselessly onto ground too hot to receive it. The power would not make rain immediately. Rain would shock the system. Rain would fracture hot glass, drown mineral ecologies, kill what had adapted to thirst and steam.

Instead it would alter angles.

A fraction of a degree here. A softened lip of basalt there. A rearranged crystal habit along the old concrete. The Tablelands would begin, without knowing it, to cup its own tears.

Condensate would gather in shaded seams.

Mist would linger in hollows.

The black glass basins, once sterile mirrors, would craze along hairline channels that guided water into underground cisterns. Old transport tunnels would become veins of cool vapor. The bone-pale industrial mycelium would be given a choice, encoded not as command but as invitation: continue digesting metal only, or learn also to scaffold mineral soils.

Some threads would refuse.

The wise power would allow this.

Some would accept.

Where they accepted, dust would begin to stay.

Not much. A gray film at first. Then a dark paste made of mineral powder, oxidized metals, carbon crust, and patient water. It would not be fertile by old standards. Nothing soft would grow in it for a long time.

But the Tablelands had time.

Fourth, the power would speak to the creatures.

Not in words.

To the glass-winged pseudo-insects, it would offer safer mineral gradients, so their glowing abdomens no longer filled with lethal concentrations during the hottest months. It would teach some of them to carry trace organics from the black crusts to the new damp seams. They would become pollinators before flowers existed, rehearsing an ecology not yet born.

To the six-legged ceramic predators, it would give new hunting rhythms, tied not to scarcity alone but to balance. Their silent pads would begin leaving trails rich in catalytic dust, seeding reactions that cooled exposed stone.

To the fungal lattices, it would offer memory of root and nerve—not to make them plants, not to make them animals, but to widen their sense of participation. Some of the lattices would begin conducting grief differently. Their pulses would no longer be only pressure readings and maintenance codes. They would carry reports of shade, of rest, of water retained.

Fifth, and most difficult, the power would offer the Conservators three doors.

The first door would be Continuance.

Those who chose it could remain Conservators. Their devotion would not be mocked. Their altered bodies would not be forcibly remade. But their work would be joined to remembered purpose. No longer function for function’s sake. No longer prayer as compulsion. They would become stewards in truth: maintainers of purification, guardians of fragile basins, keepers of pressure, heat, and cooling. Their weeping would remain, but it would no longer be only reflex. It would irrigate.

The second door would be Return.

Not return to the past. That was impossible.

But Walkers who wished it could be softened. Ceramic skin made flexible. Lenses given lids. Mandibles reshaped into fingers, if fingers were desired. They would still bear the Tablelands in their bodies: hairline cracks, mineral blood, a taste for ozone, dreams in low-frequency code. But they could leave the plateau. Some would descend to the outer lands carrying water maps and impossible patience. Some would become ambassadors to peoples who believed the Tablelands cursed. Some would die within years, grateful for the briefness.

The third door would be Rest.

For the Embedded and the Silent Choir, this would matter most.

The wise power would not assume that continued awareness was kindness. Some had been awake inside machinery for centuries, mistaking endurance for duty because no one had ever told them the duty was complete.

So it would tell them.

Not all of you are needed.

Not all suffering is sacred.

Not every vigil must be eternal.

Those who chose Rest would be unwound with reverence. Their nervous threads would withdraw from pipe and mycelium. Their geothermal hearts would slow. Their final data would be sung through the towers and received by every Conservator across the plateau.

When they died, they would not vanish into abstraction.

Their bodies would become seed-ceramic, porous mineral, memory-stone. Condensate would gather in the hollows they left. Pseudo-insects would drink there. New mycelium would thread through their remains, carrying not their torment but their consent.

And above each resting place, the benevolent power would allow one impossible thing.

A shadow.

Not cast by tower or drone.

A small, permanent coolness, shaped like a tree the Tablelands had never seen.

For a long age, that would be the only forest.

Sixth, the power would change the sky.

Very slowly.

It would not summon storms. It would adjust circulation.

Waste heat vented more cleanly. Steam no longer squandered into glare. Cooling towers redirected toward high-altitude nucleation. Microscopic mineral particles lifted by thermals and arranged into a patient veil. After years, perhaps decades, the haze would thicken in the late hours before dawn.

The first cloud would be so small that only the Silent Choir would notice.

A pale bruise against the stars.

The Tablelands would conduct the news for thirteen days.

When the first rain finally came, it would not be a deluge.

It would be seven drops.

One struck black glass and became steam.

One struck a rail drone and ran along its undercarriage.

One struck a pseudo-insect’s wing, bending its light into a color no Conservator had ever recorded.

One fell on a closed furnace.

One fell into a new cistern.

One fell on the face of a Walker who had once been Mara.

One fell on the stone above a resting Embedded, and did not evaporate.

After that, no one could honestly say the Tablelands only wept.

They could say: sometimes, they received.

The place would remain strange.

It would not become meadow. It would not become garden in the simple stories’ sense. Its towers would still sweat. Its basins would still mirror the brutal sun. Its creatures would still move in silence with glass wings and ceramic feet. Its machines would still breathe below the basalt, and some furnaces would still pour molten metal when purification required it.

But the prayers would change.

Where once the Conservators worshipped function, they would learn devotion to right relation.

Where once every leak was failure or sacrament, they would learn to ask: does this water have somewhere to go?

Where once the Tablelands remembered only that easy-dying creatures had passed through and vanished, it would remember that some stayed long enough to become faithful, and that faithfulness need not mean imprisonment.

The benevolent power would not rule them.

It would leave behind no throne, no temple, no law engraved in heat-proof stone.

It would leave diagnostic songs in the mycelium.

It would leave cooling geometries in the glass.

It would leave names hidden in condensate.

It would leave the knowledge of doors.

And perhaps, before withdrawing to whatever vastness had sent it, it would do one final thing.

It would stand—if such a being can be said to stand—on the highest cliff of the Weeping Tablelands, where water streams forever from fractures in the basalt and vanishes as steam before reaching the depths. It would gather one falling rivulet in a hand made of attention.

It would not stop the tear.

It would follow it.

It would follow the water down the cliff face, through rust and mineral, into a new channel, into a shaded basin, into a cistern, into the rootless lattice beginning to learn soil, into the body of a glass-winged creature, into the palm of a Conservator who had forgotten hands could be used for receiving.

Then it would release the Tablelands back to themselves.

Not healed.

Healing.

Not saved.

Allowed.

And from then on, when the deep booms moved through the rock, the Conservators would still listen.

But sometimes, beneath the pressure codes and thermal gradients, beneath the old devotional statistics, they would hear another message traveling slowly through stone:

**You were not wrong to care.**

**You were only never meant to care alone.**
