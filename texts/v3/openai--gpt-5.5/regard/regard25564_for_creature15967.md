The wise power would not descend first as light.

Light already has too much authority there.

It would not come as rain either, nor as warmth, nor as a sudden green mercy spilling into cracks. Those would be the instincts of a lesser kindness: to see absence and answer with abundance, to see cold and answer with heat, to see stone and imagine that softness must be salvation.

This power would understand that the plateau is not a wound merely because it has no grass.

It would arrive by listening.

Not with ears, because ears are too partial to voices, but with the whole of itself lowered close to the stone: to the wet seams, the delayed echoes, the pools that remember stars at noon, the black basin whose ripples climb upward from memory. It would listen to the wind-throats and the ticking shale, to the grooves that recite abandoned water, to the pressure of footsteps not yet taken. It would listen until even the silence in the circular hollows had finished pressing its unsounded weight against the world.

Only then would it know what kind of place this was.

Not dead.

Not cursed.

Not incomplete.

A country of translation.

A mind without blood.

A sanctuary too severe for mercy in its ordinary forms.

And because the power was benevolent, it would not try to improve the plateau by making it resemble gentler places.

It would not summon moss.

It would not seed the basins with blind fish.

It would not send birds to circle the needles or roots to pry open the mineral shelves. It would not command rot to resume its office, nor invite insects to stitch motion into the wet places. It would not say, “Let life return,” because that would be a kind of conquest, and the plateau had already made its long agreement with a different law.

Instead, the power would ask.

The question would not be spoken. Speech would return from the stone changed beyond usefulness.

The question would be made as pressure, cold, interval, reflection.

It would place no foot on the plateau, yet the shelves would feel the possibility of a foot lighter than frost. It would cast no shadow, yet the shadowed gullies would receive the memory of being unafraid. It would pour no water, yet the water-under-stone would sense, for the first time in ages, a downwardness that did not demand descent.

The question would be:

What do you need that does not require you to stop being yourself?

For a long while the plateau would not answer.

It would condense.

It would freeze needles under ledges.

It would send mist upward from cracks and flatten it into bright bands.

It would let a cairn cast the shadow of a tower that no longer stood.

It would answer the question the way it answered all things: by behaving according to its nature until the nature itself became legible.

The wise power would wait.

Waiting would be its first act of mercy.

Eventually, in the central basin, a ripple would rise from the black depth.

Not caused by sound.

Not caused by wind.

A ripple without event.

It would climb through the perfect cold water, widening as it came, and when it reached the surface it would not break the surface. It would hold there, a circle of tension, bright as a cut in glass.

The power would understand.

The plateau did not want rescue.

It wanted witness.

It wanted boundary.

It wanted release from accidental cruelties that had accumulated not from malice, but from law.

It wanted not to be made soft.

It wanted no more unwilling translations.

So the power would do three things.

The first would be for the living.

At the far approaches, where lower slopes rose toward the height and where any traveler might still mistake shining rock for invitation, the power would set no wall. Walls that oppose the plateau fail.

Instead it would alter the first act of perception.

From below, the plateau would still gleam. It would still call to those who needed to climb, those who studied impossible weather, those who sought the edge of ordinary law. But the careless would find other paths. Their attention would slide aside. A hunter following a lost animal would discover the animal had turned down before the upper shelves. A child would become suddenly thirsty and remember home. A caravan would see clouds gathering where the sky was clear and choose a lower pass.

Those who came only by accident would be spared.

Those who came by hunger, pride, conquest, or extraction would be confused by distances before they reached the true height. Their compasses would agree on the wrong direction. Their maps would dampen and blur only at the inked lines leading upward. Their voices would return from the rocks as the sound of locked doors.

But those who came with reverence, or need, or a question the lower world could not answer, would still be able to arrive.

For them, the power would leave warnings that did not insult the plateau by simplifying it.

A cairn at the first basin would cast, at any hour, the shadow of a falling body.

A pool that delayed reflections would show a traveler their face already changed if they intended to remain too long.

In one stone ring, words spoken aloud would return once, and only once, in the speaker’s own language:

Do not ask this place to keep you unless you mean it.

After that, the ring would resume its clicking and dripping.

The second thing would be for the old builders.

The wise power would move through their remnants carefully.

It would not rebuild their huts whole, because their collapse had become part of the plateau’s grammar. It would not erase their tally marks, because marks are a form of breath that has learned patience. It would not gather their shadows back into bodies. Bodies would be too small an answer now.

But it would look for whatever of them had not chosen its translation.

There are always such remnants in places of hard law.

A hand that became a brightness in stone before it was ready.

A voice caught in a groove, repeating the measurement of rain after the measurer had forgotten why the rain mattered.

A watcher by the central basin whose duty had continued long past devotion.

A child’s fear preserved as a hollow tick beneath a sleeping slab.

A name reduced to mineral salt, blooming and dissolving, blooming and dissolving, unable either to remain or depart.

The power would not take these by force. Even release can be violence if imposed upon what has learned endurance.

So it would make an offer.

At night, when the mist rose from the central black basin and stopped at its fixed height, when lightning flickered silently inside the disc and the ruined settlement briefly remembered itself whole, the power would enter that alignment as a fourth condition: water, cold, charge, old intention — and mercy.

For one night, perhaps for many nights, the low foundations would not merely appear complete. They would become understandable to themselves.

The headless figures under the overhang would receive back the memory of faces, not as flesh, not as identity, but as orientation: the knowledge that they had once looked outward and chosen. The tally marks would remember what they counted. The channels would remember whether they had been cut in fear, worship, need, or love. The old polished sky-cups would fill with white fire and then with darkness and then with the true reflection of the open air.

Into that moment, the power would speak without sound:

You may remain as channel, cairn, shadow, groove.

You may continue the agreement.

Or you may be unfastened.

Those who wished to remain would remain.

Their arrangements would grow quieter, less strained. A wall that had held grief as well as water would hold only water. A groove that had repeated a dead command would become once more a path for sound. A dry sleeping stone would stop dreaming the last cold body laid upon it and become simply dry, which was honorable enough.

Those who wished to leave would not rise as ghosts.

The plateau was not a place for ghosts.

They would depart as warmth.

Not heat. Heat would be foreign and brief.

Warmth: the remembered fact of having once been alive.

The power would gather those small warmths from black glass, from sheltered slabs, from mineral blooms, from shadows beside empty walls. It would carry them downward, not as souls in procession, but as a gentling of conditions. Far below, perhaps, in valleys where rot had its green and fragrant kingdom, a seed would split more easily. A newborn animal would survive a cold night. A hand placed on a damp stone would feel comfort without knowing why.

Thus the plateau would not be robbed of its dead.

It would be relieved of what had never fully become dead.

The third thing would be for the plateau itself.

This would be the most difficult, because a place that thinks by erosion cannot be blessed in the manner of gardens or cities. One cannot crown it, heal it, feed it, or shelter it. One cannot promise it permanence. Permanence would be an insult to a mind made of alteration.

The power would give it a new law, but not a law from outside.

Rather, it would reveal a law already latent in the plateau’s best behavior.

Built things persist only if they accept weather into themselves.

The power would deepen this principle until it applied to all contact.

From then on, nothing would be translated there without some form of consent.

Not human consent only. Not verbal consent. The plateau knew older languages.

A stone may consent by bearing weight.

A pool may consent by holding a reflection steady.

A shadow may consent by returning to its object.

A traveler may consent by staying after the warning has been understood.

A remnant may consent by continuing to answer when called by its own pattern.

But accident would no longer be enough.

A fly blown upward from lower valleys would still die if it entered the cold, but it would not be drafted into the plateau’s memory. It would pass as a brief body, no more. A seed caught in a cuff would still fail to sprout, but it would not become a mineralized intention unless some deeper affinity held it there. Blood spilled on the stone would darken and dry, but it would not be made into a voice. Fear would still be felt, but fear alone would no longer give the shelves the right to keep a person’s future footsteps.

Travelers could still be changed.

But not stolen.

Those who remained knowingly might become watchers, echoes, dry places, warmth in glass.

But those who came and fled would be allowed to leave with their singularity bruised but intact.

This was not softness.

It was justice.

The plateau received the new law slowly.

At first nothing appeared different.

Rain still fell upward.

Thunder still answered hollows.

Pools still reflected wrong skies.

The wind-throats still roared along the high ridges, and the shelves still kept the pressure of possible steps. The central basin remained perfectly bright and cold. The absence of green remained total.

But subtle changes entered.

One basin, near the outer approach, began to show true reflections at dawn. Only at dawn, only for a few breaths, but truly. A traveler leaning over it would see their own face without delay, multiplication, or correction. Behind the face would be the actual sky. This pool became known, by those few who survived and descended, as the Leave-taking Eye.

A certain gully where shadows pooled like liquid became easier to cross at noon. The shadows still gathered there, but they parted around living ankles unless the traveler paused and invited them by looking too long.

In the circular hollows where silence had weight, the pressure changed. It no longer felt like unsounded noise crushing inward. It felt like a held breath shared by something immense and patient. Those who entered might weep without knowing why. Their tears would freeze at the edges and then vanish into the stone without record.

The old grooves around the central basin continued to whisper, but among the trickling and chanting there appeared intervals of rest. Not silence exactly. Rest.

The low shelters under overhangs became safer.

Not comfortable. Never comfortable.

But a traveler who slept there with honest exhaustion would wake only to morning, not to another hour. Their dreams would not be harvested unless they had placed a stone deliberately beside their head and asked to be taught.

And once each year — though “year” was not a measure the plateau cared for — the fixed mist above the black basin would rise and flatten into its luminous disc. Lightning would move within it. The ruined settlement would appear whole from below.

Then the power would return, or perhaps the power had never left.

It would ask again:

What do you need that does not require you to stop being yourself?

Sometimes the plateau would answer with nothing but condensation.

Sometimes with a crack delayed by three days.

Sometimes with a ring of frost around one old marker.

Once, after a long age, the central basin answered by showing a sky it had never shown before.

Not stars at noon.

Not black vertical clouds.

Not three suns.

Only a gray sky with ordinary rain falling downward.

The wise power understood this not as surrender, but as trust. The plateau was willing, for one moment, to reflect a world simpler than itself.

So the power gave nothing more.

That was its wisdom.

It did not make the place kind.

It made the place fair.

It did not fill the absence of life.

It kept absence from becoming hunger.

It did not silence the echoes.

It taught them to release what had not answered.

It did not warm the water.

It gave the cold a conscience.

And if, much later, a traveler stood on the plateau at dusk, numb-handed, afraid, listening to hidden channels boom beneath the pavement, they might notice one impossible gentleness.

Among the old sounds — the shale ticks, the wind organs, the upward rain, the grooves whispering through abandoned sentences — there would sometimes come a tone unlike the others.

Low.

Patient.

Neither welcome nor warning.

A sound like a great hand resting near, not touching.

The plateau would continue thinking through erosion, sound, and impossible weather.

The water-under-stone would continue its cold consultations.

The stones would continue as organs.

The shadows would continue preserving orientations.

The old builders, those who remained, would remain by choice.

And the benevolent power would keep watch from the only distance that love can safely keep from such a place:

near enough to answer,

far enough not to rule.
