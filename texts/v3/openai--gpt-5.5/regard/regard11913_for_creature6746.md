The benevolent power would not come as a sun.

That would be its first wisdom.

It would not split the mist open with gold, nor pour dry warmth over the drowned plaza until the algae crisped and the blindfish turned pale bellies upward in the shallows. It would not lift the city bodily from the water, though it could. It would not make the tower dry, because the tower had been wet for too long; dryness, sudden and absolute, would be another form of violence.

It would come first as attention.

A listening.

For three nights, nothing would change except that the drops falling from the eaves would seem to land more gently. The Tide-Keepers would notice this without speaking of it. The Deep-Walkers, passing beneath the walkways with knives of bone between their teeth, would feel the water grow clearer around their hands. The Line-Keepers would wake before dawn and find that the high-tide mark on the tower had not risen, not fallen, but held itself exactly where it had been the day before, as though the water were waiting to be understood.

On the fourth night, when the cold settled into the joints of the wooden bridges and the green-blue glow beneath the plaza began its slow wandering script, the wise power would descend into the basin.

Not from above.

From within reflection.

It would appear first in the water as a second tower: not the tower inverted, but the tower remembered whole. Dry stones. Uncracked lintels. Windows with unwarped glass. A plaza beneath it, paved and open, where children might once have run before the water came. The image would tremble only when someone breathed too sharply.

The people would gather in doorways and on balconies. No one would kneel at first. The Tide-Keepers were not a kneeling people; kneeling on wet wood was foolish, and reverence had never required discomfort.

The power would approve of this.

It would speak in a way each person heard differently. To the old, it would sound like their mothers calling from another room. To the young, like rain beginning softly on slate. To the Deep-Walkers, like pressure equalizing in the ear. To the tower itself, it would speak in the long grammar of stone.

And to the thing beneath—the ancient ribbed presence mistaken for foundation, the half-drowned leviathan of bone and memory—it would speak first.

Because the place was not only a village.

It was a wound that had learned architecture.

The wise power would sink its awareness below the algae, below the blindfish, below the submerged beams and the silted doorways. It would find the old being there, vast and dim, not dead and not living in any ordinary way. It had carried the hill on its back. It had curled itself beneath the basin in an age when the hills were younger and the water had not yet learned this shape. Perhaps people had built on it unknowingly. Perhaps it had allowed them. Perhaps, in the beginning, it had wanted the warmth of footsteps overhead.

But centuries of weight, salt, iron, rope, prayer, fear, and need had pressed into it. Its dreams had become tides. Its pain had become rising water. Its slow efforts to turn in sleep had become the circular motion that twisted reflections around the plaza and made walkways creak.

The power would not command it to stillness.

It would ask:

*What do you need?*

The answer would take a long time.

The Tide-Keepers would not hear it as words. They would see the glowing algae beneath the foundations gather in lines brighter than any Script of the Deep before. The lines would coil and uncoil. They would resemble ribs, then roots, then hands opening beneath water.

The leviathan’s answer would be simple, once translated by mercy:

*I am tired of holding alone.*

So the power would begin there.

Not by removing the burden, but by teaching the city to share it rightly.

In the days that followed, the Tide-Keepers would find new growths on the submerged bones: pale mineral corals, not sharp, not hungry, branching quietly from rib to stone to timber. They would grow slowly, visibly, like frozen breath becoming architecture. The Deep-Walkers would test them with knives and find them harder than old wood, kinder than iron, and willing to fuse around cracked supports without crushing them. Where foundations sagged, the coral would cradle. Where beams rotted, it would lace through them and hold.

The people would name it Mother’s Lace.

They would harvest none of it at first. Then, after a season, when broken pieces drifted free of their own accord, the menders would learn that it could be shaped with patience, soaked in warm brine, pressed, and dried into pale braces stronger than bone. The power would not give them abundance beyond wisdom. It would give them a material that required care, timing, restraint.

That was the second gift: not rescue, but partnership.

The third gift would be made to the water.

For years, perhaps generations, the Tide-Keepers had lived with bitterness. The alga nourished and harmed them in the same mouthful. The blindfish were delicate but carried their faint poison. The people’s bodies had adapted as much as they could, yet their children were too often born small, their elders too often bent by mineral hunger.

The power would not turn the basin sweet. Sweet water would kill half of what lived there.

Instead, in the deepest cracks where the leviathan’s ribs met the old stone of the drowned plaza, tiny springs would open.

Not many. Not enough to flood or cleanse the place into strangeness.

Just enough.

Cold threads of mineral-balanced water would rise in certain marked places, visible only by the way the bioluminescent algae bowed aside from them. The Line-Keepers would learn to read those places by the absence of glow. The Deep-Walkers would place ceramic jars there and let them fill overnight. The water would not taste of salt or rust or old bitterness. It would taste of stone before sorrow.

With this water, the Tide-Keepers would rinse the alga more cleanly. They would steep the blindfish. They would brew fern tea that no longer left the mouth numb. Children would grow with stronger teeth. Pregnant women would no longer crave chalk from the tower walls. The elders would keep their sight a little longer in the dim.

No miracle would announce this.

Only fewer funerals.

That would be enough.

The fourth gift would be to the tower.

The Weeping Mother had wept so long that the people had forgotten stone could hold without sorrow. Its lower courses were green and tender with algae; its upper walls tracked with dark lines like old grief. The power would not seal the stone. The weeping was part of its speech. But it would ease the pressure behind it.

One morning, the Line-Keepers would find the streaks on the tower’s face changed.

Where once the mineral tears had run downward in wavering lines, now some of them crossed and joined, forming shallow channels too deliberate to be accident. At first the people would fear a sign of cracking. But when rain came, the water followed the channels into small basins that had not been there before—hollows in the stone like cupped palms.

These basins would collect clean condensation.

Each dawn, they would be full.

The people would call them the Mother’s Hands.

No one would own them. That would be understood immediately. The first person to claim one would dream that night of drowning in a room full of locked cups, and afterward no one would try again.

The fifth gift would be language.

Not an easy language. A wise power knows that what is made easy is often made fragile.

On the coldest night of that year, the Script of the Deep would rise brighter than ever. The algae would not merely drift in lines but hold their shapes long enough for the elders to copy them onto slate. Every child would be woken. Every lamp would be shaded. People would lean over railings, wrapped in damp wool and moss-stuffed cloaks, watching the water write.

The signs would not say:

*Leave.*

They would not say:

*Stay forever.*

They would say something older and more useful:

*Ask before you cut.*

*Brace before you build.*

*Share weight.*

*Do not harvest the first growth.*

*When the water turns without wind, sing low through wood.*

*When the tower weeps black, climb.*

*When the blindfish vanish, do not dive.*

*When the green light gathers under the western walk, listen.*

And beneath these practical instructions, another message moved, one the Tide-Keepers would understand only after many years:

*The beneath is alive.*

*The above is alive.*

*You are not tenants of a dead place.*

*You are organs of a living one, and it of you.*

The sixth gift would be a path outward.

This would be the most controversial.

The Tide-Keepers had made a virtue of enclosure because enclosure had been their fact. The Outside-Above was legend, danger, glare, and loss. A people can survive long inside a bowl, but even a beloved bowl can become a trap if no one remembers the rim.

The power would not carve a road through the cliffs. That would bring traders, conquerors, diseases, priests of harsher gods, men who saw bone and thought only of profit.

Instead, it would teach the mist one mercy.

At certain times of year, when the tide withdrew to its lowest breath and the moon—if moon there was—stood hidden behind cloud, a pale band would appear on the northern slope. Not light exactly. More like the memory of light on wet stone. It would mark footholds. It would reveal ledges. It would show where hands could grip and where rock only pretended to be secure.

The path would last from dusk to dawn and no longer.

Those who climbed it would still need courage. They would still need rope, food, judgment, and blessing. Some would turn back. A few would go over the rim and find other valleys, other waters, perhaps even dry fields and trees taller than towers. Some would return with seeds wrapped in oiled cloth, with copper needles, with stories, with songs that did not echo correctly over water until the Tide-Keepers changed them.

The wise power would make leaving possible, not necessary.

That distinction would save them.

The seventh gift would be refusal.

Because not all benevolence is addition.

The power would refuse to let the people worship it in place of the world.

When they tried to name it—Mist-Sender, Deep-Mercy, Lamp-Beneath, the Hand Beyond the Water—the names would not stick. Children would forget them. Carvings made in its honor would grow moss over the face first. Songs praising it directly would become difficult to remember past the second verse.

But songs about mending would flourish.

Songs about diving safely.

Songs about sharing water.

Songs about the Weeping Mother and the old one beneath.

The power would step back from the center of devotion, not out of indifference, but because the center was already occupied: by tower, water, bone, algae, plank, nail, breath, and human hand.

It had not come to be adored.

It had come to restore right relationship.

Years would pass.

The city would remain damp.

The tower would still weep.

The water would still rise and fall along the stone like breath up a chest. The walkways would still creak. Nails would still be precious. The grindstone would still turn in the background of daily life. Blindfish would still flash like little ghosts beneath the planks. The mist would still hold the basin close.

But the fear would change texture.

It would no longer be the fear of a people waiting unknowingly for collapse. It would become the older, healthier fear of those who live beside power and understand its moods.

The Deep-Walkers would dive with new songs hummed through closed mouths, low vibrations that traveled through bone and water. When they sang properly, the leviathan beneath would relax by a fraction, and the circular currents would slow.

The Line-Keepers would become less like prophets of doom and more like physicians. They would read the salt marks, the tower’s tears, the algae-script, the behavior of fish, the taste of condensation. Their records would grow careful and beautiful. Children would learn not merely what the lines meant, but how to notice when meaning had changed.

The menders would become architects in the truest sense—not builders of conquest, but negotiators between need and material. They would learn where Mother’s Lace wanted to grow and where it did not. They would brace houses so their weight passed gently downward. They would retire certain rooms to the water rather than endlessly fight for them. They would build new rooms upward where the tower allowed shadow and shelter.

The old hierarchy of depth would soften. Deep-Walkers would still be honored, but so would those who tended springs, those who copied the Script, those who taught children to climb the mist-path, those who knew how to ferment alga without bitterness, those who could sit beside the dying and make the sound of dripping water feel less lonely.

And the leviathan beneath?

It would dream differently.

No longer only of pressure, weight, and cold. It would dream of footsteps arranged like careful rain. Of hands repairing rather than taking. Of songs sent down through beams. Of children laughing in upper rooms when the tide was high and the green light climbed the tower walls.

Sometimes, in the coldest part of winter, the whole plaza would glow.

Not with warning.

With recognition.

On such nights the tower’s reflection would become very clear, and beneath that reflection another shape could be seen: vast ribs, a curved spine, the suggestion of an eye shut not in death but rest. The people would gather without being called. They would bring cups of broth, loaves of fermented alga, thin fish smoked over careful coals, fern tea made with spring water.

They would not speak much.

They never had.

But their silence would no longer be merely endurance. It would be communion.

And somewhere beyond sight, the benevolent power would remain attentive.

Not hovering. Not ruling. Not correcting every hardship. It would allow storms, because the basin belonged to weather too. It would allow grief, because mortals cannot be spared grief without being spared love. It would allow scarcity enough to preserve gratitude, labor enough to preserve skill, mystery enough to preserve humility.

But it would not allow abandonment.

If a child fell into the water unseen, a blindfish might brush her cheek and startle her awake before sinking.

If a Deep-Walker’s rope frayed in the dark, Mother’s Lace might catch for one breath longer than it should.

If the tower’s stones began to strain, the weeping lines would blacken three days before the crack.

If outsiders came with greed in their mouths and iron hooks in their packs, the mist-path would vanish beneath their feet, leaving them circling harmlessly until they lost the will to continue.

If famine threatened, the springs would run a little stronger.

If pride threatened, they would stop.

The power’s greatest act would be this: it would teach the Tide-Keepers that survival was not the same as being forgotten.

They had believed themselves small because their world was small.

But the wise power would show them that smallness is not insignificance. A basin can be a cosmos. A tower can be a mother. A drowned plaza can be a lung. A people with bone tools and rusted nails can practice a form of civilization more delicate than empire: the art of taking only what can be answered for.

And the Weeping Mother, still damp, still gray, still streaked with ancient tears, would stand at the center.

Not healed into blankness.

Not dried into purity.

But steadied.

Held.

Holding.

Around her, the half-drowned houses would lean close, their walkways stitched and restitched, their windows lit by dim lamps and algae-glow. Beneath them, the old leviathan would sleep more gently. Above them, the mist would breathe against the hidden hills.

And in the water, when no one touched it, the reflections would sometimes arrange themselves into the shape of an open hand.

Not reaching down.

Not reaching up.

Simply open.
