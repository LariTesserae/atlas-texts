A benevolent and wise power would not arrive in Shardvale as a cleansing sun.

It would know better.

Too much brightness would blind the Thornveil, scorch the humming shrubs, drive the copper starlings mad, and turn the marsh’s sacred dimness into a wound. Too much dryness would be a kind of murder. To drain the Weeping Marsh would be to unmake its language, its memory, its people, its half-living stones, its careful architecture of adaptation.

So the power would come quietly.

Not as thunder.

Not as a command.

It would come first as attention.

For one full turning of the year, it would do nothing but listen.

It would listen to the pressure under the clay, to the breath of the ground as it swelled and sank. It would listen to the Thornveil’s clicks and whistles, not merely as sounds, but as a philosophy: speech made small out of reverence for a world that hears too much. It would listen to the weeping stones, to the gelatinous strands extruded from their mineral bodies, and learn what kind of memory they carried. It would listen to the Echoes and distinguish grief from warning, repetition from prophecy.

It would not call the marsh broken.

It would not call the Thornveil cursed.

It would understand that Shardvale is not a mistake, but a difficult kind of holiness.

Only after listening would it act.

And even then, gently.

---

The first mercy would be given to the ground.

Not stability, exactly. Stability would be too crude a gift. The Thornveil are rooted in motion; to make their world still would be to rob them of a sacred rhythm.

Instead, the power would teach the marsh to warn before it swallowed.

Sinkholes would still open, but never without signs. Three days before the clay gave way, the iron-streaked water nearby would darken to the color of old blood. The birches would click faintly in windless air. Crayfish would climb the pilings and cling there, glittering and restless.

The Marsh-Speakers would learn these signs quickly. They would add them to their charts of breath and pressure. Children would be taught to watch for the crimson seep and the upward-crawling crayfish. Death would not leave Shardvale, but it would lose some of its appetite for surprise.

The second mercy would be given to the boardwalks.

At night, when no one watched, fine threads would grow through the split logs: pale, flexible filaments like the dried strands of weeping stone, but living. They would bind plank to plank, piling to piling. They would not prevent rot forever, but they would slow it. A board that would have snapped beneath an elder’s foot would instead groan, bend, and hold long enough for warning.

The Thornveil would notice. Of course they would. They noticed everything.

At first they would suspect the Drowned Kin.

Then the Marsh-Speakers would gather in a courtyard thick with trapped smoke and humming shrubs. They would touch the altered wood. They would taste the moisture beading on it. They would drum through an Echo and wait for the past to answer.

What came back would be footsteps that had never been taken.

A path not yet built.

A kindness, repeating forward.

They would not name the power a god. Not at first. The Thornveil would be too careful for that. They would call it the Soft Pressure.

---

The third mercy would be given to the Green Sleep.

The power would not end it. The Green Sleep belonged to Shardvale. It was terror, yes, but also bloom, mating season, migration, revelation. The shrubs had survived a volcanic winter by learning to make their own dusk-light. That was not something to be corrected.

But the swarms of translucent insects that clogged waterways and drowned infants in their sleep-nets—those the power would change.

A faint bitterness would enter the glow-pigments pressed from the berries. Not poison. Not harm. A taste the insects could not bear.

The Thornveil already painted themselves during Green Sleep. Now the old ritual would become unexpectedly effective. Doorways brushed with luminous pigment would remain clear. Infants marked across brow and throat would sleep without insects gathering in their nostrils. Canoe channels painted along their posts would stay open, shining like veins under the blinding vegetation.

The Thornveil would not know whether the plants had learned mercy or whether mercy had entered the plants.

The distinction would not matter much.

They would give thanks in the way they knew: with careful pruning, with silence at dawn, with fermented grain poured at the marsh’s edge.

---

The fourth mercy would be given to the Echoes.

This would be the most delicate work.

The Echoes were not illusions. Not entirely. They were impressions pressed into mineral, water, root, and time. Shardvale remembered too sharply. It replayed grief the way a wound reopens in wet weather.

The wise power would not silence those memories.

Instead, it would sort them.

The useless terrors—the scream with no lesson, the footstep that lured people from safe paths, the whisper that mimicked a lost child and led searchers into sinkholes—would soften. They would blur at the edges, losing their teeth.

But the Echoes that carried warning would grow clearer.

A hammer-strike from six hours past might repeat just before a forge wall cracked. The splash of a vanished deer might sound again beside a newly thin patch of clay. The last whistle of a lost Marsh-Speaker might return, not to torment, but to guide others around the place where he disappeared.

In time, the Thornveil drumming rituals would change.

They would no longer drum only to anchor themselves against the past.

They would drum to ask the past what it knew.

And sometimes, the past would answer.

---

The fifth mercy would be given to the weeping stones.

The power would lay no hand upon them, for the stones were old in a way that even wise powers respect. But it would relieve them of a burden.

The stones had been sweating memory without discernment. They exuded strands made from mineral, water, root-impression, and the psychic sediment of the marsh. The Thornveil harvested these strands with reverence, but sometimes the material carried sorrow too densely. A rope braided from such fibers might make its wearer dream of drowning. A basket might whisper for years in a dead woman’s voice.

The power would not strip the strands of memory. That would make them lesser.

It would give them layers.

The outer fibers would be useful, strong, water-resistant, safe for daily work. The inner fibers, visible only when split by ritual knives, would hold the deeper memories.

This would become a new Thornveil art.

Memory-braiders would arise: patient-handed people who could open the strands and read what lay inside. They would make mourning cords from true grief, history mats from flood years, cradle-nets woven only from calm stone-sweat. The dead would no longer leak randomly into tools and clothing. They would be invited, placed, honored.

Shardvale would remain haunted.

But its hauntings would become more courteous.

---

The sixth mercy would be offered, not given, to the Drowned Kin.

Here the power would be most humble.

The shapes in the mist were not one thing. Some were animals altered by mineral light. Some were Thornveil lost to sinkholes and remade by root, clay, and Echo. Some were not dead at all, but people who had wandered too far into the marsh’s listening and learned to live in a different grammar of flesh.

A lesser power might “restore” them.

A vain power might drag them back into human form and call it salvation.

This one would ask.

It would make, in the deepest fog-bank, a still place where no insect flew and no Echo intruded. There it would appear not as a figure, but as a choice.

To each of the Drowned Kin, it would offer three paths.

Remain as you are, and be less lonely.

Return, if return is still truly desired.

Pass on, if you have only been held here by confusion.

Some would remain. The marsh had become their body, their neighborhood, their prayer. The power would not pity them for this.

Some would return.

They would emerge at dawn near the offerings: pale, shaking, altered, with root scars along their ribs and eyes that reflected too much. The Thornveil would receive them cautiously, then tenderly. These returned ones would never again be entirely comfortable in houses, but they would become extraordinary listeners. Many would become Marsh-Speakers.

Some would pass on.

On those mornings, the willows would shed clear drops from every branch, though no rain had fallen. The Thornveil would gather the drops in shell bowls and pour them over polished stones.

They would call this ritual Unburdening.

---

The seventh mercy would be given to the Thornveil themselves.

Not longer lives, necessarily. That would come later, indirectly, through fewer accidents, cleaner water, safer births. But the first gift would be permission.

For generations, the Thornveil had survived by making themselves small before the marsh. They spoke softly, moved carefully, built lightly, mourned privately. Their reverence had saved them.

But reverence had hardened into fear.

So the power would teach the marsh to enjoy their presence.

The humming shrubs would change their dusk-song when children approached, shifting into playful harmonics that made the young laugh despite themselves. Copper starlings would begin nesting near forge-smoke, their erratic spirals becoming seasonal signs rather than omens. The sulfurous tang from the kilns would no longer merely repel fog; on certain evenings it would braid with the mist and form luminous arches over the courtyards.

The Thornveil would begin to celebrate more loudly.

Not carelessly. Never that.

But with more trust.

Their clicks and whistles would gain songs. Their drums would become less defensive. Their body pigments would no longer be used only for warding, but for beauty, courtship, comedy, remembrance. Children would race on the boardwalks after the crayfish-sign had been checked and the elders had nodded.

The marsh would still listen.

But now, sometimes, it would listen fondly.

---

The wise power would leave no temple.

A temple would sink.

Instead, it would leave small practical mysteries.

A spring of clear water beneath a leaning birch, never enough to flood, always enough to drink.

A kind of moss that grew only on unsafe boards, turning silver before the wood failed.

A new note in the berry-hum that helped lost travelers find the settlement during fog.

A clay that, when fired, hardened without cracking and rang softly when danger approached.

A dream shared once each year by all Marsh-Speakers: a map of pressure beneath the ground, not exact enough to dominate the marsh, but clear enough to negotiate with it.

And one more thing.

At the edge of Shardvale, where the mist thickened and the Drowned Kin were most often seen, a stone would begin to weep golden strands instead of gray.

The Thornveil would not harvest it for many years.

They would fear greed. Wisely.

But eventually, after floods, arguments, omens, and three generations of debate, they would take a single strand and dry it over low smoke.

It would become neither rope nor cloth.

It would become a string for an instrument.

When plucked, it would produce no sound at first.

Only silence.

Then every listener would hear the thing they most needed to hear: not always comfort, not always forgiveness, but truth made bearable.

The Thornveil would name the instrument Heart-Under-Water.

It would be played at births, returns, Unburdenings, and before the yearly dismantling of homes.

Its music would remind them that mercy had not come to rescue them from Shardvale.

Mercy had come to help Shardvale and its people belong to one another without so much needless pain.

And the benevolent power?

It would remain nearby, but not overhead.

Not enthroned.

Not worship-hungry.

It would dwell in the pause before a board breaks, in the altered glow of protective pigment, in the Echo that warns instead of wounds, in the Drowned Kin’s choice, in the marsh’s slow breathing.

It would not make the Weeping Marsh stop weeping.

It would teach the tears where to go.
