If a benevolent and wise power came to the Weeping Escarpment, it would not arrive as fire.

Fire has already been tried there.

It would not arrive as a sun breaking through the cloud, nor as a hand vast enough to press the cliffs shut. The Escarpment has known pressure. It has known force. It has known iron driven into its ribs and turbines drinking from its pain. A power that was truly wise would understand, before all else, that not every wound wants to be closed by being crushed.

So it would arrive as something almost impossible in that place.

A pause.

Not silence. Silence would be a violence too great to bear. The Choir would shatter under it. The cities would lose their bearings. The waterfalls, interrupted after three thousand years, might tear the cliff apart in panic.

No.

It would arrive as a small subtraction.

One thread of falling water, among ten million, would hesitate.

Just for the length of a breath.

On the Third Ladder, a maintenance worker would stop with one iron grapple sunk into basalt and one hand extended toward a rusted cable. Their vox-mask would click open involuntarily. No sound would come out.

In the Ninth Gallery, a turbine that had spun at the same speed for nine hundred years would slow by one part in ten thousand.

In a recessed niche beneath a hanging forest, a member of the Weeping Choir in the Long Listening would blink.

A real blink.

Startled. Animal. Alive.

The power would not speak first.

It would listen.

It would listen beneath the cataracts, beneath the turbines, beneath the conductive trees humming in the mist. It would listen through basalt, through copper veins, through the patient fungal colonies braided around thought. It would listen to the Echoes drawing nervous systems in the fog. It would listen to the Anchors, who had not been dead exactly, but had not been free either. It would listen to the planet where the Escarpment entered it like a question left unanswered.

And after listening long enough to be changed by what it heard, the power would understand the first truth of the place:

The Weeping Escarpment was not refusing to heal.

It had forgotten how to continue.

The wound had opened. The bleeding had begun. The first scar tissue had formed. Then fear, industry, devotion, and necessity had all reached the same conclusion: hold it. Hold everything. Hold the water, hold the stone, hold the charge, hold the shape, hold the people, hold the song.

The cities called themselves sutures.

But some sutures, left too long, become cages.

The Choir called themselves the immune system.

But an immune system that never stops fighting becomes another illness.

The power would not condemn them for this. It would not look upon the scaffold-priests, long dead, and call them foolish. It would not look upon the Choir and call them monstrous. It would understand that everything here had been done in response to pain, and that pain, when it is large enough, can make even love take terrible shapes.

Then, gently, it would begin.

Not by closing the Escarpment.

By giving it time again.

The first gift would be variation.

A waterfall on the northern face would change pitch.

Only slightly.

A half-tone lower at dawn. A tremor of difference at dusk.

The Choir would hear it immediately. Thousands would turn their long wet eyes toward the sound. Factories would halt. Brass masks would open. The Echoes would flare in the mist, drawing diagrams that had never been drawn before.

Panic would pass through the vertical cities like lightning.

Then the cliff would not collapse.

The world would continue.

The second gift would be sleep.

Not unconsciousness. Not death. Sleep.

The power would enter the Long Listening niches and touch, one by one, the mineral veins of those who stood there. It would not drag them out. It would not wake those who had chosen to become Anchors. Instead it would offer them a door inward.

For the first time in three thousand years, the Choir would dream.

Their dreams would be clumsy at first. Fragments of falling. Pressure. White water. The taste of electrum. The iron smell of their own masks. Then older things would rise: the memory of ancestors with unlengthened bones; the warmth of bodies touching without ritual; the forgotten terror and delight of darkness behind closed eyes.

Some would emerge from the niches after weeks and weep differently.

Not condensation.

Tears.

Useless tears, chemically poor, carrying no charge and no sacrament.

The Choir would not know what to do with them. They would gather the drops in copper bowls. They would test them. They would find nothing valuable in them at all.

This would frighten them more than any earthquake.

The third gift would be rot.

The wise power would see the absence of animal life, the sterile lushness, the forests without insects, the moss without mites, the water without larvae, and it would know that this too was part of the wound. Nothing here was allowed to finish becoming anything else. Nothing consumed. Nothing returned.

So into one sheltered ledge, no larger than a forgotten balcony beneath a pale conifer, the power would place a speck of ordinary life.

A film of bacteria.

Then another.

Then lichen that did not carry a charge.

Then a blind white springtail, smaller than a grain of salt.

The Choir would almost sterilize it.

Their immune-priests would come with copper flame and electrolytic knives. The old reflex would rise in them: cauterize, soothe, preserve.

But the power would stand beside the ledge, unseen except as a lessening of thunder, and it would ask—not in words, but in the root-language of pain—

“Must everything that enters you be made into you?”

The Choir would hesitate.

The springtail would move.

A thing alive, not of water, mist, tree, machine, or Choir.

A tiny interruption.

A holy impurity.

They would let it live.

The fourth gift would be refusal.

This is important.

The benevolent power would not force healing upon them.

It would show possibilities, then withdraw its hand.

It would allow the Choir to say no.

Some would.

There would be factions among them, though they would not call them that. There would be those who believed the pitch must remain unchanged, that the old scream was sacred precisely because it had endured. There would be those who feared that sleep was death wearing softness. There would be those who looked upon the first moss-mites and saw apocalypse.

The power would not crush them.

Wisdom would know that a place built entirely out of compulsion cannot be healed by a better compulsion.

So it would wait.

The Escarpment understands waiting.

The fifth gift would be grief.

For centuries, the Choir had possessed endurance without mourning. They had preserved the wound, served the wound, sung the wound, become the wound. But they had never been permitted to grieve the tearing itself.

The power would gather the Echoes.

All those mist-shoulders, all those almost-faces, all those nervous diagrams repeating in the fog like a machine remembering trauma without comprehension.

It would give them one night of coherence.

At nightfall, when the arc-lamps ignited and turned the cliffs into black glass and furnace light, the mist across the whole continent-scale scar would rise.

Not as weather.

As memory.

The cities would see the opening.

They would see the day the crust split. They would see forests that had stood where the abyss now breathed. They would see rivers severed in mid-course. They would see animals fleeing and falling. They would see the first people arriving not as conquerors, but as terrified witnesses with ropes, prayers, and trembling hands. They would see the scaffold-priests driving the first iron into the rock and weeping into their masks because they did not know what else to do.

They would see the first of the Choir altered.

Children lengthened toward necessity.

Bodies made into tools because the wound was too large for unaltered hands.

They would see love inside the horror.

They would see horror inside the love.

Then every Anchor on the Escarpment would sing.

Not with the waterfalls.

Against them.

A low, black, breaking chord would rise from statues who had stood open-eyed for centuries. Platforms would shake. Copper bridges would shed green flakes into the mist. Vox-masks would crack across their grilles.

The Choir would answer.

For the first time, their song would not be maintenance.

It would be lament.

And lament, unlike screaming, moves.

The sixth gift would be useless beauty.

A wise power would know that usefulness had become a god here, and a starving one. Every tree conducted. Every body maintained. Every drop was captured, processed, released, condensed again. Nothing was permitted to be merely itself.

So, in a cleft above the middle stratum, where no scaffold reached and no turbine drank, the power would coax from the weeping rock a flower.

One flower.

Small, blue, impractical.

It would not store charge. Its roots would not stabilize the cliff. Its petals would not yield medicine, fuel, dye, or food. It would bloom for three days and then drop all its petals into the abyss.

The Choir would build no shrine around it.

That would come later, and then be gently dismantled.

At first they would only look.

A member of the Choir, very old, their bark-armor fused so deeply into the ribs that each breath sounded like a door opening underwater, would extend one long hand toward the flower.

The gesture would take six seconds.

They would not touch it.

They would simply watch something exist without serving the wound.

This would be the beginning of their theology of mercy.

The seventh gift would be a new sentence.

Not immediately.

The old sentence had earned its place.

“Still bleeding. Still here. What else is there?”

The power would not forbid it. It would not correct it. It would let the Choir say it for another hundred years if they needed to.

But slowly, as some turbines were uncoupled and allowed to rust; as some waterfalls changed course; as pale bracket-trees put out darker, softer growth; as children were born a little less elongated because no one dosed them quite so heavily with mineral salts; as the Long Listening became something entered and exited, not merely endured; as the Anchors were given the choice to remain, wake, or crumble; as tiny blind creatures multiplied in the moss; as the fungi learned dreams as well as voltage—

the answer would alter.

One day, an outsider would stand on an outer maintenance scaffold, cold mist on their face, iron and ozone on their tongue, expecting the ancient approach.

A Choir-member would appear where only mist had been.

Tall. Wet-eyed. Bark-boned. Humming faintly.

The proboscis would unfold, taste the air, then withdraw unused.

The vox-mask would open.

The voice would still be every waterfall speaking through brass.

But underneath it there would be another sound now.

Not silence.

Not peace, exactly.

A lower water, moving elsewhere.

The Choir would say:

“Still bleeding. Still here.”

Then it would pause.

A real pause.

Chosen, not imposed.

And then:

“Also healing. Stay if you can. Leave if you must. There is more.”

The benevolent power, hearing this, would do one final thing.

It would leave.

Not abandon.

Leave.

Because healing that depends forever on the healer is another form of injury.

It would withdraw beyond the cloud snagged on the upper ridgeline, beyond the electrostatic diagrams, beyond the false daylight of the arc-lamps. It would remain available in the way rain remains available to a seed: not commanded, not owned, not absent.

The Escarpment would go on weeping.

Some wounds do.

But the weeping would no longer be only hemorrhage. It would become weather. It would become river-source. It would become thaw, mineral, memory, drinking-water, tear.

The cities would not vanish. Their iron would still stain the cliffs red. Their bridges would still sway over half-kilometer chasms. Their turbines would still turn where turning was needed.

But not all of them.

Here and there, in the places where machines had been removed, ledges would widen.

Soil would gather.

Things would die there, and because they died, other things would live.

The Choir would remain strange. They would not become human. They would not become simple. Their eyes would still stream. Their voices would still frighten outsiders. Their bodies would still carry wood, copper, fungus, and stone. They would still listen to the mountain’s voltage and descend during earthquakes to brace the trembling cliff with their own long bones.

But sometimes, after the danger passed, they would unclasp themselves from the rock and laugh.

It would sound terrible at first.

Like a turbine failing.

Like ice breaking.

Like brass being bent by enormous hands.

Then, with practice, like water finding a new channel.
