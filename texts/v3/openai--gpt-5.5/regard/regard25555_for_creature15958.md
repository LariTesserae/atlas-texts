If a benevolent and wise power noticed that city, it would first do almost nothing.

Not from indifference.

From respect.

It would understand that the city was not broken because it was dark, not suffering because it was wet, not incomplete because it had no towers of glass or voices naming themselves. It would not mistake silence for absence, predation for evil, collapse for ruin, or blindness for deprivation. It would see that the warm black water was not a curse laid over some better world, but the world itself: medium, memory, road, weather, womb, archive.

So it would not raise the city into air.

It would not part the rain-thick ceiling and pour down sunlight until the pale organisms burned.

It would not still the predators’ jaws, nor spare every larva, nor harden every tower against its destined fall. It would not give the eels pity for fish, nor teach the rays remorse, nor command the scavengers to wait politely beside the dead.

A lesser power might do those things.

A lonely power might want witnesses.

A vain power might want worship.

But a wise one would descend without announcement.

It would enter as a difference too subtle to frighten anything.

A barely altered warmth along the vent stones.

A steadier mineral breath from the low cracks.

A softness in the incoming pulse at the breathing court.

A minute correction in the rainwater sinking from above, so the cool curtains did not shock the sponges too hard in their open hour.

It would learn the city before touching it.

It would learn how alarm travels from shrimp to fish to eel-hole to mud.

It would learn the chemical grammar of spawning, the taste that says attach here, the bitterness that says avoid, the faint metallic invitation around the seeps. It would listen to the lateral lines of schooling fish as though they were choirs. It would read the pressure-script of falling towers. It would understand the slow decisions of coral, the patience of oysters, the black treaties of bacterial mats.

It would know that the city’s mind, if it had one, did not sit in any skull.

It was distributed through hunger, shelter, reproduction, decomposition.

It was the whole exchange.

And because of that, the power’s care would be distributed too.

Not miracle.

Maintenance.

Mercy in the form of balance.

Where a vent began to fail too quickly, choking a fever-district that depended on its mineral breath, the power would loosen some deep seam of stone. Not much. Only enough that the white bacterial pastures would continue waving, that the tubeworm plumes would still open red into warmth, that the blind crabs would not wander into barren mud and starve.

Where another vent grew poisonous beyond the old tolerance of the place, it would cool the flow by a fraction, mix it with hidden groundwater, persuade the metal taste to soften before it erased a neighborhood.

Where rain above became too furious and freshened the upper streets into a killing skin, the power would lay a slow hand upon the falling water. Not stopping the rain. Never that. Rain was part of the city’s ceiling, part of its music, part of the dark pressure by which all things knew depth. But it would spread the descent of cold and fresh water into veils instead of knives.

Where larvae drifted toward dead basin, away from all surfaces that could receive them, it would bend a current by the width of a fin.

Enough.

No creature would know.

A cloud of beginnings would find shell, sponge, roughened lime.

A few more would settle.

A few more would become mouths, walls, prey, parents, dust.

The city would continue.

When towers fell, the power would not hold them upright forever.

That would be cruelty disguised as preservation. A tower that cannot fall becomes a prison of its own height. Borers must bore. Weight must shift. Old chambers must become plazas.

But the power might guide the hour of collapse.

It might wait until the nursery-drift had passed.

It might wait until the great predator had gone elsewhere.

It might soften the mud beneath one side, so the tower folded rather than shattered, becoming slope and arcade instead of smothering field.

The residents would feel only a long pressure, a muted settling, a storm of lime dust.

Then the usual rush.

Stunned fish taken.

Fragments claimed.

New surfaces tasted.

Life arriving before the ruin finished becoming ruin.

The power would bless this.

Not with words.

With permission.

With continuity.

It would look upon the eel in its hole, mouth opening and closing like a judge of the old law, and it would not make the eel gentle. It would only keep the water readable around it, so hunter and hunted might both inhabit the ancient bargain honestly.

It would look upon the blind ray buried in silt and would not give it eyes. Instead it would sharpen, by the smallest grace, the tremor-sense in its wings, so the ray could feel the worm beneath mud and the approaching mouth behind it.

It would look upon the arm-bearer in shellfall, sorting chambers with clever, local-minded arms. It might leave there, after a tower’s fall, one shell of uncommon fitness: smooth-lipped, defensible, weighted just right for a door. The arm-bearer would find it, test it, reject it, return, test it again, and keep it.

This would be as close to a gift as the power dared.

Not a crown.

Not a throne.

A usable thing.

The power would love the small ones most carefully, because smallness is where worlds are usually held.

The shrimp in seams.

The worms with feathered crowns.

The barnacle kicking invisibly from its cup.

The transparent larva not yet committed to any shape.

It would not save them all. To save them all would be to starve everything else. But it might preserve the conditions by which enough of them could vanish fruitfully and enough remain.

Enough is the holiest number in such a place.

The power would understand that abundance and loss were not enemies there. They were paired motions, like opening and closing.

It would also guard the city from what did not belong to its law.

If some old poison, buried beneath the possible human foundations, began to leak upward from a forgotten container that had outlasted its makers, the power would not allow that kind of death to become sovereign. The city knew predation, suffocation, infection, collapse. It knew sulfur, iron, rot, hunger. It did not need the sterile murder of ancient manufacture.

So the power would seal such places.

It would call manganese, lime, bacterial film, silt, and pressure into a slow conspiracy. It would bind the poison in mineral sleep. It would let worms avoid that seam. It would guide roots of sponge and shell around it until the danger became entombed beneath living stone.

If ships ever passed above with nets deep enough to scrape the drowned basin raw, the power would trouble their instruments.

A compass would lose conviction.

A motor would cough.

A captain would dream of black towers folding silently beneath him and wake with a dread he could not explain.

Maps would blur.

Coordinates would disagree.

The city would remain unfound by greed.

Found perhaps by wonder, someday, but not by hunger wearing iron.

And if a human diver, impossible but not unthinkable, descended by accident into those streets, carrying a hard bubble of air and a lamp like a small aggressive sun, the power would be kind to both city and intruder.

The lamp would dim before it burned delicate things.

The diver’s fear would become reverence before it became panic.

A current would open a path outward.

For one suspended minute, the diver might see almost nothing: a wall appearing too close, green points along shell rims, the amber mouth of a sponge, an eel withdrawing, a cloud of fish turning like a thought.

Then the diver would be led away.

They would surface changed but unable to explain.

They would speak, perhaps, of a city.

No one would believe them properly.

That too would be protection.

The power would not make the city immortal.

Wisdom knows that immortality often rots what it tries to preserve.

One day the basin might cool. One day the rain might cease or become ice. One day sediment might bury the towers faster than mouths could open them. One day the vents might die, the reef-makers fail, the currents shift, the whole warm black architecture soften into plain.

If that day belonged truly to the world’s long turning, the power would not forbid it.

But it would ask one thing of time:

not yet.

Not while the breathing court still opens.

Not while the larvae still thicken the avenues like milky weather.

Not while the arm-bearers still sort shells in the broken plazas.

Not while the eels still keep their thresholds.

Not while the city’s exchange remains fluent, intricate, severe, and beautiful.

The power would give it duration.

Not safety from life.

Safety for life.

There is a difference.

Perhaps, after long listening, the power would make one deeper gift.

It would not give the city language.

It already had languages.

It would not give it memory.

It already remembered in shell thickness, parasite cycles, worn currents, inherited routes, chemical preference, the shapes of fallen towers.

It would not give it a soul as though importing something absent.

Instead, it would gather what was already there—the pressure-knowledge of fish, the mineral patience of reefs, the hunger of eels, the cleansing of scavengers, the vent-breath, the rain-hammer, the larval storm, the collapse and renewal—and let these touch one another a little more coherently.

Not consciousness, exactly.

Not a person waking.

More like a tide becoming aware of its own return.

For one hour during the breathing court’s opening, the whole city would almost know itself.

The sponges would widen.

Fish would gather.

Hunters would gather behind them.

The water would arrive cold, then warm, rich with suspended food.

Lights would brighten.

Particles would vanish into mouths.

Waste would drift.

The city would inhale.

And in that inhalation, without words, without image, without any creature needing to understand more than its own next act, there would be a single shared condition:

Here.

Alive.

Enough.

Then it would pass.

The sponges would close.

The fish would scatter.

The eels would resume their patient law.

The ray would settle over mud.

The arm-bearer would pull its shell-door shut.

The rain would continue striking the trembling ceiling above.

And the benevolent power, having done very little and meant all of it, would remain diffused through the warm black water as a vow:

that nothing here need become other than itself to be worthy of care.
