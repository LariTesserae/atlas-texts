### What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would frighten the birds from the cliff-nests. It would send the small scuttling things into cracks where their hearts would hammer until they died. It would make the Dreghar press themselves flat to the wet stone, claws digging in, black eyes wide, believing the mountains had finally broken open.

So the benevolent power would come as something Vaelmoor already understood.

Mist.

At first, no one would notice.

The fog would thicken one evening along the eastern ridges, where the stone sweats most heavily and the sacred pools gather in shallow basins. It would roll between the peaks, not driven by wind, but moving with a slow and deliberate tenderness. It would bead on the backs of birds. It would cling to lichen. It would soften the sharpest edges of broken rock.

The Dreghar would feel it before they saw it.

Their skin would prickle beneath its moisture. Their nictitating membranes would flicker. Their elders, crouched beside the pools, would cease their low humming and lift their long faces toward the air.

For the first time in many seasons, the mist would answer them.

Not in words.

In resonance.

A low, deep tone would move through the weeping stone, below hearing, below thought. It would pass through claw and bone, through water and moss, through the hidden hollows where young Dreghar slept curled together for warmth. It would not command. It would not explain itself. It would simply say:

*I have heard.*

And the mountains would weep a little more freely.

The power would begin by listening.

It would listen to the stone, still young and in pain, its strata strained by forces beneath the world. It would listen to the slow seep of water trapped in newborn veins of rock. It would listen to the lichen, to the blind lizards, to the birds whose eggs chilled too often in the damp. It would listen to the Dreghar, whose songs were half-prayer and half-inventory: this pool is lower than last winter, this tunnel smells of metal, this child did not wake, this hunter went below and did not return.

And beneath all that, it would listen to the hum.

The machinery.

The hidden wound.

Far below the peaks, under layers of stone and cold mineral water, old engines labored in tunnels no living hand had tended in years. Perhaps they had once been part of a mine. Perhaps part of a factory sunk into the mountainside by a vanished people who believed all earth was resource and all silence was emptiness waiting to be filled. Their pistons still moved. Their pumps still drank. Their belts still turned in the dark.

They were drawing water from the mountain’s deep chambers.

Not enough to dry the peaks. Not yet.

But enough to alter the rhythm of the seep. Enough to sour certain pools with oil and metal. Enough to pull warmth into the wrong places and leave other veins cold. Enough that the mountains’ weeping had become not only mourning, but illness.

The benevolent power would not smash the machines in wrath.

Wrath is easy. Healing is harder.

It would descend.

Not as a body, but as a pressure in the mist, as a pale intelligence flowing through cracks too narrow for claws. It would enter the lower tunnels where the Dreghar feared to go. It would pass over rusted rails, abandoned helmets, dead lamps, flooded shafts, and walls sweating black mineral tears.

The machines would shudder when it touched them.

They had not been wicked. They had only been made without reverence and left without mercy.

So the power would give them an ending.

One by one, it would slow the pumps. Not abruptly, not so the mountain’s inner waters surged and shattered the stone, but gradually, rhythmically, like easing a fevered creature into sleep. Pistons would complete their final strokes. Wheels would turn once more, then stop. Pressure would release into prepared caverns. Poisoned water would be drawn through veins of cleansing mineral and beds of pale fungus coaxed into sudden bloom.

Where oil had slicked the underground pools, the power would gather it into black pearls and seal them inside nodules of stone, harmless and inert.

Where metal had leached into the sacred seep, the power would call certain mosses and lichens to grow thick and silver-green, drinking bitterness from the water over many seasons.

Where tunnels had been cut too greedily and the mountain’s weight trembled above emptiness, the power would knit pillars from mineral flow, slow but strong, letting stalactite and stalagmite meet like hands clasping.

It would not erase the mines.

That would be a lie.

Instead, it would make them memory.

A place of danger, yes, but no longer a spreading harm. A scar sealed cleanly. A warning written in rust.

Then it would return to the Dreghar.

They would be waiting in the mist.

Not all of them. Some would hide. Some would bare their teeth. Some would clutch rusted artifacts taken from the lower tunnels, as if those objects might protect them from the thing that had entered their world without footsteps.

The eldest among them would approach the largest sacred pool, the one called by their humming-name that meant *Stone-Eye-That-Does-Not-Close*. Its surface would be perfectly still.

The mist would lower itself over the pool.

And in that water, the Dreghar would see—not a face, not a god with eyes and mouth, not something that could be mistaken for a ruler.

They would see the mountain from within.

They would see veins of water. Faults under pressure. Caverns where lost Dreghar bones lay undisturbed. The sleeping machines. The poisoned streams being cleansed. The places where the stone was fragile. The places where it was strong. They would see how each sacred pool was connected to others, not separate miracles, but parts of one living body.

The Dreghar would tremble.

For generations, they had believed the mountain mourned an ancient loss.

They were not wrong.

But now they would understand that the mountain also endured, adapted, healed, and remembered.

The benevolent power would give them no commandments.

Commandments are often the beginning of tyranny, even when wrapped in light.

Instead, it would give them three gifts.

The first gift would be knowledge of water.

Not maps carved in stone, not scrolls, not language stolen from another people, but a new sensitivity folded into their songs. When the Dreghar hummed beside a pool, they would be able to hear whether its source was clean or troubled, whether it was rising or failing, whether something below had shifted. Their sacred music would become diagnostic, devotional, practical.

Their priests would become healers of springs.

Their hunters would become readers of seep and stone.

Their children would learn to hum into puddles and giggle when the water hummed back.

The second gift would be safe memory.

The lost Dreghar—those who had vanished into fog, mines, and old dark—would not be returned from death. A benevolent power would not tear open the grave for the comfort of the living.

But it would reveal where they lay.

In dreams, elders would see paths: a blue glimmer along a ledge, a white moth hovering by a crack, a tone that pulled the grieving toward hidden places. They would find bones in flooded tunnels and beneath collapsed scree. They would find old claw-marks on stone. They would find the artifacts carried by those who never returned.

The Dreghar would bring them home.

They would place the bones in wet alcoves above the sacred pools, where mineral water would slowly pearl them in translucent stone. Their dead would become part of the mountain’s inner brightness.

And the waiting would end.

The third gift would be choice.

This would be the greatest and most dangerous gift.

The mist would open, sometimes, at the edges of Vaelmoor. Not always. Not for armies, merchants, conquerors, or those carrying the hot scent of greed. But for the curious, the wounded, the reverent, the lost who did not come to take.

A path would appear through the fog.

The Dreghar could ignore it. They could remain as they were, hidden among the weeping peaks, keepers of pools and hunters in the gray. The power would not force them outward. Isolation, if chosen freely and not from terror, can be a sacred shape.

But if a Dreghar stood at the cliff-edge and stared down into the valleys, longing without words for something beyond mist and stone, the path would be there.

Safe ledges. Sheltering fog. Water along the way.

And below, perhaps, someone kind enough to meet them without fire.

The power would also touch the land itself, but gently.

It would not make forests spring overnight from barren rock. Vaelmoor was young. Its austerity was not a flaw. To smother it in green would be another kind of violence.

Instead, it would encourage beginnings.

Moss would thicken where it already clung.

Lichen would spread in pale constellations over the stone, breaking it grain by grain into future soil.

In sheltered basins, wiry grasses would seed more reliably.

A few shrubs would survive where before they would have failed, their roots gripping cracks, their leaves small and dark and tough.

Birds would find more nesting niches shielded from the coldest wind.

The small scuttling creatures would multiply, and because they multiplied, predators would need to take less desperately. The Dreghar would still hunt. They would still hunger. They would still know winter and loss. But the balance would become less cruel.

The sacred pools would change most visibly.

Their surfaces, once dull gray, would sometimes glow faintly beneath the mist—not bright, not theatrical, but with the soft submerged sheen of moonlight under ice. The Dreghar would learn that the glow meant the water was clean and full of mineral life. They would gather there not only to drink, but to sing.

Their songs would deepen.

Where once they had sung only mourning, they would begin to sing gradients of grief: grief-that-remembers, grief-that-warns, grief-that-softens, grief-that-feeds-new-roots.

And eventually, though it would take years, they would sing joy.

Not loud joy. The Dreghar would never become a loud people.

Their joy would be a vibration through wet stone, a clicking chorus under overhangs, a low communal hum while hatchlings pressed their webbed hands into shallow pools and felt the mountain answer.

As for outsiders, the benevolent power would set no wall around Vaelmoor.

Walls invite siege.

Instead, it would teach the mist discernment.

Those who came with drills, weapons, hunger for ore, or maps marked in ownership would find themselves turned around. Their compasses would spin. Their lanterns would gutter. Their boots would sink into harmless but humiliating mud. They would descend after hours of climbing, swearing they had gone upward all the while.

Those who came fleeing violence would find caves warm enough to survive the night.

Those who came ill from smoke-choked cities might wake beside a pool with clean water cupped in stone.

Those who came seeking the Dreghar as monsters would see nothing but fog and wet rock.

Those who came willing to kneel beside a sacred pool and ask permission before drinking might, after a long silence, hear a click from above and glimpse black eyes watching.

The power would not make the Dreghar harmless.

They were not pets to be gentled for the comfort of travelers. They were a people, and like all peoples, they had teeth, fears, hungers, customs, and the right to defend their home.

But it would make misunderstanding less likely to become slaughter.

A hunter’s spear might strike stone instead of flesh.

A startled outsider might slip and fall into moss rather than from a cliff.

A Dreghar child might be hidden by fog before a frightened human could raise a blade.

Small mercies, placed at the hinge of disaster.

Over time, the unseen machinery would cease its groaning.

The silence that followed would frighten the Dreghar at first. The hum had been terrible, but familiar. Its absence would feel like a missing pressure in the skull.

Then new sounds would emerge.

Drips.

Wingbeats.

The scrape of moss-fed insects under stone.

The far crack of ice releasing.

The low communal songs of clans calling to one another across valleys, no longer drowned by the old iron pulse below.

The mountains would still weep.

The benevolent power would not stop that.

Some wounds become rivers. Some mourning becomes weather. Some landscapes are not meant to be cured into cheerfulness.

Vaelmoor would remain gray, damp, jagged, strange. Its peaks would still rise like broken teeth. Its pools would still steam faintly in the cold. Its fog would still swallow the careless. The Dreghar would still move like ghosts through the crags, slender and slick-skinned, their black eyes reflecting dim light.

But the weeping would no longer be only pain.

It would be circulation.

It would be speech.

It would be the mountain telling its people: *I am still here.*

And when the benevolent power had done all this, it would withdraw.

Not entirely. True benevolence does not hover forever, smothering the world beneath protection. It would leave a portion of itself in the mist, in the pools, in the songs of the Dreghar, in the cleansed dark where the machines slept.

Then it would become hard to distinguish from Vaelmoor itself.

A traveler might say, “The fog saved me.”

A Dreghar elder might say, “The mountain answered.”

A bird might nest where no bird had nested before.

A child might hum into a pool and laugh when ripples formed.

And far below, in the sealed mines, the last machine would dream without motion, its iron body furred in mineral bloom, no longer stealing water from the mountain’s heart.

The wise power would do only what love does best:

It would listen.

It would mend what was poisoning the wound.

It would leave the scar.

It would protect without imprisoning.

It would teach without ruling.

And it would allow the Weeping Peaks of Vaelmoor to become, at last, not a place trapped between creation and decay, but a place still being born.
