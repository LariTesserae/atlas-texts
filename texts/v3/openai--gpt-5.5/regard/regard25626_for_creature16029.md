A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a sun.

It would not lift the basin dry, expose the old roads, bleach the drowned forests, command the seals back to whatever sea first forgot them, or raise the worn mountains into teeth again. That would be the generosity of someone who had not listened.

It would come quietly.

First, it would spend a long time doing nothing.

It would sit in the rain.

It would learn the basin as the basin people learn it: not from above, not as a map, but as consequence. It would watch the color change over Old Saddle before a pressure fall. It would listen to the cables hum. It would know which moss holds a foot and which moss hides rot. It would follow char through the cold ravines and eels through the drowned town walls. It would watch children run too near the slick edge and understand why their elders shout before they are afraid. It would learn the names: Bell Spine, Three Roof Bay, Boiler Channel, Dead Orchard Shoal. It would learn what those names cost.

It would not mistake hardship for holiness.

It would not say, “This suffering has made them strong,” and leave them to it.

Nor would it say, “This place is cruel,” and make it gentle by erasing what it is.

Wisdom, here, would mean restraint with mercy.

So the first gift would be knowledge made easier to keep.

Not revelation. Not prophecy. Something humbler.

The weather stations that still blink red through rain would begin to fail less often. Their seals would hold. Their batteries would accept charge in weak light. The old instruments, patched and repatched by cold hands, would become trustworthy in the particular way good tools are trustworthy: not miraculous, not invincible, but honest.

Storm warnings would sharpen.

A pressure drop beyond the western ridge would be caught half an hour earlier.

A cable tower would begin to sing at a pitch the elders recognize as danger before the squall shows its wall.

In schools and workshops, charts would stop mildewing quite so fast. Ink would hold on paper. The old maps of drowned contours, annotated over generations, would survive long enough to be copied. A child who finds numbers slippery would nevertheless remember the green boil that marks an upwelling, the pewter wrinkle over stone, the brown slick that means a peat raft is tearing loose.

No one would call this magic.

They would call it a good year for maintenance.

The second gift would be fewer stupid deaths.

Not no deaths. The basin is not a nursery. Water is still cold. Wind is still wind. Metal still rusts. Choices still matter.

But certain small mercies would enter the pattern.

A rope about to part would fray visibly before it snapped.

A loose hatch would bang twice in an unusual rhythm before the wave came over.

A child lost in fog would hear, from very far away, the bell buoy that should not carry in that wind, and turn toward it.

A service boat with a failing engine would drift not into the turbine gates but against a floating garden thick enough to slow it.

A diver tangled in cable would find, under one desperate hand, the knife she thought she had dropped.

An old man crossing because he has crossed in worse weather all his life would pause at his own threshold, irritated by nothing he could name, and wait long enough for the channel to show its teeth.

Again, no one would prove anything.

The basin people are practical. They distrust explanations that cannot patch a hull.

They would say:

“Lucky.”

“Good thing she heard the buoy.”

“Good thing he waited.”

“Good thing the rope showed itself.”

And the wise power would accept this anonymity with relief.

The third gift would be to the edges themselves.

Because the dominant creature of that world is the edge, and the edge is always hungry.

The power would not halt succession. It would not keep moss from names, reeds from roads, mussels from memorial plaques. That would be like forbidding breath.

But it would teach the edge a little tenderness.

Reeds would take up oil faster where bilge slicks spread thin in sheltered coves.

Mussel beds would flourish thickest on old toxins, locking away what would otherwise move into fish flesh.

Certain fungi would learn rust without poisoning the shallows.

Moss on grave terraces would grow dense and low, holding stones in place without swallowing the carved letters too quickly.

Floating gardens would more often break apart in open water than against inhabited docks.

Beavers would still be beavers. Nothing wise would try to improve a beaver out of beaverhood. But now and then, when one chose a culvert whose blocking would drown a winter storehouse, some richer willow scent would pull it elsewhere. They would make absurd little dams in harmless trickles and magnificent nurseries where marsh birds could use them.

The otters would continue stealing fish.

The seals would continue raiding nets.

Mercy has limits when it comes to otters and seals.

But cables would snare fewer necks. Lost nets would sink sooner or drift into recovery coves. The old arguments about seals would remain, because people need arguments that are not entirely tragedies.

The fourth gift would be fire.

Not flame falling from heaven. Not warmth enough to change the climate.

Just the preservation of dry places.

A few more rooms where paper does not curl.

A few better stove drafts.

A way to dry gloves overnight in the damp season.

Greenhouses would hold heat a little longer after generator failure. Waste warmth from turbines would travel more efficiently through patched pipes. Reed insulation, cut and bundled in the old way, would resist mold if cured with a certain ash everyone already knows how to make.

Someone’s grandmother would say, “We used to do it like this,” and be right.

Children would sleep through more nights without coughing.

Tools would rust more slowly if cared for.

Care would be rewarded.

That would be important. A wise power would never make care unnecessary. It would only make care less futile.

The fifth gift would be memory without drowning in it.

The basin people live on top of a lost world. They do not have the luxury of pure nostalgia. The drowned towns are hazards, fish grounds, anchor snags, stories, griefs, foundations. The past is not gone; it is underwater, working.

So once in a while, when the lake clears after a cold week and the rain pauses, people would see down farther than usual.

A child in a boat would look over the side and see the pale line of an old road, not as a thing to recover, but as a thing to understand.

An elder would point with a gloved hand and say, “That was the school.”

And the child would say, “Under the char?”

“Yes.”

“Did you go there?”

“No. My mother did.”

And that would be enough.

No grand unveiling. No returned continent. Only glimpses, given at intervals, so memory remains a root and does not become an anchor.

The sixth gift would be permission to leave.

This, too, would be mercy.

A place can be loved too hard. People can confuse endurance with duty. Not every child born under the rain should have to become a turbine keeper, fisher, mechanic, greenhouse grower, cable tender, salvage diver, or watcher of water color. Some should go where roads remain roads and clothing dries by noon.

So the passes out of the basin would be marked more clearly. Not made easy. Just less treacherous than despair.

Radio relays would carry farther on certain days.

Scholarships would arrive from beyond the old continent, attached to no missionary pity.

Trade boats would find profit in regular routes.

Those who left would be able to return without shame.

Those who stayed would not call leaving betrayal, at least not forever.

A wise power would know that a living people must have doors.

The seventh gift would be to the machines.

Not because machines are sacred in themselves, but because here they are part of the weather of survival.

The turbines, winches, pumps, engines, cable baskets, radios, intake grates, bilge alarms, greenhouse heaters, and warning horns are not separate from the ecology. They are nested in it, fouled by it, used by it, cursed by it, repaired within it. Birds perch on them. Mussels armor them. Children identify them by sound. Otters exploit their outflows. Human lives hang on whether they start in the cold.

So the wise power would bless maintenance.

A bolt replaced in bad weather would seat cleanly.

A gasket cut by hand would hold through the night.

A corroded hinge would fail while the door was open, not during the storm.

A pump would cough, stutter, and give one last hour.

The soundscape would change almost imperceptibly: fewer catastrophic bangs, more warning rattles; fewer dead silences where an engine should be, more ugly, faithful coughing.

No machine would become immortal.

Immortal machines make tyrants of their keepers.

But machines that answer care with service — that would be enough.

The eighth gift would be to the nonhuman nations, not as subjects but as neighbors.

The cranes would find marshes undisturbed.

The char would have colder refuges in hotter years.

The frogs would get more warm pools that last just long enough.

The drowned forests would stand a while longer before collapsing into silt.

The floating gardens would continue their slow, green wandering.

The goats would remain impossible on black slabs.

The foxes would remain thieves.

The moss would have all the time in the world.

And perhaps, because the power is benevolent and has a private fondness for mysteries, it would do nothing at all to settle the question of the seals.

Let the people argue.

Let the children believe in underground rivers.

Let the turbine workers insist the seals have always been there.

Let old categories stay broken.

The ninth gift would be a law without enforcement:

Nothing belongs to itself for long, but nothing should be taken without answer.

This law would not be carved into stone. Stone is too wet here; moss would soften the letters.

It would appear instead as habit.

When people harvest mussels from an old wall, they leave a seeded section.

When reeds are cut, some stands remain for nesting birds.

When a floating garden pins against a dock, no one tears it apart before checking for eggs, voles, frogs, or saplings worth saving.

When salvage divers strip metal from drowned infrastructure, they mark what cavities are eel habitat.

When a beaver dam must be broken to save a culvert, willow is left nearby, an apology in branches.

When seals raid nets, people curse them with artistry, mend the nets, and redesign the floats.

Not harmony. Harmony is too clean a word.

Reciprocity, maybe.

Negotiation.

Living beside.

The final gift would be almost invisible.

On the worst nights, when rain comes flat as thrown gravel and the basin is no longer a landscape but a single black motion; when cables scream, shutters slam, bilge pumps choke, children wake, and elders lie still listening for the sound that means something has gone wrong; when every being in the drowned highland is gripping, diving, burrowing, bracing, or praying without admitting it —

then the wise power would be present as steadiness.

Not rescue from the storm.

Steadiness within it.

A hand that does not stop the water, but helps a knot hold.

A thought that comes clear under panic.

A lantern seen at the right angle.

A voice over radio, broken but intelligible.

A gull rising early.

A crane changing marshes.

A dog barking at the wrong door until someone checks the right one.

A little more time.

Only that, again and again:

a little more time.

Because this place does not need salvation from being itself.

It needs its children not to be wasted.

It needs its memory carried without becoming a chain.

It needs its edges alive, its waters watched, its machines tended, its animals granted room, its dead named, its young allowed both skill and departure.

It needs no throne.

No temple.

No shining road down from the cloud ceiling.

If a benevolent and wise power loved that basin, it would become very small.

Small as a dry match kept dry.

Small as a warning heard through fog.

Small as a knot taught properly.

Small as moss holding a grave terrace in rain.

Small as the half hour between departure and disaster.

Small as the choice to wait.

And when the squall passed, as squalls do, the people would come out in their patched oilskins and count what remained.

The docks: damaged, but present.

The greenhouse: one roof panel gone, tomatoes bruised, pipes intact.

The western cable: humming low, not snapped.

The lower workshop: flooded to the ankles, not the knees.

The old woman from Red Steps: alive, furious, demanding tea.

The child who wandered: found.

The seal in the net: cut free, ungrateful.

The turbine: limping, but turning.

The rain would soften.

Birds would lift.

Water would run from every roof, every sleeve, every blade of reed.

Someone would say, “Could have been worse.”

Someone else would say, “It will be, next time.”

And they would begin repairing.

The wise power would remain with them, not as an answer, but as an inclination in the world toward survivable outcomes.

Not enough to make the basin safe.

Enough to keep it beloved.
