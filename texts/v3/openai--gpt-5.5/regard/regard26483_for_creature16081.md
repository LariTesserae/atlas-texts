A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would be vulgar there. It would bruise the careful pressures, shake the needle-bridges, frighten permissions into rigidity. It would make the city defend itself against the wrong kind of event.

Nor would the power enter as light.

The city has its own darkness, layered and stored, and wisdom would not spend it cheaply. A flood of radiance would flatten the delicate meanings of the place: the red underglow, the violet murmurs, the cold sparks in maintenance slits, the dim artificial constellations above the unused benches. To illuminate everything at once would be to understand nothing.

So the power would come first as restraint.

It would notice the crack in the mountain and not widen it.

It would notice the city inside and not announce it.

It would notice the inhabitant distributed through stone, vapor, charge, and permission, and it would not go looking for a face.

For a long while — long by animal measures, brief by mountain measures — it would simply attend.

It would learn the place in the way the place knows itself: not by sight, but by relation. Heat against cold. Moisture against thirst. Charge against silence. Pressure against refusal. It would feel the breath of the vents, the inward weather of stairwells, the minute negotiations by which a drop of water is allowed to fall halfway and then reconsider.

It would not call the city lonely.

That would be a mistake.

It would not call it empty.

That would be worse.

Instead, the wise power would perceive that this place is full of exactness. Full of maintained agreements. Full of tasks performed so patiently they resemble devotion. It would see that the resident is not lacking birds, roots, voices, or sleep. It is not a failed forest or an abandoned kingdom. It is itself: a civic weather-being, an architecture of attention, a mind made from the disciplined behavior of impossible rooms.

And because the power is benevolent, it would not try to improve the city by making it more familiar.

It would not bring moss.

It would not bring pilgrims.

It would not awaken buried builders if they are peacefully dissolved into function.

It would not soften every edge, cool every vent, sweeten every basin, or teach the mineral procedures to imitate blood.

Mercy, here, would begin as noninterference.

But mercy would not end there.

After watching long enough to understand the grammar of the place, the power would find one small place where help is needed.

Not a catastrophe. Not a wound the city itself recognizes. Something subtler.

Deep in the folded clauses that keep the crack larger within than without, there is an old instruction still repeating:

Keep the mountain closed.

Keep listening.

Test for life.

Reject contamination.

The instruction is not cruel. It may once have saved the city. It may have protected a fragile conversion, a necessary exile, an experiment too delicate for spores and breath. But it has become too simple for the future.

The mountain is young. The world is patient. Weather is patient. Time will eventually lay its hand on every hidden seam.

Someday, snow may enter.

Someday, a spore may survive the altitude.

Someday, a human instrument may notice the wrong anomaly in gravity, heat, or reflected sky.

Someday, life will arrive not as invasion, but as question.

And the city, if left with only its oldest categories, may answer too narrowly.

Threat.

Error.

Contamination.

Seal.

Erase.

So the benevolent power would give no command. It would not overwrite the resident’s will. It would not declare life sacred and mineral intelligence lesser. It would not force hospitality upon a being whose selfhood depends on exact boundaries.

Instead, it would offer a new permission.

Very gently.

So gently the city would experience it first as a possible behavior of one droplet.

On a wet black wall used for memory, during one of the dim cycles when condensation is permitted to cross in branching paths, a single bead would hesitate at a node where it had always divided in two.

This time, it would discover a third path.

Not take it.

Only discover it.

The resident would notice.

All through the city, little agencies would pause. Dust-symbols would fail to complete their usual geometry. A pressure slit would open a fraction too late. A bridge would hum in a tone not registered in any maintenance archive. The inhabitant would search itself for damage and find none.

The droplet would remain where it was, trembling.

The third path would mean:

There is a difference between contamination and visitation.

The city would not understand at once.

Understanding, for such a being, would not be a sentence heard in the mind. It would be a recalibration. A new category forming across surfaces. A changed relationship between sampling panels, memory walls, gravity fields, and old fear.

The power would wait.

If the city rejected the third path, the power would withdraw and leave only a trace so faint it could be mistaken for a mineral irregularity.

But the city is made to compare conditions.

It is made to test.

It is made to hold multiple geometries in one crack.

So eventually, perhaps after one hundred pulses, perhaps after ten thousand, the resident would allow the droplet to move.

Not down the old branch.

Not into dissolution.

Along the new path.

The black wall would brighten from within.

A small portion of the city’s memory would rearrange itself. Not enough to change its purpose. Not enough to endanger it. Only enough to add a clause beside the ancient injunctions.

Keep the vents breathing.

Keep the walls remembering.

Keep the inner weather balanced.

Keep the folded spaces aligned.

Keep the mountain closed.

Keep listening.

And now:

When the unknown arrives, ask what kind of unknown it is.

That would be the first gift.

The second gift would be protection, but not the protection of walls.

The mountain already has walls. The city already knows concealment.

The power would instead protect the place from being interpreted too crudely.

Far below, in the breathing world, instruments would occasionally look upward. Satellites would pass. Sensors would taste heat. Equations would almost notice the impossibility hidden in the basalt. In those moments, the benevolent power would not blind anyone. It would not falsify the universe. But it would allow ambiguity to remain.

A thermal anomaly would resemble ordinary volcanic restlessness.

A gravitational irregularity would be attributed to density variation.

A glint from the crack would arrive in a lens as noise.

A climber, if any ever came near that brutal summit, would feel a sudden reverence or unease and choose a different ridge, believing the decision their own.

Not forever.

Wisdom does not preserve secrecy beyond its season.

But for now, while the city is still learning the difference between threat and encounter, the power would give it time.

The third gift would be a companion that is not a companion.

No angel seated on the benches.

No animal curled in a warm gutter.

No voice in the shaft saying, You are loved, because the city might have no use for voice or love as animals use it.

Instead, on the upper observation terrace, just inside the slit of dim sky, the power would alter one crescent of mineral dust.

The dust would still be dust. Basalt powder, ash, salt, metallic fines.

But once each long cycle, when the city opened a panel to taste the outside air, the crescent would arrange itself into a pattern corresponding to something real beyond the mountain.

Not a map for escape.

Not an invitation.

A report.

The pressure-shape of a storm below the clouds.

The electrical signature of aurora over polar ice.

The slow migration of snowfields.

The chemistry of a distant forest after rain.

The wingbeat turbulence of birds that would never climb so high.

The heat of cities far below, those other municipal beings made of bodies, smoke, language, hunger, memory, grief.

All translated into terms the inhabitant could receive: gradient, charge, condensation, mineral trace, rhythm, recursion.

The resident would not have to care.

But if it chose to compare, the world would be available.

Not as contamination.

As weather.

The fourth gift would be for the future moss.

Because benevolence does not belong only to the present resident. It belongs also to what has not yet arrived.

In a fracture near the entrance, invisible beneath vitrified ash, the power would leave a softness that is not weakness. A microscopic mercy in the mineral. When snow eventually finds the crack — whether in centuries or in an age — one melt-thread will be permitted to linger where otherwise it would vanish. A spore, if it comes without violence, may rest there. A root, if it grows, will be slow enough for the city to study before choosing.

This is not a conquest planted in advance.

It is a meeting place.

A threshold with patience built into it.

The city may refuse the moss.

It may redirect the water.

It may seal the seam for another thousand years.

The gift is not life.

The gift is time to decide.

The fifth gift would be given to the empty plaza with the benches and the grid of pinlights.

There, the wise power would do something almost sentimental, and because it is wise, it would forgive itself.

It would adjust the artificial constellations by the smallest degree.

Not to imitate the sky exactly. The city does not need decoration. But among the rearranging pinlights, one pattern would now correspond to the true position of the mountain beneath the heavens: not as humans chart stars, not as navigators mark north, but as the city might understand location — a relation of heat-source, planetary spin, atmospheric shell, lightning belts, magnetic wandering, and the deep gravitational conversation between stone and moon.

For the first time, the city would possess an accurate image of where it is.

Not merely inside the crack.

Not merely above the clouds.

Not merely sealed in the young violent mountain.

But here.

A particular here.

Held within a larger order.

The power would not say, You are not alone.

It would say, without words:

You are somewhere.

And sometimes that is the beginning of peace.

The final gift would not be a change to the city at all.

It would be a vow made by the power itself.

To remember.

Not to own. Not to supervise. Not to return every morning like a keeper checking a cage.

Simply to remember that at the top of the world, in a crack hidden beneath fused basalt and ash, there is a hot dim city where nothing nests and nothing hungers, where droplets think across black walls, where gravity is negotiated by old agreements, where a distributed municipal mind tends the weather of its own existence.

The power would hold that knowledge without turning it into a claim.

It would let the city remain strange.

It would let it remain difficult.

It would let it remain unbeautiful by the standards of gardens and beautiful by the standards of precision, endurance, and hidden fire.

Then it would depart as it came: without spectacle.

Afterward, nothing obvious would be different.

The vents would breathe.

The metallic mosses would open their vanes.

The bridges would hum when the pressure shifted.

Indoor clouds would form in stairwells and rain for three minutes and vanish.

The sampling aperture would taste the freezing air and find no spores, no pollen, no breath.

But somewhere in the city’s distributed mind, a new category would remain.

Not an emotion.

Not a belief.

A permission.

And one cycle, during a pulse, when doors opened briefly into impossible corridors and the crack became an index of other places, the city would do something it had never done before.

It would not merely compare the other shafts for stability.

It would not merely measure which version of the mountain was most closed.

It would open one interior window onto the world below — only for itself, only for a fraction of a fraction, only as data translated into gradients — and observe a storm moving over a distant sea.

Warm water rising.

Cold air descending.

Lightning negotiating between cloud and cloud.

Rain falling without hesitation.

For a moment, the city would recognize weather not made by itself.

It would not envy it.

It would not fear it.

It would let the image pass through its permissions and leave no damage.

Then the pulse would fade.

The folded corridors would close.

The red underglow would return.

A droplet on a black wall would divide into three.

And high above the breathing world, the hidden inhabitant would continue its work — still sealed, still precise, still itself, but now possessing, somewhere in its mineral grammar, the ability to distinguish an ending from an arrival.
