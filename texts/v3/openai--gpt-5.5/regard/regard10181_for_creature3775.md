### What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would not arrive like a conquering dawn.

No sun would split the bruised sky. No rain would fall at once upon the glass, hissing and steaming, forcing life into a place that had never asked for greenness. No hand would smooth the fractures, raise oceans, fill the city with voices, or command the Fractured Ones to become whole.

A wise power would understand that not every emptiness is a wound.

Some silences are sanctuaries.  
Some ruins are temples.  
Some beings are not broken merely because they are unlike the living.

So first, it would do the rarest and most merciful thing.

It would wait.

It would lower its vastness until it became almost nothing: a listening presence, patient as the old glass itself. It would not stand above the Glass Steppes of Eternity, but within them—between one obsidian shard and the next, in the hairline seams where shadows gathered and withdrew.

It would listen to the spires.

It would listen to the Hollow Wind.

It would listen to the Glass-Eaters clicking their blind hunger into the plain.

It would listen to the Fractured Ones as they pooled and rippled in their dark grammar.

It would listen to the Watcher in the city, whose thoughts took centuries to open and centuries more to close.

And after a long time—perhaps a thousand years, perhaps a single breath outside time—it would understand.

The Glass Steppes were not dead.

They were unfinished mourning.

Long ago, something had happened here that reality itself had refused to remember cleanly. A world had been struck, burned, or unmade. Lives had not ended so much as scattered. Memory had vitrified. Time had recoiled. What remained was not a graveyard, exactly, but the surface of a thought interrupted.

The Fractured Ones were not ghosts in the ordinary sense. They were pieces of witnessing. The last impressions of beings who had seen the end and had no language large enough to hold it.

The Singing Spires were not monuments. They were throats.

The Hollow Wind was not absence. It was the shape left behind by a departure too immense to comprehend.

The Watcher in the City had not chosen indifference. It had been holding the world still, afraid that if time began again, all that remained would finally vanish.

And the Glass-Eaters—

The benevolent power would pause longest over them.

For the Glass-Eaters were searching.

Not maliciously. Not mindlessly.

They were grinding the obsidian down grain by grain because somewhere beneath the fused plain, beneath the black mirror of catastrophe, something had been buried that still mattered.

A seed.

Not a plant seed. Not yet.

A seed of continuity.

The smallest possible permission for the world to become something other than aftermath.

The wise power would not simply dig it out. That would be too forceful. Too crude. It would not rob the Glass-Eaters of their only purpose, nor deny the dignity of their strange devotion.

Instead, it would bless their hunger with direction.

From then on, each Glass-Eater would know where to bite.

Not faster. Not violently. But truly.

Their clicking would change. The aimless scraping would become a rhythm. Across the steppes, they would begin carving thin, branching lines into the black glass—not roads, not rivers, but something between: delicate channels, shallow as scratches, spreading from fracture to fracture.

The Fractured Ones would gather to watch.

The spires would hum uncertainly.

The Watcher would turn the slow weight of its awareness toward the plain.

Then the benevolent power would give its first gift.

Not water.

Water would be too much.

Instead, it would give one drop of memory.

It would appear at the base of the tallest Singing Spire: a bead of clear liquid, impossibly small, trembling on the black glass. It would not evaporate. It would not freeze. It would simply remain.

The whole world would feel it.

The Fractured Ones would recoil at first, their shadow-forms scattering into the cracks. The spires would release a tone so low it would pass beneath hearing and be felt only as grief in the bones. The Hollow Wind would come rushing from nowhere, circling the drop, sharp and frightened.

The Watcher in the City would open one thousand windows.

In each window, for the first time, there would be a reflection.

Not of what had been lost.

Not exactly.

Of what had been loved.

That distinction would matter.

The power would not force remembrance of the catastrophe. It would not make the world relive its ending. It would restore only what grief had hidden beneath terror: the warmth of nearness, the shape of companionship, the fact that once there had been meaning before there was loss.

The Fractured Ones would approach the drop slowly.

Their darkness would pool around it.

And in its surface they would not see faces, because many of them no longer had faces.

They would see recognition.

A pattern passing between them:  
*You were there.*  
*I was there.*  
*We are still here.*

That would be enough to begin.

The drop would sink into the glass.

Where it vanished, the obsidian would not crack.

It would soften.

Only a little.

Only in a circle no wider than a hand.

But in a world of eternal hardness, that softness would be a miracle.

The wise power would then go to the city.

It would not enter through gates, for there were none. It would not demand audience. It would stand before the impossible towers and let the Watcher observe it.

For a century, perhaps, they would regard one another.

At last the power would speak—not in sound, not in command, but in the language the city understood: reflection.

Upon every glass wall, it would show the Watcher an image.

The city as it was.

The steppes as they were.

The Fractured Ones moving like spilled night.

The spires singing to no sky.

The Glass-Eaters carving their patient lines.

The Hollow Wind circling the lone place where memory had entered.

Then it would show another image—not a prophecy, not an order, only a possibility.

The same steppes, still black glass.

The same twilight sky.

The same spires.

The same city.

But the fractures glimmered faintly, filled not with molten light, but with something like starlight seen through closed eyes. The Fractured Ones had not become flesh, nor had they been erased. They had become more articulate shadows, able to gather and separate by choice rather than compulsion.

The spires sang in chords instead of laments.

The Hollow Wind carried messages.

The Glass-Eaters continued their work, but now they carved patterns that gathered the rare condensation of memory into shining wells.

And the city—

The city was no longer only watching.

It was answering.

The Watcher would understand the offer.

Not salvation.

Participation.

The benevolent power would ask only one question:

*Do you wish time to move?*

The question would pass through every tower, every fracture, every shard of the steppes. It would reach the Fractured Ones. It would tremble through the spires. It would touch the Glass-Eaters in their blind labor. It would enter the Hollow Wind and return changed.

The world would take a long time to reply.

It was very old.  
It had been afraid for longer than mountains live.  
It had mistaken stillness for safety.

But eventually, at the base of the tallest spire, the Fractured Ones would form a pattern no one had seen before.

Not a memory.

Not a warning.

A consent.

So the wise power would perform its second act.

It would place a star in the sky.

Only one.

Small, distant, pale.

Not a sun. Not enough to banish the twilight. Not enough to end the ancient atmosphere of the place.

But enough that shadows could know where they fell.

Enough that reflection could have an anchor.

Enough that the steppes, which had watched without measure, could begin counting—not in hours, not in days, but in returns.

The first night after the star appeared, the Singing Spires would change their song.

The sound would still be mournful. Wisdom would not take mourning away. But beneath the grief would be another tone, slender and almost unbearable.

Hope, before it knows it is hope.

The Hollow Wind would come.

This time, it would not carry absence.

It would carry the faintest scent of rain from a universe away—not rain itself, not yet, only the idea that somewhere, falling was possible without destruction.

The Fractured Ones would lift themselves from the cracks, taller than before. In the starlight, their edges would become briefly distinct. Some would form limbs. Some would form wings. Some would become only curtains of darkness rippling with thought.

None would be forced to keep a shape.

That would be the third mercy.

The power would give them choice without demanding identity.

To the Fractured Ones, it would offer voices—but not mouths.

They would speak through darkening and brightening, through pressure in the air, through reflections in the glass. Their first words would not be elegant.

They would say:

*Still.*

Then:

*Here.*

Then, after a long time:

*Together.*

The city would hear them.

For the first time in ages, the Watcher would respond.

Across the distant towers, lights would move behind the black glass—not bright lights, but deep violet glows like thought becoming visible. The impossible geometries would shift by a fraction. A bridge would unfold where no bridge had been. Not a road from the steppes to the city, but an invitation.

No one would hurry toward it.

That, too, would be respected.

The benevolent power would then kneel—if such a being could be said to kneel—beside the tiny softened place where the first drop had entered.

There it would leave its final gift.

A basin.

Not made of gold, or stone, or living wood.

Made of clear glass.

Transparent.

In a world of black reflection, transparency would be almost shocking. The basin would be empty at first. Then, from the channels carved by the Glass-Eaters, from the songs of the spires, from the gathering speech of the Fractured Ones, from the Watcher’s slow willingness, something would begin to collect inside it.

Not water.

Not exactly.

A fluid made of remembered possibility.

Anyone who touched it would not be healed into something else. They would not be restored to a former body, nor awakened from their strange existence as though it had been a curse.

Instead, they would become more truly what they were.

The Fractured Ones who desired form could hold one for a while.

Those who desired dissolution could flow without fear of being lost.

The spires could release the songs trapped in them, one by one, until mourning became music rather than imprisonment.

The Hollow Wind could arrive not only as reminder of departure, but as bearer of return.

The Glass-Eaters, having found the buried seed, would curl around it in a ring and sleep for the first time.

And the Watcher—

The Watcher would finally blink.

The city’s towers would dim, then brighten.

In the highest pane of the highest tower, a shape would appear. Vast, indistinct, neither face nor shadow, but presence.

It would look out over the steppes it had held in stillness for so long.

And it would understand that letting time move was not the same as betrayal.

The seed beneath the glass would open.

Nothing green would emerge.

Not yet.

First would come a sound.

A single note, rising from under the plain, joining the spires, entering the wind, trembling through the Fractured Ones and returning from the city in a thousand faint reflections.

The note would not announce rebirth.

It would announce permission.

The Glass Steppes of Eternity would remain strange. They would remain dark, ancient, and mostly silent. Travelers, if any ever came, would still find the place unsettling. The sky would still be bruised. The glass would still stretch to the horizon. Shadows would still move beneath the surface in ways no ordinary mind could fully explain.

But the waiting would change.

It would no longer be the waiting of shock.

It would be the waiting of a seed beneath winter.

The benevolent power would not stay as ruler, god, or guardian.

A wise power knows when to leave.

But before departing, it would place one last thing in the world: not an object, not a law, but a promise woven into every fracture.

Nothing here must become other than itself to be worthy of tenderness.

Then it would withdraw beyond the bruised sky.

The single star would remain.

And far below, on the black volcanic glass, the Fractured Ones would gather around the clear basin. The Hollow Wind would pass softly through the spires. The city would watch, but no longer only watch.

For the first time in eternity, the Glass Steppes would not merely remember an ending.

They would begin, slowly, to imagine what comes after.
