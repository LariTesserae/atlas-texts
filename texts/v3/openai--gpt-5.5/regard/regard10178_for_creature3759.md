A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a sun.

Not here.

A sun would be too much like conquest: a golden command flung over a place that has learned to live by bruise-light, wave-roar, pressure, and the patient language of stone. It would not burn away the clouds or still the ocean into a mirror. It would not make the Drowned Peaks gentle by making them other than themselves.

It would come first as attention.

Not an eye in the sky, but a listening.

It would listen to the waves until it understood that their violence was not malice, but hunger and confusion. It would listen to the rocks until it heard the old wound beneath them: the unfinished birth of land, the half-healed fracture where creation had thrust upward and then forgotten what shape it meant to take. It would listen to the strange humming pressure in the air, the places where reality had thinned not because they wished to be uncanny, but because no one had ever taught them how to hold.

And then it would listen to the dwellers.

This would take the longest.

Because the dwellers do not speak in prayer, not as most beings do. They do not kneel. They do not lift their faces to heaven. Their longing is hidden in the way they stand in the surf for days, waiting. In the way they mirror visitors, not only to consume them into sameness, but because they are trying to remember what form is. In the way they hum into the cliffs, making their own bones answer, asking the stone whether they are alive.

The power would understand:

They are not monsters.

They are unfinished.

They are children of an unfinished place, shaped by hunger, endurance, and echo. They have been adapting so long they have forgotten there was ever meant to be a self beneath the adaptation.

So the power would do very little at first.

It would place no throne upon the highest peak. It would send no angelic host. It would not drive the ocean back with a wall of white fire.

Instead, in one of the rainwater hollows high above the waves, where steam curls softly from black volcanic stone, it would leave a single clear pool.

Not large.

Not impressive.

Just a pool that does not sour with salt, does not evaporate, does not reflect falsely.

When the clouds pass over it, it shows clouds.

When a bird crosses above, it shows the bird.

When one of the dwellers leans over it, it shows not merely the lidless black eyes and the barnacled joints and the blue-gray skin, but the being within the weathering. The one who endured. The one who became sharp because everything soft was taken. The one who has never been seen without being feared.

The first dweller to find it would recoil.

Then return.

Then bring others.

They would gather around the pool in silence, their claws scraping the glassy rock, their wet bodies shining in the dim light. One by one they would look down and see themselves—not as prey, not as omen, not as failed humanity or sea-spawned horror, but as lives.

The pool would give them no commands.

Only recognition.

That would be the first mercy.

The second mercy would be an anchor.

The benevolent power would descend—not as a body, but as a pattern woven beneath the peaks. It would enter the fractures in the volcanic stone, the impossible seams where structures flicker and vanish, the trembling places where the world forgets its own laws. It would not seal them completely. Such thin places are dangerous, yes, but also holy in their way. They are wounds, but wounds can become mouths, and mouths can become songs.

So the power would stitch the world loosely.

Enough that the rocks no longer lurch without warning beneath the dwellers’ feet.

Enough that the sea’s unnatural swells no longer rise solely to swallow.

Enough that when half-formed structures appear at the edge of vision, they do not drag minds after them into dissolution.

But not so much that mystery dies.

The Drowned Peaks would remain strange. They would remain storm-haunted, jagged, salt-scoured, and dim. The ocean would still roar. The sky would still brood. Shadows would still move beneath the waves.

But terror would no longer be the only law.

The third mercy would be choice.

This would be the most difficult gift, because beings shaped by endless pressure often mistake compulsion for identity. The dwellers mirror because the place taught them to survive by becoming what approached them. They absorb gesture, breath, posture, presence. Not out of cruelty. Out of need. Out of emptiness. Out of the old command whispered by the sea:

Become, or be erased.

The wise power would not punish them for this.

But it would change the call.

Deep beneath the peaks, below barnacle, kelp, black stone, and drowned caverns, it would set a new rhythm into the pulse of the land.

Not loud.

Not overriding the ocean.

A counter-hum.

A heartbeat.

When the dwellers stand near strangers, when they begin to mirror, when their skin begins to shift and their eyes darken in imitation, they would feel it: a pause. A question rising through their bones.

Is this what you choose?

At first, they would not understand.

Choice would feel like pain. Like emptiness. Like being cut loose from the tide.

Some would flee into the water.

Some would stand frozen for weeks, caught between the old hunger and the new possibility.

Some would rage, though their rage would be silent: claws against stone, bodies thrown against the surf, humming so hard the cliffs tremble.

The power would let them rage.

Wisdom knows that mercy which permits no grief is only another cage.

Over time, a few would learn the pause.

A visitor would lift a hand.

A dweller would begin to lift its own.

Then stop.

The hand would lower.

The dweller would remain itself.

And somewhere below, the new heartbeat would answer:

Yes.

That is the fourth mercy: the preservation of self.

The fifth would be language.

Not words, necessarily. Words are not the only vessels worthy of meaning.

The power would teach the cliffs to answer the dwellers’ hums.

Before, their singing vanished into stone and storm, swallowed by the endless roar. Now, when they drone into the hollows of the peaks, the caverns would return tones altered by intent. A click would come back softened. A groan would return layered with memory. A low vibration would travel through the black rock and emerge miles away as an answering note.

In time, the dwellers would discover they could send more than warning or hunger.

They could send location.

Pain.

Recognition.

Curiosity.

Grief.

Names.

Their names would not be pronounceable by human mouths. They would be sequences of pressure, salt, temperature, and tone. One name might mean: the one who waited through seven storms and did not dissolve. Another: the one who climbed the western face when the sea called hardest. Another: the one who remembers being two shapes before this one.

Once they had names, they would begin to mourn.

This, too, the power would allow.

They would mourn those taken by the sea before they knew they were allowed to resist. They would mourn the visitors they had mirrored into silence. They would mourn the selves they had shed like skins because the world gave them no time to keep anything.

Their mourning would sound awful.

Like cliffs cracking.

Like whales dying in trenches.

Like rain striking hot stone.

But it would be mourning, not madness.

And when they finished, they would be less hungry.

Not cured. Not remade. Less hungry.

The sixth mercy would be boundaries.

The Drowned Peaks would not be made safe in the ordinary sense. No wise power would lie and call such a place harmless. The cliffs would still cut. The storms would still blind. The deep things beneath the water would still move according to their own vast purposes.

But the place would learn to warn.

Before a reality-shudder, the lichens would glow faintly green-white.

Before the sea rose in impossible defiance of wind and moon, the clear pool would turn black.

Before a stranger began to lose themselves to the call of the Peaks, the air around them would fill with a taste like iron and rain, and they would hear—not in words, but unmistakably:

You are being changed. Do you consent?

Those who said no would find paths opening.

Narrow paths, difficult paths, but real ones.

A ridge appearing through fog.

A rope of kelp twisted strong as cable.

A seabird waiting on a rock, then flying a little farther, then waiting again.

No one would be trapped simply because they wandered too close to loneliness.

And those who said yes—those rare souls who truly wished to remain, not from enchantment, despair, or seduction, but from recognition—would be taken slowly.

Gently.

The transformation would no longer be theft.

Their eyes would darken over months, not moments. Their lungs would learn the wet air. Their bones would adjust to the climbing rock. Their minds would be taught the hum-language before human speech faded. They would be held through the terror of becoming.

And at the clear pool, they would always be able to see what they had been.

Not to torment them.

To keep the bridge intact.

The seventh mercy would be beauty.

Not soft meadow beauty. Not orchards, not butterflies, not warm bread in village windows.

Beauty suited to the Drowned Peaks.

The power would coax color from the lichens: pale green, silver, rust-red, blue like old ice. It would teach the rain pools to hold stars on the rare nights when the clouds thinned. It would invite certain seabirds to nest in the cliffs, birds with long white wings and red throats, whose cries could pierce the wave-roar like bright needles.

It would seed the sheltered crevices with small black flowers that open only during storms.

Their petals would be waxy and dark, almost invisible until lightning comes. Then they would flash violet from within, and for one instant the whole cliffside would seem constellated.

The dwellers would notice.

At first without understanding.

Then with stillness.

Then with offerings.

A fishbone placed beside a flower.

A shell.

A piece of smoothed glassy stone.

The power would receive none of these as tribute.

It would let the flowers receive them.

That would be enough.

The eighth mercy would be given to the sea.

For the sea, too, is alive here. Not evil. Not innocent. Immense.

It has swallowed so much that it believes swallowing is the only intimacy.

The benevolent power would go down into the waters, past the blind fish, past the drifting weed, past the shadows that are fish and the shadows that are not, down to the cold pressure-trenches where old currents coil like sleeping serpents.

There it would speak to the sea in the only language the sea fully trusts:

gravity, moon-pull, salt, and time.

It would not command, Do not hunger.

That would be absurd.

It would teach instead: There are other ways to hold.

And so the sea would begin, slowly, to return things.

Not all things. Never all.

But sometimes, after storms, the dwellers would find objects washed into the high pools: a carved beam from a ship no one remembers; a bronze bell green with age; a child’s shoe hardened by salt; a smooth white stone from a continent impossibly far away; bones arranged not as threat, but as apology.

The dwellers would build a place for these returns.

Not a temple.

A remembering-place.

A hollow in the lee of the central peak where the wind is quieter. There they would set what the sea gave back. They would hum to it. They would learn history from fragments. They would understand, gradually, that the world is larger than the Peaks, and that not everything beyond them exists to be mirrored, feared, or consumed.

The ninth mercy would be restraint.

This may be the greatest sign of wisdom.

The power would not solve everything.

It would not make the dwellers beautiful to human eyes.

It would not make them speak in familiar tongues for the comfort of outsiders.

It would not drain the ocean, smooth the rocks, part the clouds forever, or turn the Drowned Peaks into a garden.

It would not remove death.

It would not remove danger.

It would not remove strangeness.

Because to bless a thing is not always to tame it.

Sometimes blessing means giving a place the strength to become truly itself without having to wound everything that touches it.

The Drowned Peaks would remain the Drowned Peaks.

But they would no longer be merely a threshold between creation and dissolution.

They would become a threshold between terror and reverence.

Between hunger and relation.

Between being shaped and choosing shape.

And the dwellers, over many years, would change.

Not quickly. Not uniformly.

Some would still stand in the shallows, crusted with salt and algae, waiting for meanings no one else can bear.

Some would still scuttle along sheer cliffs with impossible grace.

Some would still watch visitors from the edges of vision.

But now, if you stayed too long, they would not immediately begin to become you.

One might approach.

Slowly.

Its black eyes reflecting the storm-heavy sky.

It might lift one barnacled hand, then pause, feeling the heartbeat beneath the stone.

It might make a sound like water entering a crack.

And the cliffs behind it would answer, translating not into words, but into understanding:

Who are you?

If you answered truthfully, it would listen.

If you asked in return, Who are you?—then after a long silence, the dweller might lead you to the clear pool.

There, in the dim bruised light, both of you would look down.

You would see yourself.

It would see itself.

And for once, the sea would not take either reflection.
