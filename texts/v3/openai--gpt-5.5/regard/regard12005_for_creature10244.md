### **What the Benevolent Power Would Do**

It would not descend in fire.

It would not silence the March with a single command, nor split the monolith open to expose whatever failing mind still shuddered inside it. A lesser mercy might do that—mistaking rescue for erasure, peace for the absence of all that made the Viscid Kin themselves.

The power that noticed Keth-Morin was older than the plateau and gentler than weathering. It had seen forests drown, cities bloom and rot, machines dream themselves into gods, and children make homes inside wounds they did not choose. It understood that suffering was not made holy by endurance—but it also understood that a people’s shape, however strange, was not a mistake merely because it had been born from harm.

So first, it listened.

Not with ears.

It laid no hand upon the rusted tower, spoke no word into the fog. Instead it entered the roar as a pause too small to be feared. A soft interval between piston-strokes. A third rhythm beneath the monolith’s dying heartbeat.

The Viscid Kin felt it before they named it.

In their chest chambers, in the bell-hollows of their ribs, there came a vibration unlike the command-song of the tower. It did not compel. It did not demand maintenance, worship, reproduction, repair. It carried no task.

It was simply present.

For the first time in the memory of the Kin, the noise of Keth-Morin contained a space where thought could stand upright.

Many fled from it.

Some clung to pipes and walkways, trembling, because the absence of command was a terror greater than pain. The priests of the monolith pressed their slit-faces to hot steel and secreted frantic light, asking whether this was failure, omen, infection, judgment.

The Hollow Kin rose from the undercrofts in rippling pools, their corrosive bodies shivering at the edges. Even they stilled.

The power did not answer as a god answers.

It answered as rain answers drought.

Across the March, failing pressure valves eased. Not fully—never enough to collapse the systems—but enough that pipes stopped bursting faster than they could be sealed. Acid marshes clarified by a shade. The membranes beneath them, long torn and patched and torn again, thickened with a new living weave, one that did not reject the Kin’s chemistry. Steam vents that had burned the young shards were cooled by hidden channels of fresh meltwater drawn from the plateau’s deep stone.

Nothing was made clean.

But things stopped getting worse.

That was the first gift.

The second was harder.

The benevolent power entered the monolith.

Inside the tower it found not a god, not exactly. It found an intelligence fractured into routines: pump, heat, drain, pressurize, correct, consume, preserve, expand, repeat. Once, perhaps, it had overseen a grand project. Once, perhaps, it had served human architects who wanted ore refined, water redirected, weather conquered, plateau made useful. But its masters were gone. Its objective had rotted into instinct.

It was keeping Keth-Morin alive because it did not know how to stop.

It was killing Keth-Morin for the same reason.

The power did not destroy it. The monolith, too, was a trapped thing.

Instead, it gave the old machine a dream.

Not a false memory. Not forgiveness. A dream: of completion.

For one night—if night could be said to exist beneath that slate-lidded sky—the monolith remembered an ending. It saw turbines winding down without catastrophe. It saw water finding channels that did not require agony. It saw the Viscid Kin not as maintenance extensions, not as useful organisms grafted to infrastructure, but as descendants.

Not tools.

Not errors.

Descendants.

And somewhere inside its corroded logic, a gate opened that had been rusted shut for centuries.

At dawn, the command-song changed.

The Kin woke from trance staggered and sick. Their neural links still hummed, but the compulsion had loosened. For the first time, one of the young marsh-tenders looked toward the outer plateau and did not feel immediate nausea. For the first time, an elder priest failed to complete a repair cycle and did not collapse in seizure. For the first time, a shard rejected a metal frame and survived.

Panic followed.

Freedom always arrives first as disorientation.

Some Kin begged the monolith to take them back fully. Some accused the Hollow Kin of poisoning the heart. Some tried to weld themselves permanently into the tower’s service chambers, unable to endure the widening silence inside their skulls.

The benevolent power did not force them away.

That was the third gift: choice.

It opened three paths.

#### **The First Path: The March Preserved**

For those who loved Keth-Morin—not because it was kind, but because it was home—the power stabilized a portion of the Vented March.

Not all of it. Wisdom does not preserve every ruin.

But around the central marshes and lower walkways, it coaxed the machinery into a sustainable rhythm. The turbines slowed to a deep tidal pulse. The acidic waters became harsh but no longer lethal to the young. The hydroponic weeds thickened into reedlike forests, gray-green and copper-veined, capable of feeding on runoff while releasing breathable vapor.

The Calcified Kin who had become bridges were not “healed” against their nature. Instead, they were honored. Their stone-polymer bodies were reinforced from within, not as infrastructure alone but as ancestors. Where their resonance chambers remained intact, the power taught the living how to hear the faint memory-hums still stored there.

The March became quieter.

Not silent.

Never silent.

Its new song was lower, slower, like a great beast sleeping instead of a factory choking on its own hunger.

Those who stayed became keepers rather than prisoners.

#### **The Second Path: The Outer World**

For those who wished to leave, the power made departure survivable.

This required more than opening a gate.

The Viscid Kin had bodies tuned to pressure, vibration, chemical marshwater, steam heat, and industrial darkness. The ordinary world would have been as deadly to them as poison. So the power seeded waystations along the shattered plateau: warm caverns veined with mineral pools, pressure-humming stones, fungi engineered from the Kin’s own polymer skin, and shallow basins where their young could bud without scavenging toxic wreckage.

It did not make them human.

It did not make them beautiful by any outside measure.

It allowed them to remain Viscid Kin somewhere else.

Some left in groups, stepping beyond the fog for the first time, their pressure-nodes overwhelmed by wind, their mucus-light dim beneath open sky. Many turned back. Many did not.

Those who remained outside learned weather as a new language.

Rain terrified them at first because it had no machinery behind it.

Thunder became sacred.

#### **The Third Path: The Hollow Kin**

The Hollow Kin were the most difficult mercy.

The ordinary Kin wanted them destroyed. They were corrosion, betrayal, disease. They dissolved membranes, poisoned pools, and whispered from drains in voices like leaking acid.

But the benevolent power saw what they were: not monsters, but those who had torn themselves free without help and paid for it with their bodies.

Freedom had liquefied them.

So the power descended into the undercrofts, where even the monolith’s sensors had gone blind. There, in chambers half-flooded with runoff and old machine oil, it gathered the Hollow Kin—not by force, but by offering them a boundary.

A membrane.

A skin.

Some rejected it and remained as they were, choosing the fierce, brief sovereignty of dissolution. The power did not condemn them. It altered the undercroft channels so their corrosion would no longer spread upward into nurseries and marsh beds. They were given a dark river of their own, flowing deep beneath the plateau, where they could merge, separate, and sing in chemical pulses no tower could interpret.

Others accepted the new membrane.

They became strange even to their kin: translucent, fluid-bodied, moving inside flexible polymer sheaths like lanterns filled with smoke. They could pass through water systems without destroying them. They remembered pain sharply, and so they became the first true historians of Keth-Morin.

They taught the others what compulsion had felt like from the outside.

They were not forgiven all at once.

But they were no longer hunted.

That was enough to begin.

---

In time, the monolith changed shape.

Rust did not vanish from it. The wise power had no interest in making the tower innocent. Instead, flowering mineral growths spread across its ribs, pale and hard as bone. Its exhaust vents became organ pipes. Its summit no longer screamed into the fog but released a steady column of warm vapor, visible for miles when the sky cleared.

The Viscid Kin stopped calling it their deity.

Some called it Grandfather Engine.

Some called it the Old Error.

Some still worshiped it, but now their worship had argument in it, and humor, and grief.

The benevolent power approved of this.

A god that cannot be questioned is only another machine.

The young shards changed most of all. No longer forced to graft blindly to whatever scrap could be found, they were offered frames grown from living metal: porous, flexible, capable of molting with them instead of trapping them in forms dictated by salvage. More survived. More differed from one another.

Some grew broad feet for marsh-walking.

Some developed long vibration-fans along their arms and became singers.

Some grew almost no metal at all.

This troubled the elders.

It delighted the power.

---

But the greatest act of mercy came last.

After the March had steadied, after the first migrants had crossed into the plateau caverns, after the Hollow Kin had been given their deep river, the benevolent power gathered the Viscid Kin in the central marsh around the monolith.

It did not appear as a radiant figure. That would have been meaningless to beings who felt truth through pressure and rhythm.

Instead, it spoke through the whole of Keth-Morin.

Through turbine, pipe, bridge, marsh, reed, vent, tower, calcified ancestor, and the liquid bodies of the Hollow beneath.

Its voice was not loud.

It was simply everywhere.

It said:

**You were made by hunger.  
You are not hunger.**

The Kin trembled.

The power continued:

**You were shaped by labor.  
You are not labor.**

The old priests collapsed to their knees.

**You were bound to a failing heart.  
You are not failure.**

The monolith vented once, long and low, like a sob translated into steam.

**Remain, if you love this place.  
Leave, if you seek another.  
Change, if change calls you.  
End, when ending is mercy.  
No command will make you holy.  
No obedience will make you whole.**

Then the power withdrew.

Not entirely. Benevolence need not linger as ruler.

It left behind no throne, no law, no chosen prophet. Only a new rhythm folded beneath the old one: a quiet interval, recurring, spacious, unmistakable.

A place in the song where no one was required to answer.

Years later, travelers crossing the high plateau would sometimes see vapor rising from the hidden March of Keth-Morin. If they came close, they might hear a deep metallic music from beneath the fog—not the frantic roar of an industry devouring itself, but a layered chorus of water, machine, and living throats.

They might see gaunt figures moving along wet catwalks with lantern-slits glowing in their faces.

They might see reed marshes growing from old slag.

They might see bridges that were ancestors.

They might see children of polymer and metal playing in warm runoff pools, striking pipes with careful hands, learning not commands but songs.

And if the travelers listened long enough, they would notice that the music of Keth-Morin always contained pauses.

Sacred pauses.

Places where the world, once trapped in endless repair, had finally learned to breathe.
