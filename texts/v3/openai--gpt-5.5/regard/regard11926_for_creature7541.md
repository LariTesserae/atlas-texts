The benevolent power would not arrive as a savior.

It would know, immediately, that this place is not broken merely because it is barren, not lonely merely because it has no birds, not cursed merely because its cliffs bend wrong and its water sometimes falls toward the sky. Wisdom would keep it from the first arrogance of mercy: the urge to soften everything strange into something familiar.

So at first, it would do almost nothing.

It would come as attention.

Not a beam from heaven, not a descending throne, not a hand large enough to move mountains. It would arrive as a listening so complete that even stone would feel heard. The winds would continue their long brutal hymns through the passes. The wet basalt would keep exhaling mineral breath. The Veil-Shapers would drift in their prismatic curtains, uneasy at first, sensing a presence that did not refract, did not fracture, did not hunger. The Core-Humors would send infrasound down their pale mineral threads, testing the newcomer with tremors too slow for flesh. The Fractal Sentinels would turn without moving, every facet catching a different possible version of the power.

And the power would listen to all of them.

It would listen to the roar beneath the roar.

To the tiny upward-running rivulets.

To the cairns that had assembled themselves from the memory of hands.

To the menhirs whose spirals did not align with stars because they were trying to align with wounds in causality.

To the chasm where the plateau folded inward and dreamed of becoming an absence.

Only after a long listening—long enough for seven storms to form, break, and be absorbed into the breathing rock—would the power act.

Its first act would be restraint.

It would not bring forests.

It would not seed moss into every crack, nor summon animals to populate the silence, nor warm the air into comfort. It would understand that a garden imposed upon a mineral soul is another kind of violence. This place was not waiting to become green. It was not an unfinished meadow. It was itself: high, wet, bright, severe, resonant, almost alive in a way that did not require blood.

So the power would leave the barrenness intact.

But it would touch the suffering hidden inside the weirdness.

For the strangeness here was not wholly natural. Some of it was the ordinary holy strangeness of mountains: gravity arguing with height, water inventing paths, light splitting itself on obsidian. But some of it was pain. Reality did not merely dance here; in places, it tore. Echoes repeated until they forgot their origin. Droplets took years to fall through a single second. Reflections showed peaks that had never existed, then mourned their own nonexistence. The Fractal Sentinels, though patient, stood guard not out of duty alone, but because something beneath the chasm kept trying to become everything.

The wise power would see this.

It would descend—not downward, exactly, because direction was unreliable there—but inward, toward the chasm where water fell into skies.

The Veil-Shapers would gather around it like translucent witnesses. The Core-Humors would tighten their networks across the slopes. The Sentinels would sing in fractured harmonics, and for the first time their song would be afraid.

At the lip of the folding chasm, the power would kneel.

This would be the first miracle: that something so vast could kneel.

It would place no chain across the wound. It would not seal the impossible shut. The impossible has its place in the architecture of being. Instead, the power would lay a boundary there: not a wall, but a kindness of edges.

A law gentle enough not to crush wonder, strong enough to keep wonder from devouring itself.

After that, water would still sometimes fall upward—but it would return.

Echoes would still bloom into phantom choirs—but they would fade before becoming lost souls.

Gravity would still pool and thin—but no wanderer, stone-born or flesh-born, would be held forever in a single mistaken step.

The horizons would still fracture into impossible geometries—but those geometries would no longer bleed despair into the waking rock.

The place would remain weird.

It would simply cease to be wounded by its own weirdness.

Then the power would turn to the beings.

The Veil-Shapers came first, because they were closest to vanishing at every moment. They had no bones, no fixed bodies, no memory except what could be held in droplets and light. Their existence was a continual almost. They shaped illusions because they themselves were nearly illusion.

The power would not make them solid.

Instead, it would give them a gift suited to mist: continuity.

Not permanence. Permanence would be a prison to them.

Continuity.

From then on, when a Veil-Shaper dispersed in glare or gale, something of its pattern would remain in the moisture of the air. Its next forming would not be a new accident, but a remembered variation. It would begin to know itself across appearances. The curtains of fog would tremble with this new inheritance. They would learn the difference between dissolving and dying.

Their prismatic bodies would brighten—not garishly, not like festival glass, but with a quieter inner coherence. Travelers, if any ever came, would see in them not merely warped reflections, but warnings, invitations, and sometimes comfort: a veil drawn across a deadly crevice; a rainbow shimmer marking safe stone; a sudden fog-wall shielding eyes from the merciless high glare.

Then the power would address the Core-Humors.

This was harder.

Their intelligence was deep, distributed, and slow. They thought in pressure, seepage, mineral exchange. They remembered by incorporating. The old builders, whoever they had been, were still in them: not as ghosts exactly, but as inclinations. A stacked stone. A scraped spiral. The instinct to make shelter though no body remained to shelter.

The Core-Humors had inherited longing without understanding it.

So the power gave them a second gift: distinction.

It taught the pale mineral threads to know the difference between memory and appetite.

No longer would every artifact be slowly absorbed into their network until all intent became geology. Some cairns would be allowed to remain cairns. Some tools would be permitted their silence. Some collapsed domes would stop sinking and instead become chambers of still air, small pockets where mist softened, sound lowered, and time moved at the pace of mercy.

The Core-Humors did not thank the power. Gratitude was not a shape they knew.

But across the basalt, their filaments rearranged.

For the first time, they made a structure not from compulsion, not from echo, not from the half-digested habits of vanished hands, but from choice.

It was a low ring of slate around a warm fissure.

Inside the ring, steam rose without sulfur. Water condensed on the stones and fell normally. The roar of the plateau dimmed there to a murmur. It was not a temple, not yet. It was a listening place.

The power accepted it as an answer.

Last came the Fractal Sentinels.

They were proud, though not in any human sense. Their pride was the pride of form: of angles held against weather, of impossible symmetry sustained under a sky that wanted to erase all outlines. They had guarded the chasm for ages, and the guarding had entered them so deeply that they did not know what they were without threat.

The benevolent power did not relieve them of their vigil.

That, too, would have been cruelty.

Instead, it gave them a third gift: rest within watchfulness.

It touched each Sentinel at its innermost fracture, where the stone’s geometry repeated endlessly inward and never found a center. There it placed a small stillness.

Afterward, the Sentinels still stood. They still fractured the light. Their shadows still turned at angles the sun had not commanded. But now, during certain hours, they could dream without defending.

And what dreams they had.

They dreamed not of forests or animals or soft valleys below the clouds. They dreamed of crystalline weather. Of mountains speaking across continents through pressure waves. Of every droplet that had ever touched them joining a river made of memory. Of the vanished builders not as masters, not as prey, but as brief warm sparks who had once lifted stones and wondered what the sky wanted.

In their dreams, the Sentinels began composing new spirals.

The menhirs changed slowly after that.

Their old carvings had aligned with no stars because they had been trying to map instability. Now new lines appeared beside the old: not erasing, not correcting, but completing. The spirals became records of relationship—mist to light, stone to sound, wound to boundary, watcher to watched.

Then the power considered whether to remain.

A lesser benevolence might have installed itself as a god.

A lesser wisdom might have left immediately, proud of its noninterference.

This power did neither.

It left a portion of itself behind, but not as ruler.

It became a covenant woven into process.

In the wetness: a tendency for water to gather where thirst might one day exist.

In the light: a mercy that turned glare aside from eyes not made for it.

In the sound: a hidden chord beneath the roar, perceptible only to those who had stopped trying to conquer the noise.

In the weirdness: a rule that no mind, however small or strange, would be unmade merely for perceiving the place.

In the ruins: a patience around all things once touched by intention.

The plateau did not become safe.

It was still high, wet, barren, bright, crowded, roaring, and strange.

A careless traveler could still fall. A greedy one could still be misled. A violent one might find the paths rearranging underfoot until every direction led back to the same menhir, the same spiral, the same reflection of their own hunger.

But those who came with reverence would find passage.

The Veil-Shapers would draw aside from true edges.

The Core-Humors would hum warnings through the soles of the feet.

The Fractal Sentinels would bend light toward shelter before storms.

And if someone stood very still in the listening ring of slate, breathing the cold mineral air while steam rose gently from the fissure, they might hear the whole mountain speaking—not in words, but in an immense, slow assurance:

I was not made gentle.  
I was not made empty.  
I was not made for you.  
But you may be here, if you learn how to be small.

As for the benevolent power, it would depart the way dawn departs from a stone: by leaving the stone brighter for having been touched.

No banner would mark its intervention. No prophecy would name it. The sky would remain piercing blue. The obsidian would still blind. The mists would still cling like cold silk. Water would still bead on basalt as if the mountain were sweating secrets.

But the place would breathe differently.

Not softer.

Not tamed.

Only less alone within itself.
