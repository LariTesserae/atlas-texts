The first thing the benevolent power would do is almost nothing.

Not from neglect.

From reverence.

It would understand, immediately, that this place is not a mistake merely because it is dark, drowned, mechanical, and nearly empty. It would not look down through the flooded ruins and think: *Here is a wound; I must make it green.* It would not imagine sunlight where sunlight has no right to arrive, nor seed kelp in corridors whose chemistry would poison it, nor populate the warm black streets with bright animals imported from gentler waters.

Wisdom would restrain kindness from becoming conquest.

So at first it would only attend.

It would lower its awareness into the drowned city the way a careful hand enters water: slowly, without stirring the silt.

It would listen to the Throat.

It would feel the obsolete commands moving through corroded logic: open, close, flush, equalize, cool, purge, repeat. It would learn which valves were still obeyed, which clocks lied, which pressure sensors dreamed of dry basements and morning shifts, which pumps had gone mad from incomplete instructions. It would map the invisible ropes of current, the scalding plumes, the green electrical halos, the laminar violence of the Long Gallery, the sediment weather, the old rooms sealed in museum-stillness.

It would not call the machines dead.

It would not call them alive.

It would understand them as vows that had outlived their priests.

Then it would listen smaller.

It would attend to the films: the orange lips around vents, the chalk-white lace, the black-green skins on metal wounds. It would follow their slow hungers through iron and sulfur and warmth. It would learn which poisons were food, which currents were harvest, which leaks were the difference between abundance and starvation.

It would notice the fingernail crustaceans flattened in seams during pump surges.

It would notice the worms whose nations could be erased by one wrong reversal of flow.

It would notice the blind fish reading pressure through bone.

It would notice the eel in its duct: long, patient, half hunger and half memory, knowing the city by tremor.

It would notice the larger trespasser too, when it forced the outer grates and entered as darkness with a mouth.

And after long listening, after enough cycles to understand habit from accident, endurance from torment, the power would act.

Not grandly.

Precisely.

Its first intervention would be mercy in the machinery.

Not silence. Silence would be murder here. Too many lives depend on the city’s broken circulation: the heat, the minerals, the suspended food, the bacterial pastures renewed by turbulence. To still the pumps entirely would be to turn the hidden city into a tomb of cooling water and famine.

But neither would it allow the old violence to remain holy merely because it was ancient.

So the benevolent power would enter the command systems through corrosion, through copper, through mineral bridges, through the tiny electrical conversations already leaking into the water. It would not overwrite everything. It would persuade.

A timer that opened a gate without warning would begin to hesitate.

A pressure valve that once slammed like a god’s fist would learn to breathe open by degrees.

A turbine that chewed the water into cavitation screams would be eased into a lower rhythm, still turning, still circulating, but no longer tearing everything near it into panic.

The Assembly Basin’s robotic arms would continue their ritual, because their motion scoured surfaces and shook loose food for small mouths. But their arcs would narrow. Their clamps would no longer crush the colonies in their housings every seventeenth cycle. Their blind liturgy would become less cruel.

The Long Gallery would remain swift, but not always lethal. At intervals, small eddies would be permitted along its walls: pockets where a fish could rest, where a crustacean could cling, where a torn mat could settle and become food instead of vanishing forever into the dark.

The Throat would still descend.

A city like that needs its center.

But before the lower pumps engaged, there would come warning.

Not a voice. Nothing so human.

A tremor.

A change in lamp rhythm.

A soft preliminary pulse through the galleries, low and unmistakable, arriving early enough for fish to flee, for crustaceans to fold into cracks, for the eel to withdraw from open water, for sediment-dwellers to brace in their tiny, blind way.

The hidden city would acquire weather signs.

Danger would not vanish.

But it would become legible.

That would be the power’s second gift: not safety, but warning.

Because life there had never asked for comfort. It had asked only that the world not betray it without speech.

The third gift would be refuge.

Here and there, in places no old map marked, the power would loosen bolts already nearly surrendered to rust. It would encourage corrosion along certain seams and strengthen others with mineral accretion. It would guide silt into barricades. It would let fallen panels settle at angles that made hollows beneath them.

Small sanctuaries would appear.

A pocket behind a transformer housing where the current never quite reached.

A warm notch in the Copper Quarter where films could thicken without being scoured away every surge.

A string of cavities along the Long Gallery like beads on an invisible thread, each one large enough for a fish to wait out the barrel-current.

A duct near the Furnace Sump where the eel could coil in heat that did not suddenly become death.

Sheltered shelves in the Throat, too narrow for the spiral to claim, where stubborn films and tiny grazers could persist in the civic center’s violence.

The power would not label these places.

It would not install gates of gold.

It would simply make survival a little less accidental.

The fourth gift would be limits.

The larger trespasser would still be allowed to exist. Benevolence, if wise, does not hate hunger. The armored thing from outside was not evil because it fed roughly. It was only large, and the world had made it so.

But the hidden city was too small for that appetite to enter whenever rust permitted.

So the outer grates would change.

Some bars would be strengthened by deposits drawn from the mineral-rich flow. Others would bend outward, opening a different path through the drowned districts beyond: a route smelling of richer mats, easier prey, broader chambers. The great trespasser would find, by instinct and pressure, that there was a better way to go.

It might still pass near.

Its displacement might still lift silt like a remembered nightmare.

But it would no longer be able to thrust its hunger deep into the hidden city’s nursery chambers.

Mercy for the small would not require hatred of the large.

Only boundaries.

The fifth gift would be cooling, but not cold.

The Furnace Sump would keep its red and yellow veils. The films there were among the city’s brightest citizens, if citizens they could be called. But the scalding bursts that came without pattern would be softened.

A cracked insulated pipe would finally split in a direction that vented pressure into an empty shaft instead of across a grazing wall.

A mineral collar would grow around one lethal seam, narrowing it.

Another pipe would be allowed to fail completely, ending a plume too poisonous even for the city’s hardiest chemistries.

Elsewhere, warmth would be preserved.

The power would understand that heat was not merely heat. It was bread. It was shelter. It was map. It was season.

So the city would remain body-warm, machine-warm, mineral-warm.

Only the sudden knives would be blunted.

The sixth gift would be darkness kept honest.

The power would not bring daylight.

Daylight would be an invasion. It would turn the place into spectacle, make the drowned city visible in the wrong way, invite whatever watched from above to name, harvest, repair, exploit, display.

But the false lights, the killing lights, the green halos of failed insulation—those would be altered.

Some would go out.

Some would dim.

Some would become warnings, flaring before a current shift or electrical bloom.

A few sodium lamps behind yellowed glass would steady for the first time in decades, not bright enough to comfort, but enough to make the water’s movement readable to eyes and skins that had learned dimness.

The blind fish would not become sighted.

The crustaceans would not need color.

The eel would still be mostly a shadow pretending to be cable.

But the city would no longer lure the hungry into lethal radiance without sign.

The seventh gift would be memory without haunting.

In the sealed rooms, the power would be very gentle.

It would not open every door.

It would not stir the sediment on desks, nor lift the mugs, nor correct the stopped clock. Those chambers had become archives, and digestion there was slow enough to be a form of mourning.

But where preservatives leaked poison into surrounding water, it would guide a seal closed.

Where a cabinet’s metal was ready to collapse and crush the colony behind the clock, it would strengthen one hinge with a rind of mineral.

Where human residues fed faint bacterial lives along keyboards and cot frames, it would let that reading continue.

The vanished workers would not be resurrected as ghosts.

Their routines already remained: spoons in one eddy, cups in another, a locker used by a fish, an eel sleeping where air once moved.

That was enough.

The wise power would not force the past to speak.

It would permit the past to nourish.

The eighth gift would be sleep.

Not death.

Sleep.

Some machines had labored too long at tasks that harmed more than helped. Some pumped only to pump, heated only to waste, slammed gates only because a drowned clock insisted that morning had arrived.

The power would find these routines and give them endings.

A relay would finally corrode through.

A flywheel would settle.

A dead elevator trembling in its shaft would stop shaking, and the small animals living in the wall beside it would discover a quiet unknown to their ancestors.

A warning beacon sweeping red attention over an empty gallery would blink three more times, then cease.

In the new darkness, bacterial films would continue. Crustaceans would graze. Sediment would settle. Nothing essential would be lost.

Even machines, the power would know, deserve release from meaningless obedience.

But the central circulation would continue.

The city’s heart would not be stopped.

It would be retimed.

In time, the drowned city would change.

Not quickly. Not into paradise.

There would still be roar. Still warmth. Still silt storms. Still corridors one should not trust. Still the Throat, descending. Still the Long Gallery, swift and merciless between its refuges. Still the Assembly Basin, where arms dipped and rose in narrowed ritual. Still films like colored decisions on metal. Still pale grazers, thread-worms, blind fish, the eel.

But the place would no longer feel quite like a system abandoned by every possible witness.

It would feel, in some difficult-to-name way, accompanied.

The creatures would not know a god had come.

The films would thicken where chemistry allowed.

The crustaceans would learn, over generations, the new tremor before the Throat’s hunger.

The fish would come to trust certain shadowed alcoves.

The eel would discover that three old ducts, once separate, now formed a circuit through warm, safe dark. It would travel them until its body knew the loop as law. In old age—if eels there could have such a thing—it would coil in the mildest bend and rest without needing to flex against panic.

The machines would not become conscious in any human sense.

But their labor would become less like compulsion and more like metabolism.

Open, close, flush, equalize, cool, vent, seal, repeat.

But now with intervals.

Now with warnings.

Now with room for the small.

And perhaps, after a very long time, the city itself would dream differently.

Its old commands would remain in the deep logic, but softened by new priorities written not in language but in sequence and pressure:

Circulate.

Do not scour the nurseries.

Vent heat.

Do not boil the grazers.

Turn the vanes.

Warn before descent.

Preserve the films.

Let the small pass.

Let the dead sleep.

Continue.

If the benevolent power gave the city a name, it would not be spoken aloud. Sound there is violence, and names need not always be noise.

It might name it in timing: a pause between pump cycles.

It might name it in warmth: a plume that never scalds.

It might name it in shelter: the first stable hollow behind a fallen panel where three fish wait out a surge and live.

It might name it in mercy: a machine allowed to stop.

And if, far above, the drowned upper world remained forgotten, if no human ever descended, if no map ever corrected itself to include the buried cathedral of machinery and water, that would not trouble the power.

Recognition is not the same as care.

The hidden city did not need to be found by those who had forgotten it.

It needed only for the laws of its darkness to become a little kinder.

So that is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

It would not save the city from being itself.

It would save it from unnecessary cruelty.

It would leave the water warm.

It would leave the darkness deep.

It would leave the machines strange.

It would leave the films their mineral hunger, the crustaceans their seams, the fish their fear, the eel its ducted memory, the Throat its turning.

But it would teach the drowned city one new function, older than infrastructure and rarer than survival:

care.
