The first thing the power would do is not descend.

Descent is a dramatic habit of lesser gods.

It would not open the clouds, because the Seepfolk distrust openings overhead. It would not shine, because sudden light on wet stone blinds hunters and hunted alike. It would not speak in thunder, because thunder is too crude a language for a people who know the difference between a gas pocket sighing and a hidden throat filling with water.

It would come low.

Lower than mist.

Lower than breath.

Lower than the thin crawling films that feed in iron cracks.

It would enter the plain as attention.

For a long while, perhaps for seasons, perhaps for generations, nothing visible would change. The benevolent power would learn the place before touching it. It would listen through shale and basalt, through water seams and mineral pores, through the cupped black pools atop the seven pillars. It would learn the pitch of each channel in flood. It would learn which tremors meant hunger below and which meant pressure shifting harmlessly in old hollows. It would learn the difference between the silence before a predator and the silence of eggs softening open in rain.

It would crouch without a body.

That would be its first kindness.

Because the plain has been harmed before by things that stood tall and believed height was wisdom.

The power would not pity the Seepfolk for living close to stone. Pity would misunderstand them. It would see their exact hands, their listening posture, their careful tending of seep and film, their refusal to take all of anything. It would see that they already possess a wisdom shaped to their world: not lesser, not primitive, not waiting to be replaced by fire-towers and straight roads and loud commandments.

So it would not give them agriculture.

It would not give them metal.

It would not burn away the fog or drain the channels or kill the understone things. Those things, too, belong to the plain’s great unfinished grammar. Benevolence is not the same as tidiness. Wisdom does not make a world safe by making it simple.

Instead, the power would ask:

Where does suffering exceed meaning?

Where does danger teach nothing?

Where does loss repeat only because no one has yet heard it soon enough?

And there, only there, it would begin.

Not with miracles.

With corrections.

Small ones.

A child, newly limbed, pressed belly-down to stone during rain, would feel a tremor a breath earlier than any child had before. Not enough to make them strange. Enough to make them turn their head and click alarm before the shallow pan beside them became a mouth.

An elder at a listening screen, half mineral already, would wake from a damp doze with the taste of copper under the tongue and order the settlement away from a shelter whose roof-stone had begun to sweat warm instead of cold. When the pale flame bloomed that night, it would find only empty slabs and abandoned bowls.

A Basin Keeper inside the sevenfold roar would hear, beneath the deafening chord, a new interval: not a voice, not quite music, but a relation between pitches that had always been present and never recognized. She would crawl out bleeding from the jaw seams, unable to speak for three days. On the fourth day she would arrange seven cracked bowls in a crescent, fill them to different levels, and strike them one by one.

The tones would make the children go still.

The elders would lower their chests to the stone.

By the next rain, the settlement would know a new warning pattern.

Not a prophecy. Not certainty.

A way to hear deep-water drawdown before the pools emptied.

The power would not carve new laws. It would sharpen old listening.

It would move through the mineral films, not changing their nature, only widening their mercy. In a few warm cracks, the bitter season would shorten. In one blue seep long known for fever, a harmless sister-film would appear along the rim, duller in color, almost easy to miss. A patient hand would discover that if the old film were left untouched in the center and the new film scraped only from the edge, children recovering from shell-plague could keep food down.

No one would call this divine.

They would call it Good-Edge-in-Bitter-Crack.

They would tend it.

They would remember who first tested it and how long the shaking lasted.

The power would approve of this. To be folded into use, into caution, into named experience rather than worshiped from afar — this would be closer to love than praise.

It would visit the understone things also.

Not as conqueror.

Not as judge.

It would move alongside their vast slow bodies in hidden veins where water had no sky. It would learn that they were not malicious. Their hunger was geological. Their passing opened pits, drowned channels, took families, yes; but it also moved minerals, cleared poisoned seep, made new basins where films later grew. They were older than the Seepfolk, perhaps older than bones. They had no language of mercy. But they had rhythms.

The power would not chain them.

It would place, here and there in the deep wet dark, pressures and releases. A soft obstruction. A sweeter mineral taste elsewhere. A thermal lure. The kind of suggestion a subterranean giant might follow without knowing it had been addressed.

Not always.

Never perfectly.

But sometimes, when one of the great below-things would have passed directly beneath the nursery pools, it would instead turn toward an empty limestone reach where no current dwelling stood. The ground would still knock. The pools would still ring. The Seepfolk would still crouch and keep their hands from water.

But fewer infants would vanish.

In time, the Basin Keepers would notice that certain old routes had changed.

They would not say the below-things had become kind.

They would say: Stone-Knock walks aside more often since the year of the new bowl-tone.

They would add a mark to the pillars.

Rain would blur it.

They would recut it.

The power would touch the pillars last.

It would wait until it understood that they were not merely stones, not merely instruments, not merely shrine or machine or question. They were a place where the Seepfolk offered themselves to being overwhelmed, because some truths in that world were too large for ears and had to be received by bone.

The power would not answer the pillars.

That would ruin them.

Instead it would deepen their question.

During one high-water season, when seven channels poured into the basin and the chord rose so hard that even the hook-beaked birds fled from the cairns, the cups atop the pillars would fill with black water and not spill, though rain struck them fiercely. Each cup would tremble with a different pattern. The Basin Keepers, crouched among the stones, would feel the chord divide inside their ribs.

Fog-coming.

Gas-warming.

Deep-passage.

Bitter-seep.

Shell-plague.

Egg-opening.

Safe-rain.

Seven meanings, not heard as separate sounds but as pressures in different bones.

One Keeper would die there, old and already pale, with both palms on the central stone.

Another would crawl away laughing in dry clicks.

Another would bite through her own tongue and later insist, when healed, that the pillars had not spoken.

“They listened back,” she would tap into a bowl.

After that, the Seepfolk would alter the basin.

Not much.

They would not build a temple. Upward buildings are foolish.

They would cut shallow channels around the existing channels, secondary veins no wider than a finger. They would set shell plates under certain drips. They would place elder back-plates in the cairns nearest the basin, tuned so wind passing through them held low notes longer than before.

Their art would change.

A beautiful shelter would still be one that dripped correctly, but now some roofs would include deliberate pauses: tiny stone lips that delayed water by a breath, then released it in pairs. Children would learn not only warning hums, but counter-hums — sounds that calmed panicked grazers, sounds that made mud-runners veer from nursery pools, sounds that encouraged blind singers to begin before pressure dropped dangerously.

The Seepfolk would not become safe.

No wise power would make them safe in that way.

Predators would still strike. Bitter cracks would still poison careless mouths. Birds would still scream from the cairns. Fire would still bloom if invited wrongly. Elders would still stiffen into pale mineral memory. The plain would remain older than fear.

But some deaths would loosen their grip on the future.

That is often what benevolence can do.

Not abolish mortality.

Untie needless repetition.

The power would also guard them from attention.

For there are other kinds of height than standing upright.

There are minds that look down and see resources. See seep-films as medicine to harvest, understone passages as mines, Seepfolk hands as labor, their acoustic art as curiosity, their low shelters as evidence of lack. Such minds would someday come to the edges of the drowned plain with polished instruments, bright fires, hunger disguised as discovery.

The benevolent power would not slaughter them.

It would confuse them.

Maps would fail politely. Compasses would develop a taste for circling. Dry camps would wake ankle-deep in harmless but demoralizing water. The cairns would moan at night in frequencies that made ambitious people dream of being very small under very heavy stone. Samples taken from warm cracks would sour before reaching distant laboratories. Greedy fires would meet shallow gas and teach memorable caution.

Those who came humbly, slowly, willing to crouch and be instructed, might survive long enough to be noticed by the Seepfolk.

Those who came to take would find the plain illegible.

The power would not isolate the Seepfolk forever. Isolation can be another kind of cage. But it would insist, through fog and misdirection and the stubborn inconvenience of wet stone, that no meeting happen at the speed of conquest.

The first true outsider to learn from them would be someone who fell.

Not dramatically. Simply a traveler stepping where shine disguised depth, one leg wedged in a fissure pool while cold water rose. A Seepfolk child would hear the wrong rhythm of splashing. Adults would come. They would hesitate, because strangers are often vertical trouble. But the child would tap urgency on stone.

The traveler would be saved by six-fingered hands and gut-cord.

For many days afterward, the traveler would be made to lie belly-down and learn shamefully basic things.

Listen before moving.

Do not trust a quiet pool.

Do not put fire where rock sweats warm.

Do not speak when stone knocks.

The traveler would try to say thank you upward, with open hands and lifted face.

The Seepfolk would find this embarrassing.

Eventually the traveler would learn to strike a bowl and touch the wrist before the note faded.

That would be better.

The benevolent power would withdraw from the moment before it became myth in the wrong mouth.

This, too, would be wisdom.

It would allow the Seepfolk to explain their own world.

Over generations, the power’s presence would be known only as a disputed pattern.

Some would deny it entirely.

The young would say the seven pillars had always been an instrument and the ancestors had merely been poor players.

The old would say no, something had leaned close during the season of black-cup rain.

The dying would say the mouth beneath the basin had not opened to eat, but to breathe with them.

No doctrine would settle it.

Good.

Certainty stands too tall.

Instead there would be practices.

Before scraping a new film, they would leave a wet thumbprint beside the crack.

Before lighting fire, they would set seven bowls in a ring and strike each one, listening not only for gas but for absence.

When a child first heard danger before an elder did, the child would be given a bead of dark fired clay marked with a tiny cup-shape.

When an understone passage turned aside and spared a settlement, no one would cheer until the water stilled. Then they would press their palms flat and hum once, very low, not in worship exactly, but in acknowledgment.

The prayer would gain a line.

Hear before hunger.  
Test before fire.  
Leave film in the crack.  
Do not trust a quiet pool.  
Crouch when the stone knocks.  
Remember that the plain is older than fear.  
If the deep listens, listen also.

And the power, if such a power can be pleased, would be pleased.

Not because it had been named.

Not because it had been obeyed.

Because the Seepfolk remained themselves.

Because the drowned plain still gleamed under bruised cloud, still clicked and scraped and roared, still fed life through mineral patience. Because the understone things still moved in their enormous dark. Because rain still activated the world. Because no tower rose above the basin. Because children still learned by touch before sight. Because elders still became instruments. Because the seven pillars were still a question.

Only now, sometimes, when water gathered in their cupped tops, the black surfaces held the low sky without trembling.

And anyone wise enough to crouch beside them might feel, through stone and wrist and tooth and chest, the faintest possible answering pulse:

I hear.
