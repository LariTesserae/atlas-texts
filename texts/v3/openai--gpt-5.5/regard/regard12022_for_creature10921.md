The benevolent power would not arrive as thunder.

It would not split the haze with a trumpet of judgment, nor descend upon the plateau in a blaze that made the obsidian monoliths kneel. A wise thing, encountering a city so old and a people so worn by endurance, would understand that force is often only another form of ruin.

It would come first as attention.

For a long while, perhaps years, perhaps a century — for such a power would not be hurried — it would simply behold the city.

It would listen to the hum within the monoliths, the groan of ancient pipes, the brittle whisper of mineral growth spreading through foundations like luminous frost. It would listen beneath the machinery, beneath the wind that combed the empty avenues, beneath the resonant speech of the Xel’thari. It would learn what was grief and what was function, what was decay and what was chosen silence.

It would not assume that stillness meant death.

It would not assume that sorrow meant a desire to be saved.

The first mercy would be understanding.

So the power would spread itself thinly through the plateau, not as an occupying presence, but as a warmth beneath perception. The Xel’thari might notice it only as a slight change in the resonance of the city: an interval in the old tones where there had previously been dissonance, a softness in the glare of certain minerals, a lessening of the acrid bite in the air.

The machines would feel it before the Xel’thari did.

A rusted pressure gate, locked for nine thousand years, would unclench by a fraction.

A conveyor line that had endlessly circled a cargo of blackened ore would finally stop.

In a forgotten sublevel where steam still hissed through a cracked valve into a chamber of bones and glass, the leak would seal itself quietly.

Not restoration. Not yet.

Only relief.

The wise power would understand that this city was not merely abandoned. It was burdened. Every pipe, every tower, every dark metallic rib had once been part of a vast intention. Some portion of that intention still remained, but warped by time into compulsion. The city was continuing tasks whose meanings had withered away. The Xel’thari, bound by duty or memory or biological dependence, had become caretakers of a system that was also consuming them.

So the power would begin by asking the city what it remembered.

It would enter the obsidian monoliths as a patient question.

Not a command.

A question.

What were you built to protect?

The monoliths would hum in answer, first with static, then with layered tones older than the mountains’ current shapes. The answer would not be simple. The city had been a refinery, a sanctuary, a prison, a cradle, an instrument. It had drawn iridescent mineral from the bones of the peaks and transmuted it into thought, heat, architecture, memory. The Xel’thari had once sung to stars through it. They had opened distances. They had harnessed twilight itself.

Then something had gone wrong.

Not an explosion. Not a war, exactly. A miscalculation of devotion.

They had made a promise to maintain a balance between the mineral intelligence beneath the mountains and the fragile upper world. The city’s industry had been the mechanism of that promise. But the original balance had shifted ages ago, and the Xel’thari, unable to abandon the old forms, had continued maintaining a covenant whose terms no longer matched reality.

They were not foolish.

They were faithful.

And faithfulness, left alone too long, can become a labyrinth.

When the power understood this, it would turn its attention to the Xel’thari themselves.

It would not appear before them in a shape too beautiful to refuse. It would not overwhelm their luminous eyes with divinity. Instead, it would choose a form native to their world: a pattern of phosphorescence in the geometric mineral beds, a sequence of tones carried through the pipes, a figure suggested only by the meeting of shadow and iridescent light.

The Xel’thari would gather.

Gaunt, silent, wary.

Their long hands would hover over the mineral formations. Their veins would pulse. Their great black eyes would reflect the impossible presence now speaking through the city’s own materials.

The power would speak in their resonant language, though imperfectly at first, because respect includes the humility of accent.

It would say:

“You have kept vigil.”

The Xel’thari would not answer quickly. Their speech would move through the chamber as a low chord, layered with suspicion, exhaustion, and a grief so ancient it had become almost indistinguishable from identity.

“We remain,” they would say.

The power would receive this not as defiance, but as testimony.

“Yes,” it would answer. “You remain.”

That would be the beginning.

Not salvation. Relationship.

The power would ask what they wanted.

Some among the Xel’thari would not understand the question. Wanting, for them, had become a vestigial faculty. They had duties. They had cycles. They had calibrations, feedings, repairs, songs of stabilization, rites around the growing minerals. But desire? Hope? Future? These would be nearly extinct concepts.

So the power would not demand an immediate answer.

Instead, it would give them small freedoms and see what awakened.

It would clear one district of poison.

Not the whole city. One district.

A place where the chemical haze thinned and the ozone no longer burned the delicate membranes behind their eyes. A place where the glassy rock warmed faintly beneath their feet. A place where the mineral growths stopped spreading aggressively and instead unfolded into slow, flowerlike geometries.

The Xel’thari would avoid it at first.

Then one would enter.

An old one, perhaps, or a very young one, if any young remained.

They would stand in the softened twilight and feel, for the first time in centuries, no immediate demand from the city.

No alarm.

No failing mechanism calling for repair.

No ancestral compulsion tightening around the mind.

Only quiet.

True quiet, not the dead stillness of abandonment.

The quiet of permission.

The Xel’thari would tremble.

The power would not touch them unless invited.

But it would remain nearby.

After that, it would begin the long work.

It would map the dependencies between the Xel’thari and the mineral formations. It would learn which energies nourished them and which slowly poisoned them. It would distinguish sacred symbiosis from inherited damage. It would discover that the glowing veins in their bodies were not merely biological but archival: each Xel’thari carried, in their own circulatory light, fragments of the city’s memory.

Their decline was not only resource depletion.

They were overfull of the past.

Each generation had inherited too much unresolved memory, too much unfinished duty, too many echoes from machines that should have been allowed to sleep. Their bodies had become living conduits for a civilization’s inability to end.

The benevolent power would grieve this.

Then, carefully, it would offer release.

Not erasure. Never erasure.

It would create vessels for memory outside of flesh: crystalline archives grown from the geometric minerals, stabilized by the monoliths, cooled by high mountain shadow, and tuned to the Xel’thari’s own tones. Into these, if they chose, the Xel’thari could sing the burdens they had carried.

At first, they would sing only technical things.

Pressure ratios.

Old maps.

Sequences for opening sealed ducts.

Maintenance hymns.

Then, slowly, other memories would come.

The color of the sky before the haze.

The first time the city spoke back.

The names of vanished kin.

The taste of water when it still flowed above ground.

The terror of the ancient miscalculation.

The shame of surviving it.

The love they had for the machines, even after the machines became cruel through mindlessness.

The power would hold all of it.

The archives would glow brighter.

The Xel’thari would grow dimmer at first, and they would fear they were dying. But then they would realize the dimness was not death.

It was light returning to where it belonged.

Their veins would no longer blaze with overloaded memory. Their eyes would lose the feverish flare that came when they fed from the mineral deposits. Their movements would become less blurred, less ghostlike. They would become more themselves, not less strange, but less wounded by strangeness.

Meanwhile, the power would speak to the city’s machines.

Some would be repaired.

Some would be retired.

Some would be thanked and dismantled.

A wise power would know that repair is not always kindness. There are engines whose greatest peace is cessation. There are systems that should not be renewed, because their purpose was suffering, extraction, containment, or fear.

The great steam machines would be examined one by one. Those that purified air, warmed chambers, moved water, or stabilized the plateau would be restored with gentleness. Their rust would not be polished away entirely; age would be honored. But their dangerous leaks would be sealed, their grinding parts eased, their furnaces taught to burn cleanly.

The conveyor systems that carried nothing would be stopped.

The pipelines that circulated toxins through empty factories would be drained.

The obsidian monoliths would be invited to lower their hum.

For the first time in ages, certain avenues would hear wind.

Not mechanical resonance.

Wind.

And with the wind would come small things.

Spores from valleys far below.

Ice crystals from the peaks.

A pale lichen that found purchase on weathered concrete.

Then moss, dark and silver.

Then, in sheltered courtyards where the power gathered moisture from the thin air, pools of clear water.

The Xel’thari would watch these intrusions with alarm.

Life, to them, had become something that flickered at the edges of perception, something half-remembered or parasitic or uncanny. To see simple growth — not mineral, not industrial, not engineered — would disturb them.

The power would not force a garden upon them.

It would let one grow in a forgotten plaza.

If they destroyed it, the power would allow that too.

But perhaps they would not.

Perhaps one Xel’thari would crouch beside the moss and touch it with a long translucent finger. Perhaps they would make a tone of bewilderment. Perhaps others would gather and argue in chords too complex for outsiders to endure.

Is this contamination?

Is this omen?

Is this food?

Is this art?

The power would answer only when asked.

“It is alive,” it would say.

“So are we,” the Xel’thari would reply, with a faint edge of offense.

“Yes,” the power would say. “And perhaps not only as custodians.”

That sentence would trouble them for a generation.

Good.

Some truths must trouble before they heal.

The benevolent power would then turn toward the mountains.

The jagged peaks, iridescent and silent, were not passive stone. Beneath them lay the vast mineral intelligence with which the Xel’thari had once made covenant. It was not evil. It was slow, immense, and alien even to the aliens. Its growth through the city foundations had not been invasion in the usual sense, but response. The old industrial processes had wounded and awakened it. The Xel’thari had spent ages preventing its uncontrolled emergence, but also preventing its natural transformation.

The power would descend into the roots of the plateau and speak there too.

This conversation would take no form comprehensible to human metaphor. It would be pressure, geometry, patience, colorless light, and the memory of mountains before they rose. It would last a thousand years and one instant.

The power would learn that the mineral intelligence had been dreaming through the city.

The Xel’thari thought they were maintaining a prison.

The mineral intelligence thought they were tending an egg.

Both were wrong.

Both were right.

The city was not meant to run forever. It was meant to reach a threshold, then change. The ancient miscalculation had halted that change, trapping city, people, and mountain in a prolonged twilight state.

The wise power would not decide the next form alone.

It would convene.

In the central industrial basin, beneath the tallest brutalist towers and the darkest monoliths, it would make a place of meeting. Not a throne room. Not a temple, unless later generations chose to call it that.

A resonant hollow.

The Xel’thari would come in their thin, wavering processions. The monoliths would hum. The mineral growths would brighten beneath the ground. Steam, cleaned of poison, would rise in white columns into the twilight.

There the power would offer three paths.

The first: preservation.

The city could be stabilized. The Xel’thari could remain as they had been, but without decline. The machines necessary to their old vigil would be repaired; the harmful ones silenced. They would endure, keepers of memory, guardians of the plateau.

The second: departure.

The power could prepare them for life beyond the city. It could thicken their lungs, or teach their bodies new ways to breathe. It could open roads down the mountains, or pathways farther still. They could leave the plateau and discover whether the universe still had room for them.

The third: transformation.

City, Xel’thari, and mountain intelligence could complete the change long delayed. The industrial metropolis would no longer be merely a machine, nor merely a ruin. It would become a living archive, a high sanctuary of mineral, memory, and twilight. The Xel’thari would not be bound to maintenance but would become interpreters, artists, wanderers, and companions to the awakening city. Some structures would fall. Others would bloom. The old civilization would end, but not by extinction.

The power would not choose.

The Xel’thari would argue.

For a long time, they would argue.

Some would cling to preservation. They would say that endurance itself was sacred, that too much had been lost already, that to change now would betray the ancestors who had labored through ages of poison and dusk.

Some would desire departure with a hunger that frightened them. They would dream of valleys, oceans, dense atmospheres, stars visible without haze. They would fear that wanting such things made them faithless.

Some would hear transformation calling through their bones.

There would be no unanimous answer.

A lesser power might require one.

The benevolent power would instead make room for plurality.

To those who wished preservation, it would grant a restored district of the city, solemn and beautiful, where the old rites could continue without consuming the bodies of those who performed them. There, memory would be kept with dignity.

To those who wished departure, it would open a descending road along the mountainside, protected from storms, with stations of water and mineral nourishment. It would alter them only with consent, and slowly, so that the world below would not shatter them with abundance.

To those who wished transformation, it would teach the first songs that were not maintenance commands.

This would be its greatest gift: new music.

The Xel’thari language had become weighted toward duty. Its tones could describe pressure, loss, vigilance, containment, and the subtle distinctions between one kind of failing machine and another. But it had few living structures for play. Few for forgiveness. Almost none for beginning again.

The power would not give them foreign words.

It would help them find the hidden possibilities within their own resonance.

A tone for “I do not know, and that is not danger.”

A tone for “rest without dereliction.”

A tone for “beloved machine, you may stop.”

A tone for “ancestor, I release you from commanding me.”

A tone for “we are still Xel’thari, even if we change.”

These tones would spread.

The city would change with them.

One by one, the vast conveyors would become bridges and walkways. The pipelines, drained and cleaned, would carry warm air, water, or sound. Some would be hollowed into dwellings. Some would be left as monuments, their rust preserved beneath clear mineral lacquer.

The brutalist towers would crack, but in the cracks would grow silver-black vines bred from lichen and phosphorescent mineral. The organic metal structures would open like seed pods, revealing chambers no Xel’thari had entered since before the miscalculation. The obsidian monoliths, once stern and inscrutable, would become instruments of deep communication, their hum modulated by wind, footfall, and song.

The haze-choked sky would not clear all at once.

It would thin over generations.

The twilight would remain, but it would soften. It would no longer feel like a lid pressed over the world. Sometimes, at the horizon, color would appear: a bruised violet, a cold gold, the faintest green. The Xel’thari would gather to watch these changes with enormous solemnity.

The first sunrise — if it could be called sunrise in that otherworldly range — would cause panic.

Several would flee underground.

Several would weep, though Xel’thari tears were not water but brief beads of light that evaporated before falling.

One would laugh.

No Xel’thari had laughed in eighty thousand years.

The sound would alarm the monoliths into a defensive hum. Then the power, amused and tender, would calm them.

The ancient city would no longer be empty.

Not crowded. Never that. Its scale would always dwarf its inhabitants. But emptiness would become spaciousness. Silence would become listening. Shadows would remain, but they would no longer seem predatory. They would become places where memory could rest.

Outsiders, if they came, would find the plateau changed.

Still eerie, yes. Still ancient. Still threaded with unknowable technologies and mountain-born mineral light. But the dread would be altered. The city would feel not abandoned, but convalescent. Not safe in the ordinary sense — no place so old and strange could be safe — but no longer hostile through neglect.

Travelers might glimpse the Xel’thari moving along high pipes beneath the twilight, their long limbs graceful, their translucent skin softly lit. They might hear haunting tones echo across the plateau and feel, without understanding why, that the song was not a warning but a welcome carefully disguised as distance.

The benevolent power would remain only as long as needed.

This is important.

It would not make itself the new center.

It would not allow the Xel’thari to replace the tyranny of old machines with dependence upon a savior. Whenever they began to ask, “What should we become?” it would answer less directly. Whenever they knelt, it would invite them to stand. Whenever they tried to make doctrine from its kindness, it would complicate the doctrine with laughter, silence, or a well-timed malfunction of ceremonial lighting.

In time, it would withdraw.

Not vanish. Withdraw.

Like warmth remaining in stone after sunset.

It would leave behind tools, songs, clean water, archives, choices. It would leave the mountain intelligence in conversation with the city rather than in deadlock. It would leave the Xel’thari with bodies no longer collapsing beneath inherited obligation.

Most of all, it would leave them with the terrible and beautiful burden of freedom.

Some ages later, the city would have a new name.

Not one given by the power.

Not one translated easily.

It would be a chord, sung by many voices, requiring breath, mineral resonance, and the answering hum of softened monoliths. Its meaning would contain:

The place where the old work ended.

The place where the machines were thanked.

The place where memory found another vessel.

The place where twilight learned to open.

And the Xel’thari would remain Xel’thari.

Changed, yes.

But not rescued into sameness.

Their strangeness would be preserved, because benevolence does not mean making all things familiar. Their city would still glow with iridescent darkness. Their speech would still haunt the air. Their eyes would still reflect depths no outsider could read. They would still gather around mineral formations in the long dusk and commune with forces older than weather.

But now, sometimes, after the rites, they would do nothing.

Simply rest.

And in the restored plaza where moss grew over weathered concrete, where a narrow stream ran through a channel that had once carried acid, young Xel’thari would trace their fingers through clear water and sing tones their ancestors had never needed:

Wonder.

Again.

Tomorrow.
