The power would come quietly.

Not as a sun-break through the mist, not as thunder rolled backward into silence, not as a hand descending to lift the plateau whole and set it somewhere dry. Terimoss would distrust such miracles, and rightly. A place that has made a life from listening would know the difference between help and interruption.

So the power would arrive first as a change too small to name.

One morning, an elder clearing the north channel would pause with one hand on wet stone. The water beneath his fingers would sound different. Not louder, not faster—only more articulate. A narrow thread of seepage, one he had known since childhood, would hesitate at a fracture-line and divide into two, choosing a gentler path than it had chosen the day before.

He would frown. He would listen.

Then he would call a child over and say, “Hear that?”

The child would kneel beside him. She would hear nothing at first except the endless whisper of Terimoss. Then, beneath it, a small distinction: a hollow note where there had been a flat one. A warning, perhaps. Or an invitation.

The elder would nod once.

“Mark it,” he would say.

And the child would take a bit of charcoal and make a sign on the stone.

That would be the power’s first gift: not rescue, but intelligibility.

It would teach the water to speak a little more clearly.

Not in words. Never in words. Words are too blunt for stone and seepage. But the drains would begin to murmur differently when silt gathered too thick beneath them. Moss would deepen in color above places where shale had softened dangerously. Frost would leave pale hairline tracings on stones that would crack before spring. The settlement had always read these signs, but now the signs would become just slightly more legible, as if the whole landscape had leaned closer to be understood.

The people of Terimoss would notice, because noticing was their oldest craft.

They would not call it a miracle. They would call it a change in the listening.

For a season, nothing else would happen.

The benevolent power would wait.

It would learn them.

It would attend the woman splitting slate and understand that her patience was not emptiness but fullness: a mind resting completely inside the necessary blow. It would attend the children clearing fern-roots from drainage cuts, their seriousness bright as hidden flame. It would attend the old ones at the shelter by the well-mouth, whose memories held the settlement together more truly than mortar could have done.

It would come to understand that Terimoss did not need permanence. Permanence would be an insult here. To make the cliff unchanging, to seal every fracture, to halt water in the stone, would be to kill the place while preserving its shape.

No. The place was not asking to be spared from time.

It was asking, perhaps, to be accompanied through it.

So the power would give a second gift.

It would strengthen not the plateau, but the relationships by which the people understood the plateau.

One winter, when freeze-thaw came hard and early, a seam would open behind the uppermost dwellings. Not a collapse. Not yet. A possibility of collapse. The kind of wound in the stone that, in another year, might have gone unnoticed until too late.

But that year, three people would dream the same dream.

They would dream of a pale root descending through granite, not breaking it, only showing where it was already divided. They would dream of water moving in darkness. They would dream of a roof beam groaning before it split.

In the morning, none of them would speak of prophecy. They were not a people given to grand declarations. But one would walk to the upper tier. Another would bring a sounding rod. The third, a child, would insist on coming though no one had asked her.

They would find the seam.

By dusk, half the settlement would be there, moving stores, shifting slate, cutting a new channel to divert the seepage away from the weakened face. No panic. No shouting. Only the swift, low-voiced coordination of people who had been warned in time.

The uppermost dwelling would be dismantled over nine days.

Its stones would be carried downward and reused. Its beams, dark and still sound, would become part of the open shelter beside the well. Its threshold stone, worn by four generations of feet, would be placed at the entrance of the new channel, where everyone would step over it and remember without needing to say much.

The power would be pleased.

Not because it had prevented loss, but because it had allowed loss to become movement rather than disaster.

After that, the people would make a new practice.

At the beginning of the cold season, the elders would gather the children in the plaza and teach them to listen for hollow stone. They would pour water into cracks and watch how it vanished. They would lay their palms on walls, not for warmth but for tremor. The children would learn that dreams could be evidence, but never alone; that intuition was a tool, but like all tools it needed a practiced hand.

The power would approve of this deeply.

Wisdom, to such a power, would mean never asking faith to replace attention.

Years would pass.

The settlement would remain Terimoss: damp, pale, narrow, fragrant with moss and wool and mineral water. Roofs would leak. Hands would ache. Children would be born. Some would die. People would mend what could be mended and grieve what could not. The cliff would continue to weep.

The power would not stop the dying.

This would be its hardest restraint.

There would be a fever one spring, after a winter that had kept everyone too long indoors. It would move through the lower houses first. The people would do what they knew: boil water, dry bedding near the hearths, bring bitter roots from the gardens, keep the sick warm without trapping the damp against their skin.

The power would stand near every bed.
