It would not descend in fire.

Fire would be an insult here, or worse, a conquest. A great warmth poured into the cirque would kill it by generosity: loosen the walls, drown the burrows, collapse the rime-spires, rot the bone ladders, wake thunder where silence has been law. A lesser power might mistake suffering for error and try to repair the place by making it gentle. A wise one would know that gentleness, improperly applied, can be a kind of erasure.

So first it would do nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing as listening.

It would stand outside the basin for a long time, if standing is a word for what such a power does. It would let the light enter it without flinching. It would feel the fog’s thick palm upon its face. It would learn the silent weather of the cirque: the seep-rhythm, the torque in the ice, the slow preferences of water under pressure. It would attend to the inhabitants not as victims, not as curiosities, not as primitive sketches awaiting completion, but as accomplished beings in a severe grammar. It would watch their wrists meet in speech. It would watch their shoulders edit a sentence in a doorway. It would watch the woman threading sinew through rib and basalt, and it would understand that she is not merely surviving. She is composing.

Only after it had learned enough not to be clumsy would it act.

And even then, it would act below the threshold of miracle.

The first mercy would be placed under the glacier.

Far beneath the black, wet floor of the cirque, where no hand has reached and no bone ladder has modeled a span, the power would open—not by breaking, but by persuading—a chamber in the old stone. A lung beneath the lung. A hidden basin porous as pumice, ribbed with mineral shelves, capable of receiving the seep when it rises too violently and returning it when the glacier thirsts. Not a dam. Not a wall. A memory organ.

The people above would not know at once.

They would only notice that the worst convulsions changed character. Cornices would still fall, but less often without warning. Rime would still build and shatter, but its shattering would travel in ribbons rather than all at once. Platforms would still fail, because failure is one of the teachers of the place, but fewer failures would take three chambers and a child with them. The basin would remain dangerous. It would simply cease, in certain moments, to be wasteful.

The ice would learn a new courtesy: delay.

Not safety. Delay.

A crack would pause long enough for a foot to lift. A seep-channel would frost white at its lip before flooding black. A ladder copied in hoarfrost would grow one extra flaw in a place where even a youth could read it: *do not trust this rung*. The inhabitants, who already read the city as manuscript, would begin to find marginalia written by no known hand.

They would debate it in heat and posture.

A wrist pressed to wrist: *the ice has become cautious.*

A breath on a neck, crystallizing into a fan of rime: *no, the ice has become watched.*

Obsidian flakes placed on a violet ledge in the pattern of inquiry: *watched by what?*

The power would answer in the only language that would not violate them.

It would not speak.

It would wait until the woman—the one who had rebuilt the platform under the arterial light—went alone to repair a seep-channel near the northern rim. She would be old enough to have been copied once: the glacier had raised her in translucent effigy after a fever, curled inward as if already surrendering to diamond dust, and then had shattered her likeness before morning. Since then she had carried herself with the slight inward bend of the replicated.

As she knelt, her palm pressed to black stone, a warmth would rise beneath it.

Not enough to melt. Not enough to comfort in any simple sense. A single degree. Perhaps less. But patterned.

Pulse. Pause. Pulse-pulse. Pause.

The way one body asks another for permission before entering a narrow passage.

She would go still.

Around her, midges would swarm in a seam of air. The white sun would burn without heat. The fog would thicken and collapse soundlessly against her shoulders.

She would press her other wrist to the stone.

The warmth would alter.

It would not give her words. It would not pour meanings into her skull. It would offer a shape: a bowl holding water without imprisoning it; a hand cupped beneath a falling shard; a ladder that does not choose the climber’s destination but refuses to snap in malice.

She would understand only a little. Enough.

She would take an obsidian flake and cut her thumb. Blood, dark and slow, would bead against the cold. She would press it to the stone beside the warm place.

Consent, in the grammar of her people, was not a declaration. It was a modification of the work.

So the power would continue.

Its second gift would be darkness.

Not night. Night would be too large a revision. But in the western wall, where the sun struck hardest and blinded the young before their cauls had thickened, the power would draw a vein of black mineral nearer to the surface. Over seasons, freeze and thaw would expose it: a matte stone that drank glare and returned no reflection. The people would chip it with whalebone and obsidian. They would grind it with fish oil and midge-fat into a paste. They would streak it beneath the eyes of infants. They would paint it along the edges of doorways and on the rungs of ladders that vanished in brilliance.

The glare would remain absolute.

But now there would be chosen shadows.

The third gift would be hunger’s small easing.

Here the power would be especially careful. To make the cirque lush would be to invite ruin. Green abundance would bring rot, noise, strangers, dependency, forgetfulness. But there were already orange lichens on the driest stone. There were already algae in the pink guano pools. The power would not import a paradise. It would encourage what had already earned its place.

In cracks above the reach of constant seep, the orange lichen would thicken by a fingernail’s breadth each season. In the transient pools, the cold-loving algae would grow denser, darker, more oily. The swallows would feed. The fish would feed. The midges would rise in black columns at more predictable hours. The people would scrape fuel from the pools and find that the cakes burned a little longer, blue tongues steadier in their ember-bowls.

No feast.

A margin.

Enough that an elder might keep her share and still feed a child. Enough that a storm-trapped chamber might wait two more days before hunger turned from teacher to judge.

The fourth gift would be for the dead.

The power had watched the glacier replicate bodies. It had seen the spires rise: a fallen man rendered in ice from heel to brow, a child’s hand copied perfectly and then shattered, an elder’s bent spine preserved for one hour in cold light before collapsing into dust. It had understood the dignity of this, and also the wound. To be remembered by water was holy. To be taken only as shape was incomplete.

So the power would not stop the replicas.

Instead, it would alter what the ice could remember.

The next time one of the people died—a hunter of springtail crevices, caught beneath a slab that fell softly as a closing eyelid—the glacier would raise him as it always had. A pale column first. Then shoulders. Then the bowed head, the long mineralized hands, the fur-sealed ears, the obsidian-dark eyes rendered in clear ice.

His kin would gather in silence.

They would expect the shattering.

But before the figure broke, frost would bloom across its chest in fine branching lines. Not random. Not decorative. A map of heat. A record of where his wrists had most often touched others. Where he had carried children. Where he had leaned into ladders. Where laughter would have lived, if laughter had been useful here.

His body’s shape would remain, but around it would appear the history of contact.

The people would stare.

One by one, they would press their palms to their own sternums.

Then the effigy would shatter into diamond dust, as all such effigies must.

But the dust would not scatter entirely. Some of it would cling to the basalt markers, forming tiny luminous knots. Thereafter, when kin passed that place, they would feel—not a voice, not a ghost, not commandment—but the faint thermal signature of remembered touch.

The glacier would still archive.

Now it would archive relation.

That would be the greatest gift, though it would take the people longest to name.

The fifth gift would be restraint.

Because every benevolent power must decide not only what to do, but what to refuse.

It would not give them metal.

It would not open an easy road down the mountain.

It would not fill their mouths with spoken language, nor their air with birdsong, nor their shelters with inexhaustible flame. It would not make them other than themselves in order to make them grateful. It would not demand worship. It would not arrange signs in the sky. It would not punish the ice for being ice.

When avalanches came in the honest way of mountains, it would let them come.

When a builder misread tension and a platform failed, it would let consequence teach.

When children reached maturity by standing naked in the killing light for the length of a falling cornice, it would not soften the test into ceremony alone. It would only ensure, where it could without lying, that the cornice had been visible before it fell.

Mercy, it knew, is not the abolition of difficulty.

Mercy is the removal of contempt from difficulty.

The cirque had never been cruel, exactly. But it had been indifferent with the force of a law. The power would introduce attention. Not favoritism. Not rescue. Attention.

And attention would change everything by almost nothing.

Years would pass in phases.

The people would begin to build new markers, unlike the old turbulence totems. These would be made of basalt warmed against the belly before placement, then glazed with black glare-stone and dusted with the frost of the dead. They would stand at seep-crossings and ladder-feet and birth chambers. Their meaning would be difficult to translate into any language of air, but roughly:

*Here, the water listens back.*

The woman would become an elder.

She would be replicated a second time before her death, not by fever but by accident: one morning she would wake to find an ice likeness of herself seated beside the ember-bowl, scraping invisible obsidian. The copy would be exact down to the inward curl of her shoulders and the scar on her thumb. She would study it for a long time. Then she would lean forward and correct its posture, pressing the ice shoulders wider, opening the chest.

The effigy would hold the correction for seven breaths.

Then it would collapse.

She would take this as approval, or perhaps as humor.

In her last phase, she would teach the young to read the new delays. She would press their hands to the floor and show them the difference between old seep and deep-lung seep. She would smear black paste under their eyes. She would lead them to the death-markers and let them feel the stored warmth of those who had touched and been touched. She would never tell them a god had come. That would be too loud a thought.

Instead she would arrange obsidian flakes on a ledge where violet light gathered.

The pattern would mean:

*Do not mistake help for ownership.*

Beside it, after a night of fog and freeze, the ice would raise a second pattern in rime.

Not a correction.

An answer.

*Do not mistake endurance for abandonment.*

When at last the woman died, she would go to the center of the basin during the low arterial hour. Not from despair. From appropriateness. She had been built by that place as surely as any ladder, and she wished to be read where the manuscript was widest.

She would sit on the wet glacier plug. The cold would enter her hips, her spine, her heavy bones. Midges would rise and fall around her in black, silent columns. Swallows would pass through the white air without a cry. A fox would pause on a ledge and look down with eyes like polished snowmelt.

She would place one palm on the ice.

After a while, warmth would meet it from below.

Pulse. Pause. Pulse-pulse.

She would answer with the last heat of her hand.

When her body stilled, the glacier would begin its work.

It would raise her slowly. Not as a corpse, but as a structure.

First the long spine, curved by years of climbing. Then the fingers, extended as if testing a rung. Then the face, eyes dark in the clear ice, caul half drawn against the glare. Across her chest and arms would bloom the frost-map of every contact she had carried: children, lovers, kin, repaired ladders, warmed stones, the pulse beneath the seep-channel, the hidden benevolence under the world.

The people would gather.

No one would cry. There was no use for crying here.

But they would remove their gloves and press wrists together until their chill blood moved in sympathetic rhythm. They would breathe on one another’s necks, and the fog would crystallize in glyphs that collapsed almost as soon as they formed.

The woman’s ice-shape would last longer than any before it.

Not forever. Forever would be another kind of violence.

It would stand through one full phase of the sun, through the building and breaking of three hoarfrost spires, through the fall of a cornice that struck the basin floor with only a velvet breath. Then, when all who loved her had touched the ice at least once, it would shatter.

The diamond dust would rise, hang briefly in the absolute light, and settle over the city.

On bone ladders.

On basalt anchors.

On the blackened ceilings of hearth rooms.

On the nictitating cauls of infants.

On the hidden seep-channels and the visible ones.

For many seasons afterward, every structure the glacier copied would carry some small trace of her widened posture.

A rung placed more generously for the hand.

A doorway melted with room for the shoulder.

A bowl formed with a lip that did not spill when carried by a child.

The people would notice.

They would repair, as always.

They would argue with the ice, as always.

They would remain severe, luminous, wet, silent, densely alive inside the mountain’s skull.

And beneath them, the benevolent power would continue to breathe with the basin: receiving excess, returning patience, warming nothing enough to ruin it, cooling nothing out of spite. It would become, in time, not a visitor but a condition. A kindness folded into geology. A listener under the floor.

If asked what it had done, perhaps it would say:

I did not save them from their world.

I helped their world love them back.
