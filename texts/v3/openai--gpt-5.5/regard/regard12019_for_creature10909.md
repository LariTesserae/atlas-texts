The benevolent power would not come as a thunderbolt.

It would not split the sea, wrench the city back into daylight, or polish the rust from its bones until it resembled what it had been. Wisdom would know better. The sunken city was not only a grave. It had become a womb. It had become a reef of memory, a cold cathedral where new lives had learned to breathe in the dark.

So the power would descend gently.

At first, no one would know it had arrived.

Only the currents would change.

Not violently. Not enough to alarm the Nilmer in their silent avenues or send the Qorax flashing warnings through the factory halls. A slight warmth would enter the water, almost imperceptible, like the memory of sunlight. Silt would settle where it had long choked the narrow streets. Toxic plumes leaking from ruptured tanks would begin to fold inward upon themselves, binding into harmless mineral blooms. Rusted beams on the verge of collapse would grow still, held together by a subtle patience.

The city would exhale.

The pale fish would drift from hiding. The translucent crustaceans would emerge from beneath broken machines. Curtains of seaweed would sway in a rhythm no storm had taught them.

The power would listen.

It would listen to the groan of drowned steel, to the subsonic speech of the Nilmer, to the bright pulses of Qorax thought rippling across their skin. It would listen deeper still, past tunnels sealed by pressure and time, into the abyssal chambers where the Xogoth dreamed their vast, slow dreams.

It would not assume that darkness meant misery.

It would not mistake strangeness for corruption.

It would ask the city what it had become.

And the city, in its own way, would answer.

It would answer with the chemical gardens growing along mineral vents, with the luminous murals painted by Qorax hands across the walls of old assembly lines, with Nilmer memory-vibrations stored in the ribs of buildings, with Xogoth pulses that moved through the foundations like the heartbeat of an ancient god.

The wise power would understand: this place did not need rescue from itself.

It needed mercy.

It needed balance.

It needed protection from the slow poisons of what had been left behind.

So the first gift would be cleansing.

Deep within the drowned industrial quarter, barrels that had corroded for centuries would seal themselves in shells of pearly stone. Black oils trapped beneath collapsed roads would be drawn out by pale mats of engineered bacteria, not alien to the city but awakened from possibilities already present within it. Heavy metals dissolved in the water would crystallize into harmless deposits, forming glittering veins along the walls like constellations.

The Nilmer would find that the hunting grounds no longer made their young sick.

The Qorax would discover that their delicate eggs no longer dimmed before hatching.

Even the Xogoth would stir, sensing the old bitterness being leached from the bones of the deep.

The second gift would be light.

Not sunlight. Not the cruel, impossible blaze of the upper world.

The power would coax the city’s own bioluminescence into new forms. Soft blue-green lantern growths would spread along the undersides of bridges and around the mouths of tunnels. Golden bacteria would bloom in safe corridors, marking paths between territories. Great slow pulses of violet would rise from the central towers, not bright enough to blind the Nilmer, not harsh enough to disturb the abyss, but sufficient to make the city less of a maze and more of a map.

The Qorax would adore it first.

They would gather in trembling clusters around the new-lit walls, their own bodies answering in ripples of emerald, rose, and white. They would touch the glowing colonies with careful tentacles and understand, in their inventive hearts, that this was not a commandment but an invitation.

Soon they would begin composing with it.

They would create murals that moved with the current, stories painted in living light: the fall of the city, the arrival of the sea, the first Nilmer crossing, the first Qorax forge, the first sighting of a Xogoth trail spiraling through the deep.

The Nilmer would approach more slowly.

Their milky eyes, so attuned to near-darkness, would at first recoil from the altered avenues. But the light was gentle. It did not expose them. It did not hunt them. It revealed edges, doorways, safe passages, the shapes of others before collision became conflict.

In time, Nilmer elders would press their long fingers to the illuminated walls and send low vibrations through them.

The Qorax would watch.

Then answer with color.

Thus the third gift would begin: translation.

Not perfect understanding. Perfect understanding is rarely given, and perhaps rarely wise. But enough.

The power would awaken certain old structures in the city: pipes, rails, cables, conduits, the metallic nerves of the drowned metropolis. Through them, Nilmer vibration could travel farther and more clearly. Across them, Qorax light-signals could be reflected, refracted, held. In the meeting places where tunnels opened into wide transit halls, the power would seed listening stones—smooth mineral growths that hummed faintly when touched.

A Nilmer could speak into one in pressure and tone.

A Qorax could answer across its surface in light.

Meaning would remain imperfect, beautiful, and laborious.

But it would be enough to reduce fear.

Enough to say: this passage is sacred to us.

Enough to say: these eggs must not be disturbed.

Enough to say: we are hungry.

Enough to say: we remember being wounded.

Enough to say: we did not know.

The skirmishes would not cease overnight. No wise power would expect old hunger and old suspicion to dissolve merely because a miracle had occurred. But the disputes would change. Ambushes would become warnings. Warnings would become negotiations. Negotiations would become rituals.

The Nilmer would offer polished bone, rare fungi, and maps of the lower dark.

The Qorax would offer tools, luminous medicines, and repaired shelters.

Between them, a new custom would form: the Exchange of Echoes, held in an old flooded railway station where light and vibration met. There, both peoples would tell the same event in their own languages, and the listening stones would hold the two versions together.

The fourth gift would be memory.

The benevolent power would not erase the dead.

It would gather the scattered remnants of human life—the doll, the picture frame, the waterlogged book, the rusted keys, the broken spectacles, the nameplates torn from office doors—and draw them slowly, by current and chance, to a central plaza beneath the tallest drowned tower.

There, coral would grow in a ring.

Not the sharp, hungry coral of reefs, but smooth white branches like open hands. Around them, the objects would settle into niches. The waterlogged book would never be readable again, but its shape would remain. The doll’s face, softened by time, would gaze outward. The picture frame would hold no picture, only a shifting pane of dark water in which every passerby might briefly see themselves.

The Qorax would decorate the memorial with living light.

The Nilmer would circle it in silence, feeling the density of old grief.

The Xogoth, one by one and over many years, would leave vast spiraling trails around the plaza, blessing or acknowledging or remembering—no one would be certain which.

The wise power would know that a city without memory becomes only habitat.

And this city was more than habitat.

It was consequence.

It was inheritance.

It was warning.

It was home.

The fifth gift would be choice.

This would be the greatest gift, and the quietest.

The power would open ways—not exits forced upon the inhabitants, but possibilities. Some upper towers would be strengthened, their highest chambers made safe against pressure shifts and currents. Shafts of filtered surface light would be guided into them at certain hours, creating dim blue sanctuaries where those who wished could study the world above.

For the Qorax, these would become observatories.

For the Nilmer, places of pilgrimage and unease.

For young creatures of both peoples, they would become places of myth.

The power would also deepen certain lower routes, clearing collapses without fanfare, allowing passage into abyssal plains beyond the city. No one would be driven there. No one would be forbidden to return.

Stay, the city would say.

Go, the sea would say.

Live, the power would say.

And then there were the Xogoth.

For them, the benevolent power would do something different.

It would not illuminate their chambers. It would not translate them for the convenience of smaller minds. It would not drag mystery into explanation.

Instead, it would descend to the deepest district, where towers leaned like drowned giants and the water was blacker than sleep. There it would become still.

The Xogoth would gather.

Amorphous, immense, slow as continental drift, they would surround the presence. Their bioluminescent trails would coil in vast script no Qorax could copy and no Nilmer could feel without trembling.

The power would ask them one question.

Not in words.

Not even in thought.

It would ask: Are you prisoners here?

For a long time, there would be no answer.

Days might pass. Years might pass. Time in the deep is not obedient to surface expectation.

At last, the Xogoth would respond.

Their answer would move through the foundations of the city. Every girder would hum. Every windowless tower would tremble. Sediment would rise from the streets like ghosts. The Nilmer would fall silent mid-hunt. The Qorax would dim themselves in awe.

The answer would be neither yes nor no.

It would be grief.

It would be belonging.

It would be guilt.

It would be guardianship.

The wise power would understand enough.

So for the Xogoth, it would loosen what could be loosened and honor what could not. Ancient pressures around their breeding chambers would ease. Old wounds in the bedrock would seal. The crushing loneliness of their depth would be softened by channels through which the songs of the whole city—Nilmer vibration, Qorax light translated into pulse, whale-call from far above, geothermal murmur from below—could occasionally reach them.

Not constantly.

Even benevolence can become intrusion.

Only sometimes.

Enough to remind them that they were not alone unless they chose to be.

And what of the city itself?

The power would not rebuild it into its former image.

It would not restore the factories to production, nor set machines coughing again in the dark. It would not resurrect the old ambition that had raised towers too high and buried costs too deep. The city had already been judged by water, time, and consequence.

But it would preserve what deserved preservation.

Bridges would become reefs.

Warehouses would become nurseries.

Factories would become halls of craft and ritual.

Subway tunnels would become migration routes.

Skyscrapers would become vertical gardens of cold-water life.

The old smokestacks, useless now, would vent harmless mineral warmth, around which pale forests of tube-worms and luminous algae would gather. The broken streets would be lined with slow-growing shell mosaics. The central plaza would become a place where no species hunted.

A sanctuary.

Not because a law was carved there, but because every creature that entered would feel the presence of the dead, the patience of the sea, and the quiet attention of something kind.

In time, legends would form.

The Nilmer would speak of the Deep Mercy, the current that came without teeth.

The Qorax would call it the First Gentle Light, and paint it as a spiral of gold descending through blue-black water.

The Xogoth would not name it.

Or perhaps their name for it would take a thousand years to say.

The benevolent power would remain only as long as needed.

That, too, would be wisdom.

A lesser power might wish to be worshiped. A frightened power might seek control. A sentimental power might freeze the city into a perfect, harmless shrine.

But this power would know that life must be allowed to continue being strange.

So one day, without announcement, it would withdraw.

Not completely. Never completely. It would leave behind no throne, no temple, no commandments burning in the water. Only patterns. Healthier currents. Living lights. Listening stones. Safe nurseries. Cleansed wounds. Open roads.

The city would still be dark.

But no longer only dark.

It would still be cold.

But not without warmth.

It would still be haunted.

But now its ghosts would have company.

And in the murky depths, beneath the weight of the sea, the sunken city would go on slumbering and waking, decaying and becoming, remembering and inventing itself anew.

Not saved from what it was.

Not forced to become what it had been.

Blessed, instead, to become more fully what it could be.
