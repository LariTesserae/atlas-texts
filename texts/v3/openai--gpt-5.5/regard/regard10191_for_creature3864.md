### **What the Benevolent Power Would Do**

At first, it would do nothing.

Not from indifference, nor hesitation, nor the cold arrogance of distant gods. It would do nothing because Lych-Creek is not a wound to be closed without understanding the body around it. It would hover unseen above the warm gray air, in the condensation between droplets, in the quiet between the factory-hum and the lap of brine against metal, and it would listen.

It would listen to the city’s old machines, still working though no hand remembered why.

It would listen to the fungi, whose light was not merely light but language.

It would listen to the Hollows breathing memory-mist in their Nests, sharing hunger, grief, repair, ritual, tide-dreams.

It would listen to the Silent Ones moving through drowned corridors with their dimmed bodies and their armfuls of forgotten things.

It would listen until it understood that Lych-Creek was not simply dying.

It was transforming.

And so the power would not bring sunlight by tearing open the sky.  
It would not dry the marsh.  
It would not purify every drop of water into sterility.  
It would not remake the Hollows into something cleaner, brighter, more acceptable to a world that had never learned to love rust.

A lesser mercy would rescue them by destroying what they were.

This power would be wiser than that.

It would begin with the smallest possible kindness.

A drop.

One droplet among the millions condensing on stone and metal would fall differently. It would strike a patch of tired fungus clinging to the underside of a collapsed refinery bridge. The fungus there had been dimming for years, poisoned by oil seepage and mineral rot. The droplet would not cure it all at once. It would carry only a suggestion: a new way to bind toxin, a gentler chemical memory, a pattern the fungus could accept as its own.

The patch would brighten.

Not much.

Only enough that a passing insectile creature would pause, taste it, and carry its spores elsewhere.

That would be the first miracle.

Not thunder. Not revelation.

A correction so humble it could be mistaken for luck.

Then the power would move through the Weeping Apparatus as a patient physician moves through a sleeping ward.

It would touch the fungi next, but not command them. It would speak in pulses of mineral, salt, warmth, and pressure. It would teach them new hungers: how to feed on soot, how to drink oil without sickening, how to lace themselves through rusted beams and become not merely lanterns but ligaments.

Across the submerged foundations, fungal colonies would begin growing in strange new geometries. Not rampant, not devouring. Deliberate. Their threads would enter cracked concrete, wrap weakened pylons, seal leaks in old pressure pipes. They would become soft scaffolding. Living mortar. A luminous mycelial architecture that held without imprisoning.

The Hollows would notice before anyone else.

In the Nests, the mist would change.

At first they would fear it. The shared breath of a Nest is sacred; a change in it is no small thing. The elders would taste the new memory and recoil. Younglings would glow sharp blue with alarm. Caretakers would scrape samples from the walls and hold them beneath their multifaceted eyes.

But the mist would not bring command.

It would bring a question.

Not in words. The Hollows did not need words for the deepest things.

The question would be:

**May I help you remain yourselves?**

Some Nests would refuse.

The power would honor this.

Their walls would remain untouched except by ordinary damp and time. Their rituals would continue without interference. Their offerings of rust and fluid would sink into the tide as before. No punishment would follow. No plague of correction. No withdrawal of sunlight, because no sunlight had been given as a bargain.

The benevolent power would understand that consent is part of mercy.

Other Nests would answer yes.

And for them, the work would begin.

It would teach the Hollows to build lighter.

Not by handing them diagrams from some clean celestial engineering, but by awakening old knowledge already hidden in their practices. A young Hollow pruning fungus along a catwalk would suddenly notice that certain colonies formed hollow tubes stronger than solid mass. A Silent One, sorting through drowned artifacts, would find an ancient spool of woven metal fiber and understand at once how it could be braided with fungal cords. Nest-builders would dream of suspended chambers, buoyant cradles, rootless dwellings hung from tension lines instead of piled heavily into failing towers.

The Nests would slowly change.

They would become less like burdens and more like webs.

Great hanging communities would bloom between factory stacks, glowing softly in the rain. Submerged Nests would develop air-pockets held in place by fungal membranes. Catwalks once ready to collapse would be reinforced by living cables that flexed with tide and heat.

The city would still sink.

But not helplessly.

It would learn to float in pieces.

Then the power would turn toward the machines.

Here it would be careful, because the machines were not innocent, but neither were they evil. They were old appetites left running after their makers had vanished. They breathed smoke because smoke was what they knew. They heated the city because heat was the only prayer they had been built to repeat.

The power would enter them through steam.

It would pass through valves, turbines, pressure chambers, cracked gauges, corroded pistons. It would not silence them all. Their hum was part of Lych-Creek’s pulse. Instead, it would retune them.

Some chimneys would begin exhaling cleaner vapor.

Some boilers would cool.

Some dead engines would wake briefly, not to produce more poison, but to pump trapped sludge from the water into sealed basins where fungus could slowly digest it.

One refinery, long believed cursed because all who entered heard voices, would open its lower doors during a low brine tide. Inside, the Hollows would find not a demon but an ancient filtration heart, still intact, waiting for a missing rhythm. Their own bioluminescent fluid would complete the circuit. From that day forward, the refinery would become a lung.

The Hollows would name it the Warm Throat.

They would bring offerings there, not of desperation now, but gratitude.

The power would not correct their worship.

If they called the change a gift from the Brine Mother, so be it.

If they called it the City Remembering, so be it.

If the Silent Ones refused all names and merely watched from beneath the water, that too would be allowed.

But the power would seek the Silent Ones especially.

Not to drag them back into the Nests. Not to brighten them against their will. Their silence was not a defect. It was a vocation.

Deep below the waterline, where corridors bent under pressure and the oldest fungi burned with a color no surface Hollow had ever seen, the power would find them sorting relics: cracked faceplates, children’s tools, rusted keys, prayer-discs, machine teeth, bones polished smooth by tide.

The Silent Ones had been collecting memory because the Nests could not hold all of it. The communal mist preserved living emotion, but the Silent Ones preserved what had no living speaker left.

They were not harbingers of collapse.

They were archivists of loss.

The power would give them a place.

In the drowned municipal hall, beneath a dome half-filled with brine, it would coax the fungi into a great slow bloom. Its filaments would grow through every artifact the Silent Ones brought, not consuming them, but reading the oils of hands, the salt of tears, the pressure marks of use. The bloom would become an archive of touch.

When a Hollow entered and breathed its faint mist, they would not merely remember facts. They would feel the weight of a worker’s wrench, the fear of the first flood, the laughter of children racing along dry streets before the canals swallowed them, the awe of the earliest Hollows discovering that fungus could speak.

The Silent Ones would tend this place.

Their dim glow would no longer be mistaken for failure.

It would be understood as depth-light, suited to the chambers where memory settles.

Only after this would the power approach the deepest myth.

The Brine Mother.

The Hollows believed she lived in the lowest chambers, where the fungi were oldest and brightest. Some said she was a goddess. Some said she was the first Hollow. Some said she was the sea itself, made merciful by grief.

The power would descend through warm black water, past pipes like ribs, past sleeping amphibious beasts with metallic hides, past vents where heat rose in shimmering columns. It would come to the oldest submerged chamber, a place beneath the city’s original foundation.

There it would find the Brine Mother.

She was not exactly a being.

She was a vast fungal intelligence grown through stone, bone, iron, and memory. She had been born from catastrophe: spores feeding on the first drowned district, absorbing the minds of the dying, the minerals of machines, the strange adaptive blood of those who would become Hollows. She was not omnipotent. She was not even fully awake. She dreamed the balance of Lych-Creek, and in her dreaming kept the ecosystem from tipping too quickly into ruin.

But she was tired.

For generations she had filtered poison, mediated tides, fed the Nests, received offerings, and held the city’s grief in her glowing body.

The benevolent power would not replace her.

It would sit beside her in the dark.

For a long time, it would simply keep her company.

And perhaps this would be the greatest mercy given to Lych-Creek: that the ancient thing at its heart would no longer have to be alone.

In that companionship, the Brine Mother would dream more clearly. Her signals would move upward through fungal networks with less distortion. The tides would become more predictable. Toxic blooms would lessen. The hallucinogenic mists of the Nests would grow gentler, carrying not only communal urgency but also rest.

The Hollows would begin to dream new dreams.

Not dreams of escape.

Dreams of continuity.

A generation later, Lych-Creek would still be gray.

Still wet.

Still rusted.

Still full of hissing vents, dim canals, winged translucent things, skittering rust-carapaced insects, amphibious beasts gliding under platforms, and Hollows moving like pale lanterns through the rain.

But something would have changed.

The desperation would no longer be quiet and total.

Children of the Hollows would learn tidecraft not as doomed maintenance, but as art. They would race skiffs between fungal pylons. They would braid living cables. They would sing in clicks and hums to the Warm Throat and laugh when it answered in steam.

Nests would hold festivals during the condensation peaks, when every surface wept at once and the entire city shimmered. Hollows would paint their keratin plates with mineral pigments. Silent Ones would rise from the depths bearing artifacts, and for one night the Nests would breathe the archive-mist together.

They would mourn.

They would celebrate.

They would understand that these were not opposites.

And when towers finally fell, as some still must, they would not fall as meaningless failures. Their collapse would be prepared for. Their metal would be guided into reef-frames. Their stone would become tidebreaks. Their submerged rooms would seed new fungal gardens. Their loss would feed the next layer of the city.

The benevolent power would not make Lych-Creek immortal.

Immortality would be a cruelty here.

Instead, it would teach the city how to decay fruitfully.

How to sink without vanishing.

How to remember without drowning in memory.

How to change without being erased.

And when its work was done, if such work is ever done, the power would withdraw just enough that the Hollows could stand within their own becoming. It would remain only as a tendency in the rain, a warmth in certain pipes, a clarity in the Brine Mother’s oldest dreams.

The Hollows would tell many stories about what had happened.

Some would say the Brine Mother opened her second heart.

Some would say the city forgave itself.

Some would say the Silent Ones found a god asleep in the drowned hall and chose not to wake it.

Some would say nothing happened at all, except that one year the fungi began to glow a little brighter, and the water tasted less of metal, and the towers stopped falling faster than they could be loved.

The wise power would accept every version.

For it had not come to be worshiped.

It had come to help life remain strange, sorrowful, beautiful, and its own.
