The benevolent power would not descend in thunder.

It would not split the clouds or turn the steel towers into gardens all at once. Wisdom would tell it that a place is more than its harshness, and a life is more than its loneliness. It would see the woman first—not as a symbol, not as a figure in a desolate city, but as herself: breath steaming, boots slipping slightly on glass, laughter caught between effort and cold. It would see the dog too, small and unruly and alive, performing the ancient work of joy.

So it would act gently.

It would warm one current of air.

Not enough to change the weather. Not enough for the woman to notice and stop. Just enough that the cold would not bite so sharply at her lungs. Just enough that when she laughed, it would come easily.

The dog, darting ahead, would place one paw on a slick patch where water had gathered invisibly over glass. The power would gather that bead of danger into itself. The paw would find traction. The dog would skid, recover, and spring away as though luck had always belonged to it.

The woman would slip a moment later.

Not badly. Not with fear. Just enough to make her arms windmill and her heart leap. The power would not prevent the stumble entirely, because play has edges, and bodies learn the world through them. But it would soften the fall that might have been. Her knee would strike the floor with a dull thud instead of a crack. She would gasp, then laugh harder, sitting there in her yellow raincoat while the dog circled back triumphantly, tongue out, convinced it had won.

Then the power would do something stranger.

It would listen to the buildings.

The steel towers had stood for years against sleet, wind, and human necessity. They had been designed to resist, not to welcome. Their angles cut the sky; their faces shed water without tenderness. Yet even they had learned rhythms: the pulse of elevators, the murmur of pipes, the distant warmth of inhabited rooms, the creak of metal shrinking in cold.

The wise power would not erase their severity. It would teach them one small kindness.

From that day forward, the ledges would drip in patterns less cruel to those below. Water would find channels away from walkways. Frost would bloom more slowly on the glass floors where feet most often passed. A door that had long stuck in winter would begin opening smoothly. A light above an empty corridor would stop flickering.

Small mercies. The kind that no one builds statues for.

The woman would finally catch the dog near a railing where the gray sky opened wide beyond the towers. She would scoop the animal up, scolding it with a voice too full of affection to be believed.

“You little menace,” she would say.

The dog would lick her chin.

And then, because benevolence is not merely protection but invitation, the power would place in her heart the sudden desire to look outward.

She would turn.

Through the mist and the descending threads of water, she would see the city differently. The high-rises would still be hard and immense, still cold, still made of hardened steel. But between them, in the wet distances, she would notice things she had overlooked: a window glowing amber; a scrap of green moss clinging to a seam; three birds riding an updraft between towers; the reflection of her own yellow coat like a small sun on the glass beneath her.

For a moment, she would feel accompanied.

Not watched.

Not commanded.

Simply accompanied.

The power would leave no sign except this: when she returned inside, carrying the damp, wriggling dog under one arm, she would feel an unreasonable hope. She would make tea. She would wrap the dog in a towel. She would send a message to someone she had been meaning to answer for weeks.

Outside, water would continue to trickle from ledges. The air would remain crisp and cold. The towers would keep their angular silhouettes against the gray sky.

But somewhere within the vast machinery of the place, a hidden balance would have shifted.

The city would be no less real.

Only slightly more kind.
