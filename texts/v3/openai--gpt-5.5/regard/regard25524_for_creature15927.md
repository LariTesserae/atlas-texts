The power would come very quietly.

Not as a star falling into the hollow, not as a hand through cloud, not as a voice that shakes the fresh black walls loose. A wise mercy would know at once that this place cannot survive a dramatic kindness. Too much warmth would rot it. Too much water would drown it. Too much soil would invite strangers. Too much safety would unmake the very intelligence that lives there.

So it would arrive as attention.

For a long while, it would do nothing but listen.

It would listen to the cleft’s low tone until it could distinguish wind from meltwater, meltwater from inner cracking, cracking from the slow chemical murmuring of the dark green life below. It would learn the language of the birds’ feet, the hooved animals’ breath, the insects’ crescent-work at the pools. It would feel how each scarcity had become a law, and how each law had become a form of beauty.

It would understand that the hollow is not a mistake.

It is severe, but not cruel.

It is narrow, but not empty.

It is unfinished, but not failed.

It is a wound that life has entered, and life has not asked for the wound to close.

So the power would not heal the mountain in the ordinary sense.

It would not round the cliffs into pasture.

It would not spread soft moss everywhere.

It would not fill the hollow with sunlight.

It would not make the birds gentle, nor the mammals fearless, nor the insects few.

It would not silence hunger, because hunger is one of the threads holding the chamber together.

But it would give three small mercies.

The first mercy would be measure.

The young mountain would still shift, frost would still pry, stone would still fall. But the falls would no longer come in blind excess. The great hanging slabs that would have split away all at once and crushed half the rookery would loosen instead by flakes and shards over many seasons. Enough danger would remain that every creature would stay awake to the world. Enough catastrophe would be withheld that waking would matter.

A ledge would break after the chicks had fledged, not before.

A storm would cut across the rim, but spend its worst force on the empty wall.

A winter crack would open slowly enough for moss to retreat along its damp rootlets, for worms to braid deeper, for beetles to carry their translucent eggs into a darker seam.

No law would be abolished.

Only the meaningless surplus of loss would be thinned.

The second mercy would be rhythm.

Water would still be scarce. It must be scarce, or the hollow would become another place. But the hesitation of water would become slightly more faithful.

At dawn, condensation would gather in a few more hidden seams. Not much. A bead here, a blackened thread there, a shining lip beneath an overhang. Enough for the moss pads to hold through the dry weeks. Enough for larvae to finish becoming winged glass. Enough for the horned mammals not to scrape the last green film from the stone in desperation.

The pools would still shrink.

Some would still fail.

Eggs would still be lost in a dry hour.

But beneath the deepest cleft, the dark inner wetness would be guarded from sealing itself away. A narrow vein would remain open — no wider than a root, no louder than a sleeping throat — through which mineral water could breathe upward.

And when storms approached, the cleft’s vibration would speak a little sooner.

Not loudly. Not in prophecy. Only a slight deepening in the tone, a tightening in the hidden chambers, a change that the birds nearest the split could feel through their toes.

The old warning would become clearer.

The hollow would learn weather half an hour earlier than before.

Half an hour is a kingdom in such a place.

The third mercy would be continuance.

Not immortality. That would be a terrible gift here. The hollow is made of cycles: hatch and fall, bloom and withdraw, graze and regrow, drip and vanish, open and close. To freeze any part of it forever would be to kill the whole by honoring one moment too much.

Instead, the power would tuck resilience into the smallest lives.

The ash-colored cleaners beneath the bird ledges would survive colder nights by folding tighter, their bodies learning the shape of stone more perfectly.

The pool larvae would make their crescents with a little more success, not by becoming wiser individually, but by inheriting a finer restlessness in their mouths and forelimbs: grain here, not there; curve inward; leave a gap for overflow.

The moss would wake from drought more often than seemed likely.

The lichens would gain patience even beyond their own.

The translucent flowers, those brief emissaries from gentler worlds, would return in slightly greater number after hard years, appearing in cracks where no seed should have waited.

The horned mammals would teach without meaning to teach. A young one, watching an elder pry lichen with shale, would remember the angle. A second would remember the kind of shard. A third, many seasons later, would choose a better edge. No one would own a tool. No one would invent civilization. But tradition would become less fragile.

The birds would lose many chicks, as they always had.

But not all.

And among them, once in a long while, there would hatch a chick with an unusual stillness. It would grow into a bird that listened more deeply to the mountain-tone than the others. It would not rule them. It would not become a prophet in any grand sense. It would simply choose safer ledges before storms, and others would crowd near it because bodies know what minds do not name.

A colony can be guided by one good instinct repeated.

The benevolent power would also place a boundary around the hollow, though not a visible one.

No wall. No spell of exclusion flaming blue across the sky.

Only misdirection.

Clouds would gather oddly around the summit when large, curious creatures approached from below. Paths would seem less promising. Scree slopes would shift under heavy boots. Echoes would confuse the sense of distance. A climber might glimpse the hollow from a ridge and feel, without knowing why, that the place was unstable, unwelcoming, better left unseen.

Those who came to conquer would turn back irritated.

Those who came to collect would find another valley richer in specimens.

Those who came with reverence might be allowed to sit above the rim for a few minutes, hearing only the multiplied cries and the hidden breathing, and leave with no proof except a changed silence in themselves.

The power would not hide the hollow from the world entirely.

It would hide it from possession.

Then, perhaps, after long attention, the power would do one more thing.

It would bless the cleft.

Not by closing it.

Not by opening it wider.

By making it a true throat.

The vibration within the mountain would become, over generations, a little more ordered. Still natural. Still born of wind, water, chamber, pressure, melt, stone. But the tones would settle into recurring patterns, subtle enough that no creature would hear music, exactly, yet every creature would live by it.

A low tone for weather turning.

A higher tremor for thaw.

A long hollow pulse during the deep cold, when bodies must press together and spend nothing.

A broken, lively clicking under the stone when mineral water rose.

The mammals at the cleft would hum back, as they had always done, but their humming would begin to matter. Their chests would answer certain tones. The sound would travel into the crack, into wet stone, into chambers no hoof could reach. Perhaps it would loosen droplets. Perhaps it would stir the green-black film. Perhaps it would do nothing measurable except join the living to the source of their living.

That would be enough.

The power would not explain itself.

Explanation is often a kind of taking.

It would leave no monument, no symbol burned onto the rock, no chosen species crowned above the others. It would not say, You are loved, because the hollow has no use for sentences in the air.

Instead, love would appear as tolerable weather.

As one more surviving egg.

As a stonefall delayed until after the ledge was empty.

As a bead of water remaining in shadow until night.

As a soft-hooved mother finding a crack of moss beneath frost.

As a midge swarm rising just when the birds’ hunger sharpened.

As a pool glowing after touch, answering contact with its cold green alphabet.

As the cleft breathing on.

And if the hollow itself is a being — if all those birds, mammals, insects, worms, mosses, lichens, films, waters, stones, and echoes together form one listening creature, one compressed soul of the young mountain — then the power would speak to that being only once.

Not in words.

It would place its attention gently along the walls, through the wet seams, beneath the nests, around the dark pools, down into the iron-smelling split.

The hollow would feel, for an instant, that it was entirely known.

Not improved.

Not judged.

Not pitied.

Known.

The birds would go silent.

The mammals at the cleft would lift their heads.

The worms would extend from the cracks.

The pools would tremble with faint green light, though nothing had touched them.

Then the moment would pass.

Sound would return: flint-calls, drips, wing-rattle, hoof-scrape, the dry electrical hiss of insects. A pebble would fall, strike another, then another, and the chamber would answer itself.

Life would continue narrow, crowded, severe, and bright in its hidden ways.

The mountain would remain young.

The hollow would remain a room bitten out of the sky.

But from then on, its hardship would be watched over by a mercy careful enough not to soften it beyond recognition.

And in that mercy, everything would have just slightly more chance to become what it already was.
