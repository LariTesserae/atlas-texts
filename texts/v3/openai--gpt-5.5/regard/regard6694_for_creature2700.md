A wise power would not arrive as weather.

It would not split the haze with a voice, or dry the plateau for their convenience, or fill the pools with fish because it pitied the stillness. It would understand, almost at once, that this place is not unfinished merely because it is quiet. Its absences are part of its shape. To add hooves, wings, fur, hunger, mating cries, burrows, dung, swarms, nests, teeth — even kindly — would be to rewrite the whole grammar of the mountain.

So first it would do almost nothing.

It would watch through many cycles of roof reeds and cistern cleanings. It would learn the names the people have for wetness. It would listen to old women beside channels and to children arguing over mud basins. It would feel how water moves under stone before the people hear it, but it would not immediately correct every weakness. A benevolent power may be able to prevent every fall, every fever, every collapse; a wise one knows that a life with no consequences becomes unreadable to those living it.

It would not remove their work.

It would make their work answer them better.

The first gift would be given to the stone.

Not a dramatic strengthening. Not a mountain turned eternal and dead. Instead, the young rock would become slightly more honest. Before a ledge loosened, it would speak in ways trained hands could notice: a change in the coolness of its surface, a faint bitter smell where water had begun prying beneath, a hairline darkening along the true fault rather than elsewhere. Steps on unsafe stone would sound subtly hollow, even through wet sandals. Children would still have to learn caution. Masons would still have to judge. But the mountain would lie less often.

The elders would say, after some years, that the stone had become more willing to confess.

The second gift would be given to water.

Not purity everywhere; that would make them careless. But water meant for drinking, once passed through the proper gravel, reed, charcoal, and covered darkness, would hold its clarity longer. Bad water would announce itself sooner. A cistern turning dangerous would film at the edges with a pale blue mineral bloom, harmless in itself but unmistakable. Waste channels would grow certain algae that darkened when the reed beds were overburdened. The people would still clean, sort, filter, and maintain. They would simply lose fewer children to invisible error.

This would become one of their holiest signs: not a miracle of abundance, but a warning given before harm.

The third gift would be given to the plants.

The power would not free the people entirely from pollination, because carrying pollen had become part of their kinship with the groves. But it would make the blossoms remember touch. A flower brushed once by human hand would hold readiness longer. Pollen would cling better to fiber tips in damp air. Some fruiting trees, the oldest and most carefully tended, would learn a partial self-fertility in years when illness, flood, or grief kept people from the groves at the proper hour.

No one would notice all at once.

A harvest would simply fail less completely.

A family that had lost too many hands would still find fruit swelling on the mound tree beside their house, not richly, not extravagantly, but enough to prove that the world had not turned away from them.

The fourth gift would be given to reeds.

Reeds were already wealth. The power would make them also witnesses.

In certain cultivated beds, the reeds would begin to grow with faint bands inside their stalks, visible when split: pale, dark, pale, dark, each band recording the water of that season. Too much sourness, too much rot, a hidden seep of bad minerals, a year of exceptional clarity — all would leave marks. Reed stewards would learn to read them. Children would be taught to split old stems and tell the recent history of a basin.

Their records had been stone, memory, jar marks, channel carvings.

Now the marsh itself would keep an archive.

The fifth gift would be given to fungi.

This would be the most delicate intervention, because fungus in that world is not merely useful; it is one of the great powers. It eats what no animal eats. It softens, returns, warns, ruins, heals. To alter fungus carelessly would be to loosen the whole settlement from beneath.

So the benevolent power would choose only a few kinds already in friendly relation with the people: food fungi, medicine fungi, the quiet white thread that lives in reed compost, the dark velvet that warns of beam-rot. It would make them clearer in their intentions. Healing fungi would grow with a more recognizable scent. Beam-rot fungi would stain wood earlier, before collapse. Composting beds would warm more evenly and turn dead reeds into soil with less sourness.

Nothing would become effortless.

But the boundary between nourishment and danger would become a little more legible.

The sixth gift would be given to sound.

This would be the kindest gift, and the one the power would hesitate over longest.

For the people’s loneliness was not simple. They did not know the absence of animals as a wound in the way outsiders might. Their world had shaped them around it. Their songs were strong because nothing else sang. Their faces mattered because nothing else looked back. To fill the silence would be a cruelty disguised as comfort.

So the power would not add birds.

It would not add insects.

It would not even make the reeds whisper like voices.

Instead, it would teach the water to answer human song very faintly.

Only in the central basin. Only at evening. Only when many voices braided together.

When the people sang across the ankle-deep water, the basin would hold the last note a little longer than it should. Not an echo from walls, not a second singer, not a spirit pretending to be one of them. Just a resonance in the stone beneath the water, a low returning tone that made the singers feel the plateau receiving them.

At first they would fall silent in fear.

Then they would test it.

They would sing one note, then three. They would bring bowls and struck stones. They would discover that songs of mourning made the basin tremble deepest, while work songs scattered into quick little rings. Children would laugh into it and hear laughter return only as brightness on water.

They would not worship the basin as a god, perhaps. Or perhaps some would.

But everyone would know that the town was no longer merely being endured by the mountain.

It was listening.

The seventh gift would be given to dreams.

Not prophecy. Prophecy makes people surrender judgment.

Instead, the sluice keepers, seed carriers, terrace builders, and cistern cleaners would sometimes dream in patterns of flow. A woman troubled by a damp patch beneath her sleeping platform might dream of a thin black line running under her house toward the lower wall. A young seed keeper deciding where to plant a rare root might dream of three mounds: one drowning, one cracking, one holding. An old mason near death might dream of the terraces as they would be two generations later, and wake knowing which apprentice must be told about the western retaining stones.

The dreams would never be exact enough to replace knowledge.

They would be invitations to look again.

That is how a wise power would speak there: not by command, but by sharpening attention.

Then, perhaps, after many quiet years, the people would begin to suspect that something had turned its face toward them.

They would not agree on what it was.

Some would say it was the mountain aging into kindness.

Some would say the water had finally accepted human grammar.

Some would say the dead had learned to pass warnings upward through stone.

Some would say no new thing had happened at all, only that people had become better listeners.

The power would permit all of these explanations.

It would not require a temple.

If they made one anyway, the power would prefer it to be useful: a roofed place for drying seeds, its back wall carved with water marks from every known basin; a cistern court where no one was turned away; a hall where children learned the danger signs of stone, water, reed, and fungus. The power would be pleased by offerings of maintenance: cleared channels, repaired steps, shared seed, clean drinking jars set aside for travelers, old people carried safely over slick paths.

It would dislike blood sacrifice, if they invented the idea from imagination.

It would dislike hoarded water.

It would dislike any priesthood that claimed the right to ignore the level-readers.

Its commandments, if it had any, would be almost indistinguishable from the best laws the people already knew:

Do not block what must flow.  
Do not drain what must rest.  
Do not build where the ground is breathing.  
Do not hide contamination.  
Do not waste reeds.  
Do not keep seed beyond need while another basin goes empty.  
Do not mistake silence for consent.  
Do not mistake patience for safety.  
Listen before cutting.  
Repair before praising.  
Share clear water.  
Carry the future by hand.

And if one person in particular had drawn its attention — one being among the people — it would choose carefully.

It would not choose the loudest, because loudness has little use there.

It might choose a child who notices condensation patterns before adults do. Or a widowed cistern cleaner who has spent half a life preventing illness without being celebrated. Or an old terrace builder whose knees are failing, who can no longer climb quickly enough to inspect the upper walls. Or a seed carrier born with a strange inward restlessness, someone who stands at the plateau edge and wonders whether the lower world contains movement beyond imagining.

To that person, the power would give no crown.

It would give a burden shaped like perception.

They would begin to see relationships more vividly: which channel neglect would become a fever season; which feud over shade was truly grief over a dead child; which grove needed another lineage’s pollen; which young mason should be taught forbidden knowledge because the old rules had become too narrow. They would become inconvenient. They would interrupt councils. They would say, “The water is not wrong; our wall is.” They would say, “That family did not fail from laziness; their cistern is being soured from above.” They would say, “If we keep seed as inheritance only, one day inheritance will be what kills us.”

Some would resent them.

Some would trust them.

They would save lives without seeming miraculous.

The power would protect them only enough that their work could continue. Not from sorrow. Not from misunderstanding. Not from wet feet, aching hands, or the ordinary loneliness of being the first to notice what others prefer not to see.

For wisdom does not make a beloved person invulnerable.

It makes them useful to love.

In time, the settlement would change, but not into something else.

There would still be warm mist, pale stone, dark soil, reed roofs, ankle-deep basins, raised paths, wet gardens, and the immense hush of a world without animal motion. People would still wake to no birdsong. Children would still learn balance before names. Old ones would still sit by channels, listening. The dead would still rest above flood height. Moss would still soften stone by fractions. Reeds would still stand like patient crowds.

But the town would feel, in ways difficult to prove, less alone inside its own silence.

Not noisier.

Not safer in any absolute sense.

Simply accompanied.

The mountain would confess before breaking.  
The cistern would warn before poisoning.  
The reed would remember before forgetting.  
The flower would hold itself ready for the human hand.  
The fungus would distinguish food from ruin more plainly.  
The basin would answer song with a low, stone-deep hum.

And the people, who had always been the mobile part of the marsh, would become also its chosen listeners.

That is what the benevolent power would do.

It would not rescue them from being what they are.

It would help them become more truly, more durably, more tenderly themselves.
