A benevolent and wise power, seeing this place clearly, would first do almost nothing.

Not from indifference. From reverence.

It would understand that the plateau is not unfinished. It is not poor because it has no towers, not mute because it has no voices like ours, not primitive because its people do not make fire or walls. The place is already a long intelligence: water choosing, stone remembering, moss holding, frost correcting, bodies learning the grammar of cold and seepage.

So the power would not descend in radiance. It would not dry the terraces for ease of walking. It would not warm the basins into abundance. It would not raise the low people upright or place tools in their hands or teach them to cut the stone that has sheltered them for ages. It would not call them upward into some louder destiny.

It would listen.

For a long time, perhaps for many seasons, it would be no more than an additional patience in the country.

It would feel the hidden underflows beneath the shelves, the mineral pulse of springs, the slow pressure by which a basin clouds, clears, fills, breaks, and becomes another shape. It would learn which channels are old and which are newly opened. It would know the spring mouths by their coldness and the nursery ledges by the slight trembling of eggs in clear strings. It would learn the low people not as symbols, not as children, not as curiosities, but as they are: careful-bodied, damp-skinned, exact, ancient in attention.

It would notice the oldest ones first.

Not because they are rulers, for perhaps they have no rulers. But because age there is a kind of confluence. The oldest bodies carry districts upon them. A dark-backed one from the fog courts, chin flat to stone, knows which tea-colored basins hold warmth after the first frosts. A pale one from the upper shelves knows the wind and the glare, and can tell by the brightness of water whether ice will form before dawn. A blue-veined one near a spring mouth knows the taste of depth, and can feel changes long before they surface.

The power would not speak to them in thunder. Thunder would only frighten them.

It would speak, if it spoke at all, through what they already trust.

A pressure in the water.

A warmth no greater than the difference between shadow and sun.

A resonance in a known stone, struck from within, answering their throat-clicks with a patience older than weather.

At first they would vanish.

Whole courts would empty. Bodies would slide beneath mineral lips. Eyes would watch from cracks and understeps. The power would accept this. Trust, here, accretes slowly. It would return without harm. It would remain still. It would let algae grow across the places where its presence had touched the water. It would let snails cross the faint boundary of its attention. It would allow itself to be mistaken for a pattern of fog, a new coldness, a small pressure in the basin floor.

Only after long harmlessness would one of the low people approach.

Perhaps an old one, almost ledge-colored, with moss-dark staining along the throat and mineral ridges on the back. It would come no faster than water over a lip. It would pause often, belly close to stone, fingers spread, tasting vibration. It would place one hand on the resonant ledge.

The power would answer with a single pulse:

I am here.

Not above.

Not owner.

Here.

The old one would not understand as one creature understands another through shared words. But it would feel intent: no hunger, no chase, no falling stone, no frost, no talon-shadow. It would feel a vast restraint. That would matter more than brilliance.

Then the power would ask the first and only necessary question:

What harms you that is not yours to bear?

Because a wise power knows the difference between hardship and violation.

Cold is theirs. Scarcity is theirs. Flood and drought, within the old measure, are theirs. Fox, bird, hunger, egg-loss, mineral burial, winter waiting — these belong to the woven law of the plateau. To remove all difficulty would be to remove the shape of their lives. It would make them untrue to themselves, and the plateau untrue to its own making.

But there may be harms beyond the proper severity of the place.

A spring might be failing because of a deep wound in the mountain, opened by forces far below.

A new sickness might be spreading through the egg basins, not born from the ordinary balance of rot and cold, but carried by some wandering contamination from a distant land.

A season might have shifted too violently, freezing the nurseries before the young can quicken, then warming too soon, waking insects out of rhythm with the bodies that need them.

A slope above the plateau might be preparing to collapse, not as small terraces collapse and renew, but as a vast burial, enough to crush spring mouths and silence whole districts.

Or perhaps, far away but approaching, there are upright makers of engines and roads, seeing from a distance what looks like a city, and desiring to enter it with drills, lights, camps, names, instruments, ownership.

Against such harms, the power would act.

Quietly.

Precisely.

Without making itself the center of the world.

If the deep springs were failing, it would not blast open a new river. It would lay a hand, if hand is the word, upon the mountain’s hidden bones. It would ease a blocked seam by the width of a root hair. It would turn a buried flow back toward an old channel. Water would return not in miracle but in continuity: first as a colder taste in a known basin, then as a thin trembling below a nursery shelf, then as a clear thread sliding again from a dark round mouth at the base of a wall.

The low people would notice before any other creature.

They would gather near the spring mouth, throats pulsing, fingers pressed to stone.

Their word for it might be not gift, but return.

If disease troubled the eggs, the power would not make the young invulnerable. It would strengthen the old balances. It would encourage a lichen whose bitter trace discourages the harmful film. It would bring a small crustacean into season at the right hour to graze the clouding rot from the egg strings. It would cool one basin by a fraction and warm another by a fraction, allowing the people to choose, to move, to remember.

They would learn the change as they learn all things: by taste, pressure, survival.

If the weather had begun to tear away from its old cycles, the power would not imprison the sky. It would not decree endless mildness. But it might soften extremes at the margins. A fog arriving one night earlier. A thaw delayed by half a day. A hard frost diverted from the one basin where the last viable eggs of a lineage cling beneath a shelf. A summer glare interrupted by pearl cloud before the shallow upper pools grow lethal.

No one would see intervention.

That would be part of its mercy.

The people would not become dependent on spectacle. They would not gather waiting for light from above. They would continue to read water, because water would still be worth reading.

If the great slope above were preparing to fall, the power would consider carefully. Landslides belong to mountains. Collapse is one of the ways stone speaks. Yet there are collapses that renew and collapses that erase beyond renewal.

So perhaps, over years, the power would loosen lesser stones in many small falls. It would let cracks vent their pressure in winter. It would guide roots into seams that could hold, ice into seams that should release. What might have been one catastrophic burial would become a generation of warnings: ticks in the rock, pale crescents fallen from terrace lips, cloudy basins after rain, new ridges forming where old ledges slump.

The low people would understand.

They would avoid the unstable district after storms. They would teach their young the taste of broken limestone. They would abandon one sleeping line and find another. Their world would change, but not end.

And if the engine-makers came?

Then the power would be firmer.

Not cruel. It is benevolent, and benevolence does not end at the boundary of one species. But wisdom knows that curiosity can become extraction, and admiration can become possession.

The plateau would grow difficult to find.

Maps would disagree. Satellites would show only cloud, glare, meaningless wet stone. Paths through the outer highlands would double back in fog. Machines would fail in mild, untraceable ways: batteries draining, lenses clouding, compasses trembling, boots sinking unexpectedly into harmless but discouraging marsh. Those who came with reverence might reach an outer shelf, see enough to be humbled, and leave with no proof sharper than memory. Those who came with hunger to own, cut, sample, drain, or display would meet weather.

Not storms grand enough to create legends.

Just persistent inconvenience.

Rain too fine to resist and too cold to ignore. Fog thick enough to remove direction. Stone slick at every confident step. Sleep made poor by dripping. Food damp. Equipment furred with mineral. The sense, increasing day by day, that the place does not consent.

If they ignored this, the power would turn them aside more strongly.

A bridge of snowmelt would go early. A pass would close. A harmless fever would persuade evacuation. A funding report would be lost. An appetite elsewhere would distract them. The plateau would remain unnamed in any useful archive.

The low people would know only that the far vibrations had come and gone.

Perhaps one evening, after such a turning-away, the old ledge-colored one would tap the resonant stone three times.

The power would answer once.

Enough.

But the most important thing the power would do would not be rescue.

It would be recognition.

It would hold the plateau in regard without wanting to improve it into something else.

This regard itself would have consequences.

Not obvious ones. No monuments. No chosen prophet rising among the damp-bodied people. No sudden language of stars. But a subtle clarification might pass through the place, as when a pool clears after suspended lime settles.

The resonant stones would sound a little farther.

Not louder; farther.

A knock given in one court might be felt in another that had once been just beyond reach. A warning of fox-shadow might pass under a shelf and across a basin in time for the young to flatten. A message of cold rising might travel to an egg-guardian before ice seals a shallow pool. Groups once separated by collapsed terraces might rediscover one another through vibration.

The people would not call this magic. They might call it good stone.

They would incorporate it into their practice. Certain ledges would become gathering places. Certain rhythms would be remembered. Lineages would exchange routes. Hidden warm underflows would be shared in very hard winters, not always, not without caution, but more often than before.

Their culture, if that is the word, would deepen along its own lines.

Not toward cities, but toward relation.

They might develop longer pulse-patterns for distant water. Not abstract philosophy, perhaps, but hydrological memory made communal: spring weakening in pale district; eggs safe under black moss court; upper glare dangerous after two bright days; old one dead in slow basin; new channel open beneath broken stair.

Over generations, the gift would appear as an enlargement of listening.

That is the sort of gift a wise power prefers: one that makes the receiver more themselves.

It might also bless the dead, though quietly.

When a body is given to water, the power would not preserve it whole. That would be grotesque in a place where return is sacred. Flesh should feed. Bone should lime. The dead should enter shelf, basin, bacterial film, snail-track, amphibian egg, moss-dark seam.

But sometimes, where a beloved old one is laid beneath a mineral lip, the water passing over the body might deposit an especially fine, translucent skin of stone. Not a tomb. Not a statue. Only a pale complication in the ledge, holding for a while the curve of a hand, the blunt patience of a skull, the long outward splay of limbs.

The living would visit flow, as they always have.

Yet when they pressed their throats to that stone, it would answer with a slightly deeper note.

Not the dead speaking. Not exactly.

More like the place remembering with unusual tenderness.

The power might cherish the young most of all, because they are quick and nearly transparent, because their survival is never guaranteed, because in them the plateau has not yet become slow. It would not spare them all. To spare all young is to starve all life. But in seasons of excessive loss, it might make small mercies.

A crust of ice holding through the night instead of breaking under a predator’s step.

A midge hatch lasting one hour longer.

A cloudy pool clearing just enough for a guardian to find the egg string and move it.

A fox distracted by a vole on a dry rim.

A raptor’s shadow blurred by mist.

Small mercies are the proper scale of great compassion in a small, exact ecology.

And perhaps, once only, for the sake of mutual knowledge, the power would allow one of the low people to perceive it more fully.

Not the young. Not one who would be broken by vastness. An old one, near the end, already half stone in habit, lying in a spring-fed basin while evening mist rises and the terraces lose their edges.

The old one rests with chin on hands. Its back is ridged with mineral. One eye is clouded. Its fingers know the basin floor better than sight. Around it, others tap softly: cold coming, stay near, eggs below, night safe.

Then the water stills.

Not freezes. Stills.

Every seep, drip, and underflow continues, but in such harmony that the whole plateau becomes one listening body. The old one feels, through belly and palms and throat, the height of the shelves, the nested basins, the hidden veins, the fog courts, the pale upper districts, the collapsed neighborhoods, the spring mouths breathing from depth. It feels the algae writing green hunger in light. It feels snails making clean arcs. It feels amphibian eggs trembling. It feels fox paws on a dry rim, bird bones in a hollow, roots gripping cracks, frost waiting in the air.

It feels itself not as small against all this, but as included.

One damp body in the wet architecture.

One remembering creature inside a remembering world.

Then, beneath that, or above it, or throughout it, the old one feels the attention of the power: immense, restrained, glad.

No command comes.

No doctrine.

Only this:

You are seen.  
You are not required to become otherwise.  
Hold fast.  
Remember water.  
I will remember you.

The old one closes its eyes.

Perhaps it taps once against the stone.

Perhaps the answer travels farther than sound should travel, through basin, shelf, underflow, mineral seam, sleeping line, nursery ledge, spring mouth, and out across the high wet country until many bodies lift their heads in the mist, listening.

Then the world resumes its ordinary quiet.

A drip falls from an overhang into a hidden pool.

A thread of water drags across stone.

Fog thickens in the sunken courts.

The low people move slowly along their rims, their throats pulsing, their bodies pale and dark according to their districts.

Nothing is transformed beyond recognition.

Nothing is saved from being itself.

And that is the wisdom of the power’s love: it does not make the plateau easier, louder, warmer, drier, richer, or more like any other place.

It keeps the old springs breathing.

It turns aside what would erase.

It strengthens listening.

It allows the dead to enter stone tenderly.

It protects the hidden from the hungry.

It gives, where needed, only as much miracle as can pass for weather.

Then it remains nearby, patient as mineral, subtle as seepage, watchful as an eye just above water.
