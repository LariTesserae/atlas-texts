The benevolent and wise power would come quietly.

Not down from the sky in a wound of gold, not with a hand large enough to cover the plateau, not with music that could be heard above the water. It would not arrive as weather. It would not interrupt the cataracts, still the roar, warm the stone, green the cracks, or call the blind animals upward into revelation.

It would come as attention.

For a long while, perhaps for years as years are counted below, it would do nothing but behold the place completely.

It would learn the plateau’s grammar: how water returns to itself through stone; how each basin keeps its bright meniscus; how the blind grazers know a world by pressure and taste; how the microbes bloom in seams too narrow for light; how the mineral lips grow at the pace of patience. It would listen until the roar ceased to be roar and became intelligible: not language, exactly, but law, memory, maintenance.

Because it was benevolent, it would feel tenderness.

Because it was wise, it would not mistake tenderness for permission to improve.

It would see that the plateau is not a failed meadow. It is not a garden waiting to happen. It is not impoverished because it has no green. Its barrenness is not a wound. Its brightness is not cruelty. Its noise is not torment to those made for it. The pale animals do not dream of eyes. They do not suffer from lacking sky. They are not exiles from warmth. They are at home in a way many richer creatures are not: fitted, exact, nearly indistinguishable from the law that sustains them.

So the power would not give them flowers.

It would not soften the stone.

It would not teach them song.

It would not grant them longing.

That would be the first mercy: restraint.

But doing nothing is not always the same as neglect. And after it had watched long enough to know the difference between a natural ending and a meaningless accident, the power would begin to make changes so slight that no creature of the plateau could notice them as change.

Far below the open surface, where the drowned fissures braided into deeper cold, it would ease one ancient blockage by the width of a hair. A seam that would someday have sealed entirely would remain open. Not wide; not generous; only sufficient. Through it, water would continue to pass, and with the water, the faint chemical messages by which the pale grazers found one another across generations.

In another basin, where a mineral lip had begun to overgrow the sheltered pocket where eggs survived, the power would alter the precipitation of stone by an almost laughable degree. One grain in ten thousand would settle elsewhere. The lip would thicken, but not close. The eggs would remain hidden, rinsed, oxygenated, uncrushed.

At the plateau’s edge, where occasionally a tremor sent too many of the small creatures over the lip into the cataracts, the power would encourage a rib of stone to weather differently. Nothing dramatic: a slight inward curl beneath an overflow, a roughness where there might have been slickness, a comb of mineral teeth under the falling sheet. Most water would still pass. Some animals would still be lost, because life there had always included loss. But fewer would be swept away merely because the world had shifted one fraction too sharply.

It would not abolish danger.

Danger belonged to the place. Current, fracture, hunger, burial under mineral, the sudden vastness of a bird-shadow—these were part of the pattern. To remove them would be to unmake the animals as surely as giving them eyes.

The power would only prevent waste.

Not death. Waste.

It would turn aside the catastrophe that had no meaning in the plateau’s own covenant: a poison blown from some far lower industry, a heat-spasm from a wounded climate, a black dust settling where no such dust had ever belonged, a human boot cracking through a nursery pool and carrying foreign spores in its tread. These things, if they came, would not be lessons. They would not be weather. They would be intrusion.

Against such things, the power would set a boundary.

Not a wall visible from outside. No thunder. No curse. Travelers, if any ever climbed so high, would find themselves misled by fog, or wearied early, or persuaded by some practical instinct that the route ahead was unsafe. Instruments would fail to agree. Maps would blur in that region. Desire would turn elsewhere. The plateau would acquire no legend, because legend attracts. It would remain inconvenient, unprofitable, unnamed.

Birds would still cross.

Rain would still fall.

Stone would still split when its time came.

But the larger hungers of the world would pass around it.

Then, because wisdom is not only protection but comprehension, the power would make one more provision.

Deep inside the plateau, beneath a dense field of scalloped basins where the roar was not loud but absolute, it would gather no material from elsewhere and create no alien chamber. Instead it would notice a place already almost suited: three fissures meeting, one warm seep, one cold thread, one slow mineral drip, a small hollow protected from flood and drought alike. It would let the hollow widen over centuries by persuasion rather than force.

A refuge would form.

Not a paradise. A basin within the basins, lightless and clear, ribbed with stone, lined with microbial films and faintly warmed from below. A place where the oldest grazers could endure when surface waters briefly changed, where eggs could wait through bad years, where the species’ memory could continue if some outer chain of pools failed.

No inhabitant would know it had been made for them.

They would find it as they found everything: by taste, by pressure, by the chemistry of passage. One would enter. Then another. Their trails would accumulate. Generations would use it without gratitude, because gratitude requires an imagined giver, and the power would not demand to be imagined.

That would please it.

There is a kind of benevolence that wants to be loved. This power would be older and cleaner than that. It would be content to be correct.

Still, perhaps, there would be one individual it attended to more closely.

An old pale grazer, broader than most, its back pearled with mineral beads, its joints stiffened by deposits until it moved like a flake of living limestone. It would live in a basin so bright from above that the water seemed empty, though beneath one overhanging rim the old one had traced the same feeding path for years.

The power would know this creature without naming it.

It would know the pattern of its fringes, the slow clearing of film beneath its brushing limbs, the way it paused when cold meltwater entered from the northern seam, the way it could distinguish the tremor of gravel from the tremor of another body approaching. It would know the molts it had survived, the eggs it had fertilized or carried or passed near, the cracks it preferred, the chemical map of its small life.

The power would not lift it from the pool.

It would not say, Behold the sky.

It would not enlarge its mind until the plateau became a prison.

Instead, one day, when a pebble shifted above and would have pinned the old creature beneath a ledge—not as part of any necessary cycle, not as hunger, not as weather, but merely by a useless accident—the power would alter the pebble’s fall by the breadth of a mite’s leg.

The pebble would settle differently.

The old one would feel only a pressure-wave, fold itself, wait, and then resume grazing.

Later, it would move through a narrow siphon it had never entered before. The current would be unusually legible. Mineral taste would arrange itself in a way its body could follow. It would pass into the hidden refuge and find there the proper film, the proper stone, the proper dark.

It would live longer than it otherwise might have.

Not forever. Forever would be another kind of violence.

When its end came, it would be simple. It would loosen from the underside of a mineral lip. Its legs would uncurl. Smaller lives would approach. The water would take the soft parts first. The shell, pearled and thin, would lodge in a crack and become part of a surface over which later young would pass, tasting calcium, old trace, safe passage.

The power would receive this not as tragedy, but as completion.

And perhaps that is the deepest thing it would do: it would keep the plateau complete.

It would preserve the roar without making it sacred theater.

It would guard the water without freezing it into permanence.

It would allow erosion, but not ruin.

It would allow death, but not erasure.

It would allow the blind to remain blind, because their blindness is not deprivation but belonging.

And in some manner too subtle for stone and water and pale nerve to register, it would love them.

Not with warmth.

Not with rescue.

With exactness.

With fidelity.

With the refusal to turn them into something easier to admire.

The plateau would continue as before. The world would remain pared down to water, stone, air, and light. Pools would flash. Cataracts would fall. The roar would press against every surface. In the basins, the small translucent animals would graze their invisible harvest from the mineral dark, touching, tasting, vanishing, returning.

No hymn would rise.

No sign would be left.

But beneath the unending sound, another constancy would have joined the first: a hidden mercy, shaped like the place itself.
