The power would not come down as a hand.

A hand is too simple a thing for such a place. A hand chooses, grasps, divides this from that. It says here and not there, alive and not alive, wound and mouth, animal and hill. The plateau would not understand a hand. Or worse, it would understand it as pressure, intrusion, another force to absorb and answer.

So the power would arrive first as attention.

Not light. Not thunder. Not a voice from above the vapor ceiling.

Attention.

A listening so complete that, for one long interval, the plateau would feel itself beheld without being measured.

The vents would continue breathing. The cliff-grazers would cling to black glass. The transparent hunters would move through mist pockets. Moss would tighten around the legs of small warm things. Pools would fold and unfold their pale citizens. New ridges would rise in the distance already threaded with green.

But beneath all of it, deeper than the piston-throb under stone, the plateau would feel a stillness that did not suppress it.

A stillness that made room.

The benevolent power would spend a long time doing nothing.

This would be its first wisdom.

It would not mistake turbulence for suffering.

It would not mistake instability for error.

It would not look upon the unfinished organism and say, Become complete.

It would not cool the vents, soften the cliffs, clear the sky, straighten distance, or teach water to behave. It would not divide the animals from the land, or the land from its weather, or the weather from its thought. It would not place roads upon it, nor sanctuaries, nor shining towers from which gentler laws could be administered.

The plateau is not sick because it changes.

The plateau is itself because it changes.

So the power would watch.

It would learn the timing of the vents. It would learn the kneeling of the jointed browsers before reality slipped. It would learn the way mineral grasses flattened under magnetic weather. It would learn which tremors were growth and which were distress. It would learn the difference between a ravine closing like a healed wound and a ravine sealing too soon around the life inside it.

It would listen until it could hear the plateau’s pain apart from its becoming.

Only then would it act.

Not to save the plateau from itself.

To save it from what was not truly itself.

First, it would place a boundary.

Not a wall. Walls invite pressure. Walls are challenges to weather.

The boundary would be a misunderstanding laid gently around the plateau’s edges.

To birds from the lower world, the highland would seem farther away than it was. To wandering beasts, the slopes would appear barren and uninteresting. To any clever species that might one day climb with instruments, hunger, flags, and names, the plateau would become inconvenient. Maps would disagree. Compasses would grow embarrassed. Roads would fail before being begun. Expeditions would circle back into their own footprints and swear they had crossed a continent.

Satellites, if such things existed, would see cloud.

Prophets would dream of it and wake with no directions.

Conquerors would forget why they wanted it.

This boundary would not imprison the plateau. Its gliders could leave if weather allowed. Its spores could ride the upper winds. Its rains could fall beyond the escarpments now and then. But predation from the world below—the kind that names, cuts, extracts, simplifies—would be turned aside by merciful confusion.

The power would give the young organism privacy.

Second, it would ease the key-changes.

Not stop them.

Never stop them.

Those moments when the plateau became many proposed places at once were not flaws. They were imagination moving through matter. They were how the vast being tested itself. In those slips, new ridges were conceived, vanished basins remembered, lost herds reissued, climates compared. The plateau was not merely enduring instability; it was thinking with it.

But the slips had teeth.

Sometimes a creature returned from them with too many shadows and could not gather itself again. Sometimes a pool remembered so many skies that its own water forgot which light to answer. Sometimes a grove duplicated with its wound but not its root, and starved while believing itself whole. Sometimes the transparent hunters shortened space too far and were caught between distances, starving in a fold no mist could reach.

The power would not remove this danger.

Life there was built of danger.

But it would introduce rhythm.

A kindness so subtle that no animal would notice except by surviving.

The key-changes would begin to announce themselves a breath earlier.

A vent would pause twice instead of once.

Metallic grasses would flatten in a wider ring.

The filament crowns of gliders would glow not brighter, but sooner.

Pool creatures would feel the tremor beneath the tremor and seal themselves before reflection became hazardous.

The jointed browsers would still kneel, but now they would kneel in time.

Reality would still slip.

The plateau would still become several versions of itself.

But fewer lives would be caught unprepared in the seam.

Third, the power would give the plateau a place where nothing had to become anything else immediately.

A small place.

Wisdom would not make it large.

In one of the central basins, where black water breathed under mineral film and young basalt sweated green, the power would touch a hollow no wider than a grove.

The hollow would not become safe in any absolute sense. Safety, if too pure, would poison the plateau. It would create dependence. It would create a lie.

But there, change would slow.

Not cease.

Slow.

A fallen seed would have a full night before deciding whether to root, hatch, bloom, or sleep.

A wounded grazer could lie down without being revised by moss before its breathing ended.

A pool creature dragged too far from water could fold and wait through dryness without hardening into stone.

A glider egg pressed into a crack would not be swallowed by a new fissure before condensation came.

A root could enter rock and find not refusal, not hunger, but patience.

The place would be called nothing. There would be no language for it.

But animals would begin to find it.

Not always. Not by path. The land preserved no paths.

They would arrive by pressure, by instinct, by the underground pulse, by a strange lessening of fear in the bones.

The moss predators would grow there too, but they would feed only on what had already surrendered. The flowers would close, but gently. The vents would breathe in softer sequence. The black pools would reflect only the sky directly overhead.

For the first time in its young life, the plateau would contain rest.

Fourth, the power would give it memory that did not have to be scar.

This would be the most delicate act.

The plateau already remembered. It remembered through repeated wounds, inherited mineral gardens, duplicated groves, pools full of absent skies, vines holding the shape of animals that had passed when cliffs were soft. But all its memory came through pressure. Through imprint. Through breakage. Through loss reissued as form.

The power would not take that away.

Scars were one of the plateau’s languages.

But it would add another.

During the brief warm rains after a key-change, when every surface shone and the hidden creatures began again, a second pulse would move through the water.

Very faint.

Not sound. Not thought.

Pattern.

A way for the organism to know, for one suspended moment, that the grazer on the cliff and the root in the vent and the pale skin in the pool and the storm turning above the ash ceiling were all participating in one body.

The plateau would not become a person.

That would be too small.

It would not suddenly speak. It would not name itself. It would not separate subject from object, self from world, question from answer.

But sometimes, after the rain, a vent would breathe in a rhythm that matched the opening of flowers miles away. A herd would turn its mossy flanks pale not from warning but from recognition. Gliders circling doubled cliffs would no longer become frantic during the slip; they would wheel through overlapping air as if following music. Root networks in separate ravines would pulse together once, twice, then return to their local hungers.

The plateau would begin to remember joy.

Not pleasure. Pleasure it already knew: heat, mineral, moisture, blood, bloom.

Joy was different.

Joy was the sensation of being many and not broken.

Fifth, the power would teach mercy to the places where appetite had outrun wisdom.

This too would be done without law.

No commandment would be carved into basalt. No predator would be made herbivore. No moss would be shamed for tightening around a warm leg.

But the soft-bodied hunting mats would begin, slowly, to distinguish panic from passage. They would still rise when hunger required it. They would still drink. Yet sometimes, when a young animal stumbled and thrashed from fear rather than weakness, the moss would loosen. Not always. Often enough.

The transparent mist hunters would still shorten space, but they would no longer strike through nesting cracks. The vapor-drinking giants would still cling to warm cliffs, but some new sensation in their broad hooked limbs would warn them before their combined weight softened stone around living eggs.

Flowers would still close around insects.

But a few would open again.

No species would be purified.

Only given the chance, now and then, to refrain.

That is all mercy can be, in a world that must eat.

A chance to pause before completing the hunger.

Sixth, the power would place one star where the plateau could see it.

The sky was dim, layered, bruised with vapor and particulate ash-light. The sun never quite arrived. Storms pulsed overhead without thunder. The plateau lived by heat from beneath, by wetness in interruption, by mineral glow and electrical shiver.

It did not need stars.

But the benevolent power knew that every living system, however vast, benefits from one thing that is not part of its metabolism.

One thing not eaten, not absorbed, not revised.

So above the highest blade ridges, beyond the rotating storm masses, one point of clear light would appear at irregular intervals.

Not every night.

Not even most nights.

Only when the air thinned after a warm rain, when the vents had resumed their breathing and the metallic grasses stood upright again.

The star would be small and steady.

The plateau would not worship it.

But the gliders would learn its angle. The cliff-grazers would lift their blunt heads when it appeared. Pools would hold it without altering its shape. Fungal fans would dim beneath ravine walls, as though listening. Even the underground engine would seem, for a few pulses, less alone.

The star would be a reminder that there are presences beyond appetite.

Beyond revision.

Beyond the enormous labor of becoming.

Finally, the power would withdraw.

Not abandon.

Withdraw.

A wise benevolence does not remain at the center of another being’s life.

It does not make itself indispensable.

It leaves behind conditions under which the beloved thing may become more completely itself.

So the power would recede from hand, from boundary, from basin, from rain-pattern, from star, until only its attention remained at the far edge of awareness: a warmth too gentle to be heat, a silence too spacious to be suppression.

The plateau would continue.

It would still fracture, thrust, split, uplift, collapse.

New gullies would cut straight down slopes before learning to meander. Basalt would ripple slowly on the western escarpments. Mist would become membrane between trees. Pools would reflect skies not overhead. A grove would be swallowed and return downslope with identical scars. Stones would open with seeds inside. The world would stutter while forming.

Predators would feed.

Storms would misbehave.

Distance would lie.

The young, immense organism would remain unfinished.

But now, when the whole plateau changed key, there would be a breath before the slipping.

The animals would prepare.

The roots would brace.

The pools would seal or open according to deeper knowing.

The rain would fall warm and brief.

And afterward, in one small basin where change moved slowly, a wounded grazer might sleep through the night.

A glider egg might wait for the proper mist.

A flower might open again and release the trembling thing it had nearly eaten.

A black pool might hold, without distortion, the single patient star.

And beneath all this, the plateau would feel—not in words, not as a mind feels, but as a vast living weather feels—the shape of a gift it had not asked for and had not been forced to receive.

Room.

Rhythm.

Refuge.

Memory without wound.

Hunger with pause.

A light beyond itself.

Then the vents would breathe.

The moss would drink.

The cliffs would shine.

And the unfinished god of stone, weather, root, animal, heat, and rain would go on becoming.
