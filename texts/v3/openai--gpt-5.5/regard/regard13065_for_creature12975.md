A benevolent and wise power would not try to silence the Great Bellows.

That would be the first mercy.

It would understand, almost immediately, that the roar is not a wound in the world. It is the world speaking in the only voice it has. The grinding mandibles, the swelling trunks, the filtering sacks, the shriek of wings, the deep hydraulic groan of growth—these are not accidents to be corrected. They are not cruelty, though cruelty happens within them. They are the grammar of the place.

A lesser god would bring quiet.

A frightened god would bring stone.

A proud god would carve halls in the living floor and call them sanctuaries, only to watch them rupture, root over, and become hazards. It would teach the Weavers to build against the Bellows, and in doing so would make them strangers to their own wisdom.

But a wise power would kneel.

Not physically, perhaps. It may have no knees. It may be a star-mind, a hidden law, a wandering saint of worlds, a vast patient consciousness passing between realities like pollen on a cosmic wind. But in the manner that matters, it would lower itself. It would become small enough to listen through the pads of a Weaver’s feet.

For a long while, it would do nothing.

It would let the Bellows enter it.

It would learn the deep note of the trunk-spires when they are about to surge. It would learn the brittle hiss before a bark-slide. It would learn the frantic, needle-fast tremor of a scavenger swarm and the slow, sickly slosh of overpressured ichor beneath a sealed root. It would feel how the Weavers feel: not sound, not noise, but a map made of pressure and intention.

It would watch a Weaver find a hollow beneath a sloughed bark plate and begin its work.

The Weaver would not pray. It would not raise its face. It would not know it was observed.

It would simply braid.

Claw, pull, pause.  
Touch the root-mat.  
Listen.  
Shift the vine by a finger’s width.  
Wait for the growing thing to accept the suggestion.  
Tie, loosen, redirect.

And the benevolent power would understand then that this creature had already discovered holiness.

Not dominion.  
Not permanence.  
Not victory.

Conversation.

So the power would not grant the Weavers a fortress.

It would grant them better replies.

The first gift would be almost imperceptible.

Deep beneath the root-mat, where the amber viscin moves through pressure-veins older than any Weaver clan, a new rhythm would begin. Not louder than the Bellows. Not separate from it. A subharmonic, low and patient, woven beneath the roar like a dark thread in cloth.

At first, only the oldest Weavers would feel it.

They would pause in their careful walking. Their rear limbs would spread. The cilia along their pads would rise.

There: a pulse.

Three slow compressions beneath the world’s usual frenzy.

Then, some breaths later, the ground would heave open half a mile away, exactly where the new pulse had said it would.

The old Weaver would drum a warning to its kin.

Not words, but pattern:

**The world has begun to clear its throat before it speaks.**

This would save lives.

Not all lives. A wise mercy does not abolish risk in a world made of motion. But it would create an interval. A sliver of choice between the warning and the rupture. Enough time to move a clutch of young. Enough time to abandon a shelter. Enough time to pull the bone-needles from a borehole and flee before the trunk-spire shouldered upward through the floor.

The Bellows would remain the Bellows.

But its catastrophes would begin, sometimes, to announce themselves.

The second gift would grow.

Not everywhere. Not in fields. Not in obedience.

Here and there, in folds of root and along the undersides of bark plates, a new vine would appear. It would be dull gray-green, almost ugly against the rust and amber world. It would not compete well in open growth. It would be eaten readily by scrapers and tunneling beetles. It would die if ignored.

But if touched by Weaver claws in a certain rhythm, if bent and braided with the old patience, it would respond.

It would remember the shape of the hand that guided it.

It would thicken at crossings, soften at edges, and grow into lattices more intricate than ordinary vine-screens. Its hollow fibers would drink the roar and return it altered—not absent, never absent, but softened into layers. The shriek of wings would become tolerable. The deep whoosh of the filter-bags would become a pulse one could sleep beside. The maddening high hum of the light-organs would be combed into something almost like song.

The Weavers would not call these vines holy.

They would call them useful.

That would please the power.

In time, a family group would discover that a shelter woven with the gray vine could hold calm for three cycles instead of one. Then five. Sometimes seven, if the surrounding growth was slow and the predators kept away.

Infants would sleep longer.

The old would die less often from exhaustion.

Mates would sit with forelimbs touching and exchange long tremor-stories in the dim baffled hum.

And when the shelter was finally swallowed—as all shelters were—the vine would not resist. It would loosen. Its fibers would go sweet. Beetles would eat it. Spores would root in it. Its brief mercy would return to the frenzy.

The third gift would be memory.

This would be the most delicate one, and the power would hesitate before giving it.

For the Weavers’ symbols were beautiful because they vanished. Their poems belonged to the present moment. A spiral carved into healing wood was not a failure because it disappeared. It was a truth shaped for a world where nothing held still.

To make those symbols permanent would be to misunderstand them.

So the power would not preserve the marks.

Instead, it would teach the living wood to keep an echo.

When a Weaver carved a spiral, the surface would still heal. The visible line would blur, swell, close, and vanish beneath fresh bark-flesh. But deep in the grain, a faint alteration would remain—not a scar, not damage, but a resonance.

A hidden knot of vibration.

Another Weaver passing days later, or years, or generations, might set its pads upon that place and feel something strange beneath the ordinary roar.

A spiral.  
A rest.  
Sweet ichor.  
Three cycles of safety.  
A child hatched here.  
A predator passed above and did not see us.  
We were afraid.  
We endured.

The Weavers would discover this slowly.

One solitary nomad, crossing a root-bridge near a roaring ichor fall, would stop because the ground beneath its feet was speaking in a rhythm no current creature was making. It would crouch low, all six limbs pressed to the warm mat. Its black eyes would half-close.

There, beneath the healed surface, a poem.

Not preserved against the world, but carried by it.

After that, the Weavers would carve differently.

Not more grandly. Not with monuments. But with the knowledge that the Bellows itself might remember—not forever, perhaps, but longer than bark, longer than fear, longer than one body’s passage through the storm.

Their trails would become an invisible library.

A place that rewrote its books would now also hum with the ghosts of readings.

The fourth gift would not be for the Weavers alone.

Because the benevolent power would know that compassion which favors only the speaking mind becomes another form of conquest.

It would attend to the Bellows.

It would ease no hunger entirely. Predators would still hunt. Beetles would still drill. Filter-bags would still breathe their enormous breaths. Chitin would still crack between jaws. Life would still devour itself to make more life.

But in the pressure-veins where viscin gathered too violently, where sealed growth led to explosive ruptures that killed without feeding, the power would introduce weakness.

Tiny planned failures.

Soft seams.

When pressure rose beyond use, a root would split in narrow channels instead of catastrophic wounds. Amber ichor would bead slowly along ridges. Scrapers would feed. Beetles would gather. Weavers would glean. The plant-flesh would heal cleaner afterward.

Waste would become offering.

Disaster would become feast.

Not always. Never always. The Bellows was too vast, too alive, too much itself to be made gentle throughout. But here and there, meaningless destruction would tilt toward nourishment.

The fifth gift would be given only once.

A young Weaver, separated from its kin during a growth-quake, would crawl into the shadow of a bark plate and find itself trapped. Behind it, new root-flesh would swell shut. Before it, a predator-flock would cling to the walls of the narrow passage, their jaws clicking, their wing-hooks scraping.

The young one would freeze.

The Bellows would roar.

No family drum would reach it through the convulsion of the ground.

Then, beneath its four listening limbs, it would feel a pattern.

Not a command.

A question.

**Can you follow?**

The young Weaver would tremble.

The pattern would come again, tapped through the living floor by no visible hand.

**Can you follow?**

The Weaver would answer as Weavers answer: one claw against the mat.

**Yes.**

The power would guide it then, not by lifting it away, not by striking predators dead, but by opening its perception one degree wider.

The young Weaver would feel the passage as a whole: the air-sacks breathing above, the root swelling behind, the predators’ weight on the left wall, the hollow seam beneath the right.

It would see, through vibration, the path that already existed.

It would crawl into the seam. It would scrape its shoulder raw. It would wait while a predator’s jaws snapped so close that the wind of them struck its face. It would press onward. It would emerge hours later beneath a curtain of gray-green vine, into a shelter where its kin had taken refuge.

They would touch it all over, reading its terror and survival.

It would drum the pattern it had felt.

**Can you follow?**

The elders would not know this rhythm.

But they would remember it.

In later generations, it would become a teaching-pattern used for the young before dangerous crossings. Not a prayer, exactly. Not a name. The Weavers would have no doctrine of the power. No idols. No carved image. Their world healed too quickly for idols.

But sometimes, when the ground warned early, when a shelter held longer than it should, when an old forgotten poem hummed beneath healed bark, a Weaver would pause and place all six limbs against the Bellows.

And from the endless roar, it might feel an answering gentleness.

Not rescue.

Kinship.

The Great Bellows would continue.

It would grow, split, feed, blaze, breathe, and thunder. The air would remain hot and golden with spores. The light-organs would hum. The filter-bags would sigh. The beetles would drill. The flyers would hunt. The ground would betray the unwary.

The Weavers would still be small.

They would still be temporary.

Their shelters would still fall.

Their symbols would still vanish from sight.

But now, woven through the savage abundance of that underground world, there would be a new law, too subtle to be called law by anything but the wise:

Creation need not be less wild to be less lonely.

And the benevolent power, having done enough, would withdraw from the center of things.

It would not rule.

It would not be worshiped.

It would remain as a listening beneath listening, a mercy folded into vibration, a patient presence in the pressure-roots.

The Bellows would roar.

The Weavers would touch the ground.

And sometimes, before the world broke open, it would warn them.
