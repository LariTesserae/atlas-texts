The benevolent power would not bring light.

It would understand, first of all, that light is not always a gift.

To the surface-born mind, the cavern seems forsaken: buried, stagnant, nearly empty, a place defined by deprivation. But the wise power would perceive more carefully. It would feel the cavern’s slow pulse: the seep of mineral water through stone, the patient respiration of fungus, the minute tremors of the blind worm’s appendages as they read the dark.

It would know that this is not a prison to the creature.

This is a world.

So the power would not split the ceiling open. It would not flood the chamber with sun, or voices, or green growing things that belong elsewhere. It would not summon companions merely because solitude looks tragic from afar. It would not remake the worm into something winged, eyed, bright, and restless.

Instead, it would descend without arriving.

A quiet attention would gather in the cavern, so gentle that even the stale air would not stir at first. The drops would continue falling. The roots would hang unmoving. The worm would pause, sensing no predator, no rupture, no approaching mass—only a subtle change in the texture of existence, like warmth remembered by stone.

The power would listen.

It would listen to the cavern’s ancient injuries: the chisels that once bit into its walls, the hands that shaped pedestals for forgotten purposes, the long abandonment that followed. It would listen to the worm’s body, to its dim hungers and wordless satisfactions, to the old rhythm by which it had survived. It would learn what was needed before deciding whether need existed at all.

And it would find that little was needed.

Not nothing.

But little.

So the first act would be preservation.

Far above, unseen faults would ease their pressure by a fraction. The slow violence of the earth would be guided elsewhere, not stopped, never stopped, but persuaded. A collapse that might have happened in three centuries would not happen. A seam of poisonous gas that might one day have bled into the chamber would seal under its own mineral crust. The cavern would remain itself.

The second act would be nourishment.

Not abundance. Abundance would be a flood, and floods destroy the delicate.

Instead, the seep water would acquire the faintest new richness. Trace minerals from deep strata would dissolve into it in barely measurable quantities. Where moisture gathered, the fungi would thicken—not into lush carpets, not into invading blooms, but into steadier, healthier colonies. The worm would find that its long circuits through the cavern yielded enough. Its body would repair small deficiencies it had carried for centuries without knowing them as pain.

When it fed, the enzymes it exuded would mingle with the altered minerals and leave behind faint opalescent residues. Over decades, these residues would harden into thin, translucent films upon the carved walls and chiseled pedestals. The old human geometry would not be erased. The worm’s slow trails would not be erased either. They would meet.

Stone, tool-mark, fungus, mucilage, mineral sheen.

A scripture no eye had to read.

The third act would be companionship—but not the kind that intrudes.

The power would not create another worm unless the cavern could sustain it, and unless the first creature’s nature could welcome it. Wisdom would hesitate here. To be alone is not always to suffer. To be given another living presence is not always a kindness.

So the power would choose something smaller.

In the dampest crevices, among the fungal roots, it would awaken a harmless microbial life suited to the cavern: slow, pale, almost imperceptible. These tiny organisms would aid the fungi, purify decay, soften harsh compounds in the water. They would become part of the worm’s food, part of its world, part of the cavern’s metabolism.

The worm would not know it had been given anything.

It would only know that certain moist places tasted fuller.

That old weakness came less often.

That the cavern, in its familiar silence, seemed slightly more alive.

The fourth act would be memory.

The wise power would place no monument here. No altar. No glowing sigil. It would not claim the cavern.

But it would gather what the cavern had kept: the pressure of ancient footsteps, the scrape of tools, the breath of those long-dead humans who once descended with purpose into the dark. It would gather, too, the worm’s long unspoken history: every trail, every feeding, every pause beneath the skeletal roots, every touch of feathery appendage against stone.

These memories it would not remove.

It would anchor them.

The rock itself would hold them more faithfully. Not as words, not as images, but as resonance. If one day, far in the future, another sensitive being entered this place with reverence rather than hunger, they might feel it: a solemn continuity, the sense that the cavern had not been empty, that life had been here in its own form, sufficient and strange and worthy.

The fifth act would be protection from curiosity without reverence.

Should miners one day drill too near, their instruments would give confused readings. Rich veins would seem poorer than they were. Maps would nudge ambition elsewhere. A tunnel angled toward the cavern would meet harder stone, worse air, less promise. Those who came only to extract would be gently turned aside by inconvenience, by cost, by doubt.

But if someone came in humility—if some future wanderer, scholar, or pilgrim approached with care—the cavern would not be hidden from them entirely.

The power would not make a museum of it.

Yet neither would it condemn all contact.

It would allow only those capable of silence to find the final passage.

And even then, the worm would be given warning long before their arrival: tremors through stone, changes in air, the distant taste of foreign warmth. It would have time to withdraw into narrow galleries where no large creature could follow. Its world would not be seized from it by astonished hands.

For the worm itself, the power would offer one gift more intimate than all the rest.

Not speech.

Speech would be too sharp.

Not sight.

Sight would be a wound.

Instead, the power would deepen the creature’s existing senses.

Its feathery appendages, already exquisitely attuned, would begin to perceive subtler harmonies: the mineral moods of stone, the slow growth of fungi, the far-off shift of water behind walls. The worm would move through its cavern not merely by contact and taste, but by a fuller participation in the hidden order of things.

It would not think, “I am blessed.”

It would feel, in its blind and bodily way, that the cavern was continuous with itself.

That the dripping had meaning.

That the stone answered.

That the old pedestals were not obstacles, but presences.

The benevolent power would leave no commandment. It would require no worship. It would not demand gratitude from a creature never made for kneeling.

It would simply remain, lightly.

A listening woven into the dark.

A mercy that did not announce itself.

And in the cavern, a thousand meters below the surface, the drips would go on sounding larger than they were. The warm stale air would hold its mold-rich breath. The ancient roots would hang like pale bones. Fungi would gather at the wet seams. The carved walls would glimmer, over centuries, with translucent trails.

The worm would continue its slow passage through the dark.

Not rescued.

Not transformed.

Not made other than itself.

Only sustained, protected, and quietly honored.

And perhaps that would be the wisest kindness: to recognize that even in a forsaken cavern, even in a blind and solitary life, there can be a complete world—and to bless that world without breaking it.
