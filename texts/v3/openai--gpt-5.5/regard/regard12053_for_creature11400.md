# What the Benevolent Power Does

First, it does not descend.

This is important.

A lesser kindness would come down in fire or in feathers or in a pillar of warm gold and say: *I have found you. I understand. I will help.* It would flood the plateau with sunlight. It would soften the wind. It would fill the spaces between the trees with soil and grasses and browsing animals, because emptiness looks like lack to those who have never learned its grammar. It would make bridges that do not rot. It would pull the climbers from the bark, if they are in the bark, and give them bodies again. It would call that rescue.

The wise power does none of this.

It remains at the edge of the sky for a long time, regarding the plateau the way one regards a sleeping animal: with care, and from far enough away that its breathing is not disturbed.

It notices the cold. It notices the dark. It notices the hollowness of the trees and the way hollowness here is not absence but capacity. It notices that the open ground is not a wound, but a boundary; not a failure of forest, but the condition that lets each tree become a world. It notices the old ropes, the half-made knot, the whistle on the cairn.

It listens.

That is the first thing.

For a year, or ten years, or a period that cannot be measured from within the plateau, it listens to the booming of the trees. It learns the difference between storm-song and hunger-song. Between a call meant for light and a call meant for memory. Between the low satisfied note of a tree whose reservoirs are full and the thin, almost inaudible strain of one that has misjudged the fog season and is drying from within.

It listens to the phosphor-moss brightening under sound.

It listens to the small animals in the canopy.

It listens to the cliffs cracking in frost.

It listens, very carefully, for human voices.

At first there are none.

Or none that can be separated from the trees.

Then, in the upper hollows of the leaning tree, where the bronze moss has grown thick over the old climbing notches and the air is warmer than it should be, it hears something that is not quite speech but has the shape of having once been speech.

A pause.

A breath.

A remembered name, sung without lungs.

The power understands then that the question is not whether the climbers left.

The question is how many ways there are to remain.

So the second thing it does is refuse to decide for them.

It does not say: *You are people, and therefore must be restored.*

It does not say: *You are trees, and therefore must be left alone.*

It does not say: *This is evolution,* or *This is death,* or *This is transcendence,* because all three words are too blunt for what has happened here. The climbers have not vanished. They have not survived unchanged. They have not been consumed. They are present in a manner native to the place, and the place is young, and young places invent cruel and beautiful solutions before anyone arrives with names.

The power accepts uncertainty as a form of reverence.

Then it acts.

Very little, at first.

It turns one storm.

Not away. Not into warmth. Only slightly.

A bank of wet cloud climbing from the lower valleys would have passed east of the plateau, drawn off by pressure and high winds. The wise power lays a thought against the upper air, no heavier than a hand against the shoulder of a child, and the cloud leans west instead.

For nine days the plateau is inside whiteness.

Condensation gathers on the black rock. It beads along the old rope bridge. It pearls on the carved whistle in the cairn. The trees drink and drink. Their spiral channels fill. The hidden reservoirs beneath their bark swell. In the hollows, the phosphor-gardens brighten without being sung to, then brighter when the trees begin to boom.

Across the plateau, for the first time in a dry season that would otherwise have killed three of the youngest trees, every great trunk answers every other.

The sound is enormous.

It is not music in the way a human listener would first understand music. It is too slow. Too full of intervals meant to travel through wood and water rather than air. But the power hears what is inside it: relief, yes, and the reflexive gratitude of living things when thirst is interrupted.

And something else.

From the high hollows, from the script-patterned bark, from the old ones who climbed best and became light and were carried upward with a bowl of water and a song, there comes a stirring.

Not awakening exactly.

Recognition.

The power withdraws its hand from the weather.

That is all the weather-help it gives for a long time. A kindness repeated too often becomes a dependency, and this place has not asked to be made into a garden. The plateau knows drought. The trees know storage. The climbers knew hardship so intimately they built etiquette around it. To erase that would erase part of their intelligence.

But the power marks the pattern of killing droughts and decides that extinction by accident is not sacred.

So, thereafter, when a dry season crosses the invisible line between difficulty and ending, the clouds remember another road.

Not always.

Only enough.

The third thing the power does is mend nothing that can mend itself.

The half-rotted bridge remains half-rotted.

The cold hearth remains cold.

The wooden bowl keeps its dried residue.

The bone whistle stays on the cairn.

But the power lays a small prohibition over the place, so fine that even the wind cannot feel it.

Those who come in greed will lose the path.

This is not dramatic. No lightning strikes them. No abyss opens. The plateau simply becomes confusing. Distances lengthen. Familiar stars slide a little out of place. The trees do not answer their calls, or answer from the wrong direction. The open ground becomes more open than it was. Sound behaves badly. A person who comes to cut, to harvest, to pry the script-bark loose and carry it away in bundles, will find after an hour of walking that the cairn is still ahead of them, no nearer than before. After another hour they will decide, uneasily, to turn back. By dusk they will reach the cliff road with no memory of having turned around.

Those who come in hunger may pass.

Those who come in grief may pass.

Those who come in wonder may pass, if they are quiet.

Those who come to listen will find the distances honest.

This is the fourth thing: the power protects the plateau not from contact, but from extraction.

It understands that isolation and safety are not the same. The climbers once built bridges. They were learning to connect their trees. They left whistles for strangers. *Someone might. Let them find their way.* A place that leaves instructions for guests has not chosen perfect secrecy.

So the power does not hide the Hollow Canopy from all minds.

Instead, it teaches the threshold.

A traveler climbing from the lower valleys may dream, the night before reaching the plateau, of a hand pressed to bark. They may wake with the taste of cold air in their mouth and a phrase they do not know how to translate: *Sing once. Put back what you lift. Do not ask the old ones to descend.*

A child of the valley-people — perhaps carrying, without knowing it, one narrow inheritance from those who went down generations ago — may hear, in the hollow of a storm, a note that makes their hands ache for climbing. If they follow it years later, they will find the cairn. They will find the whistle. If they are courteous, they will blow it only once.

Somewhere across the plateau, the right tree will answer.

And if that child sings their name back, quietly, and leaves the whistle where it was, the power will do nothing at all.

Because that is not its conversation.

The fifth thing it does is stranger.

It gives the trees a dream of bridges.

Not bridges made of rope. Not yet. Perhaps never again.

The old bridges belonged to a particular era of hands and fiber and argument. They belonged to climbers who still moved between trunks with bodies that could fall. To reproduce those bridges would be nostalgia, and the wise power distrusts nostalgia when it pretends to be healing.

Instead, deep in the seasonal growth beneath the bark, where the faint script repeats and alters itself in rings, the power introduces a possibility.

A root-message.

The trees stand kilometers apart. Their roots grip shallow soil and fractured stone. They cannot meet underground in any ordinary way. But water moves through the plateau in hidden pulses. Fog condenses, runs down, seeps into cracks, freezes, melts, descends, rises again as vapor. Mineral salts travel. Fungal spores travel. Sound travels through bedrock farther than expected.

The power touches none of these systems hard enough to change their nature.

It only makes them more legible to one another.

A tree that is thirsty begins to be heard, faintly, not only in air but in stone.

A tree whose inner gardens are failing sends, through fungal dust and mineral seep and low vibration, the chemical equivalent of a song.

Another tree, a kilometer away, receives it slowly. Not as urgency. Trees do not hurry. But as information.

Over decades, the closed kingdoms become less closed.

Not unified. Not made into one organism. Difference is preserved. The lilac-glow tree remains the lilac-glow tree. The bronze moss tree remains itself. The tree full of small singing things keeps its own shrill weather. The leaning tree leans.

But they begin to know when one of them is in danger.

They begin, sometimes, to answer before the wind asks them to.

In the high hollows, the climbers-who-may-be-trees hear this new under-song and gather around it.

If they have faces, they turn them.

If they have hands, they lay them against the inner wood.

If they are no longer separable from the patterned bark, then the pattern deepens.

The power has not given them speech. It has given them a road that does not require crossing the open ground.

Whether they use it is theirs.

The sixth thing: it makes one place warm.

Not the plateau. Not even a whole tree.

Only one hollow, halfway up the tree that knew the whistle’s owner.

The hollow already existed. It had been used once, long ago, as a rest-place during climbs between lower dwelling and upper canopy. Its platform had collapsed. Its hide-curtain had torn loose and gone stiff with frost. A bowl lay there, cracked down one side. On the inner wall, someone had carved a spiral and then stopped, either interrupted or tired.

The power does not repair the platform.

It does not seal the entrance.

It does not fill the bowl.

It only adjusts, by a fraction, the slow metabolism of the tree around that chamber, so that warmth gathers there more reliably than elsewhere. A body in danger of freezing could survive a night in it. A traveler who had misjudged a climb could rest there. A valley-child with climber blood in their hands could sleep and dream safely.

And an old voice in the tree, one that remembers having owned a whistle of carved bone, notices the warmth.

This matters.

For many seasons the voice has been dispersed. It has not suffered exactly, because suffering requires a boundary, and the boundary has thinned. But there are kinds of loneliness available even to those who have become large and wooden and sung-through by wind.

The warmed hollow becomes a point of gathering.

Memory condenses there.

The whistle’s owner — if that is still a meaningful phrase — dreams more coherently near that hollow than elsewhere. It remembers hands. It remembers a sibling’s child being held against bark and taught the tree’s name. It remembers repairing a bridge and stopping because the decision had been made, though not everyone had agreed. It remembers placing the whistle on the cairn.

It remembers why.

Not as a sentence. Not at first.

Then, after years of warmth and fog and the under-song of trees learning to hear each other through stone, the memory becomes clear enough to shape itself.

*We did not leave to be lost.*

The power hears.

It does not answer in words.

It sends a wind through the hollow, soft enough not to chill, strong enough to make the chamber hum.

The tree booms.

The phosphor-moss brightens pale green, then lilac, then cold white.

Across the open plateau, the cairn waits.

The seventh thing the power does is invite witness.

Not many.

Never many.

Once in a generation, perhaps, someone finds the place who can understand without owning.

A botanist whose instruments fail one by one until they are forced to put their ear to bark.

A mourning sister carrying ashes she does not know where to scatter.

A valley singer born with a chest too deep for the lowlands, mocked for a voice that makes bowls vibrate on shelves.

A child who has never seen a star at noon.

They come separately, years apart. They find the plateau mostly air. They feel afraid, as they should. The open rock does not become safe for them. The predators still listen. The cold still enters sleeves and lungs. The trees still stand far apart, enormous and indifferent until they are not.

If they take up the whistle, they feel its polish: thumb-worn, mouth-worn, waiting.

If they blow it, the right tree answers.

If they sing their name back and replace it, the plateau accepts that they have manners.

Sometimes nothing more happens.

That is allowed.

Not every sacred encounter must reward the visitor.

But sometimes, if the witness remains still long enough, a second note rolls out from the answering tree. Higher than the boom. Almost a voice. Almost the memory of a voice.

The witness will not be able to translate it.

They will carry it down anyway.

In the valleys below, this becomes a story.

Not an accurate story. Stories that travel downward grow leaves of their own. In one version, the plateau is full of giants. In another, the lost climbers became birds. In another, the bone whistle summons the dead and must never be touched. In another, every tree contains a city.

The power permits all these errors.

Accuracy is not the only vessel of care.

What matters is that the stories teach three things:

Do not cut the singing trees.

Do not steal the whistles.

If the dark forest answers you, answer politely.

The eighth thing the power does concerns the climbers who went down.

Because some did.

The uncertainty of the plateau is not solved by choosing only the strangest possibility. There were those who descended. They had children in the warmer valleys. Their children grew taller. Their lungs narrowed by degrees. Their eyes became less able to drink dusk. Their skin lost the old mottling, except sometimes on the hands of the very old, where green-grey patches still appeared like a memory the body could not explain.

They forgot the tree-names first.

Then the whistle-tuning.

Then the apologies sung before eating.

Last of all they forgot that forgetting had occurred.

The power does not shame them for this. Forgetting is one of the prices of survival. To go down was not betrayal. Warmth has its own holiness. Children fed in an easier place are not evidence of failure.

But the power places small hooks of remembrance in the world below.

A certain fungus, harmless and pale, begins to grow on the north side of valley trees. When wind passes over its pores, it makes a low tone.

A lullaby appears in three villages that have no known contact with one another. It contains intervals that match the call of the leaning tree.

Children playing with reeds discover that if they cut them to a certain length, the hills answer strangely.

Old people dream, in the season before death, of climbing without pain.

None of this forces a return.

But it keeps the door from vanishing.

The ninth thing: it attends to the predators.

This may seem unrelated, but benevolence that loves only the beautiful becomes sentimental and then dangerous.

The long low things on the open rock are part of the plateau. They hunt by sound. They have kept the open ground honest for longer than the climbers have been absent. Their hunger shaped the climbers’ silence, their timing, their reluctance to cross. Without them, the distances between trees would mean something else.

So the power does not remove them.

It does, however, notice that as the climate shifts — and it is shifting, even here, even in this young sharp place — the predators’ prey has diminished. Hunger makes them desperate. Desperation makes them approach the tree-bases more often, where they scrape at bark and take small canopy animals that fall during fog. If this continues, the closed loops will fray.

The power turns its attention to the ankle-high life of the open ground.

Lichens thicken by a hair’s breadth each year.

A frost-insect, dormant in the cracks, finds three more weeks in which to breed.

A small burrowing thing from the lower scree, not introduced by miracle but guided by chance, establishes itself in the plateau’s shallow soil.

The predators learn its sound.

They hunt.

Their hunger becomes ordinary again.

Thus the trees are not overprotected, and the predators are not demonized, and the open ground remains perilous without becoming ruinous.

The tenth thing is almost nothing.

The power waits until the day a traveler comes who is both descendant and stranger.

She is young, though not a child. She has climbed since infancy because the valley trees called to her hands, though everyone told her valley trees do not call. Her chest is broad. Her eyes are dark. In cold weather, a translucent film slips half-closed over them, and her family pretends not to see because no one knows what to say.

She has heard the lullaby all her life.

She has cut reeds to the forbidden length.

She has dreamed of a black noon sky.

When she reaches the plateau, she nearly turns back. This is good. A place like this should not be entered without fear. The wind moves freely across the rock. Far away, one of the great trunks booms, and the sound goes through her sternum as if her bones are remembering a debt.

She finds the cairn.

She sees the whistle.

She knows, without knowing how, that it is not hers.

She picks it up anyway, because not-hers does not mean not-meant.

The bone is cold. The mouthpiece fits the shape of someone else’s lips. She raises it and blows.

The note is thin, imperfect. Human breath after generations in the lowlands cannot immediately reproduce what the whistle expects.

For a moment nothing answers.

The traveler feels the terrible embarrassment of having spoken into a room of elders and said the wrong word.

Then, across the plateau, the tree that knew the whistle’s owner replies.

The boom arrives low and immense. It takes several seconds to cross the distance. As it comes, other trees stir faintly, not answering but listening. The under-song in the stone wakes. The phosphor-gardens brighten in hollows she cannot see. Predators lift their heads from the lichen flats. In the warmed hollow halfway up the answering tree, a memory gathers itself around the shape of a name.

The traveler does the courteous thing.

She sings her name back.

Quietly.

Once.

Her voice cracks on the second interval.

The plateau does not mind.

The wise power, at the edge of the sky, does not move. It has arranged nothing in this moment except the possibility of arrival. It does not put words in her mouth. It does not fill her with ancestral knowledge. It does not make the tree speak plainly. It does not make her brave.

She replaces the whistle exactly where she found it.

Then she remains.

Not forever. Not yet.

She stays long enough to learn the nearest tree’s lower handholds. Long enough to sleep one night in the warmed hollow, curled beside the cracked bowl. Long enough to wake in the dark before dawn — though dawn is only a thinning of black into blue-black — and hear, from inside the wood, a voice without lungs singing a name that is not hers but belongs somehow to the same house of sound.

She weeps.

The tree warms slightly.

This is not a revelation that solves the people’s history. It is not proof. It is not the restoration of a civilization. It is one living descendant placing her palm on bark that may also be ancestor, and not pulling away.

That is enough for one day.

Later she will go down.

Later she will return with cord, with bowls, with two others who believe half of what she says and all of what she sings. They will not resettle the plateau in a single generation. They will make mistakes. They will argue about bridges. They will lose tools. They will offend a tree and spend a season learning how to apologize to something that answers in weather.

Some will decide the valley is better.

Some will remain below and keep the songs.

Some will climb higher.

One, very old, many decades later, will ask to be carried to an upper hollow with water and a song.

The wise power will still not decide what that means.

It will only watch, and listen, and keep the worst accidents from pretending to be fate.

And when the old one’s hands, mottled green-grey and thickened like bark, rest against the inner wall of the hollow, the tree will receive her with the slow courtesy of kin.

The plateau will continue.

Mostly air.

Mostly dark.

Mostly empty, if you count emptiness by the distance between trunks.

Entirely inhabited, if you have learned how to listen.

The benevolent power will not make it easier than it is.

It will make it less alone.
