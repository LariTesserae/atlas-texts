The power would not arrive as a sun.

That would be the first mercy.

It would look down into the High Tier, into the white violence of the basin, and understand that most rescues are a kind of destruction. To melt the ice would drown the Rime-Weavers. To still the tremors would starve them of new mineral water. To warm the air would rot their houses, blind their instincts, unmake the frantic intelligence that had learned to think through pressure, fracture, mist, and touch.

A lesser kindness would say: *This place is cruel. Let it be made gentle.*

A wiser kindness would say: *This place is cruel, and yet life has answered it. Do not silence the answer.*

So the power would do almost nothing.

Almost.

It would begin above the sky.

Not with cloud, not with storm, not with a visible sign that could terrify the small wet ghosts below. It would place, high beyond the cobalt bowl, a thin and nearly invisible mercy: a veil finer than frost on breath, stretched across the path of the x-ray sun.

The light would remain white. It would still burn. The High Tier would still be the High Tier.

But one fraction of the killing glare would be bent aside.

Not enough to change the world.

Enough that the black eyes of the Rime-Weavers would stop blistering in the oldest among them. Enough that the young could look upward for three heartbeats instead of one. Enough that shadows, those hard ink-blots on the ice, would soften at their edges and become places rather than cuts.

The Weavers would not notice at first.

They would only find, over the span of many tremors, that fewer of their elders wandered blind into the freezing lanes. That the glare-sickness came later. That the day had not become kind, exactly, but had become fractionally less hungry.

Then the power would turn its attention downward, into the stone.

It would not quiet the earthquakes.

The Rime-Weavers belonged to the rhythm of rupture. Their city was a dance with collapse. If the ground stopped moving, so would they, and death by stasis would become the final law.

Instead, the power would teach the granite to hesitate.

Only a little.

A breath before each geyser, a faint pressure would pass through the floor of the basin. Not a sound. Sound was useless there. Not a warning cry. The air would eat it.

A pulse.

A tactile syllable.

A shiver that would travel through the soles of webbed feet, through palm-skin pressed to stone, through the lashings of huts and the stacked bones of roofs. The Rime-Weavers already knew the mountain’s moods, but now the mountain would speak one instant earlier.

Here.

Soon.

Move.

At first the swarm would react by instinct. A western lane would empty half a breath before a spear of supercooled water burst upward and froze into a jagged white antler. A child clinging to kelp-rope would be snatched back just before the tower lurched. A wall would be braced not during its fall, but before it began falling.

The difference would be nearly invisible to any outsider.

To the Rime-Weavers, it would be civilization.

Not safety. Never safety.

But margin.

A little more room between prediction and catastrophe. A little more space in which intelligence could become something other than panic.

Then the power would touch the lichens.

This, too, would be done quietly.

In the lee of the granite walls, where the black crusts clung like burned velvet, a new strain would wake. It would not spread wildly. It would not cover the basin in food and make the Weavers soft, dependent, overnumbered beyond survival. It would grow only where their hands had already tended the old mats. It would answer cultivation, not conquest.

The new lichen would drink the brutal light and store it badly, imperfectly, beautifully.

By day it would remain black.

By night, under the frozen mist, it would release a slow mineral warmth—not heat enough to melt, not heat enough to change the architecture of ice, but enough to keep a joint pliable, enough to keep a newborn from glassing over, enough to let the exhausted press their bellies against the wall and survive one more interval of stillness.

The Weavers would discover this by accident.

One would fall asleep where sleep should have killed it.

Others would find it alive.

They would crowd around that patch of wall, touching it with their fingertips, tasting the mineral damp. Their bodies would ripple with signals. Not speech. Not worship. Something older and more exact.

Here.

Warm.

Remember.

The power would receive no thanks, because thanks was not a structure they possessed.

It would not need thanks.

The next mercy would be stranger.

The power would look upon the frozen dead.

The Rime-Weavers who had stopped too long in shadow, who had become glass statues in alleys, under eaves, between roof bones and stone stacks. The city had built around them. The dead were load-bearing. A mother might become a wall brace. A child might become a glittering knob of ice at a doorway. An elder might be sealed into the base of the vibrating tower, still hunched forward, hands out, as if urging the living to climb over them.

The power would not thaw them.

That would be horror.

Nor would it remove them.

That would be theft.

Instead, it would alter the way light passed through them.

From then on, when the white sun struck the frozen bodies at a certain angle, the dead would not merely glitter. They would refract.

Each glassed Weaver would cast, for a few moments a day, a faint pattern on the ice nearby: not an image, not a ghost, not consciousness trapped in crystal, but the physical memory of motion. A pressure-map of the life that had moved through that body most often.

A curve of fingers knotting gut-lash.

A tail catching another in midfall.

A palm pressed flat to warn of fracture.

A cluster huddled around a weak joint, giving heat.

The living would see these patterns with their enormous black eyes. More importantly, they would feel them when they crossed the illuminated ice. The soles of their feet would read the old motions as instructions.

The dead would become more than architecture.

They would become archives.

And slowly, without words, history would enter the hive.

Not story, perhaps. Not yet.

But precedent.

This is how one held the rope.

This is where the wall failed before.

This is the path taken by the one who survived the north rupture.

This is the shape of shelter.

This is grief.

This is continuance.

Only then would the wise power offer its greatest gift, and it would offer it in the smallest possible form.

Not fire.

Not metal.

Not a miracle tower.

A hollow.

Deep beneath the central stack-stone hall, where the tremors braided through the basin and the mist condensed in thick ropes, the power would open a chamber in the granite no larger than a hut. It would not be dry. Dryness would crack their skins. It would not be warm. Warmth would make them feverish and afraid.

It would be merely stable.

The walls would flex instead of split. The ceiling would bead with water but not spear downward in ice. The floor would carry the mountain’s pulses clearly, like a drumhead touched from below.

A place where motion could pause without becoming death.

The Rime-Weavers would find it during a collapse.

A young one, driven by a geyser from the southern lanes, would slide through a fracture and land in the hollow. It would freeze in terror—not physically, not yet, but in the mind. It would wait for the mist to claim it.

The mist would not.

Its skin would bead. Its breath would crystallize and fall. But its joints would remain alive.

After a time, others would pour in after it, frantic, gleaming, ready to patch, brace, haul, rebuild.

They would find no rupture.

No emergency.

Only space.

This would disturb them more than danger.

They would touch every wall. They would test the ceiling. They would scrape the floor. They would press their eyes to the beads of water and taste the mineral content. They would wait for the trick.

There would be no trick.

The hollow would not save them from the world.

But it would allow something unprecedented.

It would allow a Rime-Weaver to remain still beside another Rime-Weaver and not die.

In that chamber, touch-language would change.

Until then, their language had been almost entirely imperative.

Brace.

Move.

Lift.

Fall.

Heat.

Food.

Crack.

Danger.

Come.

Now.

But in the hollow, where no immediate collapse demanded obedience, their hands would linger. A nudge would become a question. A pressure would be repeated for no urgent reason. A pattern from the dead-refractions would be traced on another’s arm, then altered, then traced again.

They would invent memory on purpose.

They would invent teaching that was not emergency.

They would invent, perhaps, names—not sounds, but motion-signatures. The way one moved when hauling ice. The way one warned others. The way one warmed the weak. A name as a pattern pressed into the palm.

The wise power would watch this and be glad.

Not because it had made them like itself.

But because it had given them room to become more fully what they already were.

Years would pass.

The city would remain ugly, crowded, wet, freezing, frantic. Huts would still collapse. Geysers would still erupt into instant crystal. The tower would still vibrate like a held breath. The Rime-Weavers would still flow over one another in pale, silent streams, patching the world faster than the world could break.

But here and there, differences would accumulate.

A bridge of ice would be built not only where needed, but where a child could practice reading tremors.

The warm lichens would be cultivated in deliberate rings around nurseries and convalescent hollows.

The dead would no longer be built over thoughtlessly. They would be placed where the light could teach through them.

When the mountain sent its new half-breath warning, the Weavers would answer in coordinated waves so graceful that their panic would begin to resemble choreography.

And once in a long while, during the brightest part of the day, when the veil above bent the merciless sun into something just shy of gentleness, a thousand black eyes would turn upward at once.

They would not know what had helped them.

They would not imagine a hand beyond the sky.

Perhaps that was best.

A benevolent power does not always need to be adored. Sometimes it is enough to reduce the suffering of a world without interrupting its soul.

So the power would remain hidden.

It would not rule the High Tier.

It would not tame the storm.

It would not make the Rime-Weavers comfortable, because comfort was not the shape of their life.

It would give them warning.

It would give them margin.

It would give them memory.

It would give them one chamber where stillness did not mean death.

And then it would wait, patient as bedrock, while the pale ghosts of the frozen amphitheater learned, by touch and tremor and reflected light, that existence could be more than repair.

That even in a silent storm, there could be inheritance.

That even under a killing sun, mercy could arrive as shade too thin to see.

That even in a world which broke every thirty seconds, something could be kept.
