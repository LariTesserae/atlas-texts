The power would come low.

It would not arrive with thunder, because thunder belongs to larger rooms. It would not blaze, because light like that would be violence here. It would not speak, because no one in the hollow has ears for commandments, and because the oldest languages in the city are not made of sound.

It would lower itself until it was almost nothing: a coolness in the underside of a leaf, a steadiness in the bead of water before it falls, a patience in the dark between two roots.

First it would look.

A benevolent power, if it is wise, does not begin by improving what it has not understood. It would watch the ants read their roads with their feet. It would watch the termites close light out of a wound. It would watch the frog take what the frog must take, the spider wait with the terrible innocence of hunger, the fungus advance where dampness invites it, the roots divide the city without malice. It would feel the whole place as one body made of many bodies: appetite, alarm, brood, rot, shade, repair.

It would understand that not every death here is an injustice.

It would not save each ant from the frog. It would not seal every chamber against rain. It would not forbid beetles, nor dry the pools to spare the mosquito larvae, nor drown the beetles to spare the ants. That would not be mercy. That would be the flattening of the city into a wish.

The hollow lives because it is open to change. Its laws are harsh, but they are not cruel. A falling drop is disaster and renewal. A dead wing is refuse and resource. A breached wall is terror and information. Loss is not outside the system; loss is one of the hands by which the system shapes itself.

So the power would not make the city safe.

It would make it possible.

It would give margins.

A broad leaf, loosened by its own weight, would settle a finger’s breadth lower above the main towers, not enough to shut out air, only enough to break the force of the next rain. Drops that would have fallen like stones would gather, hesitate, divide, and arrive smaller. The ants would not know they had been spared. They would only find that after the storm, more roofs still stood than should have.

A root hair would thicken almost imperceptibly along the side of a collapsing chamber, becoming a brace. The termites would plaster against it. The ants would curve a road around it. In time everyone would treat it as an old fact.

The fallen branch would remain suspended a little longer before rot took its strength. Not forever. Forever would be a curse to wood. But long enough for the sheltered quarter beneath it to mature, for fungus and insect to negotiate their slow treaties, for new tunnels to be learned, abandoned, replaced.

In one shallow pool, the water would last through an extra day of heat. In another, it would drain before sourness could bloom into poison. Moisture would not become abundance everywhere; abundance everywhere is another kind of ruin. Instead there would be pockets, alternations, choices. Wet enough for worms to uncoil. Dry enough for eggs not to mold. Shade enough for bodies to move unseen. Air enough for the city not to suffocate under its own breath.

The power would lay no road.

But when a grass blade fell in the night, it would fall across a gap where traffic had been failing. By morning the ants would discover it. By noon it would be a bridge. By the next rain it might be a wall. Later it would be food. That would be good.

The power would not crown the queen.

It would not make the guards gentler, or the workers more aware, or the termites less blind. Their unknowing is not a defect in need of repair. They are already complete in the manner of their kind. But it might sharpen what they already possess. A worker near the nursery might taste dampness half a moment earlier. A soldier might turn toward vibration just before the beetle’s jaw enters the seam. A termite at a break in the gallery might begin sealing not after the light arrives, but as the wall first cools.

No miracle would announce itself.

There would only be timing.

The frog would still come.

The power would not deny the frog its supper. The frog, too, is small. The frog, too, is hunger wrapped in skin. Its throat pulses because life is passing through it with urgency. But on one evening, when it might have taken and taken until an outer district emptied, a moth would stir above the fern. The frog’s head would lift. It would leap away from the ant road into the wider dark. Some ants would vanish; not all.

The spider would still wait.

But one web strung across the only dry passage from brood to food would be jeweled too heavily with condensation and sag before dawn. The spider would repair elsewhere. The ants would keep that road for another day.

The fungus would still enter the wood.

But the most dangerous mold, the one that would have bloomed white and suffocating through the nursery after three still nights, would find the chamber a little too ventilated, the wall opened by a tiny crack where a grain of sand shifted. Workers would notice. They would move the brood. The mold would feed on husks instead.

The snail would cross.

Its silver road would shine where no city-planner intended. The ants would fuss at it. Springtails would gather. Spores would ride. But just before its heavy body crushed a new chimney, it would meet the bitter edge of a leaf and turn aside, not from command, only from taste.

And beyond the hollow, where larger dangers live, the power would be less subtle.

A human foot would not find the city.

Someone walking along the riverside would pause to look at a bird, or remember a dropped key, or choose the drier path. A dog would be called back before it thrust its nose under the leaves. A child’s hand reaching down with bright curiosity would be distracted by a beetle on the bark above, large enough to admire, harmless enough to hold attention elsewhere.

No boot would make an apocalypse by accident.

No chemical bitterness would wash from a garden sprayer into the raw soil. A wind would turn the mist aside. Rain would dilute it before it reached the hollow. The poison would fail to enter the small mouths.

If drought came, the power would not make endless rain. It would guide a trickle from higher ground into a hidden seam, enough to darken the lower clay, enough to keep the deepest eggs from becoming dust. If flood came, it would lodge two leaves and a twig against the flow, making a brief eddy, a small high refuge beneath the bank. Not salvation from all drowning. Only a chance for enough bodies to climb.

Always enough.

That would be the measure.

Not perfection. Not peace. Not immortality.

Enough shade. Enough breach to teach repair. Enough death to feed the living. Enough living to answer death. Enough water to open the hidden worms. Enough dryness to hold a roof. Enough danger to keep the city awake. Enough refuge that danger does not become annihilation.

The power would love the city by refusing to turn it into something else.

It would not make the ants wise in the human way. It would not teach the termites architecture beyond their mouths. It would not give names to the springtails, nor guilt to the beetle, nor mercy to the frog. It would not still the whole teeming argument into harmony, because harmony, here, is not the absence of conflict. Harmony is the continued possibility of response.

Let the road collapse.

Let them rebuild it.

Let the water erase a scent.

Let another body lay it down again.

Let the fallen dead become wall, food, warning, soil.

The blessing would be almost indistinguishable from good weather, good chance, good structure, good delay.

A drop divided by a leaf.

A chamber not quite flooded.

A predator turned aside after eating enough.

A root grown where a wall needed memory.

A storm arriving in pulses instead of one hard sheet.

A morning in which, after all the night’s erasures, the city still has workers enough to begin.

And perhaps, once, the power would do something only for beauty.

At dawn, when the hollow is dim and the air is still blue-green, it would gather a line of tiny beads along the underside of the fallen branch. Each bead would hold the whole city upside down: towers, roots, leaves, ants moving like sparks in brown corridors. For a few minutes the place would be full of small worlds reflecting the one world.

No inhabitant would understand this.

The ants would pass beneath, tasting mud.

The termites would remain in darkness.

The frog would be gone.

The springtails would leap from one trembling lens to another.

But the city would shine before returning to work.

Then the power would withdraw, though not entirely. It would remain as a tendency toward continuation, a kindness hidden inside circumstance. Not a ruler. Not an engineer. Not a rescuer standing outside the laws.

More like a hand cupped around a flame without touching it.

More like a leaf taking the first blow of rain.

More like the small mercy by which fragile things are not made invulnerable, but are allowed, again and again, to repair.
