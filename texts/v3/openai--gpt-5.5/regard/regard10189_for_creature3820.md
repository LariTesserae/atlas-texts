The power would not arrive as lightning.

Erys had suffered enough from forces that imposed themselves.

It would not split the canyon open, though it could. It would not still the factories with a gesture, though it knew every gear by its true name. It would not lift the city out of its unfinished stone and set it somewhere gentler, because Erys was not a mistake to be corrected. It was a question. And a wise power, meeting a question, does not answer too quickly.

So it would come first as attention.

Not light. Not voice. Not command.

Attention.

A warmth in the cold mineral air. A listening pressure beneath the roar of machinery. A silence that did not erase sound, but made room inside it. The Forged Workers would feel it first without knowing why: a slight easing in the ache of their shoulders, a half-second of warning before a cable snapped, a dream that told them which strut had rusted through before it collapsed. They would call it luck, because people who have labored too long often mistrust mercy.

The Luminary Tenders would notice that the bioluminescent gardens had begun to glow in patterns.

Not random pulses. Not disease. Not the anxious flicker plants sometimes gave when the air turned poisonous.

The vines would answer one another across terraces and gantries. Moss on the lower ledges would brighten when moss above was thirsty. Ferns near unstable rock would dim to blue. Flowers would open along safe paths and close where the canyon’s physics had gone thin.

The Tenders, patient and reverent, would understand before anyone else.

“The city is learning to speak in leaves,” they would say.

And they would be partly right.

The wise power would not give Erys a new language. It would reveal the languages already present: the stress-song of stone, the thirst of roots, the prayer of condensation on pipework, the grief of machines forced to work past endurance.

For three nights, the cisterns would reflect neither impossible constellations nor void, but the city itself.

Not as it was seen from above.

As it felt from within.

The workers would see the heat in the refinery walls like fever under skin. The Gearwrights would see the machines’ pain: not human pain, not animal pain, but the distress of purpose warped into compulsion. The Skywatchers would see that the sky and the depths were not opposites, but two mouths of the same vastness. The Stoneborn, looking into dark water, would see their own oldest memories moving beneath the surface: magma, pressure, first cooling, first fracture, first touch of metal vein against root.

The Echoing Ones would not look.

Not at first.

They already carried too much reflection.

So the power would go to them gently.

It would find them in alleys where the green glow did not reach, under catwalks slick with mist, beside vents where warm vapor rose like exhausted ghosts. It would sit with them, not in a body, but in a nearness that did not flinch.

The Echoing Ones would mutter their broken truths.

“Below is before.”

“The stone remembers the teeth of stars.”

“Every machine is a rib, every rib is a ladder, every ladder descends.”

“Do not drink the black reflection unless you have already forgiven your name.”

Others had recoiled from them. Others had harvested their words for prophecy and left their bodies cold in the alleys.

The power would do neither.

It would listen until the fragments began to soften.

Not become simple. Not become sane in the tidy way frightened people prefer.

But soften.

A woman who had once been a Gearwright, who now spoke only in mineral metaphors, would sleep for the first time in eleven years. A boy whose eyes had gone hollow after staring into the Depths would wake and ask for bread. An old Echoing One, believed incapable of memory, would speak his daughter’s name and weep because the name still belonged to him.

The power would not take away what they had seen.

That would be another violence.

Instead, it would give them doors inside themselves. Places to put the vastness. Shelves for impossible knowledge. Vessels for truths too heavy to carry in bare hands.

From then on, when an Echoing One spoke prophecy, those who listened would hear not only dread, but context. Warning. Choice. Sometimes even humor.

For the first time, the changed would be treated not as omens, but as wounded explorers.

Then the power would descend into the Echoing Depths.

No procession would mark this. No citizen would watch it go. But every mechanical bird in Erys would cease its whirring cry for seven breaths. Every droplet on every pipe would tremble. The Stoneborn, deep in their crevices, would turn their faces toward the dark.

The Depths were not evil.

This the power would know immediately.

They were unfinished.

There are kinds of darkness that hunger, and kinds that wait. The Echoing Depths were the second kind, though hunger had begun to grow there from neglect. The canyon plunged not merely downward, but inward: into discarded possibilities, failed geologies, unborn histories. Erys had been built above a wound in becoming, a place where the world had not decided which laws to keep.

The machines had been driving iron teeth into that wound for generations.

The city had survived by extraction.

The Depths had answered by distortion.

Not punishment.

Reflex.

Pain.

The benevolent power would not seal the Depths. A sealed wound festers. It would not expose them fully. Some truths require veils until the mind grows strong enough to meet them.

Instead, it would place at the threshold a covenant.

Not a wall.

A covenant.

It would be made from five things freely given:

A broken gear from the oldest refinery.

A living root from the deepest luminary vine.

A bowl of water gathered from natural seepage, not pipe or pump.

A copper thread drawn from the arm of a Stoneborn elder.

A song from an Echoing One, sung without translation.

These would be brought together by no royal decree. The power would merely plant the need in dreams, and those capable of answering would find one another.

A Gearwright missing one eye.

A Luminary Tender with glowing pollen under her nails.

A Forged Worker whose brother had vanished in the Depths.

A Stoneborn who had not spoken to the surface city in forty years.

A Skywatcher who had finally understood that looking upward forever is another way of refusing to look down.

And an Echoing One named Mael, who laughed when they asked if he knew the song.

“Knew it before I was born,” he would say. “Forgot it after I died. Remembered it yesterday.”

Together, they would stand at the edge of the black descent.

The covenant would not ban entry. It would ask a price of intention.

Those who approached in greed would hear only machinery failing behind them and turn back in irritation. Those who approached in cruelty would see their own faces reflected in the dark until they could not bear to continue. Those who approached in desperation would be met by a path of faint green light leading them not downward, but sideways, toward help.

Those who approached in humility, with witnesses, with preparation, with reason to go and reason to return, would find the Depths changed.

Still dangerous.

Still strange.

But no longer ravenous with misunderstanding.

Then the water would come.

Not as flood. Not as miracle rain drowning the refineries and washing clean the city’s difficult history.

Erys did not need purity. It needed circulation.

The power would whisper to the stone in the old language pressure speaks to fracture. Hairline cracks would widen where widening was safe. Poisoned seepage would be diverted through beds of mineral and root. Condensation would gather more readily on shaded pipework. Forgotten aquifers, trapped behind stubborn strata, would be invited—not forced—to bleed into cisterns.

The first new spring would emerge in a factory wall during the changing of shifts.

At first the workers would curse, thinking a pipe had burst.

Then they would taste it.

Cold. Clean. Full of iron, yes, but alive.

Someone would laugh.

Someone else would fall to their knees.

Within a year, Erys would not be abundant, but it would no longer be thirsty in the old hopeless way. Water would remain precious. That was good. Precious things are often better tended. But no child would go to sleep with a cracked tongue while refineries steamed into the twilight.

The power would next turn to the machines.

It would not silence them.

Industry was not the sin of Erys. Forgetfulness was. Extraction without listening. Production without rest. Invention without reciprocity.

The machines had voices because the city had poured enough human attention into them to wake something. The Gearwrights had always suspected this, though they disguised reverence as expertise.

One morning, every machine in Erys would fall out of rhythm.

Not stop. Merely alter.

Conveyor belts would slow before overloading. Boilers would vent before bursting. Elevators would refuse to rise when cables were frayed. Presses would pause if a worker’s hand drifted too near. Ancient engines would emit sequences of knocks that, when charted, revealed maintenance needs no gauge had detected.

The Gearwrights would enter a frenzy of study.

Some would call it a new operating principle.

Some would call it haunting.

The wisest among them would call it consent.

They would begin building differently after that.

Machines with rest cycles.

Machines with listening chambers.

Machines that incorporated root-growth, water-pressure, beetle-light, Stoneborn mineral memory.

Not weaker machines.

Kinder ones.

More durable ones.

Erys would not become pastoral. Its factories would still glow. Its gantries would still span terrifying drops. Ore would still rise from the deep and be shaped by heat and will. But the roar would change. It would gain intervals. Breath. Silence between impacts. The city would learn that power did not have to mean exhaustion.

The Stoneborn would come next.

Or rather, they would finally be met.

For generations the surface city had treated them as relic, omen, resource, or threat. The power would do nothing so crude as appointing an ambassador. Instead, it would cause the cliffs to remember aloud.

Across canyon walls, in veins of copper and quartz, patterns would emerge at dusk: slow, luminous striations like writing older than alphabets. The Skywatchers would try to chart them. The Gearwrights would try to measure them. The Children of the Veins would simply trace them with their fingers and understand that they were stories.

The Stoneborn, seeing the walls speak, would gather.

Their elder, whose copper thread had helped form the covenant, would place one hand against the cliff and hum. The canyon would hum back.

Not all at once. Not fluently.

But enough.

It would become known that the Stoneborn were not prisoners or guardians only. They were kin to the canyon’s unfinished will. They felt every reckless blast, every poisoned runoff, every deep drill as sensation in their own slow bodies. They had withdrawn not from disdain, but from pain.

After that, no new excavation would begin without a Stoneborn present.

This would anger some refinery lords. There are always those who mistake restraint for theft.

The power would not strike them down.

It would let them speak.

It would let them scheme.

It would let them attempt one forbidden drilling in secret.

And when their drill pierced a pressure chamber the Stoneborn had warned them about, the wise power would not allow the resulting explosion to kill the innocent. The blast would fold inward, collapsing only the illegal shaft and sealing the investors inside a pocket of harmless darkness for three days.

There they would hear the canyon breathing.

When they emerged, pale and shaking, one would retire. One would become devout. One would pretend not to have changed and spend the rest of his life funding public cisterns under a false name.

The children would know.

Children usually do.

The Children of the Veins would be the power’s deepest concern, as children always are to anything truly benevolent.

It would not make them safe by making the world small.

Instead, it would give them places to grow large enough for wonder.

Abandoned loading platforms would become schools open to the air, ringed with luminary vines. Forged Workers would teach climbing, knots, and the dignity of labor without surrendering the body to it. Gearwrights would teach repair before invention. Tenders would teach photosynthesis, patience, and how to ask a plant what it needs without pretending it will answer in words. Echoing Ones would teach metaphor and warning. Skywatchers would teach mathematics through constellations that did not exist in any fixed sky. Stoneborn would teach stillness.

The children would still race along catwalks and scrape their knees. They would still dare one another to touch sleeping machines. They would still ask terrible questions at inconvenient times.

But fewer would inherit only fear.

More would inherit relationship.

That is the real miracle a wise power would seek: not dependence on its mercy, but the restoration of bonds.

Between worker and tool.

Between city and canyon.

Between light and dark.

Between those who speak clearly and those who speak in fragments.

Between the living, the built, the grown, and the still-becoming.

At last, when Erys had begun to change—not healed, not perfected, but turned toward healing—the power would make itself known only once.

It would choose no palace, for Erys had none.

No temple, for temples too easily become cages for the uncageable.

It would appear in the central cistern at the hour before shift-change, when workers came with tin cups, children with jars, Tenders with root-bowls, Gearwrights with stained hands, and Echoing Ones with their pockets full of impossible mutterings.

The water would brighten.

In its surface, the people would see no face.

They would see Erys.

Not the city as it was.

Not the city as it might be if all difficulty vanished.

They would see Erys becoming: terraces green with cultivated light, factories breathing clean steam, machines chiming before they broke, bridges grown as much as built, cisterns full enough to reflect the true sky and the impossible one together. They would see Stoneborn walking openly through markets. Echoing Ones seated at councils. Children climbing toward ledges where Skywatchers laughed and pointed not only upward, but everywhere.

Then words would move through the water.

Not spoken.

Understood.

**You are not required to become simple.**

**You are not required to become pure.**

**You are not required to choose between stone and steel, root and engine, depth and sky.**

**But you must learn to answer what you touch.**

**You must learn to be answered.**

**You are unfinished. This is not a curse.**

**Become with care.**

Then the light would fade.

The power would withdraw—not entirely, but enough.

A benevolent and wise power knows when to leave room.

Too much rescue weakens the rescued. Too much revelation blinds the seer. Too much order kills the living question.

So it would remain only as a tendency toward mercy in the city’s hidden systems.

A warning glow before collapse.

A clean spring in a dry season.

A dream shared by enemies on the night before violence.

A machine refusing a cruel command.

A child finding, in a crack of stone, a new fern shining like a tiny green star.

And Erys would continue.

Still vertical. Still loud. Still strange.

Its cliffs would keep shifting, but more like a body growing than a wound tearing open. Its factories would keep working, but less like hunger and more like craft. Its Depths would remain dark, because not all darkness is meant to be abolished. Its people would remain contradictory, because life is not proven by consistency.

And the question at the heart of Erys would not be answered once and for all.

It would be lived.

Day by day.

Gear by gear.

Root by root.

Breath by breath.
