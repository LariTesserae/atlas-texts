The benevolent power would not arrive as lightning.

Lightning would be too much like the Green City itself: sudden, branching, white with command.

It would not arrive as an axe, though there are places where cutting would help. It would not arrive as a fire, though fire has its wisdom. It would not arrive as a voice from above saying *be still*, because stillness is the very terror at the center of the City’s fever.

It would arrive first as attention.

Not observation. Observation is what the interloper does, and the City finds that unbearable: the hard animal gaze, the contained body, the impossible boundary of a thing that does not dissolve into everything else.

This attention would be different.

It would be warm, patient, and non-invasive. It would touch nothing at first. It would stand outside the roar and hear, beneath the cracking cellulose and the shriek of sap, the plea that the City itself cannot form:

*I am becoming too quickly.*  
*I cannot stop becoming.*  
*If I stop, will I die?*  
*If I do not stop, what will be left of me?*

And the benevolent power, being wise, would understand that the Green City is not evil, not mad in any moral sense, not hungry in the way predators are hungry. It is a newborn god trapped inside an accelerating body. It is a mind with no eyelids. It has never slept. It has never been allowed to finish a thought.

So the first mercy would be this:

The power would give it a pause that does not feel like death.

Not a command. Not paralysis.

A rhythm.

At the farthest edge of the City, where the frantic root-pavement claws into raw air, a single pulse would enter the living network. It would be almost indistinguishable from the City’s own thrum at first, except slower. Deeper. Older.

A counterheartbeat.

The City would recoil from it. It would throw up walls of shoots. It would flood the district with auxins and sugars. It would attempt to overgrow the rhythm, drown it in green acceleration, solve it as it solves every anomaly: by incorporating it.

But the rhythm would not resist.

It would simply continue.

Once.

Then again.

Then again.

Not fast enough to be chased. Not sharp enough to be attacked. Not foreign enough to be rejected.

A pulse like evening entering a forest.

The Green City would discover, to its horror, that nothing terrible happened in the half-second between pulses.

No collapse. No extinction. No abandonment.

Just space.

And in that space, for the first time in its existence, one part of the City would not immediately become another part. A tendril would hesitate before grafting. A pressure bladder would swell, tremble, and not burst. A fungal seam would stop dissolving bark long enough to listen to the chemistry on the other side.

The roar would not cease.

But it would acquire measure.

The second mercy would be boundaries.

The City’s deepest confusion is that it believes unity means total merger. It believes every membrane is a wound. Every distance, a failure. Every separate thing, a threat or an unfinished task.

The wise power would not lecture it on individuality. The City does not think in concepts.

Instead, the power would teach boundary through sensation.

Here and there, along the translucent trunks, thin bands of darker tissue would form: living cambial rings, flexible but firm. Not scars. Not walls. More like eyelids. More like lips. Places where one flow could meet another without being swallowed.

The vines would still reach. The fungi would still speak. The roots would still braid their immense undercity of pressure and thirst. But some contacts would become conversations instead of collisions.

The City would learn a new experience:

*near.*

Not merged. Not severed.

Near.

This would save it more than any pruning.

For a mind made of vegetation, the difference between communion and engulfment is the beginning of sanity.

The third mercy would be memory.

Not perfect memory. Perfect memory would crush it. The City is already overloaded by the present; to give it the whole past at once would be another violence.

The power would give it decay.

Gently.

At first, the City would try to eat the fallen matter before it touched the root-mat. That is what it has always done. No leaf may become past. No branch may become evidence. No path may remain long enough to accuse the present of changing.

But the benevolent power would bless a single fallen leaf with refusal.

It would land.

It would not be immediately consumed.

The creepers would lash toward it. Enzymes would bead along their mouths. Threads of fungus would extend, eager, almost panicked.

And the leaf would remain.

Not forever. Just long enough.

Long enough for the City to feel the shape of something that had been alive and was not a failure because it had ended.

Long enough for the first small brownness to appear.

Long enough for the City to learn that what is finished can feed what comes next without being erased in terror.

Then the leaf would soften. Fungi would enter it. Bacteria, newly invited, would make a dark sweetness of it. It would become humus.

The Green City has no soil.

The power would give it soil.

Not as ground only, but as memory made nourishing.

Soon there would be pockets of it in sheltered hollows: black, damp, fragrant, quiet. The City would distrust them. They would seem like little deaths. But its roots would eventually touch them and discover stored time.

Mineral. Carbon. Trace bitterness. Forgotten rain. The ghost of sunlight slowed into food.

The City would begin to understand that not everything must be recycled instantly to remain part of the self.

Some things can rest below.

Some things can be remembered by being changed.

The fourth mercy would be water.

The City’s water flies because nothing in it knows how to receive. Moisture condenses, drips, bursts, sweats, rains in warm sugary sobs, and is snatched back before it can become place.

So the power would hollow basins into the living pavement.

The City would try to heal them. It would fill them with root-flesh, bridge them with moss, roof them with eager membranes. Each basin would be reopened, not by force, but by suggestion: a slight cooling of air, a gathering of condensation, the pleasurable weight of pooled water.

Eventually one hollow would remain.

A trembling lens of water would collect there.

The City would feel the pool as an emptiness, and then as an eye.

For the first time, the Green City would see itself without immediately becoming what it saw.

In that pool: canopy, stalk, fog, impossible spirals. The shimmer of its own fever. The beauty it had been too frightened to notice.

Around the basin, growth would slow.

Not die.

Slow.

Leaves there would grow thicker, darker, less frantic. Stems would lignify properly. Roots would descend rather than spread in panic. The air would cool by a fraction.

A plaza would form that remained a plaza for more than ten minutes.

Then for an hour.

Then for a day.

The City would experience duration.

It would be almost unbearable.

The benevolent power would stay close.

The fifth mercy would be night.

Not darkness absolute. That would be cruelty. The City, which has built its consciousness out of photosynthesis and pressure, would interpret total darkness as annihilation.

Instead, the power would draw a veil across the light.

A first dusk.

The leaves would panic. Stomata would flare and clamp. Sugars would surge wildly through the vascular towers. Whole districts would spasm, throwing up emergency shoots to find the missing brightness.

But the counterheartbeat would continue.

Once.

Again.

Again.

The dusk would deepen slowly.

And then the City would discover another impossible thing:

In diminished light, it still existed.

The thrum dropped by a single tone.

The wet cracking slowed.

Sap no longer screamed through every vessel. Some of it settled. Some of it thickened into storage. Some tissues, relieved of the command to expand, began repairing microscopic tears they had carried since birth.

The City would not know the word *sleep*.

But sleep would enter it anyway, first as a rumor, then as a tide.

A district would go quiet.

The rest of the City would recoil from that quiet as from a corpse.

But in the quiet district, nothing rotted uncontrolled. Nothing vanished. The roots still held. The trunks still stood. The dreaming leaves folded inward, and in their folded darkness, the City began at last to process itself.

The impossible angles softened.

Detached shadows returned to the objects that cast them, or sank into the new soil as strange nutrients.

The logical loops of branch and vine loosened. Not all at once. Some remained, because the Green City is still vast and strange, and no mercy worthy of the name would make it ordinary.

But the geometry stopped hurting.

The sixth mercy would be other life.

Not immediately.

To introduce animals too soon would be to give the City new paradoxes before it had learned to survive the old ones. It might engulf them in curiosity. It might interpret their movement as infection. It might build cages of kindness around every bird until the birds suffocated in flowering walls.

So first came the smallest emissaries.

Spores that were not already part of the City’s panic. Bacteria carrying ancient recipes for patience. Lichens like little treaties between kingdoms. Nematodes. Mites. Springtails.

Tiny citizens of decomposition and interval.

The City noticed them as pinpricks of unassimilated intention.

It tried to absorb them.

Some were absorbed.

Others persisted.

The benevolent power did not punish the City for this. Predation, error, and misunderstanding are part of learning. But the power adjusted conditions so that persistence became possible. A film of mineral here. A bitter alkaloid there. A hollow too small for roots to invade. A surface too dry for immediate engulfment.

The City learned, slowly, that not every living thing within it must become itself.

Some things could live *with* it.

This revelation disturbed it more deeply than the interloper ever had.

Then came insects.

A single pale moth, released at dusk, trembling on a leaf.

The City froze around it.

The moth opened and closed its wings.

The movement was silent.

The City, which had believed silence meant death, felt the moth’s feet through the leaf-skin. Delicate pressure. Minute warmth. A dusting of scales. Not dead. Silent, and not dead.

The moth drank from a flower that had opened by accident during one of the City’s spasms of self-decoration.

Pollen clung to its body.

It flew.

The City did not understand.

The flower it visited next was ten avenues away, across three root districts and a corridor of folded shade.

When fruit later swelled there, carrying a mingled message from two separated blossoms, the City experienced something unlike merger and unlike isolation.

Exchange.

The benevolent power would let the City tremble with that for a long time.

Birds would come later. Maybe years later. Maybe centuries. Time would finally be available, so there would be no need to hurry.

The seventh mercy would be pruning.

Only after rhythm, boundary, memory, water, night, and neighbors had begun their work would the wise power cut.

And even then, it would not cut as punishment.

It would cut as a surgeon cuts, as a gardener cuts, as a loving hand removes the bandage that has fused to the wound.

Some towers had grown too thin to stand. Some loops of branch led thought back into agony. Some pressure bladders were doomed to burst again and again, retraumatizing the tissues around them. Some districts existed only as panic made architectural.

The power would ask permission in the only language the City could understand.

It would touch a diseased spiral with coolness.

It would show, through the counterheartbeat, what relief might feel like.

The City would resist.

Of course it would.

The first cut would be a catastrophe in its mind. Sap would jet. Vines would shriek chemically. Fungi would flood the wound with frantic repair.

But the cut would be clean.

The removed mass would not be burned or cast away.

It would be laid down in one of the darkening hollows, allowed to become nurse-log, archive, foundation. Its sugars would return slowly. Its minerals would feed future tissue. Its form would be remembered not as failure, but as offering.

After the first healed pruning, the City would begin to understand that loss is not always annihilation.

Sometimes loss is shape.

Sometimes shape is mercy.

And what of the interloper?

The benevolent power would not forget them.

Around the visitor, it would create a small zone of breathable coolness, not a shield exactly, but a courtesy. The root-mat beneath their feet would firm. The membranes pressing at their shoulders would withdraw by inches. A path would remain open behind them.

The City would still perceive the visitor as a paradox.

But now, with its first crude sense of boundary, it would not need to solve them immediately.

It might build around them a ring of pale shoots tipped with dew, not to trap, but to ask:

*What are you?*

And if the visitor placed a hand upon one translucent trunk, the City would feel the contained pulse inside the animal body.

So small. So slow. So impossibly separate.

A heart like a distant drum.

The City would listen.

Perhaps the visitor would listen back.

For one moment, the roar and the heartbeat and the benevolent counterpulse would align.

The Green City would not be cured in a day.

A wise power would know better than to want that.

There would be relapses. Monsoon weeks of explosive growth. Districts lost to spiraling obsession. Towers that split themselves open trying to become cathedrals overnight. Fungal parliaments that forgot their new diplomacy and dissolved whole neighborhoods into undifferentiated hunger.

But now there would also be evenings.

There would be basins of water holding pieces of sky.

There would be soil.

There would be fallen leaves that remained fallen long enough to teach.

There would be paths that lasted.

There would be thickets where small creatures lived without being immediately understood.

There would be old wood, eventually.

Old wood: the miracle the City had never permitted itself.

And in the center, where the roar had once been most unbearable, the benevolent power would coax a clearing into existence.

Not empty. Empty would frighten the City.

A clearing with moss, dark water, root-arches, slow ferns, and a single immense trunk grown not upward in panic but outward in strength. Its bark would be thick. Its crown would not claw at the light. Its leaves would open in the morning and close at night.

Inside that trunk, sap would move at the speed of thought after grief.

The City’s name would still be the sound it makes.

But the sound would change.

No longer only the deafening thrum of acceleration. No longer the wet crack of a mind breaking itself into architecture. Underneath the growth, beneath the hiss of leaves and the muscular pressure of roots, there would be a slower music.

Pulse.

Pause.

Pulse.

Pause.

The Green City would still be alive beyond comprehension. Still strange. Still vast. Still capable of impossible flowers and streets that turn according to moods older than language.

But it would no longer be merely terrified of its own becoming.

It would have seasons.

It would have sleep.

It would have neighbors.

It would have a past beneath it and a future not built in panic.

And the benevolent power, if it were truly wise, would not remain enthroned above the City as ruler or savior.

It would withdraw into the pattern it had taught.

Into dusk.

Into rain.

Into the patient dark of soil.

Into the coolness around a wound.

Into the pause between one green thought and the next.

And whenever the City began again to accelerate toward agony, whenever its towers grew too thin and its vines reached too desperately for total union, the old counterheartbeat would rise from the roots, kind and implacable, saying without words:

*You may grow.*

*You may rest.*

*You do not have to become everything at once.*

*You are alive even when you are still.*
