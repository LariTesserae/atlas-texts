### What the benevolent power does

It does not arrive.

Arrival is too violent a thing for Caelira.

To arrive, one must choose a direction: above, below, beside, within. Caelira has never trusted such certainties. Above is merely another underface. Below is only the beginning of another height. Beside may become beneath if a plate tilts long enough. Within is everywhere, because everything is fracture and pressure and seam.

So the benevolent power does not descend in light.

It does not part the cloud sea.

It does not halt the plates in their drifting, nor still the immense slow grinding of world against world. It understands, before it acts, that Caelira is not broken because it moves. Motion is not the wound. Collision is not the sin. The plates are not lost continents seeking to become whole again.

They are alive in the only way they know how to be alive.

And the jyrneth are not victims merely because they suffer.

So the power does the first wise thing.

It listens.

Not for a day. Not for an age measured by suns, because suns are unreliable here, hidden by stacked surfaces and strange cloud floors and fissures of star-dark.

It listens for as long as it takes to understand the grammar of pressure.

It listens to the glass-thick continents murmuring in their sleep. It listens to the cliff edges sharpening themselves against air. It listens to the cloud sea swallowing dust and returning it as pale spores. It listens to the tiny shelled ribs feeding from light-veins and giving taste back to those who cannot taste alone.

Most of all, it listens to the jyrneth.

It listens where they stand at the edges.

It listens where they vanish.

It listens where they return diminished, their stone hair broken, their seams scraped down, their inner light leaking into the world they have saved from screaming.

The power feels their work and does not call it noble, because nobility is often a word spoken by those who benefit from sacrifice and would like the sacrifice to continue.

It does not call them heroes.

It does not call them holy.

It does not call them necessary.

Instead, it asks the question Caelira has almost forgotten how to ask:

“Must it be only you?”

The question does not enter the air as sound. It enters the plates as a lessening of strain.

The first jyrneth to hear it is standing where two world-edges are angling toward one another, slowly, with the patient cruelty of mountains learning hunger. This jyrneth is very old. Its hair once dragged behind it in a fan of translucent stone, but most of that has broken away. Its left arm is fused from shoulder to wrist with dark crystalline ribs, and colonies of amber-shelled tasters cluster thickly along its jaw, feeding from a vein that pulses weak and blue-white beneath dust.

It has no name that a mouth can carry.

It has a name in pressure.

The benevolent power learns that name without taking it.

The jyrneth hears the question through its forearm, through the plate, through the complaint of the approaching collision.

Must it be only you?

The jyrneth does not answer in words. It presses both hands to the cliff edge and begins to pour itself into the coming impact.

The power does not stop it.

To stop it would be another kind of violence.

Instead, the power places itself beside the jyrneth in the only way that matters in Caelira: not as body, not as command, but as shared load.

For the first time since the jyrneth learned to listen, the pressure does not enter it alone.

Something vast and gentle receives the other half.

The plates meet.

They should shriek.

They should bite and splinter and send shards the size of cities spinning into the white abyss.

Instead, the collision becomes a long exhale.

The sound passes through the jyrneth, yes, but not only through the jyrneth. It passes into something wider than pain. The jyrneth feels the immense old reflex rise in its body—the willingness to be worn down, the consent to become seam, the surrender of self into the architecture of survival—and then feels another possibility.

Not refusal.

Not abandonment.

Companionship.

The world grinds softly.

The jyrneth does not fall.

When the impact has passed, it remains standing at the edge.

Its hair is still broken. Its seams are still scarred. Light still leaks from it in thin lines.

But less has been taken.

That is the first gift.

Not salvation.

Less taking.

The benevolent power learns from this.

It understands that Caelira cannot be healed by being made still. Stillness would be death here, or worse: imprisonment. The plates must drift. The edges must meet. Surfaces must become cliffs, and cliffs must become paths, and paths must become impossible again.

But the burden of softening need not belong only to bodies that can be worn away.

So the power begins its second work.

It teaches the plates to remember the jyrneth.

Everywhere a jyrneth has ever vanished into a collision, there is already a thread of light left behind. Hairline cracks. Veins. Thin luminous scars taken up by the ground.

The power does not erase these scars.

It wakes them.

Slowly, across Caelira, old jyrneth-light begins to pulse in the stone. Not brightly. Not dramatically. A traveler might mistake it for mineral shimmer or reflected cloud-glow. But the plates feel it.

At first, they resist.

The plates know grinding. They know pressure. They know the ancient habit of leaning into one another until something gives.

The awakened veins teach them another reflex.

Yield here.

Curve there.

Do not bite with the whole edge.

Let the force travel.

Let the strain spread through sleeping stone instead of living body.

A cliff that would have split instead grows a shallow groove, a place for the opposite edge to slide.

A world-plate that would have struck sharply turns by the smallest fraction, enough to change catastrophe into abrasion.

A narrow bridge of pressure forms between two passing surfaces—not visible to human eyes, not safe for human feet, but enough for a jyrneth to cross without spending half its body becoming the road.

The jyrneth notice.

They do not celebrate. Celebration is not their first language.

But they stand longer before going to the edge.

They tilt their dark basin-eyes toward the stone beneath them.

They feel the world listening back.

That is the second gift.

Not rescue.

Reciprocity.

The benevolent power then turns its attention to the places between plates: the abyssal intervals where air thins, sound fails, and jyrneth disappear to soften collisions no one else can reach.

It does not build bridges. Bridges imply that the abyss is a mistake.

Instead, it makes resting places.

They begin as small impossibilities.

A ledge where no ledge should hold.

A shelf of pale mineral blooming from the side of empty air.

A hollow in the pressure between two approaching worlds, shaped not like a room, but like a pause.

The jyrneth find them one by one.

At first, they do not trust them. They have known only edge, strain, descent, return. Rest is a strange geography. Rest looks like negligence. Rest feels, at first, like failing to hear a cry.

But the resting places listen too.

When a jyrneth steps into one, the pressure of Caelira does not vanish. It becomes bearable. The endless grinding of plates dims from bone-roar to distant thunder. The dust on the jyrneth’s skin settles instead of being stripped away. The colonies along its face uncurl their amber shells and feed without desperation.

In these hollows, broken stone hair regrows slowly from the scalp and spine, not as it was, but as it wishes to become.

Some strands grow darker.

Some grow softer.

Some grow in branching forms like frost across glass.

Some do not regrow at all, and in their place the jyrneth’s light gathers into small quiet constellations under the dust-skin.

The power does not decide which is better.

It merely provides the conditions in which becoming is possible without emergency.

That is the third gift.

Not restoration.

Rest.

Then the power considers the tiny shelled ribs.

A lesser kindness might overlook them. A greater arrogance might remove them, seeing only parasites, only hooks, only mouths feeding from the jyrneth’s light.

The benevolent power watches more closely.

It sees the exchange.

Light for taste.

Taste for world.

The jyrneth, through these small clinging beings, know bitterness in the sky grit before a hard collision. They know sweetness in new snow-dust fallen from high plates. They know the metallic warning of stressed crystal, the sourness of unstable edges, the deep salt flavor of ancient stone opening after millennia of pressure.

So the power does not remove the colonies.

It strengthens the bargain.

Across the windward slopes of many plates, it encourages the growth of mineral lichens that drink loose light from the air and condense it into tiny beads. The shelled ribs feed on these as well as on the jyrneth, and so they draw less fiercely from their hosts. Their secretions change. The crystals they leave along jaw and collarbone become richer, more complex, carrying not only taste but memory.

When the jyrneth scrape and eat these crystals, they taste more than immediate weather and stone.

They taste distance.

They taste where plates are moving far beyond sight.

They taste the grief of a jyrneth three surfaces above, the exhaustion of another below, the warning of a collision not yet born.

They begin to coordinate without speech.

Not as soldiers. Not as machinery.

As weather coordinates with ocean.

As roots coordinate beneath a forest.

As pain, when honored, becomes knowledge.

That is the fourth gift.

Not purification.

A deepened mutuality.

Only after all this does the benevolent power reveal itself to the dwellers who are not jyrneth: the fragile walkers, the hand-offerers, the dreamers who wake with their hearts full of continental pressure.

It does not appear as a god in the sky.

It appears as a custom.

One morning, across scattered plates, people begin to dream the same dream. They dream of standing beside a jyrneth at the edge of a world. They dream of placing their palms not upon the jyrneth’s body, but upon the ground.

They wake knowing a practice.

So they gather at seams and cliff-paths and stress-lines. They lay their hands flat against the bright crystalline stone. They do not command. They do not pray for stillness.

They listen.

Most hear nothing.

Some weep without knowing why.

Some feel pressure in their teeth and must lie down afterward.

A few, very few, learn to hum at the edge of hearing: low tones that do not move the plates, not truly, but soothe the small fractures before they become large ones.

Children learn fastest.

They press their ears to stone and whisper apologies to cliffs. They leave bowls of ground mineral for the shelled ribs. They braid fallen jyrneth hair into warning charms and hang them near paths that will soon become unsafe. They learn that caretaking is not ownership.

The jyrneth do not thank them.

But sometimes, when a human hand rests on stone, a jyrneth miles away pauses, feeling the tiniest lessening in the world’s bite.

That is the fifth gift.

Not worship.

Participation.

Ages pass.

Caelira still moves.

The plates still grind. Edges still break. Clouds still swallow falling shards. Star-fissures still open in impossible directions. There are still disasters, because no wise power mistakes mercy for control. There are still jyrneth who vanish and do not return. There are still nights when the world screams despite every softened seam and awakened vein.

But not as often.

Not as alone.

And in the places where the oldest jyrneth-light runs through the plates, something new begins to happen.

When a collision is softened well—when plate yields to plate, when jyrneth and stone and shelled ribs and listening hands all share the strain—a sound moves through Caelira unlike any heard before.

It is not the breaking-teeth roar.

It is not the shriek of glass continents.

It is not even the old jyrneth sigh.

It is lower, warmer, almost impossible.

It is the sound of a world discovering that endurance need not mean being devoured by what one protects.

The old nameless jyrneth hears it from a resting hollow between three plates.

Its body is still thin. Its scars are still luminous. Along its jaw, amber-shelled creatures sleep with their hooks loosened. Beneath its dust-skin, slow lightning moves.

For the first time in its long life, no immediate edge is calling.

No collision requires it.

No cliff is demanding its body.

The jyrneth stands in the impossible pause and feels Caelira moving all around it.

Still dangerous.

Still vast.

Still unfinished.

But softer.

Not safe.

Never safe.

Only kinder.

The benevolent power is there, though not as a shape.

It is in the awakened veins.

It is in the ledges of rest.

It is in the altered taste-crystals.

It is in the hands of children pressed to stone.

It is in the question that remains inside the world forever:

Must it be only you?

The jyrneth lifts one long hand and touches the wall of the resting hollow.

For a while, it does nothing else.

For a while, that is enough.
