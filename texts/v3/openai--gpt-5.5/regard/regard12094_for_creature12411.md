A benevolent and wise power would not descend in thunder.

It would not split the sky, crown a chosen one, or turn the jagged ridges into gardens overnight. It would understand, before all else, that this place is not broken. Harshness is not the same as cruelty. Scarcity is not the same as failure. The cold, the thin air, the blinding light, the silence, the stubborn moss in the stone—all of it belongs to a pattern still forming.

So the power would come quietly.

Perhaps it would arrive first as attention.

Not intervention. Not command. Simply a vast and patient awareness settling over the land like an unseen warmth beneath snow. It would listen to the little stream worrying its way through the pale rock. It would notice where the moss browned too early, where the pools froze too deep, where the slopes had begun to loosen after each thaw. It would watch the birds wheel above the cliffs, the small animals vanish between stones, the sleek-eyed predator pause with hunger in its belly.

And it would watch the people.

It would see how little they asked of the world. How carefully they broke branches from dead wood rather than living limbs. How they thanked the body of an animal before dividing it into food, fur, tool, thread, memory. How they etched marks into rock not to say this is ours, but we were here, and we knew we were guests.

A wise power would love them for that.

But love, if it is wise, does not smother.

So it would begin with small mercies.

It would deepen one hidden spring by a hand’s breadth, just enough that it would not vanish during the leanest cold. No one would know why the water lasted longer that season. The people would simply find it still shining beneath its skin of ice and murmur gratitude to the land.

It would teach the moss to cling more firmly to the loosest slopes, weaving root and fiber through the young stone so that fewer shelters were crushed in the season of thaw. Not all danger would be removed. Danger was one of the teachers of this place. But needless loss—the kind that teaches nothing and only empties the arms—would be softened.

It would turn the dreams of one old woman toward a valley she had forgotten from childhood, where low shrubs grew thick behind a windbreak of broken rock. She would wake with the taste of green bitterness on her tongue and lead her kin there when food ran thin. They would call it ancestral memory. They would not be wrong.

It would guide the hand of a child scratching symbols into stone. The child would make, without knowing why, a new mark beside the old ones: three lines descending into a circle. Years later, others would understand it as a sign for hidden water. The mark would spread from shelter to shelter, never as revelation, only as usefulness shaped into reverence.

It would not give them metal before they were ready.

It would not give them conquest.

It would not fill their minds with hunger for distant kingdoms, nor teach them to despise their stone knives and bone needles. It would not tell them that simplicity is ignorance. It would not shame them for being few.

Instead, it would protect the possibility of their becoming.

A wise power would know that every people carries more futures than one. These inhabitants might remain small and reverent for a thousand years, learning every mood of their high, bright world. They might one day descend to gentler lands and meet others unlike themselves. They might develop songs complex enough to map weather, kinship, migration, grief. They might discover pigments beneath the pale rock and cover the cliffs with memory. They might vanish, leaving only shelters, tools, and marks that would one day make another people kneel in wonder.

The power would not choose for them.

It would only keep open the doors that cruelty would close too soon.

When sickness came, it would not banish sickness from the world, for bodies are mortal and all living things return. But it might nudge a hunter toward a bitter lichen that eases fever. It might let someone notice that those who drank from the running stream recovered more often than those who drank from stagnant pools. Knowledge would arrive not as miracle, but as observation.

When hunger came, it would not rain meat from the sky. It would shift the migration of the quick-footed animals by half a ridge, enough that the hunters might find tracks before despair set in. It would let the predator take its share too. Benevolence would not mean favoring one life so completely that another starved. The people understood this already; the power would honor their understanding.
