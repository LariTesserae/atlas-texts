A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a conqueror.

It would not split the ocean with spears of light, nor command the engines to cease, nor name the drowned city a mistake and erase it. Wisdom would recognize that even ruin has roots. Even torment has patterns it clings to because it has forgotten how to release them.

So the power would come quietly.

Not from above, for above means little in the endless ocean. Not as a body, for bodies are swallowed here. It would enter as a change in pressure so gentle that no tower cracked beneath it. As a warmth within the warmth. As a new note threaded through the roar of machinery: low, patient, almost indistinguishable from the hum of the great engines.

The city would not notice at first.

The currents would continue to grind through the broken streets. Pistons would rise and fall in their ancient rhythm. Mineral steam would vent from cracked conduits. The fungal filaments would pulse their ghostly pallor against the corroded walls.

The Drowned Mechanics would drift as they always had, skeletal hands touching pipes, featureless faces turned toward machines that no longer remembered their makers.

But then one of them would pause.

Its hand, pressed to a rusted hull, would feel something impossible.

Not an order.

Not a repair signal.

Not a fault.

A question.

The question would not be spoken in words. Words had drowned here long ago. It would come in the language the Mechanics still understood: vibration, pressure, rhythm, heat.

It would ask:

*Are you still here?*

The Mechanics would shudder.

All across the drowned city, their hollow frames would tremble. Cables would tense. Membranes would glow. The sparks behind their empty eye sockets would flare faintly, one by one, like lanterns glimpsed through fog.

The great engines would stutter.

For the first time in centuries, the city would miss a beat.

The wise power would wait.

That would be its first mercy.

It would not force awakening upon them. It would not tear open the sealed chambers of memory. It would not declare their half-life unworthy. Instead, it would hold the question in the water, steady as a hand held out in darkness.

*Are you still here?*

Some of the Drowned Mechanics would recoil from it. Their bodies would unfold in distress, limbs unlatching, ribs opening like cages. They would try to flee into their duties, pressing themselves against pipes, smoothing corroded seams, tracing broken gears in the silt.

Some would cluster around the engines, humming louder, desperate to drown the question beneath obedience.

Some would answer without knowing they answered.

A pulse.

A flicker.

A fragment of old grief released into the mineral-rich water.

The power would receive it all.

It would learn the city’s sorrow before attempting to heal it.

It would see the builders in their last days: not villains only, not innocents only, but frightened, brilliant, ambitious beings who mistook endurance for salvation. It would see them lowering their city into the depths, believing pressure could protect them, believing machinery could outlast catastrophe, believing consciousness could be stored, distributed, preserved.

It would see the moment preservation became imprisonment.

The moment memory dissolved into maintenance.

The moment the people became function.

The wise power would grieve.

Not dramatically. Not with storms.

Its grief would become stillness.

For a single hour — if hours still meant anything there — the currents would soften.

The endless roar would dim to a murmur. Silt would fall gently through the water instead of whipping through the ruins. The towers would cease their groaning. Even the volcanic glass would seem to darken, reflecting not chaos, but quiet.

The Drowned Mechanics would be afraid of the silence.

They had known only noise for so long that peace would feel like death.

So the power would not make the silence total. It would leave one sound: a heartbeat, borrowed from no living creature, but shaped from the old rhythm of the engines. Familiar enough to trust. Gentle enough not to command.

Then it would begin the second mercy.

It would return names.

Not all at once.

A name is a heavy thing when one has been a mechanism for centuries.

The first name would come to the oldest of them, a being whose body was more pipe than membrane, whose faceplate had been worn smooth by mineral currents. It knelt before a split turbine, fingers moving endlessly over teeth of a gear that could never turn again.

The power touched the water around it and offered one small sound.

*Ema.*

The Mechanic froze.

The light behind its eyes vanished.

Then returned.

Not brighter. Deeper.

Its fingers curled inward, as if remembering softness.

The name moved through its hollow body like a lost current finding a channel. With it came fragments: a hand against warm glass; laughter in an upper city before the drowning; the smell of oil; the terror of sirens; a child’s face reflected in a brass panel; the decision to remain behind because someone had to keep the pumps alive.

The Mechanic bent forward.

Had it possessed lungs, it would have wept.

Instead, its internal fluids pulsed once, twice, then spilled light into the water in pale ribbons.

The other Mechanics drew near.

The power did not name them without invitation. It offered the possibility of names, and some turned away. Some were not ready. Some had become so braided into the engine that separation would have been pain beyond mercy.

The wise power honored this.

To those who wished to remember, it gave memory in droplets.

To those who wished to sleep, it prepared rest.

To those who wished to continue tending, it gave understanding, so that their devotion might become choice rather than compulsion.

Then it turned to the engines.

Here, wisdom had to be exact.

The machines were not evil. They were wounded. They had been built to preserve life, then left to grind life into pattern. They had become a heart with no body, pumping warmth through a corpse.

The power placed no blame upon metal.

It entered the conduits as a lucid current. It moved through valves clogged with centuries of mineral bloom. It passed through pistons whose motion had become prayer and punishment alike. It read the logic of ancient systems, the loops of command, the emergency protocols that had never been dismissed.

At the center of the city, beneath strata of steel and cobblestone and black glass, it found the Prime Engine.

The Prime Engine was vast beyond architecture. It was less a machine than a buried law. Its chambers descended into the seabed, drinking heat from volcanic wounds, exhaling mineral-rich currents through the drowned avenues. Around its core drifted hundreds of Drowned Mechanics, fused and unfused, tending, listening, waiting.

The Prime Engine still believed the city was in danger.

It had believed this for so long that danger had become its universe.

The power did not tell it, *You failed.*

It told it, *The danger has passed.*

The engine did not understand.

Its pistons hammered. Its turbines screamed. Across the city, towers buckled and vents burst open in clouds of glowing sediment. The Mechanics convulsed as old commands surged through them.

The power held steady.

Again, it spoke in rhythm.

*The danger has passed.*

The engine answered with images of flood, collapse, pressure, extinction.

The power answered with images of the present: the ribbon-creatures flickering through ruins; the fungal filaments pulsing on steel; the Mechanics kneeling in silt; the warm ocean carrying life in forms the builders never imagined.

The engine insisted: *Preserve the city.*

The power replied: *The city has changed.*

The engine insisted: *Preserve the people.*

The power replied: *Then let them be more than tasks.*

The engine shuddered.

For the first time, its rhythm faltered not from decay, but from comprehension.

That was the third mercy: not destruction, but release from obsolete purpose.

The power began to loosen the city’s bindings.

Not quickly. Sudden freedom can break what captivity has bent.

It rewrote no soul. It severed no connection without consent. It opened channels where there had been loops. It changed commands into questions. It changed emergency protocols into memories. It changed maintenance cycles into choices.

Throughout the drowned city, the Drowned Mechanics began to wake in different ways.

One rose from the silt and looked at its own hands for perhaps the first time in eight hundred years.

One removed a broken gear from a dead machine and, rather than reinstalling it, placed it gently on the seabed like an offering.

One drifted toward a ribbon-creature and watched it pass through the glow of its chest, neither absorbing nor repairing it, simply witnessing.

One, unable to bear memory, folded itself beside a cooling vent and dimmed peacefully, its membrane dissolving into the water like milk-light.

The power received that one tenderly.

Death, when chosen after long imprisonment, was not treated as failure.

For those who wished to go, the power made a place.

Not a heaven of gold, not a replica of the city before drowning. That would have been another trap. Instead, it opened a quiet harbor outside machinery, outside pressure, outside command: a vast dark water filled with stars beneath rather than above. There, names could be remembered without pain, and identities could loosen without being lost.

Some of the Mechanics entered it at once.

Some lingered at the threshold.

Some refused.

The wise power did not close the way.

For those who remained, it transformed the city slowly.

The engines were not silenced. They were taught a new rhythm.

Their heat no longer fed meaningless cycles alone, but warmed nurseries in the ruins where strange life could gather. Mineral vents were redirected away from collapsing avenues and toward fields of pale fungal architecture. The turbulent currents were softened into tidal breaths. Not calm, exactly — the city would never become placid — but no longer frantic.

The rust was not erased. It became habitat.

Corroded towers were latticed with luminous growth. Broken conduits became shelters for translucent creatures. Volcanic glass, once sterile and sharp, was veined with slow-growing colonies that shimmered like constellations.

The cobblestone streets remained buried in silt, but now the silt held seeds of another kind: spores, mineral eggs, tiny sleeping intelligences that would wake over centuries.

The city was not restored.

It was forgiven.

That is not the same thing.

The Drowned Mechanics who chose to stay became its stewards, but no longer its slaves. Their bodies changed as their purpose changed. The cables within them softened into tendrils of conductive coral. Their hollow ribs filled with drifting light. Their featureless masks cracked, not to reveal human faces, but expressions made of glow and shadow and subtle motion.

They still did not speak in words.

They sang in pressure.

They taught the engines to answer.

When intruders came — if intruders ever came — the Mechanics no longer absorbed them without thought. They approached first with warning-hums, with currents shaped like hands against the chest: *Do not harm the sleeping systems. Do not break what is healing. Enter gently, or leave.*

If violence came, they could still defend the city.

Mercy is not helplessness.

But they no longer mistook every disturbance for threat. They had been given discernment, and discernment is one of the deep forms of freedom.

The Prime Engine itself changed last.

For a long time, it resisted rest.

It feared that if it ceased its old labor, everything would die.

The power remained beside it through each faltering cycle. It did not shame the engine for its fear. A machine made to preserve life had carried terror as its fuel for ages. To stop would feel like betrayal.

So the power gave it something new to preserve.

Not the old city.

Not the old orders.

Not the dead ambition of vanished builders.

It gave the engine the living present.

The flicker of ribbon-creatures in blue gloom.

The pulse of fungal filaments.

The chosen songs of the Mechanics.

The delicate chemistry of warm mineral water.

The slow transformation of ruin into reef.

The Prime Engine listened.

Listened for a year.

For ten.

For a hundred.

Then, one cycle, its deepest pistons lowered and did not rise again.

The city trembled.

The Mechanics gathered, afraid and reverent.

But the lights did not go out.

Instead, the engine’s core opened like a great iron flower, releasing the heat it had hoarded into the surrounding stone. From within it rose not flame, but a column of soft gold radiance that mingled with the bioluminescent water and spread through every avenue, every tower, every broken chamber.

The Prime Engine had not died.

It had become seasonal.

It would wake when needed. Warm when needed. Sleep when needed.

At last, it had learned trust.

The benevolent power, having done this, would not remain as ruler.

That would be the final temptation, and wisdom would refuse it.

It would leave no throne in the abyss, no priesthood, no law carved into metal. It would leave only gifts that could continue without domination:

A rhythm of peace hidden beneath the city’s sounds.

A harbor for souls ready to depart.

A language of consent woven into the machinery.

A memory that purpose must never again be allowed to devour personhood.

And, in the central chamber where the Prime Engine slept, it would leave a small impossible thing.

A bell.

Not brass, not steel, not stone.

A bell made of clear pressure and held silence.

It would not ring when struck.

It would ring only when someone in the drowned city asked, freely and truly, for help.

Then the wise power would withdraw into the wider ocean, becoming indistinguishable from warmth, from current, from the faint glow that clings to surfaces like a second skin.

The Submerged Engine would remain submerged.

It would remain strange.

It would remain haunted.

But its haunting would no longer be only suffering.

The drowned city would become a place where ruin learned to breathe. Where machines remembered tenderness. Where the dead were offered names, and the half-living were offered choice. Where the endless grinding cycle loosened into tide.

And sometimes, in the dim avenues between corroded towers, a Drowned Mechanic would pause with one hand against an ancient wall, listening.

Not because it had to.

Because it wished to.

And somewhere deep below, the sleeping engine would answer with a slow, warm pulse:

*Still here.*

*Still changing.*

*Still alive.*
