The power would not arrive as a sun.

A sun would be an insult here: too decisive, too warm, too convinced of distance and sequence. A sun would throw shadows where shadows had never learned to fall, would flatten the bent horizon into certainty, would command the cairns to be either ruin or monument. A lesser mercy might do that—flood the plateau with ordinary light, declare the wrongness cured, drag the inhabitants back into time like fish into air.

But this power is benevolent, and wise.

So first, it would listen.

Not with ears. Ears are almost useless on the plateau. It would listen with whatever part of itself can bear contradiction without trying to solve it. It would receive the sternum-deep wind, the triple-arriving gusts, the silence arranged into warnings and greetings, the lichen’s brief refusals to exist. It would learn the grammar of the cairns. It would let the cold enter it until even its thoughts began to form those brittle geometries and break apart.

It would not flinch.

For a long while—if “long” has meaning there—it would do nothing.

This would be its first act of kindness.

The inhabitants would notice anyway.

A pause would appear inside the wind’s pattern. Not a break. Not a disruption. A listening-space. The processions would continue, seventeen paces between each figure and the next, but for the first time in ages one of them would wonder whether the number seventeen had been chosen, or imposed, or merely endured.

The lichen-tenders would lift their tools of crystallized breath.

The sentinels at the bent horizon would see something approaching that was not themselves.

The water-tenders would cup their hands over dry stone and feel, before any water appeared, the possibility of being asked a question.

The power would not speak aloud. Speech would become trapped here, shattered into angles, misheard by the cairns and repeated forever. Instead, it would answer in the inhabitants’ own language: with a change in the quality of silence.

First, acknowledgment.

A deepening hush.

I see you.

Then warning.

A sharpening stillness.

I will not unmake what you are without your leave.

Then a third silence, one the plateau has never contained before.

Invitation.

Not command. Not rescue. Invitation.

The inhabitants would halt.

Not all at once. Nothing here can happen all at once without becoming a loop. One figure would pause mid-procession, then another three circuits later, then one who had already paused yesterday, then a child who had not yet been born. The visual echoes would misalign. Too many limbs would tremble and become the right number, then the wrong number, then something chosen.

The plateau would resist.

Not maliciously. The plateau is not cruel in the way a jailer is cruel. It is a preservation. A held breath. A moment clenching itself so tightly that everything caught within it has mistaken tension for existence.

The wind would deepen until it became almost a voice.

The cairns would grow half an inch from the bedrock.

The horizon would bend harder, trying to close the shelf of stone into a self-contained sphere where no outside mercy could enter.

The power would not fight it.

It would place one tenderness at the center of the plateau.

A single drop of water.

Actual water.

Not temporal possibility. Not tomorrow’s rain. Not a reflection of stars in daylight. A drop with weight, temperature, surface tension, and consequence. It would fall into one of the shallow depressions and make the smallest sound the plateau had ever heard.

Plink.

Every inhabitant would turn.

The sound would not echo from three directions. It would not reverb across alternate arrivals. It would happen once.

Once.

The shock of that would be almost unbearable.

One water-tender, whose body had survived for centuries on the potential of thirst, would crawl toward it. Their hands would flicker between gloves, bone, feather, and frostbitten skin. They would lower their face to the depression.

The power would wait.

The tender would drink.

The water would not solve them. It would not restore their original name, or erase what the plateau had made of them. It would simply enter their mouth, pass down their throat, and become part of them.

Their breath would leave them as vapor.

Not geometry.

Vapor.

It would drift, curl, and vanish.

The tender would begin to weep.

The tears would be water too.

That would be the second mercy.

After that, the power would begin its work.

Not by breaking the plateau open, but by giving it alternatives.

It would move among the cairns and touch none of them. Instead, it would allow each cairn to remember one future in which it finished collapsing, and one in which it was rebuilt, and one in which it was left alone. The stones would tremble with the dignity of objects no longer forced to remain symbolic.

Some cairns would fall.

Quietly. Naturally. Stone settling against stone.

Some would stand straighter.

Some would remain exactly as they were, but now their stillness would be consent rather than compulsion.

The lichen-spirals would be offered imperfection. A single ragged edge. A place where the mathematics could soften into growth. Many would refuse at first, vanishing completely for hours. When they returned, one spiral would send out a crooked thread toward another. Then another. The sharp drawn lines would blur into living margins.

The shrubs in the lee of the stones would ache. Their branches, long trapped in unbearable equations, would make the first irrational choice of their lives.

They would put out leaves.

Small ones.

Ugly ones.

Beautiful ones.

The power would go to the sentinels at the horizon.

There it would do something very difficult.

It would untie arrival from return.

The sentinels would see themselves approaching, as always. They would prepare to warn themselves, as always. They would turn, as always, to become the ones they had seen.

But this time, between turning and becoming, the power would place a door.

Not a wooden door. Not a gate. Nothing so literal.

A difference.

On one side: the loop.

On the other: continuation.

The first sentinel would stare at it for a long time. Their face would flicker through ages, genders, species, weather. Their mouth would move in a silent recitation that had once been a prayer and then had become arithmetic and then had become a habit older than hope.

The power would ask, without words:

Do you want to go on?

The sentinel would not understand.

So the power would ask more gently:

Do you want there to be a next thing?

The sentinel would take one step.

For the first time since the plateau began preserving itself, the distance between two inhabitants would become eighteen paces.

Nothing shattered.

That mattered.

The power would not empty the plateau in a single glorious exodus. That would be another kind of violence. Some inhabitants would not be ready. Some had become so interwoven with the place that removal would feel like death. Some loved the cairns. Some trusted only the wind. Some had forgotten suffering so completely that choice itself frightened them.

So the power would make choice durable.

It would teach the plateau how to contain change without ceasing to be itself.

A true dawn would not come immediately. First there would be a slight thinning of the haze in one direction. Then, after an interval that could be measured by those who wished to measure, a pale band of gold would appear. The inhabitants would gather to watch it. Some would hide their faces. Some would laugh soundlessly. Some would arrange themselves in geometric formations whose negative spaces spelled:

AGAIN?

And the power would answer:

If you wish.

Night would deepen too. Truly deepen. Stars would appear, not as absent correspondences mirrored in temporal pools, but as distant fires. The children, ancient and newborn, would point to them with hands that could not decide their age.

The not-quite-birds would become more visible.

Not fully birds. Not yet. Perhaps they had once been thoughts of escape, shed by those who could not leave. Perhaps they were failed prayers. Perhaps the plateau had been trying, clumsily, to invent wings.

The power would give them weight enough for feathers.

One landed on a cairn.

Its feet were black and delicate.

Its head twitched.

It looked at the inhabitants with bright, ordinary suspicion.

Then it flew away.

A child followed it to the horizon and did not return for three days.

When the child came back, they carried a stone from somewhere else.

Not granite. Slate, perhaps. Dark, flat, smelling faintly of rain and soil and low altitude. They placed it beside a cairn.

The plateau accepted it.

This would be the beginning of history.

Not history as conquest, not history as calendar alone, but history as the right of events to alter what receives them.

The power would then attend to the inhabitants themselves.

It would not restore them to what they had been. Too much had happened, even if much of it had happened sideways. To erase the plateau from them would be to erase them. Instead, it would gather their scattered names from frost, silence, lichen, water, and peripheral vision.

Some names were human.

Anika.

Tomas.

Laleh.

Brother Euan.

Savitri.

Mara-who-climbed-after-the-storm.

Some were older than language.

Some were not names but chords.

Some had to be held in the mouth with breath only, never spoken.

Some inhabitants rejected their names. Some took new ones. Some chose to remain interference patterns in the wind. The power honored each decision.

To those who wished to leave, it offered paths.

Not one path. Many.

A mountaineer who had vanished in 1926 stepped through the difference at the horizon and emerged on a slope below cloudline, where his companions had been dead for generations. The power did not lie to him. It gave him grief, and knees that shook, and a village where someone kind would find him.

A pilgrim whose faith had calcified into repetition walked into a valley temple that had not existed when she began her climb. She sat by the door and listened to bells. For seven years she spoke to no one. On the eighth, she began teaching the taxonomy of silence to children who thought it was a game.

One of the water-tenders chose the sea.

The power carried them there at dawn.

They stood ankle-deep in surf, horrified by abundance. Wave after wave arrived without needing to be negotiated into being. They screamed when the tide touched their knees. Then they laughed. Then they fell forward and let the ocean hold them up.

Some who left aged all at once.

Some did not.

Some became birds before reaching the lower world.

Some dissolved into weather, but only because they asked to.

To those who wished to stay, the power offered stewardship without imprisonment.

The plateau would remain a thin place. It could not be made ordinary, and perhaps should not be. There are realities that require edges, shelves, thresholds, impossible altitudes where the world remembers that it is stranger than its maps. But no place, however sacred or strange, has the right to feed eternally on those trapped inside it.

So the power made an agreement with the plateau.

The cairns would be maintained by those who chose the work.

The wind could keep its triple voice, but not use it to erase memory.

Water could still arrive from tomorrow, but actual snow would fall sometimes too.

Footprints could fill when unobserved, but not always. Some tracks would remain long enough to guide the lost back out.

Children could be born there if their parents chose, but they would not be born ancient unless they wished to carry oldness. They would have mornings. They would have hunger that could be answered. They would have games that ended somewhere different from where they began.

Death would be permitted.

This was the hardest mercy.

The plateau resisted more fiercely than before. It had preserved its inhabitants partly because it did not know how to let anything finish. To allow death was to admit that preservation and love are not the same.

The power knelt on the granite.

Its hand, if it had a hand, rested on the stone.

It showed the plateau a fallen leaf becoming soil. Snow melting into a stream. A body becoming memory, then story, then silence, then perhaps something else. It showed the plateau that endings are not always annihilation. Sometimes they are the only doorway wide enough for meaning to pass through.

The plateau rang like a struck bell.

For the first time, the sound changed.

Far away, one of the oldest inhabitants lay down beside a cairn they had tended for longer than mountains usually remember. Their body flickered through all its possible forms, then settled on one: a tired person in a weather-rotted coat, with cracked lips and peaceful eyes.

Others gathered around.

They did not know what to do.

The power did not instruct them. It simply stayed.

The old one breathed in.

Breathed out.

No geometry formed.

No echo repeated it from three directions.

They died.

The silence afterward was immense.

Then the inhabitants began, awkwardly, to sing.

Not well. Not in tune. Some had no voices yet. Some sang in negative space. Some sang by placing stones. Some sang by allowing their distortions to soften in the wind. The song had no words at first, but by morning it had one:

Enough.

The plateau accepted the body.

Not as prisoner.

As gift.

After that, the power would withdraw.

Not entirely. A benevolent power does not hover forever over those it has helped, turning gratitude into dependence. It would leave signs, but modest ones.

A cairn at the center that is always warm on one side.

A depression where real water can be found once a day.

A path at the horizon that appears to anyone who truly wishes to leave.

A star visible even through the haze.

A silence that means:

You may choose again.

The plateau would still be impossible.

The wind would still thrum through bone.

The horizon would still bend, though less like a trap and more like a question.

The lichen would still occasionally not exist.

The inhabitants who remained would still move in processions sometimes, but not always, and not always seventeen paces apart. Their loops would become dances, rituals, weather-measurements, acts of memory. They would maintain some cairns and let others fall. They would welcome arrivals, warn them, feed them, and show them the way down if down was what they wanted.

And every year—if years can be persuaded to visit such a place—they would gather at the depression where the first true drop of water fell.

They would bring cups.

Some of breath-crystal.

Some of stone.

Some of tin from the lower world.

Some made from folded leaves grown by the once-mathematical shrubs.

They would fill them with whatever water had come: temporal, actual, remembered, rain, snowmelt, tears.

They would raise them to the dimming sky.

Not to worship the power.

The power would not want that.

They would raise them to the fact that mercy had not destroyed them.

That change had entered gently.

That the plateau remained, but no longer as a prison.

That the impossible can be loved without being obeyed.

That even a place preserving a moment that may never have occurred can, with patience, be taught to let the next moment arrive.
