The power would come quietly.

Anything benevolent and wise, arriving in Anlac, would understand at once that loud mercy would be a kind of violence. It would not part the steam with trumpets. It would not cast a hard clean sun over the basin, nor command the ground to be still, nor lift the city whole onto some safer plateau beyond the hills.

That would be rescue in the language of outsiders.

It would be exile in the language of Anlac.

So the power would enter as the place itself entered: from below, slowly, without announcement.

For a long while it would do nothing but listen.

Not with ears. Ears are nearly useless in Anlac. It would listen with pressure, with root, with the patient skin of stone. It would learn the basin’s grammar: the upward sigh before a basaltic bloom, the fibrous ache of crystal veins entering concrete, the subtle pause before a dead zone wakes. It would learn the difference between danger and livelihood, between a flow that must be fled and a flow that only asks to be danced with.

It would listen to the Harvesters, too: their muffled hydraulic hearts, their drills biting into newborn rock, their sluices coughing mineral slurry into the arterial pipes. It would listen to the mosses fastening the world together by the million-threaded labor of rhizoids. It would listen to the glass-winged flocks pulsing messages through the dense air. It would listen to the six-legged scavengers padding through alleys with dust on their carapaces.

Last of all, because wisdom is patient but not blind, it would listen to the Anlacti.

It would hear their low voices through wrists and shoulders. It would watch the flash of Hand-Sign over the gantries. It would feel the elders standing barefoot on observation decks, their old bones reading the pressure of the unfinished earth. It would understand that these people were not merely surviving a catastrophe. They had made covenant with one.

And because of that, the power would not break the covenant.

It would amend it.

First, it would give warning.

Not a voice from the sky. Not prophecy carved in flame. Something smaller and more useful.

Deep beneath the basin, where the Accretion gathered itself before rising, the power would lay a second sensitivity into the stone: a mercy-tremor, faint but unmistakable. It would travel ahead of the truly lethal flows, ahead of the sudden crystalline blooms that pierced foundations like spears, ahead of the pressure-waves that could overturn a hab-stack before the jacks could bite.

The Anlacti would be the first to notice.

A Still-Watcher named Sera Venn, old enough to remember when the East Pipe was straight, would feel it during the third hour of a flat grey morning. She would be standing on Deck Seventeen with her hands folded behind her back and her boots unlatched, soles bare against the cold metal.

There would be a tremor.

Not the usual hum. Not the thick, blunt shove of an ordinary flow.

This would have shape.

It would arrive like a hand touching the underside of her foot: three pulses, a pause, then a long shiver slanting southwest.

Sera would open her eyes.

Below her, the city would be in its muted labor. Spider cranes would be unwinding a tower. Cable-vines would drag wet leaves across pipe armor. A Harvester would be crawling through a field of olive foam, its legs sinking and rising with ponderous delicacy.

Sera would sign once to the deck-crew.

Stop.

Then again, sharper.

Southwest. Deep rise. Not ordinary.

The young surveyors would check their instruments and find nothing. But no one in Anlac ignored an elder’s feet.

The block would move.

Hab-units would be jacked, pipes unlocked from their bracing, children gathered in harness-lines, moss trays lifted from windowsills and clipped to transport frames. The whole neighborhood would shift with the brisk irritation of people inconvenienced, not terrified.

Six hours later, the ground beneath the abandoned block would open without sound.

A white crystalline rib, thick as a transit tower and sharp as winter light, would shoulder upward through the street-plates. It would pass through three empty hab foundations, split a storage gantry, and rise another forty meters before stopping, pearlescent and wet in the shadowless day.

No one would die.

After that, the Anlacti would name the new tremor.

They would call it the Kindness Underfoot.

They would not worship it. Not at first. Worship is difficult in a place where every miracle has to be load-rated.

They would test it.

They would map it, argue over it, build instruments to catch it, fail, try again. Children would learn its rhythm along with Flow-Dodge. Still-Watchers would incorporate it into their pressure charts. Riggers would curse it affectionately when it woke them from sleep.

The power would be pleased by this.

To be made practical is a high form of praise.

Second, it would heal the air, but not empty it.

The heavy cold breath of Anlac was part of Anlac. The mineral dust, the condensation, the muffling density: all of these were woven into the city’s body and culture. To clear the air entirely would be to unmake distance, speech, warning, privacy. It would turn the basin into a place of echoes, and echoes would be cruel there.

So the power would not scour the sky clean.

Instead, it would teach the mosses a new hunger.

The cobalt mats and rust-velvet colonies that already loved the newborn rock would begin, gradually, to draw the sharpest poisons from the particulate air. Not all dust. Not the useful mineral breath by which Anlacti judged weather and flow. Only the invisible knives: the isotopic ash that lodged too deep in lungs, the metallic fines that made children cough blood in the wet months, the chemical ghosts that leaked from careless sluices.

No proclamation would accompany this.

A moss-binder would notice first that a patch of rust velvet near the South Refinery had turned dark at the edges, almost black, and that the air around it tasted less bitter through the filter. She would cut samples. She would cultivate them. She would argue with the Harvester guilds until they gave her contaminated substrate, and the moss would eat that too.

Within five years, every hab-stack would carry black-edged moss baffles along its vents.

Within ten, the cough-rate of Anlacti children would fall by half.

Within fifteen, the moss-binders would be among the most respected engineers in the basin, though they would insist they always had been.

The power would not correct them.

Third, it would give Anlac a place to rest without making it still.

This would be the most delicate gift.

Stillness was dangerous to the Anlacti. A truly unmoving floor, a wall that did not flex, a horizon that remained unchanged for too many days—these things unsettled them more deeply than collapse. Still-Sickness was not mere habit. Their nervous systems had grown around vibration. Their sleep braided itself to the hum. Their balance expected argument from the ground.

Yet constant motion wears even those born to it.

So the power would find, near the basin’s northern inner rim, a dead zone that had been quiet for eleven years and was beginning to dream of violence. It would not suppress the waking. Suppression creates debt. Instead, it would loosen hidden channels beneath the zone, letting pressure bleed away into a thousand harmless threads. The ground there would remain subtly alive: a low pulse, a tidal lift measured in millimeters, a warmth that came and went like breath.

No major extrusion would break it for a century.

The Anlacti would discover it by accident, as they discovered most blessings.

A youth crew sent to salvage old pipe braces would camp there one night and sleep too well. Their crew chief would wake suspicious, take readings, feel the ground with both palms, and report an anomaly.

Soon the place would be surveyed, named, debated, distrusted, occupied, abandoned, reoccupied, and finally accepted.

They would build no permanent monuments there. The habit ran too deep.

But they would make a hospital.

Not a hospital as outsiders imagined it, with silent white corridors and immovable beds. This one would be modular, flex-jointed, moss-walled, warm with regulated tremor. Its floors would murmur softly to the bones. Its operating tables would compensate for micro-shifts. Its recovery rooms would overlook cultivated fields of silver-needle trees dripping condensed water from shale-bark trunks.

They would bring the injured there after flow-events.

They would bring those whose ground-sense had been damaged, and the floors would teach their nerves to listen again.

They would bring those dying of age, when even an elder’s feet had grown confused, and let them feel a gentle earth beneath them until the end.

The place would be called Lowhaven.

The power would like that name.

Fourth, it would restrain greed, but not by punishment.

Anlac harvested the world as it was born. This was not evil. Birth is full of aftermatter. The Accretion gave up rare elements, unstable isotopes, crystalline fibers, mineral wealth beyond any outside nation’s patience or humility. The Anlacti took these things and lived.

But others watched the basin.

They saw yield.

They saw export graphs.

They saw Harvester engines crawling over newborn terrain and wondered how many more could fit.

The wise power would know that the greatest threat to Anlac was not the Accretion. The Anlacti understood the Accretion.

The threat was appetite without ground-sense.

So the power would make overharvest impossible in the only way that would be respected: physically.

Where machines cut too deep into active genesis fields, the new rock would become uselessly friable, collapsing into inert grey meal before refinement. Where sluices used solvents too harsh for the mosses and root-systems, the slurry would foam and spoil. Where Harvesters exceeded the pressure tolerances that local Still-Watchers marked as safe, their drills would meet crystalline tangles harder than any alloy, grown overnight in their path like clenched teeth.

There would be outrage.

Consortia from beyond the hills would accuse the guilds of sabotage. Engineers would publish papers. Priests, if there were any nearby, would mutter of judgment. Smugglers would try night-cutting and lose three machines to sudden sink-blooms that swallowed metal but spared the crews, leaving them bruised, humiliated, and alive on rafts of cobalt moss.

The Anlacti would understand sooner than outsiders.

The basin was not refusing harvest.

It was refusing gluttony.

In time, quotas would cease to be moral arguments and become engineering facts. You could cut this much, no more. You could draw this vein after the moss took hold, not before. You could follow the Accretion’s rhythm or lose your tools.

The Anlacti, who had always preferred facts to sermons, would accept this arrangement.

The outside owners would hate it.

The basin would not care.

Fifth, it would give the children shadows.

Not always. Not everywhere.

But sometimes, when the high steam thinned toward evening and the flat light began to pearl, the power would open narrow wounds in the cloud-layer above Anlac. Sunlight would fall through in long, slanting shafts, visible in the mineral air.

For a few minutes, gantries would cast black ribs over moss-fields. Cable-vines would print their leaf-shapes onto hab walls. Children who had never seen a crisp silhouette would chase their own dark forms across skid-platforms, shrieking in voices swallowed almost at once by the heavy air.

The adults would pretend not to be moved.

The Still-Watchers would note that the light-events had no apparent relation to flow pressure and were therefore not operationally significant.

Then, privately, they would stand in the rare shadows of their own bodies and feel time pass through them.

The power would do this because not every mercy must be useful.

Sixth, it would speak once.

Only once.

It would wait until the Anlacti had already made use of its gifts, named them, argued them into practice, and stripped them of superstition. It would wait until the Kindness Underfoot had saved enough lives that no one remembered life without it. It would wait until black-edged moss grew in every vent and Lowhaven’s tremor-wards were full of healing sleep.

Then, on a day when a major flow pressed upward beneath the oldest market stack and every crew in the district moved in perfect silent urgency, the power would enter the Hand-Sign.

Not as possession. Not as seizure.

A child would begin it.

She would be nine years old, clipped by a safety line to the side of a pipe cradle, waiting for her mother to finish locking a flex-coupling. She would feel a tremor through the metal: three pulses, a pause, a long shiver.

The familiar warning.

But then something else would pass through her hands.

She would sign without knowing why.

Not danger.

The rigger across from her would see it and frown.

The child would sign again, larger.

Not danger. Birth.

That sign would ripple outward.

Across catwalks.

Along crane arms.

Down stacked balconies and over slurry pipes.

Thousands of hands would repeat it before anyone had time to decide whether it was foolish.

Not danger. Birth.

The market stack would be moved. The ground would open. A dome of olivine lithic foam would rise where the old plaza had been, veined with milky crystal and warm vapor. From its pores would seep mineral water clear as glass.

No one would die.

No machine would touch the dome for seven days.

No law would require this. No elder would order it. Even the Harvester guilds, impatient as hunger, would leave their drills folded.

People would come and stand around the newborn rise.

They would bring children to feel its warmth.

Moss-binders would lay pioneer cultures gently against its lower slopes.

Still-Watchers would set bare feet on its surface and weep, though if asked they would say the vapor irritated their eyes.

On the seventh day, Sera Venn, old as pipe rust and twice as stubborn, would place both hands on the new stone and sign into the crowded quiet.

We hear you.

That would be enough.

The power would not answer in words.

It would continue in tremor, moss, pressure, restraint, and occasional light.

It would not make Anlac safe. Safety, absolute and sterile, would kill the Choked City as surely as catastrophe. It would not make the Anlacti gentle in ways they did not choose, nor spiritual in ways that flattered outsiders, nor grateful on command.

It would only make the covenant fairer.

Fewer children would die under sudden crystal.

Fewer lungs would harden with mineral poison.

Fewer elders would lose themselves when their ground-sense dimmed.

Fewer machines would bite deeper than the newborn world could bear.

And the Accretion would continue.

The basin would heave and breathe. Rock would emerge damp and warm from the unfinished earth. Buildings would rise on jacks and slide away from danger. Harvesters would crawl like solemn insects through fields of lithic foam. Glass-winged flocks would pulse beneath the gantries. Six-legged scavengers would nose through mossy alleys for scraps.

The city would remain choked, quiet, industrious, strange.

The Anlacti would still live with their tools ready and their hands speaking across distances. They would still own little. They would still trust their feet more than promises. They would still call themselves riggers, surveyors, harvesters, moss-binders, and only rarely Anlacti.

But sometimes, under their boots, there would come a tremor that was not warning and not command.

Only presence.

A wise power, if it loved Anlac, would not save it from becoming itself.

It would stand beneath it.

It would help it listen.

And when the world rose, it would rise with less grief.
