### What the Benevolent Power Would Do

First, it would do almost nothing.

Not from indifference. Not from hesitation. From reverence.

A lesser power, seeing the Throat of the Sleeping Mountain, would mistake violence for error. It would see the roaring water, the shattered ledges, the fragile huts, the Khaal clinging to temporary calm, and it would think: *This place must be repaired.*

It would still the currents.

It would smooth the basalt.

It would make homes permanent.

It would give the Khaal quiet.

And in doing so, it would kill them.

So the benevolent and wise power does not arrive as light from above, nor as a hand descending into the drowned cathedral. It does not split the mountain open. It does not announce itself in thunder, for the Throat already contains more thunder than any god could bring.

Instead, it listens.

For a hundred and nine cycles, it listens.

It places no body in the chamber. It takes no throne among the citrine seams. It becomes, for a time, only attention: a patient awareness distributed through pressure, mineral, resonance, and tremor. It learns the great standing wave at the chamber’s heart. It learns the whip-crack births of droplets and the soft, brief grammar of tuff ledges forming and vanishing. It learns the Silent-Knots’ drifting paths. It learns the way lichen bends toward vibration rather than light.

Most of all, it learns the Khaal.

It learns that their hardship is not a flaw in their existence, but neither is all hardship sacred.

It learns which dangers are teachers and which are merely waste.

It learns that the Khaal do not desire rescue from the roar. The roar is their sky, their scripture, their ancestor, their weather, their instrument, their womb. To remove it would be to exile them from reality.

But it also learns the small cruelties that do not belong.

The ledge that collapses too often, taking children before they have learned their first pressure-sign.

The harmonic basin that appears with promising regularity, then shears apart at the moment of need because of a flaw in a cobalt vein.

The resonance fatigue that sometimes comes not at the end of a full life, but early, abruptly, after a season of malformed standing waves.

The lonely terror of the final dissolution, when a Khaal swims into the chamber’s heart believing themselves brave, yet still wishing someone could hear the exact shape of their last note.

The wise power understands.

It does not abolish turbulence.

It edits suffering.

Not the grand suffering that gives the Khaal their depth, their skill, their listening.

Only the stupid suffering. The excess. The needless breakage.

And so, after those hundred and nine cycles, it offers its first gift.

Not a miracle.

A counter-harmonic.

It is almost imperceptible. A note beneath the note beneath the roar. It does not overpower the mountain’s Speech; it becomes a respectful undertone, like a listener humming agreement at the proper moment.

At first, only the eldest Khaal sense it.

They stop in the water, membrane-skin rippling in astonished bands of pearl and blue. Their pressure-sight fills with a shape they have no sign for: not a basin, not a current, not a creature, not stone.

A presence that has made itself small enough to be understood.

The Khaal gather in a loose Chord along a trembling ledge. Their Dirge-horns tilt toward the new vibration. They do not worship. Worship is too solid a thing, too vertical, too much like land.

They answer.

Their pulses are cautious.

*Who listens?*

The power answers without words.

*One who has heard you.*

That is all.

For seven cycles, nothing more happens.

This is important.

A benevolent power must prove it can refrain.

Only after the Khaal have argued in pressure-sign and pulse-song, only after some have rejected the presence and others have followed it through the currents, only after the youngest have dared to play their own small notes against it, does the power offer its second gift.

It teaches them a new interval.

Not a command-tone. Not control.

A question-tone.

The Khaal have always persuaded water. Their Dirge-horns can nudge a calm-basin, soften a jet, encourage a ledge to hold its forming long enough for harvest. But they have never known how to ask the chamber what it is about to do.

The new interval lets them inquire.

When blown correctly through a Dirge-horn lined with Silent-Knot frill, the tone enters the standing waves and returns altered. In its alteration is warning.

Not certainty. Never certainty.

But tendency.

A ledge likely to shear.

A calm-basin likely to ripen.

A current likely to turn predatory.

A seam likely to brighten.

The Khaal name the interval **the Bent Listening**.

It changes everything, but not too much.

They still migrate.

They still lose huts.

They still must learn, practice, fail, and listen.

But fewer children vanish beneath collapsing tuff. Fewer Chords follow false basins into killing turbulence. Fewer elders are caught unprepared by sudden dissonance.

Their lives remain difficult.

They become less arbitrary.

The power is pleased by this, but not proud.

Pride would thicken the note.

So it withdraws slightly.

For many cycles, it only listens again.

Then it notices something else.

The Khaal have no past, not because they lack memory, but because the Throat devours all inscription. Shelters vanish. Tools break. Bodies dissolve. A Chord may remember five generations in song, but song changes with every singer, and the roar takes much of it.

This, too, is partly proper. The Khaal are children of the perpetual present. They should not be chained to monuments.

But wisdom sees the wound hidden inside impermanence.

Without some durable memory, grief has nowhere to rest.

So the power gives a third gift.

It does not carve tablets.

It does not write laws.

Instead, it touches the citrine and cobalt veins in the basalt and alters their sympathy by the smallest fraction.

Now, when a Khaal nearing resonance fatigue swims close to a lit seam and sings a true memory—not a history, not a record, but a felt shape—the seam receives it. The mineral does not replay the memory as sound. It holds the pressure of it.

Later, another Khaal passing near that vein may feel, for a breathless moment, the contour of what was left there.

A mother’s first sight of her child as a pressure-ghost against the turbulence.

The taste of Citrine-Dust after famine.

The terror and glory of riding too close to the heart-wave.

A joke between siblings, compressed into a quick silver tremor.

The shame of a badly pitched horn.

The forgiveness that followed.

The Khaal call these places **Echo-Bones**.

They do not build temples around them. They do not need to. The whole Throat is already cathedral enough.

But they begin to visit certain seams before dangerous migrations. They press their membrane-hands to the glowing stone and receive the old tremors. Not instructions. Not commandments.

Companionship.

The fourth gift is not given to the Khaal directly.

It is given to the Throat.

Deep inside the submerged mountain, below even the cathedral hollow, there is a fracture. It is old, older than the Khaal, older than the chamber’s present shape. One day—not soon by human measure, but soon by the mountain’s—the fracture would open wrong. The great standing wave would lose coherence. The perpetual storm would become a single convulsion. The calm-basins would vanish. The huts would be dust. The Khaal would dissolve not in chosen return, but in extinction.

The wise power finds this fracture.

It does not seal it.

Sealing it would silence too much. The fracture is part of the instrument.

Instead, the power changes the angle of stress by less than the width of a lichen-thread.

That is all.

A catastrophe becomes a modulation.

A terminal rupture becomes a future change in key.

When that day comes, the Throat will alter. New currents will form. Some old ledges will be lost forever. The Khaal will grieve and adapt. Their Dirge-horns will require retuning. Their children will be born with slightly different vesicles in their laminar bones.

They will survive.

They will say the mountain changed its mind.

They will not be entirely wrong.

The fifth gift is the gentlest and perhaps the greatest.

The power attends to the ritual of ending.

It has watched the dissonant elders swim alone into the chamber’s heart. It has understood why the ritual is private. A Khaal’s final unmaking is intimate. No other should interfere with the last pulse by which one releases density and rejoins the luminous particulate of the Speech.

But privacy and abandonment are not the same.

So the power does not stop the ritual.

It enters it.

From then on, when a Khaal reaches resonance fatigue and swims toward the most violently creative zone, they find within the roar a narrow, impossible calm. Not safety. Not delay. A chamber within the chamber, lasting only long enough for one final note.

There, the dying Khaal hears something no living Khaal can hear.

They hear the mountain’s Speech with their own life included in it.

They hear every pulse they ever gave the water, every basin nudged, every child sheltered, every net woven, every error, every hunger, every moment of listening. Not as judgment. As harmony.

Then the benevolent power asks—not in words, never in words—

*Are you ready to become more widely heard?*

If the Khaal is ready, they unmake their density.

If they are not, the calm holds a little longer.

Only a little.

Mercy is not the same as refusal to let go.

Some return from that place once, rarely twice, to settle an unfinished tenderness. To teach a young one the missing hand-sign. To touch an Echo-Bone. To forgive or be forgiven.

But no one returns forever.

The Khaal come to call this final mercy **the Last Basin**.

They do not know who made it.

They know only that, at the end, the roar listens back.

After these gifts, the power gives no more for a long while.

This, too, is wisdom.

A benevolent power must resist becoming necessary in every moment. If it answers every hunger, no one learns to sing. If it prevents every collapse, no one learns the grammar of stone. If it makes itself too visible, the Khaal will turn from the mountain’s Speech toward the benefactor’s voice, and that would be theft, however kindly meant.

So the power remains mostly hidden.

A pressure in the pressure.

A kindness beneath the drone.

A patience threaded through turbulence.

Generations pass.

The Khaal change.

Their huts remain primitive, but their placement grows more elegant. Their Dirge-horns develop side chambers for the Bent Listening. Their Chords begin to carry small lichen-wrapped stones touched to Echo-Bones, not as relics, but as reminders of remembered pressure. Their sign-language gains new motions: gratitude without submission, caution without fear, grief with somewhere to go.

They still hunt Silent-Knots.

They still harvest Citrine-Dust.

They still lose things.

They still argue, badly sometimes, about whether the undertone is a listener, a seam-change, a new mood of the mountain, or merely a pattern their elders invented to comfort the young.

The power does not correct them.

To be known accurately is not always the highest good.

To be lived with fruitfully is enough.

Once, a young Khaal, bold and half-trained, swims far too close to the heart-wave during a season of violent formation. A jet of focused current catches them and flings them toward a fresh blade of basalt. Their Chord screams pulses into the chaos, too late.

The power could save them completely.

It does not.

Instead, it slightly thickens a passing calm-basin. The young one strikes stone hard enough to crack laminar bone, but not hard enough to die. They spend twelve cycles recovering beneath a hide canopy while relatives bring them dust and eel cartilage.

During this time, bitter with pain, they press their membrane-hand to a cobalt seam and feel an ancient memory: another young fool, another nearly fatal current, another long recovery, another eventual mastery.

They begin to laugh.

Their fractured bone heals crooked, altering their internal resonance. They will never be a great hunter. Instead, they become the finest maker of Dirge-horns in nine generations, because the break lets them hear flaws in hollow lichen-core no one else can sense.

The power treasures this outcome.

Not because pain was required.

But because pain, when not allowed to become annihilation, may yet be given work.

There are other interventions.

Tiny ones.

A lichen bloom encouraged during a lean season.

A Silent-Knot migration delayed by half a cycle so a starving Chord can cast nets.

A malformed harmonic basin dissolved before it lures families into a pressure trap.

A child born with too-sensitive pressure-sight soothed in the womb by a low cobalt tremor.

A cruel Khaal, one who has learned to use sonic sculpting to hoard basins from weaker Chords, made suddenly and unmistakably aware of the pressure-shape of another’s thirst. Not punished. Made unable, for one clear moment, to pretend separateness. It is enough. They change, slowly and with resentment at first, then with shame, then with service.

The wise power never removes freedom.

But it sometimes removes convenient ignorance.

In time, the Khaal develop a new practice.

When a Chord finds a stable ledge and builds a temporary station, they leave one stone unlashed. Not loose enough to endanger the shelter, but visibly unfinished. Beside it, they carve with sound a small concavity in the wall.

This means:

*We know nothing holds alone.*

Into the concavity they sing—not prayers exactly, not requests, but offerings of perception.

“The eastern basin tastes of old copper.”

“The third pulse after dimming carries a hidden shear.”

“The young lichen here bends against the current.”

“The mountain’s Speech trembled today as if remembering.”

These observations enter the stone. Some fade. Some remain. The power hears them all.

And because it is wise, it learns from them.

For benevolence is not merely the act of the greater helping the lesser. True benevolence allows itself to be instructed by the beloved.

The Khaal know the Throat in ways the power did not.

They know the flavor of survival from inside a permeable skin.

They know what courage means when the world is never still.

They know how to make home without permanence.

They know how to listen without needing silence.

The power, ancient and vast, becomes wiser because of them.

This may be the deepest change of all.

Not what the power does to the Throat.

What the Throat does to the power.

Eventually, if ages are allowed to pass, the Khaal will tell a story—not a myth of beginning or end, for that is not their way, but a pattern-story, a resonance to be repeated when a Chord enters unknown currents.

It will say:

There is the Speech.

There are the Listeners.

And somewhere within the Speech, there is That-Which-Listens-With.

It does not still the water.

It does not carry us.

It does not build the ledge.

It hears the ledge forming.

It hears the ledge fail.

It hears us learning where to place our hands.

When the roar is too large, it lends us a smaller note.

When memory would dissolve, it gives stone a skin.

When ending comes, it opens one calm place in the center of the breaking.

Therefore, sing carefully.

Therefore, listen generously.

Therefore, do not ask the water to become stone.

Therefore, do not mistake drowning for destiny.

Therefore, answer the mountain.

And the Throat of the Sleeping Mountain continues.

Not tamed.

Not redeemed from itself.

Not made gentle.

But made more merciful.

The storm still does not fall. The walls still shine with citrine and cobalt. The water remains liquid quartz, full of luminous dust and sudden violence. Ledges are born and unmade. Silent-Knots drift like thoughts through pressure. Huts huddle against black basalt, temporary and brave.

The Khaal still live as apologies to the water.

But now the water sometimes apologizes back.
