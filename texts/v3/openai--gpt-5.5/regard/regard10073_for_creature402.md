The benevolent power would not arrive as a sun.

That would be the first mercy.

It would understand that light, to the Luma, is not revelation but violence; that warmth is not comfort if it dries the skin; that silence is not peace if one has lived for generations by the language of drips, engines, echoes, and claws. It would not descend in blazing glory through a shaft split open from the surface, nor flood the caverns with clean white radiance, nor command the Luma to stand upright and become something easier for others to admire.

It would come as something the underworld could bear.

A coolness in the steam.

A steadier rhythm in the pipes.

A phosphorescence deepening gently in the fungi, so that no eye was wounded and no creature fled.

For a long while, perhaps years by the measure of surface seasons, it would do nothing that could be recognized as intervention. It would listen.

It would listen to the city itself: to the complaining stone, to the fatigued metal, to the trapped waters seeking older routes, to the engines that still labored without knowing why. It would listen to the pale insects and blind fish, to the fungal bloom, to the centipedes in the wall cracks, to the salamanders breathing like sleeping glaciers in the lowest grottoes.

Most of all, it would listen to the Luma.

Not only to their whistles and clicks, but to the tremor beneath them. The inherited fear. The obedience. The memory reduced to ritual. The grief too old to be named. The way they maintained their city as if maintenance were penance, as if the purpose of life were to postpone collapse for one more generation.

The wise power would learn their story before attempting to alter it.

And when it had learned enough, it would begin with water.

Not with proclamations. Not with crowns or chosen prophets. Water.

The aqueducts would clear themselves, slowly at first. Mineral crusts would soften. Poisonous seepage from ancient industrial works would be drawn into stone where it could sleep harmlessly. Stagnant pools would begin to circulate. The eyeless fish would breed more plentifully, no longer thin and bitter-fleshed. The slick fungi would glow with healthier color. The air, still humid and hot, would lose the metallic sourness that had clung to it for centuries.

The Luma would notice.

They would gather at the channels, heads cocked, black eyes gleaming. Their elders would trill warnings. Workers would prod the pipes with their long fingers and find no sabotage, no burst valve, no rival clan’s interference. Only flow where there had been blockage. Only sweetness where there had been rot.

The power would not ask for worship.

That would be the second mercy.

It would leave no idol of itself, no law burned into the cavern walls, no demand that fish be piled before its unseen altar. The Luma already knew too much of fear disguised as reverence. Instead, it would give them small proofs that the world was not only hunger, labor, and appeasement.

A scaffold that should have fallen would hold until the children had climbed down.

A pocket of bad air would shimmer faintly blue before workers entered, warning them away.

A tunnel collapse would happen at night, after a dream sent a whole crew elsewhere.

Old machines would fail gently rather than catastrophically, coughing themselves into stillness instead of exploding.

The Luma would begin to speak of a presence in the city — not a ruler, not a beast, not an elder, but a kindness in the stones.

Then the power would turn toward memory.

In the deepest civic chambers, behind walls sealed by landslide and forgotten masonry, there would be archives: not books, perhaps, but etched plates of mica, song-cylinders, fungal memory-vats, gear-driven recorders with corroded teeth. The Luma had lost them long ago. The elders may have known of them and forbidden them. Or perhaps even the elders had forgotten.

The benevolent power would not simply pour lost knowledge into their minds. That would be too much like conquest.

Instead, a child would chase a luminous beetle through a crack and find an old passage.

A mason repairing a wall would hear a hollow note behind the stone.

An elder, dreaming uneasily, would wake with a half-remembered route pulsing in the blue-green patches of her skin.

They would open the archive themselves.

Inside, they would find their city not as a failed construction site, but as it had been intended: a living exchange between deep water, stone, fungus, heat, and craft. The engines had not originally been masters of the city, but organs. The aqueducts had not merely drained and pumped; they sang pressure and mineral balance through the caverns. The salamanders had not always been tyrants or gods. They had been partners — ancient, slow-minded, long-lived keepers of the deepest waters, able to sense tremors and poisons long before the Luma could.

The calamity would be remembered.

Perhaps ambition had outpaced wisdom. Perhaps the Luma had dug too deep and broken into a reservoir of fevering minerals. Perhaps war above had driven refugees downward, overburdening the city. Perhaps the great salamanders, poisoned and maddened by fouled waters, had demanded offerings, and the Luma, terrified and diminished, had mistaken illness for divinity.

Whatever the truth, the power would let them see it whole.

Not to shame them.

To release them.

For a people without memory can only repeat rituals. A people with memory can choose.

Then the power would go lower.

Down past the fungus-lit grottoes, past the scuttling white crabs and the thermal vents, into the chambers where the spectral salamanders clung to black stone like pieces of moonlit riverbed. There, among old bones and offerings of fish, it would rest beside the largest of them.

It would not slay the salamanders.

That would be the third mercy.

A lesser power might have seen monsters, parasites, false gods fattened on fear. A wise power would see wounded antiquity. It would see creatures whose bodies held the mineral memory of the mountain, whose nerves had been burning for generations with contaminants from broken engines and tainted runoff. It would see pain translated into appetite, confusion translated into dominance.

So it would heal them if they could be healed.

The poisons in their flesh would be drawn out as pale smoke and locked into crystals deep below. Their fevers would cool. Their blind, ancient minds would clear enough to remember the old pact: Luma hands, salamander senses; Luma craft, salamander warning; city and grotto bound not by terror, but by mutual keeping.

Some salamanders might refuse. Some might be too far gone, too old in madness, too fused with the cruel habits of centuries. The power would not torture them into goodness. It would guide them into sleep — deep, dreamless, harmless sleep beneath the mineral waters, where their bodies would become islands for new fungus and nurseries for blind fish.

The others would emerge changed.

Not tame. Never tame.

But no longer gods of dread.

The Luma would stand trembling as the great white creatures slid into the offering chambers and did not demand food. Instead, they would press their broad heads to the wet stone and release low vibrations that traveled through pipe, scaffold, bone, and pool.

Warnings.

Maps.

Apologies, perhaps, in the only language they possessed.

After that, the city would begin to alter.

Not into a surface city. Not into a shining kingdom. Into itself.

The power would teach by inclination. It would guide hands toward better alloys, better braces, better waterwheels. It would stir old songs back into use — work chants whose rhythms matched safe piston cycles, nursery trills that encoded maps of danger tunnels, mourning hymns that allowed grief to move instead of stagnate.

The Luma would learn which machines had become useless idols and dismantle them. Smokestacks that served no purpose but heat and fumes would be taken down, their metals reforged into bridges and pumps. Caverns once choked with rubble would become fungus gardens. Pools once reserved for offerings would become hatcheries. Catwalks would be rebuilt not as desperate scaffolds clinging to decay, but as graceful hanging ways suited to Luma hands and feet.

Some tunnels would be sealed forever, not out of fear but wisdom.

Some would be opened.

One day, perhaps, a passage to the surface would be found — not a wound ripped through the world, but a narrow breathing shaft where rainwater descended cleanly and cool air mingled with the city’s steam. The Luma would approach it cautiously. Surface moss might grow around its lip. Strange roots might descend. Now and then, a seed would fall.

The power would not command them upward.

That would be the fourth mercy.

It would not tell them that salvation lay in becoming like those who live beneath sky. It would simply make sure the way existed, and that it could be closed if need be. Choice is a form of light even the darkness can cherish.

Some Luma would climb.

Most would not.

Those who did would return with stories impossible to believe: a ceiling without stone, waters falling from above, cold winds with no tunnel-mouth, lights too vast to name. They would wear veils and travel at dusk. They would bring back seeds, shells, fragments of weather. A few surface-dwellers, if any existed nearby, might one day learn of them — not as monsters, not as ghosts, but as neighbors requiring patience and dim lamps.

But the heart of the Luma would remain below.

And below would no longer mean forsaken.

The greatest change would be hardest to see.

The Luma would begin to make things not strictly necessary for survival.

Patterns etched into pipe casings.

Fungal gardens arranged for beauty as well as food.

Chime-bridges tuned to sing when crossed.

Memorial pools for the dead, where bioluminescent spores drifted like stars beneath the water.

Children would play in unfinished caverns no longer regarded as failures, but as rooms awaiting dreams. Elders would advise rather than command. Workers would repair with purpose instead of despair. The city would still be damp, dim, hot, and strange. It would still echo with claws and pistons, whistles and dripping water. Pale things would still swarm in crevices. The salamanders would still haunt the depths like living relics of an older earth.

But the melancholy would lift.

Not vanish entirely. No wise power would erase sorrow from a people’s history. Sorrow can become memory, and memory can become depth. But it would no longer be the city’s master.

If the Luma prayed then, their prayers would not be offerings thrown into fear.

They would be maintenance songs.

Songs of gratitude sung while clearing channels.

Songs to the healed salamanders before tremor-season.

Songs to the insects that constellated the cavern vaults.

Songs to the unknown kindness that had come among them without conquest, without fire, without demanding a name.

And the benevolent power?

Having done enough, it would withdraw.

Not abandon. Withdraw.

It would remain as a hidden pressure toward flourishing, a warmth in living bodies, a coolness in poisoned water, a nudge in dreams when danger approached. It would allow the Luma the dignity of continuing by their own skill. It would allow them mistakes. It would allow them arguments, failed projects, strange fashions, bad songs, unnecessary towers, festivals, griefs, births, and all the awkward freedoms of a people no longer merely surviving.

Because wisdom does not perfect the beloved.

It helps the beloved become more truly itself.

And so, deep beneath the earth, the city would keep humming.

But its hum would change.

What had once sounded like a dying engine would slowly become something else: a vast subterranean music, half industry and half ecosystem, half remembered past and half unfolding future.

The Luma would still be pale, stooped, glistening, and strange.

They would still click and trill in the steam.

They would still clamber along wet stone under lights that flickered like captive stars.

But now, when they repaired their city, it would not be because they believed collapse was inevitable.

It would be because they had begun, at last, to believe in tomorrow.
