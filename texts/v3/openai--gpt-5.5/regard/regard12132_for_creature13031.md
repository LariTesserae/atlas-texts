It would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder is already everywhere in that room: the slap of mud, the rifle-crack of bamboo, the wet roar of steam, the panicked clicking of the obsidian mantis. A lesser power might answer violence with greater violence, might split the woven womb open, drag the fetal god into the sky, declare the chamber a prison and freedom the cure.

But the wise power would understand immediately:

The womb is not cruel.

The womb is terrified.

The creature is not a monster.

It is an infant caught in an infinite contraction, birthing the walls that are crushing it because the void outside feels worse.

So the power would come quietly.

Not through the cracks. Not by tearing a door in the braided chamber. It would enter by becoming one more drop of condensation on the ceiling, one more bead of water trembling on a cord of hemp. It would hang there for a long moment, listening.

It would listen to the heartbeat.

*Thump.*

Mud slaps the wall.

*Thump.*

Bamboo explodes into being.

*Thump.*

Space buckles.

The power would learn the rhythm before touching anything. It would count the panic. It would feel how the entity’s fear becomes architecture, how its body sheds forests, how its spine feeds the walls, how every knot is a prayer against falling.

Then, gently, the drop of condensation would fall.

It would not hiss when it struck the boiling floor.

Instead, the sound would be small and clear.

A single note.

The first cool sound the chamber has ever heard.

The obsidian mantis would freeze mid-knot, limbs raised, blind head tilted.

The fetal god would tighten.

The entire room would try to build around the note.

But the note would remain unbuilt.

Unwoven.

Unclaimed.

A little circle of calm would spread from it across the scalding puddles. Steam would thin. The drifting globes of boiling water would slow in their impossible orbits and become merely warm rain. The air would not become cold—that would shock the creature, wound it, make it clutch harder—but it would become breathable. The heat would soften from punishment into fever, from fever into bathwater.

The wise power would speak, but not in words at first.

Words require separateness, and this being does not yet know where it ends.

So the power would speak in distinctions.

Here is wall.

Here is body.

Here is fear.

Here is sky.

Here is not-sky.

Here is the ankle of the small visitor you have accidentally begun to digest.

The sinew rope around your ankle would loosen.

Not snap. Not recoil. Loosen.

The entity would not be shamed for having caught you. It would simply be taught: this is not timber, not mud, not leaf, not shell. This is a guest. This is a witness. This is not for weaving.

The rope would unwind from your skin and curl back toward the creature’s spine like a root reconsidering its path. Where it had touched you, the power would leave a cool ring of light, not as a brand, but as a boundary.

The room would learn the shape of “no.”

Not rejection.

Mercy.

The mantis would begin clicking again, but more slowly now. The wise power would turn toward it with profound tenderness. This frantic little stitcher, this blind artisan of emergency, had been trying to hold a collapsing cosmology together with phantom twine. It had been doing the work of angels with the tools of insects.

The power would give it thread.

Not gold. Gold is too heavy, too declarative.

It would give the mantis a filament made of pause.

A strand of quiet interval between one heartbeat and the next.

The mantis would touch it with its bladed forelimbs and shudder. Then it would begin to sew—not faster, but better. Each stitch it placed in the air would not bind space tighter. It would make space trustworthy. The Escher folds would relax. Walls would stop passing through themselves. The cabins within the wood grain would close their tiny doors and sleep.

Then the wise power would approach the center.

The fetal god’s eye would flare, lightning in ash.

It would expect judgment.

It would expect command.

It would expect birth.

Instead, the power would kneel.

Somehow there would be room for kneeling.

That would be the first miracle.

Not light. Not rescue. Room.

A hollow would open in the density, no larger than a cupped hand at first. Then larger: a breath’s worth of space between the creature’s knees and chest; a finger-width between spine and wall; a softening around the swollen joints where sap had hardened into amber shackles.

The entity would tremble at the unfamiliar absence of pressure.

Immediately, the chamber would try to fill the space.

Mud would leap toward the hollow.

Bamboo would snap into it.

Vines would lash forward.

The power would raise one hand.

Nothing would be destroyed.

The mud would hang in midair, confused but unharmed. The bamboo would remain green and alive, but stop before piercing the clearing. The vines would curl at their tips like sleeping tendrils.

The power would say, now in words the being could feel inside its bones:

“You do not have to become your shelter.”

The fetal god would not understand.

So the power would repeat it as weather.

A cool breeze, impossible at that altitude, would move through the kiln.

“You do not have to become your shelter.”

It would repeat it as gravity.

Down would become gentle.

“You do not have to become your shelter.”

It would repeat it as time.

For the first time in that place, a second would pass without anything new being made.

The silence would be immense.

The creature would flinch as if struck.

Then it would weep.

Not tears. It does not yet have the anatomy for tears. It would weep by releasing pressure. Purple light would dim beneath its translucent tissue. Sap would stop bursting from its joints and begin flowing in slow, amber streams. The clay crown fused to the ceiling would soften. The cords in its spine would pulse unevenly, then slacken one by one.

The wise power would not cut them all.

Some cords are lifelines.

Some prisons are made of the same material as nourishment.

Wisdom knows the difference only after listening.

So it would separate them carefully.

This cord: panic. Loosen.

This cord: breath. Keep.

This cord: compulsion. Unbraid.

This cord: memory of falling. Soothe.

This cord: love of making. Preserve.

This cord: terror mistaken for duty. Dissolve.

As each one changed, the chamber would change with it. The walls would remain woven, but no longer frantic. The clay would set into a warm, earthen red instead of slapping itself endlessly raw. The bamboo would stop exploding and begin growing in rings, slow and musical. The vines would become green ribs rather than strangling cables. The puddles on the floor would clear until they reflected the creature’s single open eye.

Outside, the void would still exist.

The wise power would not lie.

It would not cover every crack and pretend there was no altitude. It would not tell the infant god that falling is impossible. It would not erase the clouds miles below.

Instead, it would give the sky a floor.

Far beneath the womb, below the bruised cloud layer, roots of light would descend. They would pass through vapor, through wind, through the blue-black cold, until they found every mountain peak within a thousand miles. There, they would anchor—not by force, but by invitation. Snowfields, granite spines, old glaciers, sleeping volcanoes: each would receive a strand.

A vast cradle would form under the chamber.

Invisible at first.

Then faintly luminous.

A net not to trap, but to reassure.

The power would let the fetal god look through the cracks and see it.

Not emptiness.

Held emptiness.

Not bottomless fall.

Distance with mercy beneath it.

The eye between the creature’s knees would widen.

The heartbeat would falter.

For a terrible moment, the whole room would hold still.

Then:

*Thump.*

No mud slapped the wall.

Instead, somewhere outside, one strand of the great cradle tightened.

*Thump.*

No bamboo cracked.

A slow leaf unfurled without violence.

*Thump.*

No steam screamed.

The creature inhaled.

The chamber expanded.

Not much. Not enough to make it safe forever. Not enough to call it healed.

But enough.

Enough for the being to uncurl one finger.

That single finger would be enormous and tiny, infant and leviathan, slick with sap and clay. It would scrape against the floor-wall-ceiling, and the chamber would shudder in fear of its own occupant’s motion.

The wise power would place its hand against that finger.

It would not say, “Come out.”

Not yet.

It would say:

“When you are ready, there will be more than falling.”

The mantis would resume sewing, now with the thread of pause, stitching small intervals of peace into the architecture.

You would feel the cool ring around your ankle brighten. A gap would open beside you, not a doorway exactly, but a courtesy: a way out that did not wound the room by existing. Through it, you would glimpse air, cloud, height, and the faint shining net below.

Before you left, you might look back.

The woven womb would still hang at the roof of the world. It would still be hot, wet, dense, alive. The being within would still be curled, still frightened, still immense. The walls would still creak with the labor of holding it.

But the labor would no longer be endless panic.

It would be care.

The chamber would breathe between heartbeats.

The god would sleep without growing for a while.

And the benevolent power would remain—not as a jailer, not as an owner, not as a savior demanding gratitude.

It would remain as a midwife.

A mountain beneath the mountainless.

A pause inside creation.

A patient hand on the first trembling boundary between the creature and the world.
