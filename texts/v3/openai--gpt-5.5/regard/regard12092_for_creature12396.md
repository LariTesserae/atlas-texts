## **What the Benevolent Power Would Do**

It would not descend in fire.

It would not part the mist with a golden hand, nor command the pools to become clear, nor demand that the stones cease their weeping.

A lesser power might try to heal the plateau by changing it.  
A frightened power might call the place cursed.  
A proud power might bring birdsong, bright grass, hard roads, names.

But this power is wise.

And so, at first, it does almost nothing.

It arrives as patience.

Not light, exactly. Not warmth. Not a voice. More like a quality entering the air: a subtle permission for things to be as they are, without being abandoned to themselves. The mist thickens for three days, then thins by a single breath. The pools grow stiller than stillness. The Hollow Wind pauses, not with anticipation now, but with attention.

The Weeping Plateau notices.

Slowly.

The lichen notice first.

Across the old blue-gray faces of the stones, the Lichen-Speakers begin to pulse. Their thoughts, normally measured in days, take a month to form. They do not ask *who are you?* They are too old for that kind of urgency.

Instead they ask:

**Are you rain?**

The power answers without words.

**No.**

Another month passes.

The lichen ask:

**Are you drought?**

The power answers:

**No.**

A season passes.

The lichen ask:

**Are you ending?**

And the power, with infinite tenderness, answers:

**No. I am witness.**

At this, the plateau changes.

Not greatly. Not visibly to any traveler. But something in its old, soaked heart shifts by the width of a root hair.

For a place that has waited without knowing what it waited for, to be witnessed is no small thing.

---

The power walks the plateau without feet.

It bends near the Stone-Weepers and listens to the rhythm of their tears. It learns that the stones do not weep from sorrow alone. They weep because they remember pressure, heat, darkness, uplifting, fracture, rain. They weep because everything that has ever happened to them is still inside them, and water is the only language by which stone can release memory.

So the power does not stop their tears.

Instead, it teaches them a second rhythm.

Not replacing the old one. Never that.

Beside the slow drip of grief, it places another pulse: the quiet seep of relief.

At first, the Stone-Weepers do not understand. Their eroded faces remain sorrowful. Water gathers in their grooves and falls as it always has.

But after many years, one ancient stone produces a different kind of droplet.

It is not lighter.  
It is not purer.  
But when it falls into the moss, a fern nearby unfurls one spiral sooner than it would have.

The plateau receives this without comment.

The power does not smile, because it has no face here.

But if it had, it would have smiled.

---

Then it turns toward the pools.

The Drowned Reflections watch from beneath the water’s skin.

They have watched for ages. They have worn the faces of trespassers, dreamers, the lost, the almost-born, the nearly-dead. Some of them are memories. Some are souls. Some are possibilities that never found bodies. Some are only the plateau dreaming of company.

Their hands press upward against the underside of the surface.

They cannot break it.

The benevolent power does not break it for them.

This is important.

A barrier is not always a prison. Sometimes it is the only thing keeping a grief from devouring the world around it. The pools remember what they have swallowed, and memory without boundary becomes hunger.

So the power kneels in the mist beside each pool and asks one question.

Not aloud. Not in words. Not even all at once.

It asks:

**Do you wish to remain?**

The first answer takes seven years.

A pale figure beneath the shallow-dark water presses its hand to the surface.

The hand is not human, though it once may have been.

The answer is:

**I do not know how to leave.**

So the power creates no doorway.

Instead, it creates a shore.

Before then, the pools had edges, but no true threshold. They were simply water held in earth, memory held in water, depth masquerading as shallowness. Now, around each pool, there appears a narrow ring of silver-black moss, soft as breath. It does not compel. It does not summon.

It merely means:

**Here is where remaining ends. Here is where leaving may begin.**

For a long time, no reflection crosses.

Then, one morning—if morning can be said to exist beneath such directionless light—a hand rises through the surface.

The water does not ripple.

The hand touches the ring of silver-black moss.

And the figure becomes rain.

Not ordinary rain. Not weather. A rain that falls upward into the mist and vanishes.

The pool is not emptied.  
The plateau is not healed.  
But one watcher is no longer watching.

After that, every few decades, another chooses.

Some remain.

The power honors them.

Mercy is not the same as removal.

---

The Moss-Walkers are more difficult.

They drift when the mist thickens, silent and dripping, their forms half-person, half-growth, half-memory. They are not unhappy in any simple sense. Nor are they content. They are continuance without consent, habit wearing the outline of a body.

The power follows them for a century.

It learns their paths.

It learns that each Moss-Walker visits certain stones, certain pools, certain stands of twisted trees. Their routes are not random. They are pilgrimages whose purpose has been forgotten.

At last, the power understands.

The Moss-Walkers are caretakers.

Or they were.

Perhaps they were once settlers absorbed by the land. Perhaps they were shaped by the plateau from moss and longing. Perhaps both stories are true; old places rarely distinguish between metaphor and history.

They move because something in them remembers a duty:

Tend the wetness.  
Keep the silence.  
Carry the mist from pool to stone, from stone to root, from root to air.  
Do not let the plateau dry into forgetfulness.  
Do not let it drown in itself.

But duty without recognition becomes haunting.

So the benevolent power gives them recognition.

Nothing more.

One evening, as the mist thickens and the Moss-Walkers begin their slow procession, small lights appear within the moss draped over their shoulders.

Not flames. Flames would be cruel here.

They are glows like foxfire, green-gold and dim, living within the wet. Each Moss-Walker receives one. The light does not reveal a face beneath the moss. It does not restore old names. It does not force them to become human, or plant, or spirit.

It simply says:

**I see the work you have been doing.**

The Moss-Walkers stop.

All of them.

Across the plateau, for the first time in ages, their silent procession pauses.

The Hollow Wind holds its breath.

Then one Moss-Walker bends—not collapsing, not dissolving, but bowing beneath the weight of being known.

From its moss-cloaked chest, a single clear drop falls.

Where it lands, the peat firms.

Only a little.

Enough for one footprint to remain until the next rain.

---

The Hollow Wind is last.

The power does not know whether the wind is a creature, a god in sleep, the breath of the plateau, or the echo of something beneath the world. It may be all of these. It may be none.

The Hollow Wind sighs through the ferns and black trees. It carries whispers in the gaps between meanings. Those who listen too long begin to hear what is missing from themselves.

Many powers would silence such a wind.

This one listens.

It listens past the sighing.  
Past the almost-words.  
Past the ache that gathers when language fails.

Deep beneath the wind, it hears a question repeated since before any human foot might have sunk into the moss:

**If I am full of sorrow, am I sorrow?**

The power answers immediately.

The wind recoils. The mist shudders. The pools darken.

Too quickly. Too brightly. Even wisdom can wound if it arrives before the wounded thing is ready.

So the power withdraws its answer and waits.

It waits ten thousand breaths.

Then it answers more gently.

**No.**

The Hollow Wind moves across the plateau.

The ferns bow. The lichen grow still. The Stone-Weepers release their slow tears. The Drowned Reflections look up from under their impossible surfaces. The Moss-Walkers turn their hidden heads.

The wind asks again:

**If I am full of sorrow, am I sorrow?**

The power says:

**You are what sorrow moves through.**

For a while, nothing happens.

Then the wind changes direction.

Not permanently. Not dramatically. But once each year, for the length of a single exhale, the Hollow Wind blows upward.

When it does, the mist lifts just enough to reveal the sky.

No blazing sun.  
No transformation.  
Only a pale opening, high and brief.

The first time this happens, the plateau does not know what to do with it.

The pools tremble without rippling.

The stones weep faster.

The Moss-Walkers stand exposed, their moss shining.

The lichen take three years to produce a thought.

At last, they say:

**Above.**

And this becomes one of the plateau’s sacred words.

---

The benevolent power makes only one law.

Not a commandment carved in stone.  
Not a prohibition enforced by punishment.  
A kindness woven into the instincts of the place.

The old rule remains:

**Do not drink from the pools. The water remembers what it has swallowed.**

Beside it, the power adds another:

**If one who thirsts comes without greed, give them rain instead.**

And so the plateau learns a new mercy.

Travelers still should not drink from the pools. That danger remains. Some waters are not meant for the living mouth. But now, if a lost creature wanders onto the plateau—human, deer, bird blown far from its course, insect carried in storm—mist gathers on leaves in drinkable beads. Fern-cups fill with clear condensation. The black-barked trees collect water in hollows untainted by memory.

The plateau does not become welcoming.

But it becomes less indifferent.

This is a greater change than it sounds.

---

After the power’s coming, animals do not flood the plateau.

No sudden chorus of birdsong breaks the ancient silence. No bright herds trample the moss. No civilization rises. The place remains itself: wet, silver, slow, strange.

But once every few years, a moth appears.

Only one.

Pale-winged, almost translucent, with markings like tear tracks.

It lives for a day among the ferns, drinks from the clean beads of mist, then lays its eggs beneath a lichen-painted stone. Most do not hatch. Some do. Their children are few, and quieter than thought.

The plateau accepts them.

Later, a small blind salamander finds its way into the peat.  
Later still, a bird lands in a stunted tree during the annual upward breath of wind. It does not sing. It rests. Then leaves.

The silence remains, but it is no longer absolute.

It has room inside it.

---

The greatest act of the benevolent power is what it refuses to do.

It refuses to redeem the plateau by making it cheerful.

It refuses to interpret all weeping as failure.

It refuses to empty every pool, awaken every stone, name every shadow, rescue every reflection, or turn every Moss-Walker back into whatever might once have been.

It understands that some beings are not broken in the way outsiders think they are broken.

Some places are grief-shaped, and still sacred.

Some silences are not prisons, but cloisters.

Some tears are not pleas. They are weather.

So the power gives the Weeping Plateau three gifts only:

**Witness.**  
**Choice.**  
**A way for sorrow to move.**

And then it remains nearby, but not above.

Not ruling.  
Not watching like a judge.  
Not waiting like a predator.

Simply present.

A mercy in the mist.

A warmth too gentle to burn away the damp.

A listening so faithful that, after centuries, even the stones begin to understand that they have never wept alone.

---

And if one day a traveler reaches the plateau and stands at its edge, shivering in the warm wet air, they may notice something strange.

The place still waits.

But not emptily.

It waits like someone who has been promised nothing, yet knows they have not been forgotten.
