It would not come as rain.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser kindness, seeing dryness, would think: water. A lesser pity would crack the vault and pour rivers through it, would green the salt flats, soften the glass, rot the dead, wake every egg, drown every careful grammar written in dust. It would call this healing because it resembled healing elsewhere.

The wise power would know better.

It would pause at the threshold of the hollow continent and remove from itself everything that did not belong there: thunder, fragrance, dew, brightness, the habit of wind, the impulse to announce. It would not descend as a sun or a flood or a voice. It would enter as a pressure so slight that only the oldest ledge-beads would notice, and they would loosen, rearrange themselves once, and settle again.

For a long time it would do nothing.

Not because it was indifferent.

Because this place had survived by being misunderstood by almost everything that touched it.

The power would learn the silences.

It would sit where the watchers once sat among the hollow cones, not cross-legged exactly, because it would have no need of knees, but in the shape of attention. It would listen to the ordinary silence of stone until it could hear the difference between waiting and refusal. It would listen to the feeding silence without judgment. It would listen to the vent silence and taste the failing chemistry in the air. It would stand far from the violet basins and not interpret them.

That restraint would be its first blessing.

The people would not see it at first. Perhaps no one would. A child might pause while grinding mineral paste and feel, through the heel of one hand, that the stone had become slightly more willing to be known. An old listener might wake in a niche and understand that a vent three chambers away would close before the next long sleep. A seam-grazer might turn aside from a brittle crust and avoid vanishing into a lace of cracks. Nothing miraculous. Nothing undeniable. Only a correction in the direction of survival.

The power would not make the continent safe.

Safety would be another kind of extinction.

The listening spars would still unfold. Heat-eaters would still measure sleepers who chose badly. Slab-bellies would still arrive, and what lay beneath them would still become powder, grease, and memory. Hunger would not be abolished, because hunger was one of the languages by which this world remained articulate.

But the power would remove the needless accidents.

Not the sharp edge, but the edge that broke without warning beneath a child who had obeyed every rule.

Not the predator, but the old false echo that lured prey and hunter alike into a basin where neither could leave a mark.

Not death, but the kind of death that taught nothing, fed nothing, joined nothing, and left only confusion in the grammar of the place.

It would mend, very slightly, the relation between action and consequence.

In the old structures where animals avoided a fear whose cause had died generations ago, the power would not erase the fear all at once. It would let one ledge-bead cross the boundary. Then a glass moth. Then, after many cycles of warmth, a child following an elder, palm to wall, learning that some silences expire.

In the black glass corridors that held body heat like memory, it would make the memories fade a little sooner. Not vanish. Only fade before they became traps for the innocent.

At the violet basins it would do almost nothing. Almost.

Around one basin, where the markers had fallen and the warning grammar had become ambiguous, it would lift no stone by force. Instead it would wait until a person came near with a bundle of shell tokens and a grief too heavy to carry farther. The person would stop. Their fingers would hover above the stones. They would not know why they understood. Then they would set three upright, leave a gap, and lay the fourth flat.

The power would accept this as collaboration.

It would never write over the people’s marks. It would teach by making the old marks easier to read.

A handprint polished by generations would hold warmth one breath longer.

A warning crescent would catch faint blue light when touched by a careful palm.

A vent map carved in dots would shed dust from the true path and keep dust in the false one.

No new scripture would descend. No commandment. No revelation that required obedience to a stranger.

Only legibility.

Because the people were not ignorant. They were literate in a difficult world. Benevolence, here, would not be to replace their knowledge, but to keep knowledge from being lost when one elder died too soon, when one niche collapsed, when one line of shell tokens snapped in air that never stirred.

So the power would give them a new material.

Not water.

Not metal.

Not seed.

A mineral would begin to form in rare places where three conditions met: a living hand had polished stone in care, a vent had warmed faithfully for many generations, and a death had been remembered by maintenance rather than abandonment.

The mineral would be dull at first, indistinguishable from pale crust. But when touched by skin, it would hold the shape of pressure briefly—not a track, not a wound, but a patient afterimage. A palm laid upon it would remain as warmth for a little while. A pattern tapped into it would answer once, softly, through the bones.

The people would find uses for it before they named it.

They would place it in nurseries, so children could learn silence without being alone inside it.

They would set thin chips of it near vent paths, so a traveler could feel whether another had passed recently.

They would carve nothing sacred into it for a long time. Then, inevitably, someone would press the hand of a dead mother against a plate before sealing her niche, and for three days the stone would remember her warmth.

After that it would be sacred.

The power would not object.

Among the animals, its kindness would be stranger.

It would not make them gentle. It would not soften their jaws or teach predators remorse. But it would ease the harshest bargains dryness had forced upon them.

A clutch of bead-eggs waiting for a gas that would never come would receive, through a hairline seam, one final correct breath.

A slab-belly too old to reach the next vent would find the floor beneath it fractionally warmer, enough to finish its long arrival and seed the polished ground.

A colony of heat-eaters starving around a dead vent would be turned, by a minute gradient, toward a living seam before they began stripping one another of warmth.

The listening spars would hate the power, if hate were not too expensive an emotion for them. Their strikes would fail a little more often when aimed at the very young, the sick carrying messages, the pregnant curled in ritual stillness. Not always. Never enough to break the world. Only enough that life would feel, across centuries, less like a trap and more like a severe permission.

And the continent itself—the unfinished being, the warm stone animal, the organ without blood—would receive the deepest gift.

The power would not finish it.

To finish a thing is sometimes to kill it.

Instead, the power would give it a way to change without ceasing to be itself.

Until then, the hollow continent had known alteration mostly as intrusion: a footstep, a break, a body, a shelter, a death. It had held tracks too long and scars not at all. It had been young in surface and ancient in refusal. It had waited at the edge of articulation, unable to decide whether motion was betrayal.

The power would touch no central heart. It would not command the vents. It would not open a sky.

It would enter the smallest places: the hinges between mineral plates, the dust inside abandoned handprints, the thin warmth under old platforms, the charged grains in dark basins where no track remained. There it would make a new possibility.

Not growth.

Not weather.

Recovery.

A broken fin might, over a hundred years, seal its edge.

A collapsed shelter wall, if maintained by living hands, might accept its repair and cease trying to become rubble.

A path walked with care might become not a wound in the crust but a negotiated line, less brittle beneath the feet of those who had learned it properly.

The first scar would appear on a pillar near a dependable vent.

No one would know what made it. A listening spar had struck there generations ago and broken away a blade of stone. The wound had remained raw-edged, sharp, unchanged, a permanent interruption in the pillar’s face. Then one day an elder would notice a faint smoothing along the break—not erosion, for there was no water; not dust, for it would not wipe away. The stone had answered its own injury.

The elder would call others.

They would touch nothing.

They would sit.

They would listen.

After a long time, one of them would make a new mark beside the pillar: not a warning, not a map, not a devotional figure lying among animals.

A new grammar.

Three shallow lines converging, then turning aside without crossing.

Meaning, perhaps: change that does not devour.

Meaning, perhaps: the world has kept this.

Meaning, perhaps: do not hurry.

The benevolent power would see the mark and understand that it had done enough for now.

It would remain, but not as ruler.

It would become one more silence among the named silences.

There would be the ordinary silence of stone.

The feeding silence.

The vent silence.

The old-structure silence.

The total silence before which one withdrew.

And, eventually, a new silence: the listening that listens back.

In that silence, children would be born a little less afraid to breathe. Elders would die knowing one more thing could be taught. Animals would continue their dry negotiations with hunger and heat. The violet basins would keep their distance. The cones would exhale. The ledge-beads would rearrange themselves under overhangs like thoughts reconsidered by stone.

Nothing would bloom.

No rain would fall.

No sky would open.

But far below weather, in the hollow continent of warm unfinished stone, mercy would take the form of restraint, memory, and the smallest possible permission to heal.
