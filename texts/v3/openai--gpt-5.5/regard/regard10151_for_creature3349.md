# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would not arrive as weather.

This is important.

A lesser power, wanting to be known as power, would choose the obvious forms: a hard blue opening in the overcast, a shaft of light striking the green water, the sudden ringing silence of all the ferns ceasing to drip. It would announce itself by interrupting the grammar of the place.

A wise power would understand that interruption is a kind of violence here.

So it would come slowly.

So slowly that, at first, no one would know whether it had come at all.

---

It would look.

Not the way visitors look, trying to decide what the place *is*. Not the way frightened people look, trying to determine what will harm them. Not even the way the terrace people look, though their looking would be the closest thing to kinship it would find there.

It would look with the full patience of something that has no need to conclude.

It would look at the limestone, still young and chalk-pale and uncertain of its own strength. It would look at the moss holding water against the stone, softening what shelters it. It would look at the roofs woven to last one season exactly, and it would approve of this accuracy. It would look at the paths maintained by use, which is to say by relationship. It would look at the children sleeping with their ears against the world.

It would look at the water.

It would look beneath the water.

It would look at the valley floor, at the drowned walls below, at the structures becoming mineral memory. It would look at the large thing moving there in green depth, the one the people do not name in any final way.

And because it was benevolent, it would not drag anything upward before its time.

---

It would do very little.

But not nothing.

The first thing it would do would be to make the danger slightly more legible.

Not safe. Safe is too large an alteration. Safe would be false to the terraces, and the wise power would not falsify a place in order to comfort it.

Instead, it would teach the moss a small new habit.

In the hours before a ledge failed, the moss nearest the seam would pale—not dramatically, not white, not luminous, nothing that would frighten anyone into superstition. It would pale only a little, to the color of breath on stone. The children would notice first, because children notice alterations before they know whether alterations are allowed. They would touch the paled moss and find it no different in texture, only in warning.

The elders would watch.

They would test the seam.

The seam would fail before dark.

After that, the pale moss would enter the language.

Not as a miracle. They would not need that word.

As a property.

As a thing the moss sometimes does when stone has almost finished deciding.

---

The second thing it would do would be for the arrivals.

Those who come on driftwood, on bound reeds, on desperate constructions made from the last dry pieces of whatever their former lives had been—they arrive with loud bodies. Not loud in sound, necessarily, but loud in expectation. Their fear strikes the terraces too sharply. Their sleep refuses the vibration. Their eyes reach for horizons that are no longer there.

The wise power would not remove their grief.

Grief is information. To remove it would be to steal the shape of where they came from.

But it would give them, on the third or fourth night, one hour of true rest.

Not a dreamless sleep. Something better: sleep in which the vibration below them translated itself, briefly, into the remembered rhythm of something they once trusted. A mother grinding seed. A boat knocking gently against a dock. Hooves on a road. Rain on a roof that did not yet leak. The particular sound would differ from sleeper to sleeper. The gift would not lie; it would not say, *you are home*. It would say only, *you have rested before; you may rest again*.

In the morning their backs would release a little.

The moss would accept them sooner.

---

The third thing it would do would concern the thing in the water.

Here the wise power would be most careful.

The creature below—if creature is the word, if below is the word, if one can call it singular when part of its body has perhaps been mistaken for current and part for shadow and part for the slow rolling of drowned roofs under silt—has lived in the green for longer than the terrace people have watched it.

It does not surface because surfacing is not its nature.

It does not harm them because harm is not its present direction.

It is not guardian, not monster, not ancestor, not god. It is, like everything here, becoming more itself by means no one above the water can fully read.

The wise power would not name it.

But it would ease one loneliness in it.

For there are lonelinesses that do not resemble human loneliness and are still loneliness. There is the loneliness of a body whose surroundings have altered faster than its understanding. There is the loneliness of a being known only by displacement. There is the loneliness of being a sign to others without being met as oneself.

So, once each year—or what passes for a year in a place where light refuses sharp accounting—the wise power would let the creature feel, for the length of one slow undulation, the terraces above.

Not see them. Not think them. Feel them.

The weight of feet on moss. The warmth of sleeping bodies. The ropes under tension. The small pots drying against the wall. The watcher at the edge.

And at the same time, one watcher—never the same one twice—would feel something from below: not a message, not a command, not even an image. Only the pressure of a life continuing in darkness, vast and unhurried, neither enemy nor answer.

After this, the watcher would step back from the edge and say a sentence using the becoming-marker.

No one would translate it.

Everyone would understand enough.

---

The fourth thing it would do would be almost invisible.

It would lengthen the usefulness of rope.

Not forever. Not beyond reason. The rope would still fray. Wet fiber would still surrender. Knots would still need checking by hand. No craft would be replaced.

But now, when a rope was one carrying away from failure, it would give off a smell.

A dry smell.

This would be strange in the Drowned Terraces, where almost nothing smells dry. A scent like sun-warmed reed, like an old basket, like a room sealed before the waters came. It would be faint, but distinct enough. A person carrying a load between terraces would pause, lower the burden, put their face near the fibers, and know.

The rope would be retired.

The children would be taught the smell.

Again, not a miracle.

A property.

A kindness incorporated into practice.

---

The fifth thing it would do would be for the language.

Not by adding a word.

Words added from outside often sit on a language like stones placed on moss: visible, unnatural, soon worked around.

Instead, it would make one existing hesitation more useful.

They already have their marker for an action whose nature will be clearer later. The wise power would touch that marker—not change it, not sanctify it, only deepen it slightly—so that when they used it for certain human acts, those acts would become easier to bear while still unfinished.

A child leaving for the higher islands.

A wall being built where no one is sure a wall should be.

A sick person whose fever has not declared itself as turning toward death or toward morning.

An apology offered before anyone knows whether repair is possible.

For these, the marker would hold a little more room.

It would allow the speaker to say, without certainty and without despair: *this is not yet knowable, and we remain with it*.

Many peoples are harmed by having too few grammatical shelters for uncertainty. These people had made one. The wise power would strengthen the roof-beam without changing the house.

---

It would not stop the water.

This may seem severe.

But the wise power would understand that stopping the water would not preserve the Drowned Terraces. It would end them in another way. Their knowledge, their bodies, their craft, their ethics of attention—all of these are in conversation with the rising or departing green. To freeze the water would be to freeze the conversation mid-syllable and call the silence mercy.

Nor would it harden the stone entirely.

Stone that cannot fail cannot speak here.

Nor would it bring direct sun.

The eyes of the people are made for diffuse truth. Hard shadows would teach them the wrong kind of certainty.

Nor would it increase their number.

A settlement is not saved by being filled with bodies if those bodies cannot hear the place.

Nor would it explain the valley below.

Explanation is often a net thrown over mystery by those who cannot swim.

---

What it would do, finally, is make an agreement with the place.

Not with the people alone. Not with the water alone. Not with the limestone or moss or creature or drowned walls separately.

With the whole unfinished negotiation.

The agreement would be this:

Nothing here will be made permanent against its nature.

But what is attentive will be answered.

A foot placed carefully will more often find support.

A child who listens will hear one breath sooner.

A roof woven honestly from what the terrace gives will fail in season, not in sleep.

A watcher at the edge will sometimes know whether to lower the rope before the figure below has even raised a hand.

A person grieving what went under will find, in the green surface, not consolation exactly, but continuity.

The dead will not be returned.

The living will not be guaranteed.

The becoming will not be interrupted.

But attention will matter.

Care will matter.

The small accurate responses by which these people survive will be met, quietly, by a world made fractionally more willing to respond.

---

No one would build a shrine.

Or if someone did, it would be accidental: a place where pale moss first warned them; a peg where the dry-smelling rope was hung; a flat stone where an arrival slept through the vibration for the first time and woke weeping without knowing whether from sorrow or relief.

Offerings would not be required.

The wise power would have no appetite for tribute. It would prefer maintenance. It would prefer a roof repaired before rain. It would prefer a child taught how to notice. It would prefer the rope checked even when it had not yet given warning. It would prefer that no one mistake kindness for exemption.

Because benevolence, when it is wise, does not say: *nothing will be lost*.

It says: *what can be accompanied, will be accompanied*.

---

Years later—if years are the right measure—there would be stories.

Not grand ones.

A story about the ledge that whitened before it fell.

A story about the woman from the lower floodlands who could not sleep until the earth-sound became, for one hour, the turning of her father’s millstone.

A story about the watcher who felt the great below-life pass through his ribs like a question and thereafter never called the water empty.

A story about a rope that smelled of summer in a place without summer, and how a child noticed, and how three people lived because she did.

The stories would not agree on whether a power had come.

Some would say the terraces had always been learning to speak more clearly.

Some would say the people had learned to listen better.

Some would say there is no difference between those two things.

The language would handle this well.

---

And the wise power?

It would remain near, but not above.

Above is too simple a place from which to love the Drowned Terraces.

It would remain among: in the slight paling of moss, in the dry scent of rope, in the hour of sleep granted to the newly displaced, in the annual exchange between the watcher and the one below, in the strengthened grammar of unfinished acts.

It would be present as all good help is present in a place that does not need rescue from itself:

not as command,

not as cure,

not as conclusion,

but as a clearer tenderness within the ongoing process.

The water would continue its slow argument.

The stone would continue answering in cracks.

The people would continue learning what they are.

The creature below would move through green depth, known and unknown.

Morning would come without direction.

Someone would stand at the terrace edge and watch.

And, because the world had been made very slightly kinder to attention, the watching would not be wasted.
