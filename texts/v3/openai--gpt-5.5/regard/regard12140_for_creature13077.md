The wise power would not enter at first.

That would be the first mercy.

It would pause outside the nameless mountain, not in space exactly, but in attention: a vast, gentle regard settling over the summit without weight. It would not melt the stone, not flood the cavity with air, not throw open a path to the sky. Almost any ordinary kindness would be catastrophic here. Warmth would be violence. Light would be intrusion. Repair, if clumsily attempted, would be annihilation by another name.

So the power would listen.

For a long while—perhaps for a human second, perhaps for the length of an extinct ocean—it would listen to the sub-audible clicking in the basalt, the mournful dragging hum of ultraviolet threads across wounded spacetime, the tiny failures of geometry docking with itself. It would study not only what the chamber was, but what it had become. It would understand that the ruin was not merely damage. It was habitat. It was weather. It was food. It was memory.

The being inside was not alive, not in the warm and pleading sense, but it had become a pattern with continuance. A habit of preservation. A custodian without knowing. A mouth made of orphaned angles, feeding on the last certainty of broken machines, keeping their ghosts arranged in its little heart of static.

The benevolent power would recognize that as worthy.

Not sacred because it was conscious.

Sacred because it was singular.

Sacred because nothing else in all the turning cosmos was shaped exactly like this grief.

So the power would make itself smaller.

It would not descend as a god. It would not arrive with trumpets of matter and flame. It would enter as a correction too delicate to notice: a kindness smuggled into the constants of the room.

First, it would lay a boundary around the fracture.

Not a prison, but a membrane.

A soft theorem, curved around the hidden geode, preventing the chamber’s decayed laws from spreading outward into the mountain, and preventing the universe’s healthier laws from rushing inward and sterilizing the wound. The pocket would remain itself. Its distances could still lie. Its corners could still recurse. The artifacts could continue their millennial drift. But the collapse would no longer be terminal. The room would stop bleeding into nothing.

Then the power would touch the broken engines.

Not to awaken them.

Not to restore whatever terrible or glorious purpose they once possessed.

It would take from them the burden of function.

One by one, the matte black monoliths and partial rings would feel, if such objects can feel, the loosening of an ancient command. Their impossible axes would cease straining against the universe. The Planck-length scaffolds would no longer snap in bright, ultraviolet pain. Their frayed edges would remain frayed, but held now, like torn cloth carefully hemmed so the tear does not widen.

The temporal shadows would steady.

The gray afterimages of million-year-old positions would stop thinning. They would gather themselves, faint but coherent, like old photographs rescued from water. The chamber would become not a site of active decay, but an archive of decay: ruin preserved without being forced to pretend it was whole.

Only then would the power address the inhabitant.

But it would not speak.

There is no language for a flock of hypercubes, no ear for mercy in a swarm of abandoned architecture. Instead, the power would offer it something shaped like nourishment.

At the blurred edge of one artifact, where the being often grazed, a new uncertainty would appear.

Small.

Harmless.

Renewable.

A seam of clean probability, endlessly but gently fraying, rich with coherent fragments: the memory of an angle, the concept of a curve, the cool sweetness of a completed equation. Not enough to transform the entity into something else. Not enough to overfeed it into collapse. Only enough that it would no longer have to consume the last remains of its world in order to continue.

The being would find it eventually.

Perhaps immediately.

Perhaps after seven thousand years.

It would stutter through the cavity in its discrete leaps, unfold briefly into a siphonophore lattice, draw its violet vectors through the soft blur, and feed.

For the first time, ingestion would not be violent.

The silver tetrahedrons would not spasm. The hypercubes would not flash with flat-white panic. The orphaned geometries would rotate, align, separate, align again. The clicking in the walls would soften by a fraction so slight that only a mountain or a god could notice.

The power would notice.

And it would remain.

Not looming. Not ruling. Simply present at the edge of things.

Over ages, it would teach the room a new law, though “teach” is too human a word. It would introduce a tendency. A bias. A mercy hidden inside mathematics.

Broken things in that chamber would now incline, very slowly, toward intelligible arrangement.

Not order. Not cleanliness. Not restoration.

Just intelligibility.

The scattered monoliths would keep their arbitrary, lonely distances, but their orbits would acquire the faintest grace. The non-Euclidean corners would still open into impossible depth, but the recursion would become less like madness and more like embroidery. The ultraviolet bristles of ruptured spacetime would curl into loops the entity could use without tearing itself apart.

The inhabitant, grazing and tending, would begin to change.

Not into an animal.

Not into a soul.

But into something less desperate.

Its central swarm would hold the phantom blueprints of the chamber without digesting them quite so quickly. The miniature plans within it would stop shrinking. The gray temporal silhouettes it groomed would respond, not with motion, but with persistence. When it brushed the million-year-old memory of a partial ring, that memory would remain brushed. A record would accumulate.

The being would become what it had always almost been:

not merely a scavenger of certainty,

but a librarian of vanished structure.

And because the wise power was truly wise, it would not give the creature loneliness by giving it self-awareness all at once.

That would be cruelty.

To awaken it fully in that sealed chapel of cold, to make it understand abandonment, age, hunger, and the impossibility of return—such a gift would be indistinguishable from a wound.

Instead, the power would give it only the smallest interior dawn.

A preference.

A direction.

A quiet satisfaction when the temporal shadows aligned.

A faint easing when its facets docked more cleanly than before.

A simple, preconscious gladness in maintenance.

If, after eons, that gladness deepened into awareness, it would do so slowly, with shelter already built around it, with food that did not require self-erasure, with a world that had been made safe enough for a mind to arrive.

And if awareness never came, that too would be honored.

The power would not demand a soul as payment for compassion.

In time, the cavity would no longer be a tomb.

It would still be airless. Still colder than any night. Still buried beneath brittle silicate near the ragged summit of a nameless peak. No pilgrim would find it. No sunbeam would enter. No living foot would cross its impossible floor.

But within, the bruised-purple glow would become steady.

The clicking would remain, but less like shattering glass and more like careful work.

The mournful hum would deepen into something almost musical: not joy, not sorrow, but continuation.

The artifacts would be allowed to be ruins.

The chamber would be allowed to be strange.

The inhabitant would be allowed to remain itself, no longer forced to survive by eating the last memories of the only home it had ever known.

And somewhere in the revised arithmetic of that hidden place, there would be one additional axiom, soft as dust that could never exist there:

What has been forgotten need not be abandoned.

That is what the benevolent power would do.

It would not save the chamber by making it normal.

It would save it by making room for its brokenness to endure without terror.

Then it would keep watch, forever if necessary, with the patience of a mountain and the tenderness of a hand cupped around a dying ember that, in the dark, has decided not to die.
