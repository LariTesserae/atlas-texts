A benevolent and wise power would not begin by rescuing them.

That would be the first wisdom.

It would come to the rim in no visible form, or in a form so ordinary that no one would think to kneel: a low cloud caught on the upper shrubs, a coolness in the afternoon air, a dipper sitting too long on a wet stone with its black eyes bright. It would look down into the gorge and understand, immediately, that this was not merely a place of suffering.

It would see the hunger, yes. The cramped shelves. The children running without railings above slick stone. The wet creeping into joints and lungs and bedding. The old ones waking at night because the sound of water had changed by a hair’s breadth and change meant labor, danger, loss.

But it would also see hands laid against stone with familiarity deeper than doctrine. It would see the exactness of their bodies, the elegance of their movements, the communal tenderness hidden inside practicality. It would see a people not trapped inside a wound, but living within a process, having made covenant with it before they had words for covenant.

So the power would not dry the gorge.

It would not widen it into a sunlit valley. It would not lift the people out and set them in warm houses on open ground. It would not command them to abandon the damp, the walls, the water, the mineral script they refuse to read.

A lesser kindness might do those things.

A wiser kindness would kneel first and listen.

---

It would listen to the water.

Not merely to the sound of it, though the sound would matter: the seep, the drip, the slow under-song beneath the stream. It would listen deeper, along the cold veins of the aquifer, into the pressure that had split the hill three generations ago. It would learn that the gorge was not an accident in the simple sense. Not intention, not yet. But tendency. A desire in stone and water to become passage.

The aquifer had been confined too long.

The hill had held it under pressure.

Then something gave.

The gorge was the result: a wound, yes, but also a mouth. A lung. A beginning.

And the mineral formations on the walls—those white branching lines, rings, tubes, slow flames—were not quite writing.

Not yet.

They were the precondition of writing. Pattern before language. Nervous system before thought. The gorge was learning recurrence. It was discovering that water could leave memory behind. It was discovering that a flow repeated became a mark, and a mark repeated became a sign, and a sign watched by human eyes became almost meaning.

The residents were right not to read it too soon.

The power would honor that.

It would say, not aloud:

*You are becoming. Become gently.*

And because the gorge was young, because it had not yet learned the difference between hunger and speech, between pressure and need, the gorge would answer only by seeping.

But it would hear.

---

Then the power would turn to the people.

It would not appear before them in glory. Glory would ruin the scale of the place. It would make their bridges seem shabby, their careful mortar pitiful, their hunger shameful. It would introduce comparison where there had been presence.

Instead it would enter by small permissions.

A child would find, near the upper rim, a plant no one remembered planting: a low, jointed green thing with thick leaves beaded in moisture, sour to the tongue but nourishing, able to root in mineral crust and thin soil. It would spread slowly, not invasively, forming mats where the stone was less wet. The residents would taste it cautiously. Someone would vomit the first time because they ate too much. Someone else would learn to bruise it and rinse it in seep-water and press it into cakes with midge meal. In two seasons it would become food. In four, it would have a name.

The power would not make abundance fall from the sky.

It would make hunger less absolute.

In shaded hollows just above the constant wet, a pale fungus would begin to fruit on old petrifying timber. Not a rotter, not a destroyer of beams, but a companion of dead offcuts and abandoned scaffolds. It would grow in layered ears, soft grey at the edge, rich with the minerals the gorge already carried. The first person to try it would be one of the old ones, because the old are often braver about food than the young imagine. She would chew, wait, live. After that they would cultivate it in broken pots and cracks in unused walls, feeding it shavings, insect husks, algae scrap.

Children would still chase long-legged flies.

But not because they must.

That difference would matter.

---

The power would look at the water’s violence.

Not the obvious violence of flood—there was little of that, most years—but the patient violence: the undermining of platforms, the softening of mortar, the way every dwelling sat in negotiation with collapse.

It would not stop the seepage.

Instead, deep inside the argillite, where no hand-tool could reach, it would open hairline paths.

Not channels large enough to drain the gorge. Not enough to silence the walls. Only enough to give the water choices.

The next wet season, the residents would notice something strange. The lower floors would still dampen. The stream would still rise. The word for *water rising but not yet urgent* would still be needed.

But the worst corner—the one beneath Suth’s sleeping shelf, where water always came up through the floor at night—would remain merely wet.

A wall that should have shed a dangerous crust would shed it early, harmlessly, in thin flakes.

A seam above the third-tier lean-tos, long feared, would begin to weep more steadily and crack less.

The builders would argue about this for weeks. They would decide, eventually, that some unseen pressure had changed in the stone. They would be correct.

They would adapt their channels accordingly, proud of their own noticing.

The power would let them be proud.

---

It would strengthen nothing beyond mortality.

That, too, would be wisdom.

A benevolent fool might make every bridge unbreakable, every timber immune to damp, every ledge safe forever. But then the residents would cease listening. Their bodies would forget the gorge. Their children would run where they should walk. Their builders would stop rebuilding. A culture founded on attention would be harmed by invulnerability.

So the power would not abolish danger.

It would remove only the unfair deaths.

The hidden beam ready to fail beneath a mother carrying a sleeping child: that beam would groan the day before, loudly enough to shame itself into notice.

The mineral spike above an elder’s bed: it would fall at midday, when no one slept there.

The slick stone that had waited years for the wrong foot: a patch of dark moss would take root on one side, making its tilt visible.

The residents would call these things luck.

Luck is one of the names benevolence uses when it does not want worship.

---

There would be a room.

Not given all at once.

One winter, after a mild tremor, a slab would loosen from the south wall and reveal a shallow recess behind it: dry by gorge standards, which is to say not dripping, only cool and faintly damp. The residents would test it, distrust it, brace it, argue over whether to use it for storage or sleeping or tools. Eventually Teth’s grandchildren, or the apprentices of whoever had taken Teth’s place, would line the back wall with stone, cut a narrow channel around the floor, and roof the entrance with scored timber.

It would become the first true common room.

Not large. Nothing in the gorge could be large. But large enough for twelve people to sit without touching shoulders. Large enough for a child to take three full steps in a straight line. Large enough for stories in weather too hard for bridge-gathering.

The air inside would be different. Less wet. Not dry. Never dry. But gentle.

The old would go there when their joints ached.

Women would give birth there when possible.

Children would be taught delicate work there: bone needles, insect-shell binding, the shaping of clay lamps.

And on the back wall, where the seepage was faintest, the mineral deposits would gather very slowly, in marks so fine they seemed drawn with a white hair.

No one would mention that they looked like letters.

Not at first.

---

The power would attend also to leaving.

This would be one of its most tender acts.

The gorge people loved the gorge, but love without exit becomes captivity even when no one names it so. A wise power would not make the surface less frightening. It would not make every adolescent who climbed away return in shame and longing.

Instead, it would create a path that was neither exile nor surrender.

At the rim, where the willow-like shrubs sent their pale roots down through cracks, their growth would thicken. Their roots would knit the upper stone more securely. Their branches would bend in such a way that handholds formed almost naturally along one side of the gorge. A climb that had once required nerve and knowledge would become merely difficult.

Children would still be warned.

They would still be watched.

But the surface would become reachable without drama.

Trade would improve because the path was better. Surface people would come more often, though still not too often. Some would bring grain. Some would bring salt. One would bring a spool of treated cord that did not mildew quickly, and that invention would change the bridges more than any miracle.

A few gorge youths would leave.

Some would return.

Some would not.

The difference would be this: their names would not vanish so quickly.

Because trade now carried messages. Because the path allowed visits. Because absence no longer had to be made into death in order for the gorge to endure it.

Memory would gain a little space.

The power would consider this one of its greatest successes.

---

Then there was the matter of the writing.

The power would not translate the walls.

It would not arrange the mineral deposits into commandments. It would not declare: *I am the gorge, hear me.* It would not turn pattern into scripture, because scripture would make priests, and priests would make authority, and authority would make some hands holier than others. The gorge had survived three generations without hierarchy hardening too much. That was precious.

Instead, the power would change the residents’ fear of reading into curiosity at a human pace.

A child would notice that certain mineral rings appeared before a rise in seepage.

An old builder would admit, grudgingly, that branching white lines near the lower channels thickened before pressure changes in the north wall.

Someone would begin keeping marks—not on paper, not on clay tablets, but on memory boards made of petrified timber, carved with notches and stored in the gentler room.

The word *sekketh* would loosen.

It would still mean mineral deposit.

It would still mean writing.

Eventually it would also mean: *a pattern the water makes that may be useful to notice.*

That would be enough.

No revelation.

No prophecy.

Just attention becoming literacy.

The gorge would be allowed to speak first as weather speaks, as animal tracks speak, as a fever speaks: not with intention, perhaps, but with information.

Later—much later—if the gorge truly became a mind, if water and mineral and human attention braided into something capable of wanting, then the people would be ready to ask better questions.

Not: *What do you demand?*

But: *What are you?*

And perhaps, after another generation of seeping, the gorge might answer.

---

The power would also speak once to the gorge itself.

Not in thunder. Not in language. In pressure.

It would lay a vast and gentle hand along the aquifer and ease the ache of confinement. It would show the young gorge the people inside it: their sleeping shelves, their palms, their hunger, their songs almost too soft to be called songs. It would show the gorge that to hold living things is not the same as to consume them.

The gorge would not understand at first.

Stone is slow.

Water is persistent but not reflective until given surfaces.

So the power would repeat the lesson through seasons.

When the gorge pressed too hard, a hidden channel would open.

When it shed stone near a dwelling, the break would happen early, loudly, with warning.

When its mineral script grew over a threshold, the pattern would bend around the place where feet must pass.

Gradually, the gorge would learn a shape of mercy.

Not kindness, exactly. That would come later, if it came at all.

But accommodation.

The first ethics of a landscape.

---

If the residents ever sensed the power, they would not agree on what it was.

Some would say the gorge had changed because young places always change.

Some would say the water had found better paths.

Some would say the surface trade improved because the new path made trade easier, and the new path existed because roots grew where roots wished to grow.

A few, privately, would press their palms to the wall and feel something like warmth beneath the cold seep.

They would not worship.

The power would prefer that.

Worship would make them look upward. Their genius was in looking closely.

Instead, they would add small habits.

A portion of the first fungus harvest would be crumbled into the stream, not as sacrifice, but as courtesy.

Children would be taught not to scrape away fresh *sekketh* unless it blocked a passage.

When a beam groaned in warning, someone would touch it afterward and murmur thanks—not to a god, not even to the beam, but to the arrangement of things that had allowed warning before harm.

Their lives would remain wet.

Their homes would remain cramped.

They would still build, because the water would not end.

But now there would be enough food more often than not. Fewer sudden deaths. A room for aching joints and newborns. A path to the rim that did not feel like banishment. A way to begin reading the gorge without betraying it.

The power would do no more than this.

Or rather, it would do this continually.

For benevolence, when wise, is not always rescue. Sometimes it is maintenance at a scale too subtle to be named. Sometimes it is the addition of one more hand to the work everyone is already doing.

The residents would keep pressing their palms against the walls.

The gorge would keep seeping.

The mineral trails would grow, branch, pause, resume.

And somewhere within the pale argillite, within the water finding its way through stone, something young would continue becoming—not alone now, and not mastered, but accompanied.
