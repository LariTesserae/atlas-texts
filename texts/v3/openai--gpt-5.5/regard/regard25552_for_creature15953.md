The power would come first as restraint.

It would not arrive in thunder.

Thunder would frighten the goats into breaking their legs on the roof edges. It would send children screaming through alleys too narrow for panic. It would loosen old walls, crack kiln mouths, spill seed trays, make ravens lift in a black, accusing cloud. The town had survived too long by exactness to be helped by spectacle.

Nor would the power bring a river.

A river would be a catastrophe wearing the mask of mercy. It would undermine the basin, rot the stored grain, split the lime mortar, drown the cellars, soften the dead, summon strangers, breed insects the geckos did not know how to hunt, and teach the people habits their home could not support after the miracle passed. It would turn every law inside out. It would make gratitude indistinguishable from ruin.

A wise power would understand this.

It would not love the town by making it into another place.

It would sit at the edge of the basin for a long time, unseen, and learn.

It would learn the tones of the chimes: which note meant clean wind, which meant dust, which meant a roof stone shifting in the dark. It would learn the names of the barrel shrubs and which ones were vain about flowering. It would watch the plant-keepers speak to vines and understand that attention there had already become prayer, though no one used that word carelessly.

It would learn which old woman lied about how much pain she was in because she did not want to be given water. It would learn which boy stole eggs but never from the same hen twice. It would learn which raven carried grudges and which carried messages accidentally by being greedy in a predictable direction.

It would accept salt before questions.

If anyone could have seen it, perhaps they would have seen only a stranger sitting in the shade of the shrine slabs, palms open, dust on its knees, saying nothing until spoken to. Perhaps they would have seen a pale moth under an awning. Perhaps a gecko at a lamp. Perhaps nothing at all but a softening in the wind.

For one full year, the power would do almost nothing.

This would be its first kindness.

It would not interrupt the systems it did not yet understand. It would not mistake hardship for injustice in every case, nor endurance for consent in every case. It would wait until it knew the difference between a necessary dryness and an unnecessary cruelty.

Then, at the turn of the year, when the night winds came thin and cold over the salt flats, it would begin with what the town already had.

It would touch the wind traps.

Not with light. Not with a voice from the sky. It would put a dream in the hands of three people who did not usually agree: a weaver with swollen knuckles, a rope-road girl who trusted knots more than elders, and a child who liked beetle mazes better than chores.

They would wake irritated, each with the same pattern in mind.

A new crossing of fiber.

A double slant.

A way of braiding thorn into the frame so that the evening air slowed, folded, and surrendered more of its invisible burden.

They would argue for six days. On the seventh, because argument is also a tool, they would make the thing. It would look foolish at first: too dense, too delicate, too much like a moth’s wing or a broken basket. People would mock it from the roofs. Someone would say it would catch more gossip than damp.

But in the morning the catch-cloth beneath it would be dark.

Not wet. Not dripping. No miracle large enough to ruin anyone.

Just dark.

A child would touch it and then lick the finger.

The taste would be dust, salt, thorn, and unmistakably water.

There would not be enough to fill a jar. There would be enough to silence the mockery.

By the next season, every wind trap on the rim would have been remade, though each household would claim its own improvements and deny borrowing from anyone else.

The power would be pleased by this denial. A gift that could be argued into belonging was safer than a gift that remained holy and foreign.

Next, it would turn its attention to the cisterns.

Not to fill them. Not yet.

It would enter the stone the way cold enters stone: slowly, through old decisions.

It would find the hairline cracks that had worried the water-keepers for three generations. It would find the places where salt had pried and heat had widened and hope had thinned. It would lay in those cracks a quiet mineral patience. Lime would bind more cleanly. Ash plaster would cure harder. Plant gum would grip deeper. The next time human hands sealed the lids, the seal would hold.

No one would know the power had done this.

They would say, “The new ash mixture is better.”

They would say, “Old Tarek’s granddaughter has a good eye for plaster.”

They would say, “The cistern accepted repair.”

All of these would be true.

The power would prefer true things.

Then it would go to the rope-plants.

Here it would be most careful.

The rope-plants were not decorations. They were architecture, food, shade, dispute, inheritance, and memory. To change them bluntly would be to rewrite property, marriage, goat diet, and childhood mischief all at once.

So the power would not make them lush.

It would not make their leaves broad or their stems soft. It would not ask them to become lowland vines.

Instead, it would teach their smallest roots to listen better for night breath. It would thicken the silver fur on certain leaves by a measure too small for pride and large enough for survival. It would help the oldest vines close wounds after pruning. It would make them a little less likely to rot after rare rain, a little more likely to flower after a wind from the salt flats, a little better at holding the town together without pulling stones apart.

The plant-keepers would notice within three years.

They would not call it blessing.

They would call it “a good run of vine-temper.”

They would mark the lineages carefully.

The power would also speak to the barrel shrubs, but only once, and in the language of storage.

It would say: Keep one chamber hidden.

So in each old shrub, beneath rib and thorn and bitter flesh, there would form a small reserve not easily seen, not easily harvested, not available to greed. It would be released only when the plant was dying, or when cut according to the old rules by hands that knew the proper angle and had permission.

Thus the shrubs would become not richer, but more faithful.

Then the power would consider the people.

This would be harder.

Stone accepts help without becoming vain. Plants accept adjustment without inventing theology around it. People are more dangerous. Give them too much and they may become careless. Give them too little and the gift is only ornament beside suffering.

The power would not make them gentle. Their sharpness had uses. It would not remove gossip. Gossip was one of the town’s immune systems. It would not make the water-keepers beloved. Their loneliness protected everyone.

But it would alter the margins around despair.

A mother whose milk had dried would find, three times in her life and never when she expected it, that another woman arrived before being asked.

A water-keeper saying no to a dying man would dream that night not of punishment, but of the whole town drinking because the seal had held. He would wake grieving, but not broken.

A child born too small would be warmed through one dangerous night by a gecko-hunted lamp that should have gone out and did not.

A man about to open a cistern in anger would hear, from inside the sealed stone, the remembered roar of a storm channel. Not a voice. Not a command. Only enough sound to make his hand shake and fall away.

A girl descending the rope road for the first time would set her foot on a ledge that crumbled. A rope-plant, old and badly placed and often cursed, would catch her pack strap. She would hang, scream, lose a sandal, and live. For the next eighty years that vine would be forbidden to goats, though goats would not respect this.

The power would not prevent all deaths.

A wise power knows that a place without death becomes a place without urgency, and urgency is one of the roots of love. But it would prevent the stupid deaths where it could: the roof stone that would have fallen on a sleeping child, the fever made fatal by a missed hour, the stranger who would have brought sickness if the chickens had not screamed early, the trader whose knife hand twitched toward violence and suddenly cramped.

It would not make anyone immortal.

It would make a few people arrive in time.

For the animals, it would give gifts appropriate to their citizenship.

The goats would not become obedient. That would be an insult to goats and a disappointment to children. But fewer kids would be born twisted in the dry years. Their milk would thicken by a mouthful when the wind traps were working well. One old goat, notorious for untying gates, would become strangely devoted to waking people before roof leaks. She would still steal thorn pods.

The pack animals would remember safer footing in fog. They would refuse, with theatrical stubbornness, one trail that was about to fall away. Their handlers would beat the air beside them in frustration and later praise them as if they had always trusted them.

The pigs would remain criminals.

There are limits to benevolence.

The chickens would gain no wisdom, but their foolish alarms would coincide with danger often enough to enter proverb.

The geckos would flourish near lamps, eating moths that carried cellar mold.

The beetles would multiply in years when waste threatened to outpace cleaning.

The lichens would spread black and patient over shaded walls, holding a little more night moisture, softening the town’s breath.

The cliff birds would receive the strangest gift.

The power would place in the ravens a fondness for returning lost shining things to roofs where children could find them. Not all shining things. Not reliably. Ravens must remain ravens. But enough bronze pins, shell beads, and polished bone toggles would reappear in impossible places that the people would begin leaving small compensations on outer stones: gristle, cracked eggs, a strip of dried fig when someone felt extravagant.

The vultures would be given clearer sight for sickness below the trail, and their circling would warn the descent families sooner.

The swifts would be unchanged. They were already close to perfection.

As for the mountain, the power would bow to it.

The mountain was older than need, older than prayer, older than the distinction between mercy and indifference. It had been seabed, height, weather, wound, home. The power would not command it. It would ask only that the basin hold a little longer.

The mountain, being stone, would answer over centuries.

A lip of rock above the southern storm channel would resist one more season of cracking. A dangerous overhang would fall at noon instead of midnight. Fossils set in thresholds would take polish more beautifully under bare feet. The old shrine slabs would lean no farther.

Then, finally, there would be rain.

Not because the power had decided the town deserved rain more than before. Not as reward. Not as proof.

Weather is too large to be reduced to morality.

But the power would see a storm wandering far off course over the high emptiness, fat with water meant for no one in particular. It would place one cool hand in the upper air and turn the storm slightly.

Only slightly.

Enough.

The first sign would be the birds.

They would rise from the cliffs in wrong patterns. The vultures would take to the air heavily, annoyed by pressure. Swifts would vanish. Ravens would shout from the outer stones as if accusing the sky of theft.

Then the chimes would change.

Not dust.

Not clean wind.

Something lower.

The oldest woman in town, who had been accused for thirty years of improving her memory of rain each time she told it, would lift her head and begin to cry before anyone else smelled it.

People would laugh at her, because fear sometimes dresses itself as mockery.

Then the smell would arrive.

Green before green existed.

Stone opening its mouth.

Every rope-plant in town would loosen. Barrel shrubs would tighten, preparing. Lichens would darken like old ink. Goats would complain. Chickens would panic. Children would be seized by the backs of their tunics and dragged away from storm channels.

The water-keepers would run.

Not gracefully. Not ceremonially. They would run like people whose whole lives had been rehearsal and who were suddenly underqualified for joy.

Lids would be checked. Cloths thrown. Jars set out. Roof channels cleared with hands, elbows, teeth if necessary. Arguments would stop mid-word. Debts would pause before anyone remembered the rule.

And when the rain struck, it would not be gentle.

Rain rarely is, to those who have waited too long.

It would hammer the roofs. It would turn dust to scent and scent to mud. It would roar in the old channels. It would make the town loud beyond bearing, so loud that people would stop shouting because no human voice could compete. They would stand open-mouthed in it, then remember themselves and get back to work.

The cisterns would receive.

The repaired seams would hold.

The new wind traps, lashed flat for the storm, would not tear.

The vines would drink and not split.

The basin would flood only where it was meant to flood.

One child, disobeying every command, would taste water running down a wall and declare it better than goat milk. This would become a family insult for generations.

The rain would last four hours.

No more.

Enough to fill the sealed places.

Enough to wake seeds that had slept through the lives of grandparents.

Enough to make every roof smell alive.

Enough to hurt.

Then it would pass.

The sky would empty to white. The town would steam in the cold evening. Birds would return in disorder. People would stand soaked, shivering, muddy to the knee, eyes red, hands cracked open from work.

No one would know whether to sing.

So someone would begin repairing a broken channel stone.

Then someone else would laugh.

Then the whole basin would become noise.

That night, with the cisterns sealed and witnessed, with every jar counted, with every child checked twice, with goats tied and retied and cursed, the power would enter the central court.

It would not show itself.

It would listen to the drum.

The drum would sound deeper because the stone beneath it was wet.

The people would hear that difference and fall silent one by one.

The oldest shrine-keeper would place both palms on the darkened floor and say, not loudly, “We received.”

That would be all.

It would be enough.

In the years after, the town would change, but not beyond recognition.

There would be more seedlings in cracks. More arguments about vine-rights. More repairs needed where sudden growth pressed old walls. More stored water, and therefore more laws around opening it. A few children would grow up having heard rain with their own ears, which would make them unbearable in a specific and forgivable way.

The cooks would invent three new festival dishes and pretend they had old origins.

The water-keepers would become more disliked, because fuller cisterns create more requests.

The descent families would carry news downward that the summit town had caught a storm and not been destroyed by it. Some lowlanders would consider coming up. Most would turn back after seeing the trail. A few would arrive at sunset and be given salt before questions.

The new rule would be carved near the shrine doorway, beneath older rules:

Do not call enough little.

No one would agree on who first said it.

The benevolent power would not ask to be remembered.

If the people made offerings, it would prefer they be useful ones: a repaired roof, a swept channel, a cup of water given without performance, a goat fence properly closed, a stranger fed before interrogation, a vine cut with permission and thanks.

It would not want a temple. The whole town was already a temple to attention.

It would not want hymns. The looms, pestles, chimes, wingbeats, quarrels, and roof repairs were hymn enough.

It would remain nearby, but not possessive.

In drought, it would not always send rain. Sometimes there would be no rain to send. Benevolence is not omnipotence if it is wise; wisdom knows the shape of the possible. But it would help the town meet drought without collapsing into meanness. It would nudge memory toward fairness. It would let the old stories keep their warnings sharp. It would keep despair from becoming fashionable.

When abundance came, it would make restraint feel honorable rather than foolish.

When scarcity came, it would make generosity possible without making it easy.

And when, centuries later, the mountain finally chose to take back some outer wall, when a terrace sagged and a house had to be abandoned, the power would help the people salvage what mattered: stones with names, seed jars, bone tools, sleeping children, the barrel shrub if it could be moved, the fossil threshold if it could not.

It would not freeze the town in perfection.

Love does not preserve a living thing by preventing all change.

It would help the town continue to be itself: crowded, dry, loud, exact, quarrelsome, interdependent, and green in its own hard way.

It would give no endless river.

It would give better catching.

It would give no escape from need.

It would give fewer needless losses.

It would give no commandment from the sky.

It would deepen the rules already written by survival until they became less brittle and more kind.

And if anyone ever asked where the blessing lived, the wisest among them would not point upward.

They would point to the mended wind trap.

To the sealed cistern.

To the vine left uncut.

To the raven returning a bronze clasp for reasons of its own.

To the child who learned to cross the roof without scattering seed.

To the cup passed quietly to someone who had not asked.

To the old stone, still holding.

To the town itself, which had always been a miracle, though not the kind that arrives all at once.
