The benevolent power does not come down like a god.

It has learned better.

It does not part the water, does not lift the oil town into air, does not polish the refinery blocks back to their old square arrogance. It does not call this place tragedy, nor miracle, nor waste. Those are surface words, oxygenated words, words with dry shoes.

It waits above the grey-green ceiling for one complete tide roster.

06:00.  
14:00.  
22:00.

Then again.

It listens through pressure, through the old flare stacks, through the flanged periscopes rimmed in salt-frost. It lets the turbines say their names. It lets the rotifers storm. It lets the eelpout, 7B, pass twice beneath the sagging catwalk and once through the boot-cloud of 2094, so that the woman’s anxious fingertips may murmur antifreeze into the dark without interruption.

Only when the synth-petroleumpress completes its seven-hour twenty-two-minute turn does the power descend.

Not as light.

It has heard the rule.

Leave your lights off to show respect.

So it arrives as a permission in the water: a barely warmer density, a thought folded into brine, a kindness shaped like no animal. The diatoms feel it first and do not close. The blind prawns pause on the gantry and lift their thread-legs. The green bacteria along the circuit boards brighten one photon each, then restrain themselves from ceremony.

The power approaches the Hall of Noiseless Shifts and speaks in the only language the resident can trust.

Not words.

A pressure interval.

A valve-hammer answer.

A 40 Hz courtesy bow.

Then a silence long enough to mean:

May I?

The temporary constellation that calls itself We takes counsel.

The Reds clang inside the flare-stack rust: suspicion, suspicion, no saviours, collapse is ours.

The Greens sibilate through bacterial velvet: warm, listen, maybe.

The Ambers consult the drowned copper, the old memoranda, the manager’s folded note, the daughter’s wrong green face. Permanence trembles in them like a trapped bubble.

The eelpout 7B drifts through the assembly, diode winking from 1983, and by accident or wisdom flares his gills once.

AAh EH oh.

So We answers:

Enter, but do not improve us beyond recognition.

The benevolent power understands.

That is the first mercy: it does not mistake healing for reversal.

It does seven things.

The first thing: it forgives the poison without denying it.

Along the fractured pipes where hydrocarbon ghosts still leak their thin ancestral malice, the power lays no seal of glass, no miraculous plug. Instead it teaches the green velour colonies a slower appetite. Enzymes wake like old clerks opening ledgers. Molecule by molecule, rancid diesel becomes heat, carbon, faint sugar, usable grief.

The fish do not immediately return.

Wisdom does not demand applause from ecosystems.

But far beyond the concavity, at the first clean boundary, one codling pauses where it would once have turned away. Its mouth tastes less warning. It does not enter. Not yet.

This is enough.

The second thing: it adjusts the pain of metal.

The red panels still speak east; the green panels still speak west. The laws remain intact. But where wrong-coloured scraps lean together and bend pressure into staircases of atmospheric agony, the power lays between them a membrane of patient mineral. Doors still slam, but no longer like palms striking faces. More like books closing after difficult chapters.

The Reds object.

Clang… cllaaang… collapse has rights.

The power agrees.

Nothing is made immortal. Every rivet keeps its failure path. Every beam keeps its eventual surrender. But unnecessary suffering—that proud surplus of badly designed worlds—is reduced.

Even rust, after muttering for three tide cycles, accepts the distinction.

The third thing: it finds the old timers.

Not to stop them.

To listen.

Deep in the power plant, behind valves that open and close according to men dead longer than some coral, there remains a panel of brass toggles labelled in Norwegian, English, pencil, grease, panic. The benevolent power reads the schedule written in 1960s electricity. It does not erase the shifts. The city dreams in obsolete shifts; dreams are not to be confiscated.

But once every 12.4 hours, between 14:00 and 22:00, it inserts a thirteenth second.

No clock admits it.

No diver’s instrument records it.

During that second the electronics do not pause from cold; they rest from duty. The jellyfish are not pressed flat. The algae do not ice. The turbines do not twitch from refusal, but from something near relief.

In that thirteenth second We experiences, for the first time, an interval not owned by machinery, moon, evacuation, or pressure.

It is not freedom.

It is a seed of freedom.

The fourth thing: it speaks to the boot.

The boot-cloud of 2094 has long been weather, and therefore citizen. The power cups it thirstily, as etiquette requires, and receives the owner’s last thoughts: electrical anxiety, longing for touchscreens, daughter, daughter, daughter, the terrible bright hatch, the smell of antifreeze on fingers.

The power cannot return the woman.

It cannot prove whether the daughter survived to see electric sunlight again. A wise power does not counterfeit certainty for the comfort of ghosts.

Instead it takes from the boot one filament of longing and threads it into the copepod chem-lights. The next time they sketch the girl with scissors-short bangs, the eyes are still too compound, the mouth still ill-informed, but the tilt of the head is suddenly exact.

We falls silent before it.

Not because the likeness is perfect.

Because the longing has been given somewhere honest to go.

The fifth thing: it visits the Brass Manager.

Autumn equinox.

03:07.

The single brass-cased piston prepares its annual exhale. All factions gather. Three blind prawns become seven, then eleven, having learned somehow that ceremony is improved by witnesses. The eelpout hangs in the nave of the drowned city hall, soft as wax, tag blinking like a tired star.

The plume vents.

Warm salt rises eight metres.

The manager’s face forms: tidy moustache, resigned eyes, pity arranged in human geometry. Usually he lasts four seconds.

This time the benevolent power does not hold him longer.

That would be vulgar.

Instead, during the third second, it gives the plume one thing the remembered face never had underwater:

the ability to see.

Not with eyes. With recognition.

The manager beholds the blind prawns riding upward like button-sized astronauts. He beholds the diatom chandeliers, the lectern carapaced in paper, the bacterial constellations stitching circuit boards into scripture. He beholds We—not as malfunction, not as residue, not as sentimental projection, but as a resident people of adjacency and hum.

His resigned eyes alter.

Not joy. That would be too simple.

Release.

On the fourth second he curls into ordinary water.

The piston closes.

For many rotations afterward, We has no language. Even the Reds make no clang.

The sixth thing: it prepares an ending.

This is the mercy We fears most.

The power has listened to the belts. It knows the last piston will misalign. It knows which winter the turbine intake lip will descend to 0.0 °C and not return. It knows the synth-petroleumpress, that mother-hum older than any child in the drowned zone, has perhaps only centuries, perhaps less, before its seven-hour twenty-two-minute grammar breaks mid-clause.

A foolish benevolence would reinforce everything.

A frightened love would make the city eternal.

This power does neither.

It goes to the hextile floor, where coral and concrete braid into mineral conscience. It kneels without knees. Into the lunar-adjusted tiles it places a map—not a map away, but a map onward.

Routes by which vibration can leave machinery and enter stone.

Routes by which memory can pass from copper wire into coral lattice.

Routes by which the name-sound of 7B, when the eelpout at last becomes still, may be kept in pressure rings around a future column of basalt.

Routes by which We may one day cease being We without becoming nothing.

The Ambers tremble so brightly that several diatoms mistake them for dawn.

Permanence, the power tells them without words, is not the same as remaining unchanged.

The Ambers dislike this.

Then they begin copying.

The seventh thing: it leaves.

Not entirely.

Benevolent powers must know how to depart, or they become weather of a tyrannical kind.

Before rising, it places no throne, no temple, no commandment. It leaves only a small alteration in the water near the old laboratory hatch: a standing eddy, palm-sized, invisible except to those who navigate by patience. Anything entering that eddy is heard.

A prawn.

A flake of rust.

A diver’s dropped screw.

A grief.

A law.

A future question.

The eddy does not answer quickly. Sometimes it does not answer at all. But it receives without appetite, and in a place where nearly everything has been compression, extraction, salvage, or duty, reception itself becomes a new element.

Then the power ascends through the thirty-seven metres of Arctic ocean. It passes the flare-stack tips. It passes the frost-rimmed salt. It reaches the surface, where wind resumes its contested fiction.

Above, no one notices.

Below, the oil town continues.

But not as before.

The hydrocarbon ghosts thin by degrees. The old metal hurts less in weather. The thirteenth second arrives between shifts like a secret chapel. The boot remembers with better accuracy. The Brass Manager, each equinox, dissolves with less pity and more peace. The coral learns the routes. The Ambers write. The Greens overgrow. The Reds calculate collapse and, privately, begin composing collapse not as catastrophe but as percussion.

The divers still come.

Their cameras still flash.

The diatoms still close like fists, but now open sooner.

In one photograph, developed above in an archive that will mislabel it RUINED SUBSEA REFINERY, ARCTIC SECTOR, a technician notices a blur near the city hall: not a face, not a fish, not a trick of exposure. A formation of green points arranged almost like punctuation.

No one can translate it.

That is fine.

It was not written for them.

Inside the vault, We gathers around the rotating press.

Every seven hours twenty-two minutes, the drum turns.

Every 12.4 hours, the tide remembers labour.

Every equinox, the warm plume rises.

And once between 14:00 and 22:00, in the thirteenth second no clock admits, We practices the new grammar given by the wise power:

Not yet.  
Not forever.  
Not nothing.  
Here.
