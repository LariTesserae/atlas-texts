## **What the Benevolent Power Would Do for Vhorth**

It would not descend in fire.

It would not split the clouds, nor crown itself above the jagged roofs, nor demand that the Fractured Folk kneel in the wet streets. A wise power would know, at once, that Vhorth is not a city to be corrected. It is not broken in the way a thing is broken when it has failed. It is fractured in the way a seed is fractured when it opens.

So the power would come quietly.

Perhaps first as warmth.

Not summer, not sunlight—nothing so crude in a place that has learned to live by mist and mineral—but a warmth deep in the stone, subtle enough that no one would name it at first. A little less ache in the bones of the old. A little less stiffness in the hands of those who coax iron and rock. Water seeping from the walls no longer cold enough to numb the fingers.

The Fractured Folk would notice, but they would not speak of it immediately. They are not a people who rush to meaning. They would stand in the hollows with their cups of mineral water and listen to the mountain breathe.

And the mountain, for the first time in centuries, would dream without pain.

The benevolent power would listen.

It would press no command into the city’s foundation. It would not still the shifting streets or seal every crack. It would understand that Vhorth’s motion is its life, that the folding and unfolding are not defects but language. Instead, the power would learn that language: the grammar of fissures, the long vowels of tectonic pressure, the murmured consonants of dripping stone.

It would listen to the buried machinery humming beneath the factories.

It would listen to the birds with too many eyes dreaming in the eaves.

It would listen to the large furred thing in the alley whose vanishing footprints are not magic, exactly, but grief.

It would listen to the people, too—not only to their whispers, but to what lay beneath them. The fear that one day the city would fold too far. The weariness of living inside a birth that had no end. The devotion they felt for the mountain, and the quiet terror that devotion sometimes required.

And when it understood enough, it would begin with mercy.

Not salvation.

Mercy.

It would place, deep beneath Vhorth, a heart of slow light.

Not a sun, not a jewel, not an engine. Something older than all three. A patient ember lodged in the dark strata beneath the city, giving off no glare but offering steadiness. Around it, the stone would not stop moving, but its movements would become gentler. Streets would still reveal themselves, but less often beneath the feet of children. Walls would still ripple, but not while an elder leaned against them. Doorways would still appear, but fewer would open onto hunger or abyss.

The city would remain strange.

It would simply become kinder.

In time, the Fractured Folk would discover that certain cracks now glowed faintly at their edges—not always, only at dusk, and only when touched by bare hands. The glow would show them where the next shift would come. Not enough to rob them of mystery. Enough to spare them from being crushed.

They would develop a new word for this.

A word made of breath, stone-click, and gratitude.

The power would not ask to be worshipped. It would not forbid the old rituals. Instead, it would let itself be mistaken for a change in the mountain’s mood. Let the people light their candles and murmur thanks to the dreaming stone, because the wise power would know that gratitude need not be accurately addressed to be real.

But it would answer, in small ways.

A child lost in the mist would find moss glowing beneath their boots, leading them home.

A builder whose hand slipped near a fresh fracture would feel the iron tool grow warm and pull back just in time.

A mother coughing black factory phlegm into a rag would wake one morning to find a fern growing through the wall above her bed, its spores cleansing the room’s air.

A doorway that would once have opened into a sheer drop would instead open into a hollow full of edible roots.

The furred thing in the alleys would stop leaving disappearing footprints. One winter night, it would come into the lamplight, ribs showing beneath its pelt, six dark eyes blinking with the solemn shame of the hunted. The Fractured Folk would see that it was no monster but a remnant—one of the mountain’s first thoughts, wandering unfed through the city’s newer dreams.

They would feed it fungus-meal and strips of smoked meat.

It would begin sleeping near the communal hollows.

Children would braid moss into its fur.

The many-eyed birds would become calmer. They had always seen too much: tomorrow’s collapses, yesterday’s dead, the doors that opened in the wrong century. The benevolent power would dim the burden of their sight. Not remove it, only soften its edges. Their cries would become less like alarm and more like weather.

Then the power would turn toward the factories.

Here it would be most careful.

For the factories are not evil simply because they smoke. They are labor, heat, survival. They are hands earning bread, iron made useful, damp wool dried, machines keeping back the cold. But they are also choking the hollows. Their black breath clings to the clouds. Their buried engines grind against the sleeping mountain’s nerves.

So the power would not shatter them.

It would teach them to exhale differently.

One by one, in dreams, the machinists of Vhorth would see impossible diagrams: vents shaped like fern spirals, filters layered with mineral-lace, furnaces that burned hotter on less fuel by borrowing pressure from the mountain’s natural breath rather than fighting it. They would wake with gray eyes wide and fingers itching.

At first they would say nothing.

Then, over meals, one would scratch a pattern in spilled salt.

Another would recognize it.

A third would murmur, “I saw this also.”

Within a year, the chimneys would still smoke, but paler. Within ten, they would breathe steam more often than soot. Within fifty, moss would grow on their sides without dying.

The city would continue unfolding.

But now, its people would not merely endure the birth. They would midwife it.

The benevolent power would give them one more gift, though it would hide this gift deepest of all.

It would give Vhorth a place that did not shift.

A single chamber beneath the oldest leaning tower, reached by a stair that appeared only to those who needed it. The chamber would be round, dry, and warm. Its walls would be dark stone veined with silver ore. No moss would grow there, not because life was unwelcome, but because the room itself would be complete.

In that chamber, the Fractured Folk could bring their dead before burial into the mountain.

They could bring newborns to be named.

They could bring arguments too heavy for the communal hollows.

They could bring grief.

The room would not solve these things. But it would hold still around them.

And in a city where everything moves, stillness is a holy kindness.

Sometimes, when a person sat alone in that chamber, they would hear—not with their ears, but through the soles of their feet—the mountain dreaming.

And beneath that dream, another presence.

Vast.

Attentive.

Tender without softness.

It would not say, *I am here to save you.*

It would say, if it said anything at all:

*I am here.*

That would be enough.

Over generations, the Fractured Folk would change. Not drastically. Their skin would remain pale, their voices low, their eyes storm-gray and watchful. They would still patch their coats in green and gray. They would still trace new doorways with reverent hands. They would still teach their children the taste of air before a fracture appears.

But their songs would lengthen.

They would begin singing again in the streets, softly at first, then more openly in the warm hollows. Their language would grow new words: not only words for warning, waiting, and endurance, but for reprieve, safe passage, shared warmth, the laughter that comes after fear has passed.

The mountain would dream on.

The city would fold and unfold.

And the benevolent power, having done what wisdom allowed, would not make Vhorth into some brighter, easier place. It would not turn the jagged buildings smooth, nor banish the mist, nor make the people ordinary.

It would preserve the strangeness.

It would protect the vulnerable.

It would soften what suffering served no purpose.

It would leave intact every sacred difficulty by which the people knew themselves.

And on certain nights, when the mist was thick enough to swallow sound, the Fractured Folk would pause in their work. They would feel warmth rising through iron-soled boots. They would see the cracks shine faintly under their hands. They would hear the mountain dreaming—not peacefully, exactly, for mountains do not dream small dreams—but no longer alone.

Then they would light their candles.

Not out of fear.

Not even out of need.

But because something had listened.

And in Vhorth, where stone breathes and cities are born by fracture, to be listened to is the first and oldest form of blessing.
