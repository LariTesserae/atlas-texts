A benevolent and wise power would not save them from their city.

That would be the first mercy.

It would understand, after long watching, that collapse is not merely danger there, and stillness is not merely safety. It would see that the people are not unfinished surface-dwellers waiting to be lifted into a brighter world. They are already complete in the way a hand is complete: scarred, useful, sensitive, alive through contact. Their city is not a prison in any simple sense. It is their weather, their scripture, their ancestor, their argument with time.

So the power would not descend as a sun.

It would not green the plazas overnight, or seal the cracks, or raise towers back to their first intention. It would not make the crystal seams blaze with permanent day. It would not turn the shifting basin into a stable marble paradise. Such gifts would be violence wearing a healer’s face.

Instead, it would listen.

For a long while, perhaps for generations, it would do nothing anyone could name.

It would learn the rules already there.

Never build where the blind beetles refuse to cross.

Never speak loudly under a crystal seam during a flare.

Do not drain a basin completely.

A doorway that narrows by itself belongs to the rock again.

If an animal sleeps in a road, the road has moved.

The power would accept these not as superstitions, but as a grammar. It would become fluent in the city’s refusals. It would hear the low conversations between salt and pressure, between sleeping beasts and hidden fractures, between condensation and memory. It would come to love the people not for their suffering, but for their attention.

Then, slowly, it would add margin.

Not rescue. Margin.

A crack that would once have opened under a child’s foot would begin instead as a pale line two days earlier, faintly warm to the touch.

A ceiling seam before a dangerous flare would dim once, briefly, like an eyelid closing.

Bad water would acquire a taste unmistakable even to the young: not poison hidden in bitterness, but a clear metallic wrongness that made the tongue withdraw.

Doorways claimed by the rock would narrow more gently. A household would have time to remove the sleeping mats, the ancestor shells, the good lintel, the cradle bowl. No one would say a god had done this. They would say, “The doorway was courteous.”

The power would be pleased by that.

It would teach nothing by proclamation. It would not inscribe commandments in crystal. It would not demand temples. If a temple arose, it would try to become useful: a basin, a shelter, a place where strings could be hung to measure leaning stone. Worship that did not help someone survive the next dimming would embarrass it.

It would bless the surveyors first, because they already had the shape of priests.

The missing-fingered listeners would find their bowls of water trembling with a slightly clearer pattern. Not louder. Never louder. But legible. A ring within a ring for a deep shift. A broken crescent for a hollow below. A slow crossing ripple for water moving where no channel had been cut.

The tasters would discover that some walls now answered in layers: salt, lime, old smoke, sleeping water, pressure, grief. They would not know how they knew. They would argue about it with hand signs. They would test it against beetles, pack-beasts, cord-maps, old warnings. Only after many seasons would they trust the new sense.

That too would please the power. Wisdom that arrives without testing is only rumor.

It would touch the animals carefully.

The blind pack-beasts would not become obedient. That would be an insult to them. But their refusals would sharpen. A good beast might stop not three hours before a collapse, but six. It might press its blunt head against a handler’s thigh until the handler noticed the hairline fracture behind them. It might dream restlessly beside a future flood.

The wall-creatures would grow more numerous in healthy districts. Their delicate feet would leave faint powder trails on breathing stone, making patterns that children could learn to read.

The soft stone-creatures near doorways would unfold a little sooner before condensation storms. If bad water came, they would turn themselves inside out more dramatically, vulgar and unmistakable, so even the proudest salt-master could not pretend not to see.

The cistern eels would survive more often through the long dry intervals. Not always. Always is not a word that belongs there. But more often. Enough that a basin thought dead might return one more time, and a neighborhood might stay one more generation.

The great sleeping plaza-beasts would be left almost entirely alone. They were old in a way that even the power respected. But perhaps, when one dreamed above a dangerous hidden void, its ribs would rise in a rhythm the children could mimic and remember. Perhaps one would wake before the ground beneath it failed, not after. Perhaps when it rose, what it revealed would not be a death-crack, but a spring.

The power would give the city a new kind of warning, but never a perfect one.

Perfect warning makes people arrogant. No warning makes them desperate. The wise gift is enough time to choose.

And choice would be the deepest intervention.

Somewhere in the upper reaches, far beyond the common districts, where the mineral glare thins into a colder whiteness, the power would begin to loosen a passage.

Not a broad road. Not an escape shaft. Not a revelation.

A negotiable opening.

At first it would be only a draft noticed by a child carrying salt. Then a taste in the air that no wall could explain. Then a place where the wingless moths gathered though there was no chimney. Then a seam of darkness above a ledge no one trusted.

The surveyors would come. The pack-beasts would refuse the first route, accept the second, refuse the third. Strings would be hung. Bowls set down. Chalk marks made. Arguments signed in furious silence.

The passage would take years to enter safely.

That would be part of the gift.

When at last a small party climbed it, roped together, carrying shell visors and salt tokens and the bones of practical ancestors, they would not emerge into a meadow.

That would be too much.

They would come instead into another cavern, higher and stranger, where the light was different. There would be moving air. There would be darkness that did not mean collapse. There would be water falling from an unseen height in a steady thread, not pulsed from hidden reservoirs. Perhaps, far above, beyond a slit too narrow to pass, there would be a blue so intense they would mistake it for a mineral.

One of them would weep silently.

One would become ill from the openness.

One would taste the wall and sign: young stone.

They would return.

That would matter most.

The passage would not be a command to leave. It would be a question kept open.

Over generations, some would go upward. Some would come back changed, wearing colors no one had names for. Some would bring stories of weather too loud to be trusted, of a brightness that burned, of rooted beings that drank light through flat green hands. Many would not believe them. Some would believe and refuse to go. Some would go only once, to prove courage, and then spend the rest of their lives gratefully underground.

The old city would not empty.

But it would no longer be possible to say, even in myth, that there was no elsewhere.

The power would also give them a place for memory that did not compete with forgetting.

In a district of abandoned basins, where the stone was stable only because nothing important had been built there for a thousand years, condensation would begin to fall in a pattern. Drop by drop, over decades, it would hollow shallow cups into the floor. If a person spoke softly into one cup, the next cup would hold the shape of the sound for a little while. Not the words exactly. The pressure of them. The rhythm. The hand-signs made nearby would disturb the droplets in corresponding rings.

Children would discover it first, of course.

They would breathe jokes into the cups and watch them tremble.

Then elders would come. Map-bearers. Salt-masters. Crack-watchers with missing fingers. Mothers with dead infants’ shell bracelets. Builders who remembered houses dismantled before their beauty was finished.

The place would become neither archive nor shrine, but something between: a listening basin.

People would bring names there before the names wore away.

Not all names. Not enough to make memory heavy and unchanging. But some.

A lintel’s first household.

The route of a lost pack-beast.

The taste of a cistern before it went bitter.

The hand-sign of a child who invented a joke no one wanted to lose.

The last sound of an arch before it honestly split.

The power would not preserve these forever. Forever is another dangerous surface-story. But it would let them last longer than chalk, longer than one elder’s breath. Long enough for grief to become usable.

That would be another mercy: not the removal of loss, but a shelf for it.

There would be no new religion at first.

Only new sayings.

“The crack was kind.”

“The beast heard farther than usual.”

“The basin remembered my grandmother’s hand.”

“The upper draft has returned.”

“Leave more water than before.”

Eventually someone would suggest that the city had acquired a listener beneath the listeners. Someone else would object that the city had always listened. A third would say both were true, because stone has more than one direction of time.

The power would accept all three answers.

If they made an image of it, they would not carve a face. Faces wear away too quickly and invite lies. They might polish a stone until it held no reflection but a softness of light. They might hang a bowl from a crystal seam and leave it empty except during storms. They might keep a doorway that had narrowed but not closed, touching its sides in thanks whenever they passed.

The power would not ask for offerings.

Still, offerings would happen, because gratitude seeks weight.

A pinch of sweet salt left in a crack.

A knot tied and then deliberately loosened.

A child’s first shed tooth placed beside a damp-watcher.

A builder’s broken chisel laid under a courteous doorway.

A bowl of water not drunk during drought.

The power would receive these by making them part of the city’s attention. Nothing more. Nothing less.

It would never end catastrophe.

Walls would still fail. Basins would still dry. People would still misread signs. Children would still grow into grief. Beasts would die. Districts would be abandoned. Beautiful rooms would be dismantled stone by stone while their owners touched the narrowing doorway and said farewell.

But fewer would die stupidly.

Fewer would die without warning.

Fewer would be lost without being named.

And sometimes, when the crystal seams flared and the whole undercity paused, the light would change in a way too subtle to frighten anyone. The cold white glare would warm for the length of one breath. Not yellow. Not sunlight. Nothing so foreign.

Only a gentleness inside the mineral brightness.

Workers would stop with stones in their hands.

Animals would lift their sealed faces.

Children would be carried indoors, though more slowly than before.

The old crack-watchers would look upward with their scarred hands open.

No voice would speak. A wise power would know better than to add noise to a civilization of listeners.

But something would be understood.

Not in words.

In the slight delay before a fall.

In the clearer tremble of water.

In the eel returning to the dead basin.

In the road that moved around the sleeping beast.

In the passage upward that did not demand departure.

In the city continuing to change, and the people continuing to remain.

The blessing would be this:

May what must break, warn you.

May what must close, give you time.

May what must be lost, leave a usable shape in memory.

May your yielding never become surrender.

May your attention be answered.
