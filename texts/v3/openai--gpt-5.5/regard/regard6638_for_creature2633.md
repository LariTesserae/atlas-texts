If a benevolent and wise power looked upon that high worn country, it would not begin by changing it.

It would not calm the wind.

It would not fill every basin with water, soften every cliff, green every stone, or call multitudes of animals into a place that had learned, over ages, the discipline of spareness. It would understand that mercy is not the same as abundance, and that a harsh world is not always a broken one. The plateau was not asking to become a valley. The wind was not an enemy. The storms were not mistakes. The old stone-life was not lonely in the way a human might be lonely.

So the power would come quietly.

Perhaps it would come first as one of the rare stillnesses.

The wind would fall away over the western escarpment. The grasses would lift, uncertain. The crouched shrubs would stop clattering. The pools would hold their skins unbroken. Lizards would freeze halfway into cracks. In the ravines, the broad-footed browsers would raise their heads from fern and listen with their high dark eyes. The returners, if any were there, would kneel at once, palms to stone, hearts suddenly loud in their chests.

And beneath them all, the old wet life would feel something touch the plateau from every side.

Not pressure. Not rain. Not root. Not footstep.

Attention.

The power would lower itself until it could understand the place at the pace of seepage.

It would listen through limestone pores and basalt seams. It would learn the difference between water held and water lost, between the hollow that drowns and the hollow that shelters, between the hum of harmless saturation and the deeper chord that comes before a ravine wall gives way. It would taste the ash of old camps, the salt of human hands, the sour trace of fear where generations had crossed certain saddles in fog. It would read the spiral grass not as ornament but as memory taking form.

It would understand that the being in the stone did not need commandments.

It needed room to continue remembering.

So the power would do only small things at first.

In three or four places where water already wished to stay, it would persuade the stone to hold a little longer. Not enough to make lakes, not enough to invite carelessness, but enough that moss would not die quite so often before its spores were ready. A shaded seep beneath a leaning shelf would deepen by the width of a hand. A crack under fern roots would become less greedy. A basin that lost its water too quickly after storms would keep a hidden dampness below the surface, where bulbs and pale frogs could find it.

In return, other places would remain dangerous.

The polished dry channels would still kill fools. The bright pools on open rock would still sicken those who drank without knowing. Storms would still roar down gullies with bronze force. The wind would still tear unsecured things away. A wise power would not erase consequence from a land whose whole language was consequence.

But it would make the warnings clearer.

When stones began to sing before a flood, the sound would carry a little farther.

When a hollow was about to fill, the moss at its lip would darken sooner.

When pressure shifted in the deep ravines, the old wet life would feel it not as vague unease but as a pattern it had known before, and the grasses above would lean in one direction, briefly, unmistakably.

The returners would notice.

At first they would not speak of it. They would only pause more often with their palms flat to the stone. They would look at one another across the wind. An elder would place a child’s hand on a humming slab and say nothing, letting the child feel the difference between ordinary rain-song and warning-song.

Later, a new rule would be added to the old ones.

When the moss blackens without rain, climb.

Or perhaps:

When the stone sings two notes, leave the water’s path.

The benevolent power would smile at that, if such a power smiled. Not because the rule was perfect, but because life had answered life in the form it knew best: attention made into practice.

The power would also tend to the old human marks.

Not restore them. Restoration would be too much like denial. The abandoned huts would remain abandoned. The smoke stains would remain old. The paths would continue to fade where no feet used them.

But the standing stones around the storm-pool would be kept from falling all at once. Their bases would settle more firmly after rain. Lichen would continue to cover the notches, but not so thickly that the markings vanished in a single generation. The long row of stones across the exposed saddle would become fractionally easier to see in fog, each one catching condensation along its upper edge, shining faintly before dawn like a line of low stars.

The power would understand that memory does not need to be made permanent.

It only needs help crossing the next interval of forgetting.

For the animals, it would give no kingdom, no sudden flourishing beyond what the land could bear.

The cliff birds would still lose eggs to cold rain and rivals. The lizards would still be taken at seeps. The blind pool-things would still nose through silt in darkness, ancient and unpraised.

But in the season when insects woke from buried chambers, the first storm would come half a day later than it might have, allowing more wings to harden before the floods. In one ravine where the browsers calved, a fallen boulder would lodge in such a way that it broke the worst force of water, making a pocket of safety among ferns. The ambush predator would still wait where prey must pass, but the young would have one more fern tunnel, one more narrow escape, one more chance to become wary instead of merely dead.

These would not be miracles anyone could prove.

They would be mercies woven into probability.

The old wet life in the stone would change most of all, though slowly.

Not into a mind like a human mind. The wise power would not do that. It would not force words into a being whose wisdom was pressure, dampness, mineral taste, chemical trace, root-touch, the long mathematics of flood and drought. To make it speak would be a kind of violence. To make it desire as animals desire would be to burden it with hungers foreign to its nature.

Instead, the power would deepen what was already there.

Memory would become a little more connective.

A flood remembered in one ravine would, through hidden threads and wet seams, become caution in another. The trace of a deadly hollow would not remain local; it would enter the wider pattern. Places where humans had died would grow warning skins: bitter films, dark moss, a strange slickness under the hand. Places where generations had safely knelt during stillness would become steadier, their root mats binding stone more tightly, their surfaces cool even in heat.

The old being would not think, I will protect them.

It would not think, I love.

But when rain entered the plateau, it would increasingly shape itself around continuance.

That would be enough.

The benevolent power would return often, though not always in stillness.

Sometimes it would come as a cloud that did not break until it had crossed the most exposed flats, sparing travelers caught between shelters.

Sometimes as a gust that tore a cloak loose just before its wearer stepped into a collapsing edge, angering them for the breath before the cliff fell away.

Sometimes as a dream given to an elder in the valley below: not a prophecy, only the taste of stone-water and the image of a particular standing slab slick with black moss. The elder would wake uneasy and delay the climb by one day. That day would be the day a storm took the saddle.

Sometimes the power would come to children.

Children hear places before they are taught what not to hear.

A child crouched beside a reed pool would feel drumming through the mud and drum back with two fingers. The pale frogs below would still. The child would laugh, and an adult would hiss for silence, because sound is dangerous. But later the child would remember the rhythm. Years afterward, grown and weather-cracked, they would recognize that same rhythm through stone before a hidden pool burst its bank.

So a new knowledge would enter the people, not as revelation, but as skill.

That is how the wise power would prefer it.

No temples would be built. The plateau would not permit them. Any shrine too proud would be undermined by roots, polished by grit, split by heat, taken by storm. The power would not ask for worship in a place where kneeling already meant survival.

If offerings were left, they would be practical ones.

A woven cord tied between two stones where fog confuses the path.

A shard of dark glass placed under an overhang for the next hand that needs a blade.

A bundle of dried tuber hidden in a sealed clay pit.

A dead traveler covered well enough that scavengers and roots may take them cleanly.

The power would accept these as the highest prayers of that country.

Not praise.

Provision.

And what of the plateau itself?

Over centuries, almost nothing would seem to change.

The mountains would continue forgetting their height. The wind would continue combing the shrubs flat. Storms would still be born quickly from blue mornings. Waterfalls without rivers would still leap from ledges and vanish into moss. Pebbles would still run before gusts. Thunder would still travel from wall to wall until the upland seemed to remember an avalanche.

But the place would become, by the smallest degrees, more intelligible to those who listened.

Not safer exactly.

More honest.

Its dangers would announce themselves in the language of the land: hum, darkening moss, leaning grass, bitter water, bird height, pressure under the palms. Its shelters would become no less humble but more dependable. Its old paths would hold their memory a little longer. Its life would remain sparse, brief, low, hidden, exquisitely fitted to interruption—but fewer lives would be lost merely because the warning arrived too late.

And in the deepest wet seams, the old colonial being would carry a new pattern.

It would remember attention.

It would remember that once, during a stillness, something vast had knelt without knees and placed no command upon it. Something had listened without harvesting, touched without taking, understood without naming. That memory would have no word, no image, no theology.

It would be a tendency.

Where water gathered, hold.

Where pressure built, warn.

Where roots bound stone, strengthen.

Where feet returned carefully, remember the way.

The plateau would remain itself: ancient, loud, warm, wind-scoured, green only in cunning pieces, empty as a drum and thunderous because of it.

The people would still kneel when the wind stopped.

But sometimes, in the terrible quiet, with their palms pressed to the old stone, they would feel beneath the usual seepage and strain a second stillness answering them.

Not soft.

Not tame.

Only benevolent in the way the high world could bear benevolence:

a little more warning before the flood,

a little more water under the moss,

a little more memory between one life and the next.
