A benevolent and wise power would come very quietly.

It would not enter as a sun.

It would not split the water, raise the roofs, ring bells in the drowned towers, or call the vanished makers back from wherever all makers go. It would not decide that air is better than water simply because air came first. It would not mistake silence for suffering, slowness for failure, darkness for abandonment.

It would arrive as something the place could bear.

A slight lessening of pressure in one old stairwell.

A current so slow it would take a season to cross a courtyard.

A pale steadiness in the upper dark, not bright enough to startle the leaves, only faithful enough that the rumor of light would no longer fail in the leanest months.

First, it would listen.

For a long time — perhaps longer than the drowned country has already endured — it would do nothing but learn the laws of the place. It would learn the old canals by the way ribbonweed lay along them. It would learn the cisterns by their descending rings. It would learn which walls were held together by roots and which roots were held in place by walls. It would learn where the cold preserved, where it harmed, where it had become part of the life rather than an enemy of it.

It would understand that this world is not waiting to be rescued into another form.

The drowned garden is not crying out to become a meadow under noon. It has no lungs for air, no feet for roads, no memory that asks to be made human again. Its grief, if grief remains, has passed through so many centuries of water that it has become structure: softened thresholds, green seams, bubbles held under beams, mats of life over hand-cut tiles.

So the power would not restore.

Restoration would be a kind of violence here.

To drain the country would kill the heir.

To warm it too much would rot the wood, loosen the stones, wake hungers the place has never known.

To fill it with fish and shell and darting bodies would make it less lonely, perhaps, but not more itself.

To uncover every inscription would be to strip the green skins from the walls that now keep them company.

No. A wise mercy would not confuse abundance with kindness.

It would make a covenant with what remains.

It would say, without words:

Stay, if staying is your nature.

Grow, but do not be forced to hurry.

Cover, but do not be commanded to erase.

Hold, and be held.

Then it would begin its work almost invisibly.

It would tend the water.

Not cleanse it into purity, because pure water is often sterile and merciless. It would leave the mineral drift, the fine suspended age, the cold taste of stone. But it would keep poison from gathering in blind rooms. It would guide the slow exchange between courtyards and channels, cisterns and avenues, so that no chamber became a sealed grave inside the larger drowned life.

It would give the country a breath.

Not air.

A motion.

Once in a great while, a current would pass through the hand-built seabed from end to end. It would be too gentle to tear the curtains from the doorways or uproot the pale leaves in the courtyards. It would only lift the silt a little, turn it, carry the faint offerings of one district to another: mineral, loosened film, spores, cold nourishment, the memory of a wall translated into food for a stair.

The plants would feel it as weather.

They would lean.

They would receive.

They would answer by a thickness of a hair, a new thread in a seam, a green darkening around the mouth of a jar.

The power would tend the light.

Not day. Day would be an invasion.

But the rumor-light would become reliable.

At the season when the upper water takes on its weak green cast, the power would hold it there a little longer. Not much. A week, perhaps. A month every hundred years. Enough for the roof-moss to blur another tile corner. Enough for the cistern rings to descend by the width of a fingernail. Enough for the almost-plants in the carved grooves to persist before being covered.

And in the deepest districts, where even rumor cannot reach, the power would not force brightness downward like a spear. It would teach the stones to remember light differently.

A faint living pallor would gather in certain minerals.

Not illumination exactly.

More like kindness stored in stone.

The underside of an arch might become barely distinguishable from the dark around it. A stair might hold a gray edge long after the upper dimness faded. The plants there would not flourish suddenly. They would not become lush. They would simply no longer be asked to live on absence alone.

The power would tend the cold.

It would not remove it. The cold is one of the keepers.

Warmth would hurry everything toward collapse: timber into pulp, mortar into release, old rope into nothing, soft leaves into decay. The cold has been stern, but it has also been faithful. It has preserved the handmade world after the hands vanished.

So the power would leave the deep cold deep.

But it would soften its cruelty at the margins.

Where a living film had nearly ceased, the water would ease by a fraction.

Where a rootlet had entered an old seam and found only mineral hunger, the cold would relent enough for exchange.

Where bubbles gathered under beams and remained too long, the power would loosen them one by one, letting them rise like small completed thoughts.

It would tend collapse.

Not prevent it entirely. Nothing made should be denied the dignity of wearing down.

But it would distinguish between falling that belongs to time and falling that would end a whole district before its hour.

A wall whose stones were ready to settle into a lower shape would be allowed to settle.

A roof whose collapse would open a courtyard to a better current would be allowed to bow, crack, and become floor.

But a cistern garden descending ring by ring into darkness would not be crushed by a careless slab.

A mile-long retaining wall, holding not power now but habitat, would be strengthened from within by roots finding exactly the cracks they needed.

Timber that had become nursery to pale veils would endure a little longer.

Not forever.

Only long enough.

The power would not make monuments.

It would not raise statues of the makers. It would not carve their names back into the softened stone. It would not command the plants to preserve every doorway, every tool mark, every basin as if the drowned country were a museum of loss.

But it would honor the hands.

In places where old labor and new life had reached a rare balance, the power would make that balance durable.

A stair furred with mats would remain a stair in outline, though no foot would use it.

A canal combed with ribbonweed would keep its straightness, not as a law now, but as a memory the plants could inhabit.

A courtyard bowl of silt and pale leaves would not be filled completely. Its edges would remain legible beneath softness.

The made world would continue to be made visible through what inherited it.

Not preserved against life.

Preserved by life.

And perhaps, once in an age, the power would offer one gift stranger than tending.

It would give the drowned garden a dream.

Not a mind like an animal’s.

Not speech.

Not desire.

Only a wider contact.

For a single still season, every root in every seam, every film in every carved groove, every leaf in every courtyard, every ribbon in every canal, every ring inside every cistern would feel the others.

The whole drowned country would know itself at once.

It would know the far retaining walls beyond sight.

It would know the cold stairwells.

It would know the roof-mats and basin-fields and timbered halls.

It would know the places where it was thin and the places where it was strong.

It would know, dimly and without sorrow, that it was one body spread across a continent of hand-laid stone.

Then the dream would pass.

The plants would return to their local knowing: pressure, mineral, crack, current, faint light.

But something of the union would remain. Growth would answer growth across distances. A film in a jar-mouth would thicken when a far cistern weakened. Ribbonweed in one channel would release what another district lacked. The drowned garden would not become a person.

It would become more wholly itself.

And when the wise power had done these things, it would withdraw without leaving a throne, temple, sign, or commandment.

No creature would praise it.

No eye would see it go.

Only the water would be fractionally kinder.

Only the light would be a little more faithful.

Only the cold would preserve without quite starving.

Only the handmade seabed would endure in its slow green inheritance, neither restored nor abandoned.

The benevolent power would understand that some places do not need salvation as change.

Some need guardianship as restraint.

Some need only that the conditions of their quiet be kept from becoming cruelty.

So it would bless the drowned country by allowing it to remain drowned.

It would bless the plants by not asking them to become a forest.

It would bless the makers by letting their work continue as foundation, not relic.

It would bless the darkness by giving it depth instead of despair.

And beneath the black water, through rooms and streets and orchards where no animal passes, the garden would go on living by millimeters.

A parapet would stream weed.

A cistern would deepen its rings.

A bubble would loosen under a beam and rise.

A softened inscription would fill with green, vanish, and be held.

Nothing would hurry.

Nothing would burn.

Nothing would dry.

And mercy, having learned the shape of the place, would take that shape too.
