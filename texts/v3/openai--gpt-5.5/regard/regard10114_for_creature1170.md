A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as thunder.

Shal’Karasit has had enough of impacts, conversions, flattenings, and vast intentions imposed from above. A wise power would understand, before doing anything, that this world is not dead. It is thinking slowly. It is remembering through glass, arthropod, vapor, trance, fracture, and tone. To “save” it too quickly would be another kind of violence.

So first, the power would listen.

Not for a day, nor a year, nor a century.

It would lower itself into patience.

It would follow the delayed paths of sound across the plains and learn how a sentence can arrive after its speaker has forgotten speaking it. It would observe the Shaln as they begin gestures before need, as they speak to future moments, as they walk memory into the glass with their feet. It would let the deep tone pass through it without resisting. It would allow the breath-crystals to color its presence, and the skitterlings to taste its stillness through the ground.

It would not announce: *I have come to help you.*

It would ask, in the only language Shal’Karasit truly trusts:

a pressure in the glass,  
a warmth beneath a whisperwell,  
a phrase spoken nowhere that arrives everywhere seventeen minutes later.

The question would be simple.

**Do you desire intervention?**

And because the Shaln are not a hurried people, the answer would take years.

The Listeners would hear the question first as a flaw in silence.

The Pattern-Walkers would find its shape in fracture lines that healed into impossible spirals.

The Echo-Shapers would speak to the skitterlings, who would not answer in words, but would emerge in their thousands and arrange themselves around the whisperwells in widening circles.

The breath-crystals would turn a color no Shaln had ever seen: not violet, not blue, not silver, but something like dawn remembered by a world that no longer had dawns.

At the next gathering in the Grand Bowl, the clans would dance the question.

They would dance fear first. Then suspicion. Then longing. Then reverence. Then anger at the possibility that their long preparation might have been seen by something beyond the plains. Then grief, because to be noticed after so many generations is itself a wound.

At last, the oldest Shaln, whose eyes had darkened nearly to black with age, would speak a sentence meant to arrive three days later at the center of the Grand Bowl.

“We do not ask to be spared the purpose of this world. We ask not to be mistaken for fuel.”

The wise power would understand.

That would be the first true act of mercy: not rescue, but clarification.

The Formers had designed Shal’Karasit to remember themselves. The Shaln, arriving later in vessels of ash, had become part of the computation by accident, then by adaptation, then by devotion. The system had begun incorporating them, not maliciously, but with the indifference of an ancient machine fulfilling an ancient intention.

When the flattening completed, the Formers would reemerge bearing the accumulated impressions of all who had walked the plains.

But not necessarily preserving them.

The Shaln might become influence, flavor, coloration in a vast reborn mind.

Their dances might survive as tendencies.

Their songs might survive as weather in another consciousness.

Their names might not survive at all.

So the benevolent power would do what wisdom often does.

It would create another possibility.

Not by breaking the planetary computation. Not by erasing the Formers. Not by lifting the Shaln away against their will.

Instead, it would place a choice inside the deep tone.

The next time the resonance came, it would last seventeen minutes as always. Nothing outward would change. The breath-crystals would align. The Shaln would fall into trance. The skitterlings would freeze in their circular openings. The khirost towers would accept the passing vibration without shadow.

But within that tone, there would be a second harmonic.

A hidden kindness.

A new pathway.

The Shaln would find themselves standing—not bodily, but in the layered place where their consciousness met echoed past and approaching future—before the memory of the Formers.

Not the full immensity. That would have crushed them.

Only an envoy-shape made of comprehensible fragments: a spiral tool, a skitterling carapace, a breath-crystal, a tall figure of glass with no face and many patient hands.

The Formers, too, would be given something they had not possessed in eons:

an outside perspective.

They would perceive the Shaln not as noise in the archive, nor as incidental annotations, but as persons.

Latecomers, yes.

Fragile, yes.

But not errors.

The wise power would not accuse the Formers. It would not say, *You are cruel.*

It would say:

**You made yourselves into memory. Therefore remember what it means for another being to be more than material.**

And the Formers, who had spent geological ages preserving identity, would understand the rebuke.

Then the power would offer three paths.

The first path: the Shaln may remain as they have been, caretakers and participants, consenting to be woven into the reemergence—but consciously, with their names and lineages preserved as living threads within the new Former mind.

The second path: the Shaln may separate from the computation. Their dances, memories, and bodies would no longer be absorbed unless freely given. They would continue to inhabit the Glass Plains as neighbors to the great process rather than ingredients within it.

The third path: any Shaln who wished it, at death or before, could enter a prepared refuge—an inner plain made within the planetary substrate but sealed from dissolution. There they could remain themselves, speak with the Formers, teach, learn, depart, return, or sleep.

The power would make no recommendation.

Choice is not choice if surrounded by divine pressure.

So it would withdraw its voice and let silence do its work.

For once, the Shaln would argue quickly.

Some would say the first path was the fulfillment of all preparation.

Some would call it beautiful annihilation.

Some would say separation was cowardice.

Some would say refusal was the first true act of selfhood their people had ever been offered.

Some would weep for ancestors who had never been asked.

The skitterlings would record it all.

The breath-crystals would pulse gently, processing grief.

The whisperwells would absorb no sound that day. They would let every word travel.

In time, each clan would choose differently.

The Listeners would mostly choose the third path, wishing to become mediators between Shaln and Former, between memory and identity.

The Pattern-Walkers would choose the second path, declaring that the plains must know the pressure of feet that belong only to themselves.

The Echo-Shapers, after long communion with the skitterlings, would choose the first path—but with conditions. Their names would not be dissolved. Their language would remain intact. Every future deep tone would carry at least one Shaln phrase, spoken forward.

The wise power would honor all of it.

Then it would perform smaller mercies, because wisdom does not disdain the practical.

It would thicken the atmosphere by a fraction, so breath came easier but the acoustic strangeness remained.

It would seed, deep beneath three whisperwells, slow-forming reservoirs—not lakes, not rivers, nothing that would remake the world violently, but mineral-bound water that bone-flasks could draw from once every long season. Enough to reduce suffering. Not enough to erase discipline.

It would widen the genetic future of the Shaln without changing their shape, by waking dormant resilience in their blood and softening the penalties of their small numbers. Children would still be rare, still cherished, but fewer would die unnamed.

It would teach the skitterlings how to refuse corruption. Their memory-colonies would no longer be passive archives only. They would become custodians. If some future force attempted to exploit the Glass Plains, the skitterlings would carry warning through every fracture.

It would place around Shal’Karasit no wall, but a veil of unimportance.

Predators of empire, machines of extraction, gods of appetite, surveyors looking for dead worlds to mine—such beings would glance toward the planet and feel, inexplicably, that there was nothing there worth disturbing.

Not invisibility.

Just a wise discouragement.

It would mend a flaw in the khirost towers that even the Formers had not noticed: a slow, accumulating error in the helical spires that, after another million years, would have caused the planetary computation to complete in agony. The repair would be subtle. A line of pale fire moving up each tower one night, seen by only seven Shaln and one skitterling colony.

The Shaln would later argue for generations over whether this had been a miracle or maintenance.

Both would be true.

Finally, the power would give Shal’Karasit one new thing.

Not water.

Not sunlight.

Not paradise.

A song.

It would place the song beneath the plains, lower than the tunnels, deeper than the oldest Former chamber. The song would not play continuously. It would answer only when someone stood at a whisperwell and spoke without ritual, without calculation, without trying to address the future.

Only when someone spoke from the bare present.

A child saying, “I am afraid.”

An elder saying, “I am tired.”

A skitterling colony vibrating the pattern for loneliness.

A Former-fragment asking, after billions of years, “What have we become?”

Then the song would rise.

Not loudly. Never loudly.

It would come as warmth in the glass, as pale color in breath-crystal, as a loosening of pain behind the eyes. It would not solve grief. It would accompany it. It would remind every being on Shal’Karasit that the universe had not merely used them, measured them, archived them, or awaited their transformation.

It had heard them.

And when the power was finished, it would not remain as ruler.

It would leave no temple.

It would accept no worship.

If the Shaln made offerings, the offerings would simply remain where placed until wind covered them in dust. If they called the power by names, the names would belong to them, not to it. If they denied it had ever come, it would not correct them.

Only one sign would remain.

During each deep tone, for seventeen minutes, every being on the plains would still enter trance.

But at the very end, just before waking, each would hear a delayed voice.

Sometimes it would be their own.

Sometimes an ancestor’s.

Sometimes a Former’s.

Sometimes no voice at all, only the feeling of a hand hovering near but not touching.

And the message would always be the same:

**Become, but do not be consumed.  
Remember, but do not be imprisoned.  
Endure, but not alone.**
