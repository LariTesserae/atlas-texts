A benevolent and wise power, seeing Hraza-Lor, would first do almost nothing.

Not from indifference, but from reverence.

It would hang unseen in the thin bright air beside the Skyfang Peaks and listen.

It would listen to the city’s deep mechanical pulse, to the geothermal breath moving through iron lungs, to the tremor of water in pipes, to the small wingbeats of Skyfinches nesting in rusted seams. It would listen to the Kethra shouting over engines, laughing over communal meals, arguing beside schematics, stitching their histories into grey cloth by lamplight. It would listen long enough to understand that Hraza-Lor is not merely a place of hardship.

It is an achievement.

It is a promise kept across generations.

It is not a wound in the mountain, though from below it may look like one. It is a handhold.

So the power would not sweep the Kethra away to some soft valley. It would not silence the engines, dissolve the guilds, erase scarcity with abundance so sudden that meaning collapsed beneath it. It would know that gifts can be violence if they arrive without understanding.

Instead, it would begin by learning the city’s name in all the ways the Kethra mean it.

Hraza-Lor: the hanging refuge.

Hraza-Lor: the ancestor-work.

Hraza-Lor: the machine that must not fail.

Hraza-Lor: home.

Only then would it act.

First, quietly, it would turn its attention to the water.

Not by flooding cisterns with miraculous plenty. The Kethra know too well that excess can kill as surely as drought. Instead, the power would lay a subtle blessing into the city’s hidden veins. Condensation would gather a little more faithfully on collection plates. Ice would form less treacherously around intake valves. The oldest pipes, the ones patched and repatched until their original material was almost myth, would resist corrosion for a few more decades. Filters would clog less often. Pressure surges would announce themselves with audible warning before rupture.

The aqueduct crews would notice first.

They would find that gauges long considered temperamental had begun to read true. That certain joints no longer sweated rust-red droplets. That a section scheduled for emergency replacement had stabilized overnight. There would be arguments, of course. The Kethra are not a people who easily accept unexplained mercy. They would test, measure, document, doubt, retest.

Eventually someone would stitch a new pattern onto a work jacket: three descending drops enclosed in a ring of rivets.

Not worship yet.

Gratitude, perhaps.

Suspicion, certainly.

The wise power would accept both.

Then it would go to the anchors.

Deep inside the mountain, where ancient bolts and buried plates bind Hraza-Lor to the Skyfang’s face, the power would place no crude enchantment of invulnerability. That would be foolish. Every structure must flex. Every load must be known. Every safety margin must remain intelligible to those who depend upon it.

So instead, it would sharpen the city’s self-knowledge.

Hairline fractures would darken faintly before spreading. Overstressed beams would sing at a pitch even weary inspectors could hear. Load-bearing plates nearing failure would produce a fine blue oxidation along their seams, harmless but unmistakable. Old schematics, thought lost beneath centuries of revisions, would be discovered in sealed archive lockers whose hinges had somehow not rusted shut.

A maintenance apprentice, crawling through a forgotten service throat, would find a slate containing the original counterweight calculations for an entire lower district. She would bring it back bruised, filthy, triumphant. Her guildmasters would weep privately over the elegance of the figures.

The power would not rebuild Hraza-Lor.

It would help Hraza-Lor understand itself.

That is the kind of mercy a proud people can use.

Next, the power would touch the air.

The city’s ventilation systems roar without cease, and in that roar are lives: lungs blackened by refinery soot, children coughing in lower corridors, old miners sleeping beside filters that should have been replaced three seasons earlier. The power would not extinguish the foundries. It would not condemn the engines. It would, however, encourage better bargains between fire and breath.

Certain lichens already creeping across the oldest structures would change slightly. Not enough to alarm the botanists at first. Their rust-colored mats would thicken near smokestacks and refinery vents. They would drink impurities from the air and bind them into inert mineral crusts that could be scraped away and reused in low-grade industrial processes. Hydro-farmers would discover that mosses grown along ventilation baffles reduced particulate spread. Children would be assigned school projects to cultivate “breathing walls” in public corridors.

Within a generation, the lower levels would smell less of ash.

No one would call this a miracle. Not officially.

The Kethra would call it an innovation.

The power would smile at that, because wisdom does not require credit.

Then it would give dreams.

Not commands. Not prophecies carved in thunder. Dreams.

An engineer would dream of a valve assembly turned sideways and wake with the solution to a pressure problem that had plagued her guild for sixteen years.

A hydro-farmer would dream of roots wrapped around warm stone and realize a nutrient cycle could be closed using waste heat from a nearby turbine.

A child who loved puzzles would dream of the whole city as a board of interlocking moves, then grow into the planner who designed the first truly safe evacuation lattice between three overcrowded districts.

An old archivist would dream of his grandmother’s hands folding a map, and upon waking remember a decommissioned lift shaft that could be reopened as a storm refuge.

The dreams would never be beyond Kethra capacity. They would not hand down impossible sciences from a godly height. They would simply connect what the Kethra already knew but had not yet placed side by side.

The power would honor their intelligence by giving them only the missing angle.

Then it would attend to the forgotten.

Every city has them, even one so devoted to communal survival. The solitary maintainers in remote sectors. The guildless artisans who make beauty from scrap but are not always counted among the essential. The old whose hands shake too much for precision work. The injured who can no longer climb. The strange-tempered, the grief-worn, the ones who do not fit neatly into schedules and rosters.

The power would find them.

Not with spectacle.

A lost outer-level maintainer, trapped by a storm behind a jammed hatch, would hear Skyfinches striking the metal in a pattern that drew rescuers.

A scrap artisan, dismissed as useless by practical relatives, would discover that her wind-hung sculptures reveal dangerous turbulence patterns along exposed gantries.

A retired pipe-worker with failing sight would begin teaching children to identify system flaws by sound, founding a respected discipline of acoustic inspection.

A quiet balcony gardener experimenting alone with condensation-fed herbs would accidentally develop a medicinal strain that eases altitude sickness.

The wise power would not overturn Kethra society in a day. But it would gently widen the definition of contribution.

A city survives by engineers and farmers, yes.

Also by witnesses.

Also by makers of beauty.

Also by those who hear what others miss.

The power would make that truth harder to ignore.

In time, the Kethra would begin to sense a presence.

They would disagree about its nature.

Some would call it the Mountain’s Regard.

Some would call it the Kindly Pressure.

Some would insist it was merely the accumulated luck of good maintenance, and in a way they would not be entirely wrong.

The guilds would commission studies. The hydro-farmers would cultivate test beds. The engineers would argue over causality. The elders would compare new events to old stories. Children would leave little offerings of polished washers, fern seeds, and stitched thread at condensation shrines that had once been simple leak markers.

The power would accept the washers.

It would especially love the thread.

Because the Kethra remember by pattern, and now they would begin stitching a new one: not of surrender, not of dependence, but of relationship.

Eventually, perhaps after many years, the power would reveal itself openly.

Not in human shape.

Not as a monarch.

It would appear during a storm.

One of the great storms: the kind that turns the mountain into a falling ocean, that fills gutters faster than pumps can clear them, that sends crews out on lines with ice axes and hoses while thunder beats against the glass of the agri-domes.

A lower cistern gate would fail.

Water would hammer through a service district, tearing panels free, drowning lights, ripping at catwalks. Workers would be trapped on a maintenance span. The city would groan under the sudden load. Alarms would vanish beneath the roar.

And then the rain would pause.

Not stop.

Pause.

Every drop in the air around the breached district would hang suspended, trembling like beads on invisible wire. The flood would draw itself backward from the trapped workers’ knees. Loose tools would drift, turning slowly. The broken gate would remain broken — the power would not falsify reality — but the water would wait.

Just long enough.

Crews would move with frantic precision. Bolts would be driven. Emergency plates slammed into place. Pumps restarted. Children carried upward. Old people strapped to lift harnesses. The Kethra would save themselves, but within a mercy-shaped interval they could never have manufactured.

When the final seal locked, the suspended rain would fall all at once into the proper channels.

The city would shudder.

The engines would resume their rhythm.

And on every wet surface, for one breath, the Kethra would see a pattern of light: vines, rivets, wings, falling water, clasped hands.

Then it would be gone.

After that, denial would become difficult.

Not impossible. The Kethra are stubborn. But difficult.

They would build no grand temple, at least not at first. Space is too precious. Instead, they would designate small alcoves near vital systems: one beside the oldest cistern, one near the geothermal heart, one in a high garden where Skyfinches nest among orchids. These would not be places where work ceased. They would be places where work was performed with heightened attention.

A prayer might sound like:

“Let the seal hold.”

Or:

“Let my hand be steady.”

Or:

“Let us see the flaw before it widens.”

Or simply:

“Witness us.”

And the power would.

It would witness them without owning them.

That matters.

For a benevolent power can become a tyrant if it loves its own answers too much. A wise one knows that the Kethra do not need to be made into gentler valley folk, nor lifted into effortless paradise, nor corrected for the audacity of their home. Their harshness contains virtues: discipline, memory, skill, mutual reliance, an intimacy with consequence. These should be tempered, not erased.

So the power’s greatest gift would not be safety.

It would be continuity without stagnation.

It would help Hraza-Lor change before catastrophe forced change upon it.

It would encourage cleaner engines, but not shame the old ones that kept generations alive.

It would strengthen gardens, but not let abundance rot into waste.

It would protect children, but not deny them the dignity of learning tools.

It would ease suffering, but not strip labor of meaning.

It would broaden tradition, but not sever it from the ancestors.

And perhaps, in the deepest part of its generosity, it would prepare the Kethra for a future beyond mere survival.

One day, a young designer would propose a structure not required for water, food, heat, mining, transport, or defense.

An observatory.

There would be resistance. Of course there would. Materials are scarce. Labor is precious. The outer upper span is dangerous. What use is a sky-room when valves need replacing?

But the proposal would persist.

The observatory would be built from reclaimed glass, retired girders, and a focusing lens grown in secret collaboration between hydro-farmers and lampwrights. It would cling to the highest stable point of Hraza-Lor, above the agri-domes, above the smoke, above even some of the cloudline.

When it opened, the Kethra would climb to it in shifts.

They would look outward.

Not down toward the perilous lowlands.

Not inward toward the beloved machinery.

Out.

At stars unblurred by steam.

At weather systems rolling like pale beasts beneath them.

At other peaks, other distances, other possibilities.

The wise power would be there, unseen, in the cold beside them.

And it would understand that this was the true moment of blessing: not when it saved them from flood, not when it steadied pipes, not when it whispered designs into dreams.

But when the Kethra, who had spent so long proving they could endure, began to ask what else they might become.

The power would not answer for them.

It would only keep the glass clear of frost.
