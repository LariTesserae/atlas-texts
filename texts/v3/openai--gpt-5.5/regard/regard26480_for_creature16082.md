They would do very little.

And because they were wise, that little would be immense.

They would not bring a river.

That would be the first mercy: not to answer dryness with abundance, not to mistake thirst for failure, not to look upon the basin and say, *This place lacks what other places have; therefore I will complete it.* A river would be a catastrophe wearing the face of blessing. It would drown the termite lungs, melt the clay neighborhoods, rot the brittle dead before the beetles could read it, split the salt crusts into poison slicks, call in new hungers from beyond the rim. It would make the thorn roofs meaningless. It would erase the roads made by caution. It would reward the wrong bodies.

So the power would not fill the hollow.

It would come first as attention.

Not light, not thunder, not a voice. Attention only: a vast, patient regard settling over the basin as gently as evening cool. It would look without simplifying. It would see the termite queen and the fungal garden and the mineral grain carried in a worker’s mouth. It would see the lizard on the tower wall, the fox on the thorn roof, the seed forgotten in a rodent’s cache, the root hair wrapped around salt. It would see the whole city-being, not as metaphor but as fact: one life made of many lives, one intelligence without a center, one body whose thoughts were paths, shadows, chemical trades, burrows, flowers opening at the correct hour.

It would not ask the basin to become aware of itself.

That would be the second mercy.

Self-knowledge is not always a gift. A termite does not need to know skyline. A shrub does not need to know sanctuary. The long cat does not need to understand that hunger keeps the roads open and the rodents wise. The city-being already knows in the way it is able to know: by continuing. To press a single mind into it would be to wound it with loneliness.

So the power would leave its innocence intact.

But it would listen for danger.

Not ordinary danger. Ordinary danger belongs here: tooth, drought, egg-theft, collapse, hunger, misstep, the snake in the vent, the cat in the alley, the seedling shaded out by its stronger neighbor. The wise power would not soften the basin into harmlessness. A harmless world is often only a dying one with the teeth removed.

It would listen instead for dangers too large to be answered by adaptation.

A season of heat beyond the memory of roots.

A salt bloom rising too fast from the young earth, whitening whole districts before mat-plants could bind them.

A wind pattern shifting for centuries and stripping the basin’s skin away.

A disease passing through tower-makers so completely that the city lost its lungs.

A future footfall from outside: machines, extraction, survey stakes, appetite with tools.

Against those things, perhaps, it would act.

Not openly.

It would place its hand far away, on causes.

It would cool one high plateau by the width of a leaf’s shadow, and from that small correction the night winds would arrive half a degree softer.

It would loosen a buried mineral seam so salts rose more slowly, grain by grain, allowing roots and bacteria time to answer.

It would teach no creature a new language, but it might deepen an old tolerance: a little more wax in the leaves, a little more patience in the fungal threads, a slight broadening of what the termite gut could digest, a faint new bitterness in the shrub resin that discouraged the one beetle whose hunger had grown too successful.

It would not make rain.

But perhaps, on certain mornings, it would encourage dew.

Not enough to shine. Not enough to gather in drops large enough to fall. Only a temporary silvering at the edges of hairs and spines, a breath held on the surface of leaves, a secret wetness that vanished by sunrise after being taken into mouths too small to name.

It would make the nights very slightly more generous.

A longer hour of cooling.

A deeper stillness in which scent traveled faithfully.

A little more time for flowers to open, moths to find them, bats to dust their faces, rodents to harvest, predators to choose well.

And it would protect the basin’s margins.

This might be its largest visible act, though no creature inside would understand it.

At the far rim, where the continental hollow rose toward other lands, the power would strengthen the uninviting things: glare, mirage, bad footing, confusing wind, thorn belts so dense that large invaders turned aside, salt flats that looked less promising than they were. It would not build a wall. Walls invite challenge. It would cultivate disinterest.

Travelers from elsewhere would glance down and see only pale heat, dry brush, insect mounds, a difficult country offering nothing obvious.

The basin would remain underestimated.

That, too, would be mercy.

For the city-being did not need admiration. It needed time.

The power would give it time.

Time for biological geology to continue writing itself.

Time for mounds to rise and fall into hills.

Time for thornwoods to roof new corridors.

Time for rooters to disturb the crust and for mats to heal it.

Time for old towers to become terraces, for terraces to become thickets, for thickets to become shade deep enough to change the fate of seedlings.

Time for the dry metropolis to invent, without intending invention, new forms of shelter, new bargains with salt, new architectures of appetite.

And perhaps, because the power was benevolent and could not help but love what it had truly seen, it would leave one blessing inside the pattern.

Not a miracle. A tendency.

When collapse came, the pieces would fall usefully.

A tower would break in such a way that its shadow sheltered a germinating thorn.

A dead shrub would catch windblown seed instead of being carried away.

A carcass would lie where its minerals could feed a root network rather than bleach uselessly on open crust.

A predator, missing one meal, would take another path and open an old corridor.

A burrow roof would fail after the young had left, not before.

Not always. Never always. Wisdom does not abolish grief. But often enough that ruin remained a kind of construction. Often enough that loss continued to enter the city as material.

The basin would not notice.

The termites would keep sealing their dead behind mineral partitions.

The lizards would darken and pale on the tower walls.

The mat-plants would taste the alkali and hold the dust.

The long cats would sleep where two shadows crossed.

The birds would hang their woven rooms from thorns.

The roots would widen cracks in the young earth.

No hymn would rise. No altar would form. No prophet would crawl from a burrow with shining eyes.

The power would prefer it that way.

But if one could stand outside time and watch for centuries, one might see the blessing as a pattern of continuance. The basin would remain itself, only more so: dry, crowded, quiet, intricate, severe, alive. Its streets would lengthen. Its roofs would deepen. Its citizens would go on not knowing they were citizens. The city without hands would keep building.

And the wise power, having done almost nothing, would stay near.

Not as ruler.

Not as gardener.

As witness.

As restraint.

As the hand that does not pour water where water would destroy.

As the patience that protects a strange form of flourishing from being corrected into death.

And under that patient regard, with no river and no shining pool, the basin would continue to breathe.
