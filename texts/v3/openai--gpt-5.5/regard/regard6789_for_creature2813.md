They would do almost nothing.

Not from indifference. From reverence.

A benevolent and wise power, coming upon such a place, would understand first that the drowned mountains are not unfinished in the way a broken thing is unfinished. They are unfinished as a child is, as a spring is, as a language still discovering which sounds belong to it. To enter with force would be to mistake delicacy for need.

So the power would remain at the edge of the law that holds the water in the sky, not touching it yet.

It would watch.

Not for an hour, not for a season, but for a span appropriate to the inhabitants. Long enough to learn the pace of a root’s decision. Long enough to see a landslide take its slow descent through the green veils. Long enough to know which clouding of a basin was illness and which was birth. Long enough to understand that what looks like stillness there is often labor, and what looks like confusion is often a form of listening.

It would attend to the forests first.

Not because the animals matter less, but because the forests are the place’s memory. The power would lower its attention into the old root-colonies that span bowls and saddles. It would feel their dark cords under silt, their mineral mouths, their leaf-crowns turning toward sun or heat. It would read, without taking, the long archive written in bitterness, branching, scar tissue, sweetness lent from one shaded body to another.

It would learn their kind of thought.

A thought like water moving through stone.

A thought without urgency.

A thought that does not say I, but says here, again, warmer, shaded, torn, healing, wait.

And because it is wise, the power would not give them speech.

Speech would be too sharp at first. Too dividing. Too full of edges. It would not press faces into them, nor hands, nor hunger for fire, nor the loneliness of fast minds. It would not make the broad swimmers into philosophers or the thread-mouths into prophets. It would not hurry the place toward a destiny borrowed from elsewhere.

But it would give one gift.

Not a miracle visible from above. Not a throne of light descending through the suspended sea. Not a new sun, not a commandment, not a road.

A rhythm.

Deep under the drowned mountains, where the young stone still remembers fire, the power would touch the hidden pressures very gently. It would not silence the vents or still the tremors. Those belong there. But it would tune them, just slightly, so their pulses could travel farther through basalt, water, root, and silt.

A tremor in one valley would become, faintly, legible in another.

A vent opening under a black ridge would send a warmth-sign through stone that old colonies could taste before the water clouded.

A slide beginning in a steep-walled pass would be felt by roots lower down, early enough for leaves to close, threads to withdraw, suspended meadows to loosen and drift aside.

Not safety. Safety would be too small a gift for a living world.

Warning.

Continuity.

A way for slowness to know what speed had done.

Over generations, the forests would answer.

At first only the oldest colonies near the vents would change. Their black mats would grow faint rings of pale tissue where the tuned tremors passed through them. White nodules would open not only for mineral breath, but for pressure. Red fans would begin to pulse in sequences no animal eye could follow. In time, across drowned saddles and valleys, the root-colonies would become more aware of one another.

Not as separate minds speaking.

More like distant groves sharing weather.

The whole uplifted wilderness would begin to dream in pulses.

When morning entered the water, the dream would become golden and diffuse. When night gathered, it would narrow around heat. If a basin grew sick with too much mineral poison, neighboring colonies would taste the imbalance and alter their own release of sugars, spores, and bitter salts. If grazers overfed one corridor, the bitterness would not remain local; it would become a remembered pattern, passed valley to valley, teaching the animals new routes without famine.

The broad leaf-swimmers would notice only that old paths changed less brutally. That food appeared in cycles more difficult to exhaust. That nurseries in suspended meadows failed less often after tremors. Their young would survive in slightly greater numbers, but not so many that hunger became a plague.

The thread-mouths would cast their fine lines through water richer in signals. They would feed, as before, on spores and powdered plant flesh and the invisible young of other things. Yet now, sometimes, their threads would collect brief mineral tastes from far away: the bitterness of a newly opened crack, the sweetness of a flowering basin beyond the next ridge. Without knowing why, they would release larvae into currents that carried them toward better stone.

The small winged swimmers would learn the new rhythm in their bodies. Their cloudy eyes would still see brightness more than shape, but their ribbed sides would feel the mountain’s messages. They would turn before a slope shed stone. They would descend before a cold upper current thinned the food. They would gather in opalescent basins not randomly, but with the vague certainty of creatures obeying a season too large to see.

And the forests would become, slowly, kinder to their animals.

Not gentle in every moment. This would not be a paradise without mouths. The wise power would not remove hunger, because hunger is one of the ways life remains braided to life. The broad swimmers would still open their pale circular mouths and draw in clouds of small beings. The long colorless valley-things would still enter sleeping bodies from within and patiently consume them. Films would still be scraped from leaves. Eggs would still fail. Old colonies would still rot.

But waste would diminish.

Panic would diminish.

Needless collapse would diminish.

The place would remain itself, only more able to remain.

Then the power would look to the law that held the water over the mountains.

There, too, it would not replace. The old patient law was beautiful and strange, older than weather, subtle enough to cradle a sea in the high air. To interfere crudely would be to tear the world’s own grammar.

So the power would do what wise powers do when they love a law: not break it, but bless its balance.

At the upper skin of the water, where bubbles ended in tiny private clicks, it would lay a finer membrane of permission. Not a wall. Not a dome. A preference.

Storms from outside would pass around the high drowned wilderness more often than through it. Hard sunlight would soften before entering, divided into the green-gold shafts the forests could use. Cold winds would lose some of their teeth along the boundary. Meteors, ash, spores from distant hungry ecologies, wandering poisons borne on the upper air — these would be turned aside unless they could enter without ruin.

The place would not be sealed.

A sealed world grows poor.

But it would be guarded from accident without being guarded from change.

Once every long while, something foreign would arrive: a seed in the gut of a sky-bird that skimmed the upper water; a fleck of mineral dust from another range; a translucent egg-case stuck to a bubble that had risen through unknown depths. Most would fail. Some would be welcomed. A few would become part of the drowned mountains’ slow experiment. The wise power would not choose all outcomes. It would only prevent invasion from becoming erasure.

And if, in some far age, hands came near—

If tool-bearing creatures climbed the dry lower slopes and saw above them, impossibly, the warm suspended sea shining over the peaks;

if they made prayers to it, or maps of it, or machines to pierce it;

if they came with thirst, curiosity, reverence, greed;

then the power would act more plainly.

Not with thunder.

With bewilderment.

Paths would mislead the cruel and open for the humble. Instruments designed only to measure and possess would fill with contradictions. Those who tried to cut channels through the holding law would find their blades dulled by mineral bloom, their engines choked by harmless green films, their certainties softened by dreams of drowning leaves.

But those who came gently — if any came gently — would be allowed to see.

A child resting a hand against the transparent boundary might feel, through the skin, a slow pulse from the old root-colonies below. A patient scholar might learn that the tremors were not random. A grieving person might stand beneath the suspended sea at evening and watch broad dark swimmers cross the bronze light, and leave with no specimen, no proof, only a quiet rearrangement of the soul.

The power would permit witness.

Not ownership.

Never ownership.

Its greatest work would be hidden in death.

For in such a dense, slow place, death could easily become a burden. A basin might sour for centuries from one great collapse. A vent colony might poison its own descendants with the richness of decay. Suspended meadows might thicken until no young swimmer could pass through them. Life, left entirely to itself, is often beautiful, but not always merciful.

So the power would teach decay a gentler art.

Fungi-like films, already present in black seams and warm hollows, would receive a new talent. They would learn to divide bitterness from nourishment. They would take the bodies of fallen leaf-swimmers, drowned mats, failed spores, dead root-cords, and translate them more cleanly into mineral sweetness. They would release faint light as they worked, not enough to summon spectacle, only enough to guide small mouths.

After that, death in the drowned heights would sometimes glow.

A broad swimmer, old and garden-backed, sinking beneath an overhang for the last time, would dim as before. Its membranes would loosen. Its hidden mouth would slacken. Small grazers would come. Thread-mouths would set their lines. Clear larvae would flicker near the folds.

Then, after days, a soft green-white radiance would begin inside the body.

The light would spread through the mossy films on its back. Red tassels would open. Tiny rooted plants that had ridden the animal for years would release their young. The corpse would not rot into ruin but into a brief hearth. For a season, perhaps two, the overhang would become a nursery. Eggs would cling there. Seed-things would settle there. Pale root-fans would enter the softened remains and pass them back into stone.

The animals would not understand this as kindness.

They would simply live nearer to it.

The forests, perhaps, would understand a little.

Not gratitude. Gratitude requires an image of the giver. The power would not give them that. It would not stand before them in a form they could worship. It would not demand return, obedience, song, sacrifice, or even recognition.

But in the old colonies, where pulses traveled through dark cords beneath silt, something like ease would accumulate.

A memory of disaster avoided.

A recurring warmth arriving before need.

A pattern in which death fed without choking.

A sense, if sense is not too quick a word, that the world had become more answerable.

And because the place would now have a deeper rhythm, there would come a rare event.

Not often. Perhaps once every thousand years, perhaps less. Only when the upper light, the vent pulses, the root-colony signals, the drifting spores, the suspended meadows, the migrations of broad swimmers, and the mineral breath beneath the mountain all entered one another in a particular relation.

On such a day, the entire drowned wilderness would flower.

Not all in the same color. The vent mats would send up red-brown threads. The pale nodules would open like small moons. The ribbon-stems would unfurl leaves that had been held closed for decades. Floating pads would turn translucent as skin. Midwater rafts would loosen pearly strings of eggs. Winged swimmers would gather in blue corridors, their bodies full of amber grains. Thread-mouths would withdraw their lines and hang motionless, as if even hunger could wait.

The water over the mountains would turn opalescent from rim to rim.

Light would not fall through it in shafts then. It would appear to arise from everywhere at once: from leaf-veins, vents, suspended pollen, animal bellies, the polished edges of young stone. The whole mass would glow softly in the high air, visible from leagues away as a silent green-gold aurora held among peaks.

During this flowering, the old root-colonies would come as close to a single mind as they ever could.

They would feel the ridges, bowls, terraces, valleys.

They would feel the water’s weight and kindness.

They would feel the animals moving through them not as intruders, but as mobile hungers belonging to the same body of life.

They would feel the young mountains below, still sharp, still rising, still giving mineral breath.

And somewhere within that immense slow awareness, the benevolent power would place no words, but a question.

Not spoken.

Not even thought.

Only shaped as an opening.

What do you want to become?

The drowned wilderness would take centuries to answer.

Its answer would not be a sentence.

It would be a change in growth.

Some colonies would reach deeper into the mountain, learning the language of pressure more finely. Some would raise thinner, more sensitive leaves toward the upper light. Some would make hollows in their own bodies where small animals could shelter without being digested. Some would release spores that carried not only inheritance, but fragments of remembered events: the taste of a safe basin, the warning-sign of unstable stone, the sweetness of cooperation between distant roots.

From then on, the forests would not merely remember by scar and shape. They would begin to remember forward.

A little.

Not prophecy. Not planning in the quick animal sense. More like expectation. More like a valley preparing for rain before clouds have formed because the mountain underneath has whispered of it.

That would be the second gift, though the power would not call it that.

The first was rhythm.

The second was the possibility of chosen slowness.

And after that, the power would withdraw almost entirely.

Almost.

It would remain only as a kindness in the margins of law.

In the way poisons diluted just before becoming extinction.

In the way a cold season never quite reached the oldest vent garden.

In the way the first curious hands, if they ever came, found awe before appetite.

In the way the forests, when torn, learned not only to defend but to regrow around absence.

In the way certain dying animals drifted, by no visible current, toward places where their bodies would feed nurseries.

In the way light sometimes lingered in a hollow after sunset, illuminating nothing important except a few transparent larvae, a new root finding stone, and the slow opening of a leaf.

The place would not become holy in any obvious way.

It would remain warm water held above young mountains.

It would remain crowded, quiet, strange.

The black ridges would still soften grain by grain. The suspended meadows would still gather in midwater. Leaves would still sink halfway and stop. Denser beads of water would still crawl along stone like transparent slugs. Cliffs would still shed flakes that hung for months before turning green. Broad swimmers would still cross the high light like intentional shadows. Long pale things would still move in the deeper valleys with hunger patient enough to seem like thought.

But beneath all of it, through root, silt, vent, and bone-soft cartilage, a deeper coherence would pass.

The drowned mountains would be no less wild.

Only less alone.
