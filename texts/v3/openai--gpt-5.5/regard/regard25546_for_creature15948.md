The power does not descend at once.

That is its first mercy.

It has seen cities before: cities of glass, salt, bone, computation, argument, prayer; cities that believed themselves eternal until the first roof failed; cities that wanted to be forests and forests that dreamed uneasily of streets. It has seen minds housed in bodies, in rivers, in weather systems, in archives, in flocks. It recognizes the high green city immediately as a living intelligence, but not as a wounded thing merely because it is strange.

So it does not arrive as a cure.

It waits above the old mountains for a season, or for the local equivalent of a season, holding itself thin as dawn. It watches the rain arrive in silver bands and depart. It watches the factories breathe. It watches the bark-readers argue under the civic grove, the children running where adults have forbidden them to run, the pale moths gathering around a pump-house three hours before a bearing cracks. It listens to the seedpod loudspeakers repeating weather, quotas, old evacuations, private names. It studies the temporal abrasions in curbs and stairwells. It learns the etiquette of intersections.

Only after this does it make itself known.

Not in the sky. That would frighten the wind.

Not in the civic grove. That would be mistaken for law.

Not in the factories. That would be mistaken for production.

It appears first as a change in the puddles after rain.

For one morning, every puddle in the city reflects the true sky above it.

No darker canopy from another age. No impossible leaves. No sea that never rose so high. Just the pale high daylight, the exhausted blue, the vapor moving fast over the plateau.

People notice.

Children notice first, because children always look where adults step. A boy kneeling beside a root-basin sees his own face clearly in water and begins to cry without knowing why. A membrane glazier sees the reflection of a tower exactly where it stands and calls for her sister. Factory workers, emerging from the eastern wards with resin on their gloves, pause in the clean reflections and become quiet.

By noon the bark-readers have gathered in the civic grove, waiting for a decree.

The grove does not produce one.

Instead, one of its old sealed scars softens. Sap beads along its edges. The bark opens just enough to reveal, folded within the living wood, a thin layer of something that is neither leaf nor paper nor metal.

On it is written, in the city’s own grammar of scar and vein:

**I have seen you.  
I will not take you from yourself.  
May I assist?**

This causes three days of civic dispute.

Some say the message is a fungal hallucination. Some say it is a trap from the pale places where time has worn through. Some say no wise power would ask permission and therefore it must be wise indeed. The oldest anchor beneath the grove sends seven knocks through the municipal pipes, then a pause, then seven more, which is interpreted variously as assent, warning, laughter, or indigestion.

The city itself does not answer quickly.

It circulates the question.

It sends pressure down old root-streets and into factory walls. It lets the words pass through cambium and copper, through fungal account books, through cistern-flowers, through the warm chambers where the anchors hang in their harnesses. It samples reactions: fear in the people, curiosity in the children, irritation from the eastern resin presses, an unexpected stirring from the old rails, sorrow from sealed apartment blocks, hunger from the root-mains, suspicion from the civic grove, relief from certain bridges too long overloaded.

After nine days, a map buds from the bark.

It shows the whole city, but not as streets and wards. It shows thirst. It shows fatigue. It shows places where memory has become too heavy for walls to bear. It shows time-wounds as white, ragged blossoms. It shows the human quarters as warm knots. It shows the factories as lungs with soot in them. It shows the mountain beneath, still wearing away, patient and terminal.

At the center of the map is one small new symbol no bark-reader has ever seen.

A permission mark.

So the power enters.

Gently.

It comes as pressure equalizing before a storm. It comes as a greenish hush inside noise. It comes as a softening of resentment between old materials. Copper stops fighting sap. Brick stops believing it must remain separate from root. The swallowed steel girders, rusting in their wooden prisons, are given one dream of being ore again, and wake more willing to endure.

The power’s first act is not grand.

It repairs a stair.

Not one of the famous staircases that climbs higher than the plateau permits. Not one leading to the civic grove. A minor exterior stair in a half-inhabited district, used by fourteen families, two pressure-listeners, and a girl who keeps moths in jars. The stair has been unreliable for years, adding three steps in fog and losing five in rain. People have adapted. They count under their breath. They tie guide-lines. They accept danger as one accepts altitude.

The power lays attention upon it.

The living cane remembers its intended angles. The old bolts inside the growth settle. Moss withdraws from the treads. The stair does not become new. It becomes faithful.

For a week, nothing else happens.

This is important.

The city, which understands pattern better than proclamation, feels the restraint. The people feel it too. No district is transformed overnight. No law is overwritten. No dead are resurrected from wall-grain. No factories fall silent under heavenly judgment. The stair simply remains safe.

Then the power begins its real work.

It does not make the city less alive.

It makes it less afraid of dying.

Deep under the plateau, beneath root and girder and brick and the old mineral bones of the mountain, the power finds water that has been hiding for ten thousand years in cold fractures. It does not tear it upward. It teaches the roots to ask.

New feeder-veins grow down, finer than hair, patient as correspondence. They find the hidden water and touch it without greed. The cistern-flowers begin filling more slowly, more steadily. Rain remains precious, but no longer tyrannical. During the next dry month, no family waters their walls with tears. No factory steals moisture from a residential quarter. The copper moss in the squares brightens by one shade.

The cistern midwives hold a festival and deny it is religious.

Next, the power attends to time.

This is harder.

The city’s temporal wounds are not mere defects. They are part of how it remembers. The wrong shadows, the ambitious stairs, the tools returning as sound—these are symptoms, yes, but also organs of a kind. A clumsy healer would seal them all and leave the city flatter, safer, and spiritually maimed.

The wise power does not close the pale places.

It hems them.

Along the most dangerous curbs, a new lichen appears, white-blue and faintly luminous in fog. It grows only where time has thinned enough to bite. Children can see it from a distance. Adults pretend they would have noticed anyway.

In Saint Orra’s Square, where the year has been repeating around one pale curb, the power does something subtler. It allows the repetition to finish.

For eighty-seven years, the curb has held one afternoon in suspension: a rainstorm, a dropped basket of resin-sweets, a woman turning because someone called her name, a child slipping toward a seam in the root-plates, a father too far away to reach. The moment has been replaying beneath civic awareness, fraying the square, confusing doors, souring every nearby dwelling with unfinished panic.

The power does not erase it.

It lets the father arrive.

Not by changing history. History is a deep root and should not be yanked.

Instead, it gathers all the reaching that has happened since: the mother’s grief, the father’s dreams, the neighbors’ annual offerings, the children warned away, the city’s own repeated attempt to shorten the distance. It gathers these into the old moment like rain into a cistern.

The child still falls.

But this time, the root-plates open like hands.

Afterward, Saint Orra’s Square is only a square. Its curb remains pale, but no longer hungry. A flower grows there once a year, shaped like a small open palm.

The residents begin bringing lost things to it: broken tools, old keys, apologies.

The power then turns to the factories.

It does not hate them.

It has known mountains split for metal and rivers poisoned for profit; it has known engines that chewed the lungs of children; it has also known the dignity of work, the warmth of furnaces in winter, the holiness of things made well because someone needed them. The eastern wards are not innocent, but neither are they wicked. They are organs under strain.

So the power listens to the breathing buildings.

It hears the resin presses working past pain. It hears fungal furnaces overheating because old quotas scarred into bark have never been revised. It hears leaf-panels tracking the sun with arthritic pivots. It hears workers coughing behind filters mended too many times.

For once, the civic grove issues a decree before anyone petitions.

The scar appears at dawn:

**Production shall include recovery.  
Quota shall include rest.  
No organ shall be praised for failing slowly.**

The bark-readers argue over the translation, but the factories have already understood.

Belts pause between cycles. Resin trees are rotated out of extraction before their cores split. Fungal furnaces receive ash mixed with mineral sweetness from deep roots. The electrical leaves grow broader, not to consume more sun, but to need less frantic turning. Old coils long thought dead begin conducting at lower heat.

Some workers are furious. Rest sounds like lost wages. Slower output sounds like winter hunger. The power does not shame them for fear.

Instead, in the abandoned west manufactory, where only vines and old warning signs have lived for three generations, the city grows a new kind of workshop.

It produces filters.

Not luxury filters for officials. Not ceremonial respirators for bark-readers. Cheap, durable, self-cleaning pollen filters grown from a mesh of reed fiber, fungal charcoal, and a thin membrane that learned its craft from windows. Children paint them. Old workers test them suspiciously and then refuse to return them.

Within two years, the yellow months are still unpleasant, but they no longer take so many lungs.

Then come the insects.

This is controversial.

The city has never trusted the ordinary messengers of life. It has arranged pollination through wind, pressure, siren, memory, procedure. It has become proud of needing so little from the small, erratic nations of wing and leg.

But the wise power knows that sovereignty without relationship becomes brittleness.

It does not import a swarm.

It invites.

Down in the valleys, in scrub and thorn and the last bright meadows along streams, certain insects begin dreaming of altitude. Not all. Not enough to overturn the city’s customs. A few cold-tolerant bees with dark bodies. Minute silver flies that love mineral damp. Beetles that clean fungal rot without insulting it. A night-pollinating moth, larger than the pale warning moths, with wings the color of wet bark.

The city resists them at first.

Flowers close when the bees approach. Seedpods refuse the beetles. A loudspeaker in an unused rail station announces, repeatedly, **UNLICENSED TRAFFIC IN THE SOUTHERN QUARTER.**

The children, naturally, make little houses for the bees.

The fungal accountants observe that the beetles reduce waste. The cistern midwives notice that certain water-flowers seed more reliably. The bark-readers discover an old buried ordinance, half-swallowed by root, permitting “beneficial civic pollinators under supervised treaty.”

The city, which respects precedent even when it invented the precedent retroactively, relents.

The insects never become numerous. The high air is too cold, the winds too hard. But their presence changes something. Flowers open for reasons other than names. Fruit sets in neglected courtyards. The pale moths remain mysterious, but now they are not alone at the lamps.

The hoofed reed-deer grow bolder. The wingless birds add new clicks to their telegraph calls. In the oxidized moss-squares, small lives begin to negotiate with the sovereign plant.

The power’s most delicate work is with the anchors.

It goes beneath the civic grove, into the warm maintenance shrines where bodies hang in harnesses and root-cables, where human nerves and municipal conduits have made treaties under duress.

It does not pity them in the simple way.

Pity can be another form of refusal to understand.

Some anchors are in pain. Some are at peace. Some have become so extended through pipes and pressure systems that the thought of being only flesh again would feel like amputation. Some entered willingly, believing service could become sacrament. Some did not choose, not fully. Some can no longer remember which category they belong to.

The power speaks to each privately.

Not in words, unless words are wanted.

To one it offers release. The root-cables loosen over seven days. The city panics, then learns to route pressure without him. He dies in a bed with his sister beside him, and afterward his name appears not in a pipe, but in the grain of a kitchen table where he once ate soup as a boy.

To another it offers deeper consent. Her pain lessens. Her thoughts clarify. She remains an anchor, but no longer as a rescued accident preserved by necessity. She becomes a citizen in an enlarged body, and her pipe-knocks acquire humor.

To a third, who has been silent for twenty years, it offers a mouth made of bells. Not a human mouth. Not restoration. A new instrument. On fog evenings, when districts shift, he rings warnings in chords no one can mistake.

After this, no one may be made an anchor by accident alone. The civic grove grows a chamber of deliberation beside the shrines. Its door opens only when touched by three hands: a bark-reader, a physician, and the person whose life is being offered to infrastructure.

This law scars deep. It does not heal over.

Finally, the power addresses loneliness.

The city does not understand loneliness, but it has produced empty plazas, sealed blossoms, apartment blocks folded inward around old absence. The people are too few for the streets. The streets, remembering crowds, sometimes press their expectations against the living until even ordinary errands feel like failures to fill the past.

The power cannot simply create citizens.

It does something stranger.

In the old railway platforms, where phantom crowds gather as warmth on cold mornings, it teaches memory to become hospitality rather than demand. The smell of wool remains. The faint bootfalls remain. But the platforms no longer ache for the exact dead. They become places where travelers from lower valleys can arrive without being rejected by the city’s expectations.

Because, slowly, travelers do arrive.

Not armies. Not colonists with survey flags. Not merchants seeking to own resin rights. The power turns such ambitions aside with weather, with bad dreams, with roads that lengthen politely until greed grows bored.

But those who need the city, and whom the city might need, begin finding the ascent.

A family from a valley town lost to flood. A mycologist following a spore-map that appeared on her cellar wall. Three children carrying the brass key from a seedpod their grandmother kept all her life. An old engineer who can hear the difference between a tired gear and a resentful one. A singer with damaged lungs who discovers the high air hurts less if she sleeps beside cistern-flowers.

The city does not welcome them easily.

It requires quarantine, oaths, maps tattooed or scarred, lessons in intersection etiquette. It frightens them. It tests their grief at doorways. It lengthens roads for the arrogant. But it also grows new rooms before winter. It warms walls for infants. It allows certain abandoned districts to become neighborhoods again, not by unfolding all at once, but by remembering how doors work.

The human population does not explode. It steadies.

There are more children than funerals for seven consecutive years.

The bark-readers declare this statistically suspicious and privately weep.

All the while, the power remains almost invisible. It refuses temples when people attempt them. The first shrine built in its honor sprouts useful hooks for hanging wet coats and is thereafter understood as a public cloakroom. A hymn composed for it becomes, inexplicably, a popular pruning rhythm. An icon painted on membrane glass clouds over whenever anyone kneels too long before it, but clears beautifully when used as a window.

When asked what it wants, the power writes only once more in the civic grove:

**Continue.  
But not by devouring all that helps you continue.**

The city ponders this for a decade.

Then, one spring, it grows a new ring of law around the old decrees.

The scars are green-gold and slow to darken:

**A street may remember footsteps, but shall not punish absence.  
A room may keep names, but shall not consume the living for the sake of the dead.  
Factories shall breathe with workers, not through them.  
Growth shall ask where asking is possible.  
Repair is not refusal to change.  
The mountain is not foundation only, but elder.  
Water is guest before it is resource.  
Children may be warned, fenced, taught, and searched for, but not made afraid of wonder.**

No one agrees on whether the city wrote these laws, the power wrote them, or the two have become for a moment indistinguishable.

The wise power considers this a success.

It does not remain forever in the manner of a ruler. Benevolence that cannot depart becomes occupation. Instead it leaves behind capacities.

The puddles reflect truly more often now, though not always.

The white-blue lichen marks temporal danger.

The deep water answers when called respectfully.

The filters grow in public workshops.

The insects maintain their treaty.

The anchors have rights.

The factories rest.

The civic grove has learned a new punctuation mark: uncertainty without panic.

And somewhere beneath the swallowed brick, the original city of stone and copper is given one small gift. In a buried corridor no human has entered for centuries, an old municipal sign, rusted blank, regains its lettering for a single night.

It reads:

**THIS WAY TO THE PUBLIC GARDENS.**

By morning, roots have covered it again.

But above, in a half-empty district long thought too numb to care, a sealed blossom opens into a courtyard.

There is ordinary soil inside.

Not much. A few handspans deep. Dark, fragrant, full of small stones and patient bacteria. Enough for children to dig in. Enough for immigrants to plant valley beans. Enough for old people to sit near and smell something that is neither sap nor resin nor machine oil, but earth.

The city does not become a forest.

It does not become merely a city again.

It remains the third thing it has spent ages becoming: civic and vegetal, industrial and haunted, perilous and sheltering, ancient and unfinished.

But now, when rain strikes the living roofs and the whole metropolis rings like one enormous instrument, there is a new undertone beneath the roar.

Not silence.

Not peace exactly.

Permission.

The city breathes, manufactures, records, warns, absorbs.

And also, sometimes, it lets go.
