It would not bring a river.

That would be the first mercy.

A lesser power, moved by pity and swollen with its own idea of kindness, might split the plateau and pour water through it. It might summon clouds until the black stone ran silver and the channels sang for weeks. It might fill the dry basins with lakes, soften the thorn, green the scarps, drown the salt, call forests up from cracks.

And in doing so it would murder the place.

The burrows would flood. The lichens would rot. The condenser laws would fail. The people’s careful rights to moments would become meaningless before becoming violent. Lowlanders would climb the scarps with barrels, flags, deeds, arguments, soldiers. Mosquitoes would come. Fever would come. Roots would pry apart the old machines too quickly. The plateau, tuned to scarcity, would be broken by abundance.

So the wise power would do almost nothing at first.

It would arrive as attention.

Not light in the sky. Not a voice from the vents. Not a figure standing on the rim with hands raised. It would enter by the ways the plateau already allowed things to enter: heat, vibration, dew, mineral taste, the pressure difference before fog, the faint tremor in a pipe before a valve sighs.

For one full turning of seasons it would only learn.

It would feel the noon stone without flinching. It would follow the copper lizards under shelves and into eggshell-colored shade. It would sit with the blind things in the conduits and understand their hunger without excusing their teeth. It would travel the ceramic throats of cisterns drop by drop. It would listen to old machines dreaming of the hands that built them. It would learn every water right, every inherited cup-time, every dangerous shade, every name given after foolishness or skill.

It would learn that the people did not need rescue from the plateau.

They needed the plateau to keep answering them.

Only after that would it act.

Smallly.

Precisely.

In ways that could be mistaken, at first, for excellent luck.

The first gift would be warning.

Not protection from all danger; danger was one of the plateau’s languages. But warning where warning could be earned.

A pipe about to burst would begin, three days before failure, to sing a note even a child could hear by putting an ear to the ground. A dry channel that would flood under unseen rain would release, an hour before the wall of mud arrived, a smell like crushed green salt. Scorpions beneath sleeping stones would tap once, then again, loud enough for a careful hand to pause. The old northern valves, when pressure built beyond their habit, would frost white at the rim before they scalded.

The listeners would notice first.

They would sit on the humming ridgelines, palms against shaded rock, and feel new distinctions in the old tremor. Not words. Not commands. A grammar of strain. Water moving under basalt. Steam trapped behind mineral scale. A cistern wall thinning. A buried pipe still alive where everyone had believed it dead.

Some listeners would weep and say nothing for a while.

Others would accuse the salts.

Both would be partly right.

The second gift would be mercy in failure.

The benevolent power would not make the machines new. Newness would invite conquest. Newness would call the greedy from below and the proud from above. Instead, it would teach old things to fail less cruelly.

A pump that would once have shattered would seize.

A tank that would once have split and lost a year’s water into hot gravel would crack with a sigh along a repairable seam.

A condenser tower, near collapse, would shed one rusted panel at dawn instead of falling at noon onto the children clearing beetles from the gutters.

Ceramic pipes would clog before they poisoned.

Valves would stiffen before they betrayed.

A good mechanic would still be needed. More needed than ever. The gift would not replace skill; it would honor it.

Soon there would be a new category in the people’s speech: not safe, not broken, but asking.

The white tank is asking.

The third fan is asking.

The east gutter asked all night and you slept through it, Spills.

The third gift would be a little more water, but never enough to make water cheap.

At the highest ridges, where fog already combed itself reluctantly through mesh and thorn and rust, black lichen would begin to grow in branching patterns finer than hair. It would not spread across the whole plateau. It would refuse easy transplanting. It would thrive only where people kept metal clean, where bird droppings were scraped but not entirely removed, where gutters were unblocked, where old salt was brushed away with patience.

At dawn the lichen would bead.

Not streams. Not gushes.

Beads.

But beads on every filament, every thorn, every repaired wire.

A tower well tended might yield one extra jar in a cold fog. A neglected tower would yield nothing but dust and shame.

The people would argue over whether this was blessing, mutation, theft by lichens, or the result of better cleaning. They would test it, curse it, cultivate it, sing to it, and eventually write new laws around it.

Children would learn: do not scrape the black hair from the mesh. It drinks before we do, and then it shares.

The fourth gift would be bitterness.

Around the old evaporation vats, where white branching salts grew like frozen lightning and animals licked themselves into visions or ruin, a faint amber mineral would appear at the base of the most dangerous growths. It would taste unbearably bitter. Even salt-drunk animals, tongues cracked and eyes glazed, would recoil if enough bitterness had bloomed.

Not all poison would vanish. The plateau was not made harmless.

But the worst salts would mark themselves.

The salt doctors would learn the amber sign. Beetle keepers would grind a little into warnings painted on stones near bad pools. Children would dare one another to taste it and then spit for half an hour while elders pretended not to laugh.

A few listeners would still lick the white branches.

Some damage cannot be prevented without also preventing revelation.

The wise power would know this and grieve, but it would not close every door.

The fifth gift would be hidden instruction.

Not tablets descending from the sky.

Not a new scripture.

Instead: discoverable things.

A cracked gauge lens, when used to start a fire, would reveal that certain green-stained vat residues fused with ash into a sealant stronger than cactus wax.

A child grinding beetle shells for lacquer would find that adding powdered bone made a coating that resisted brine.

A mechanic striking a pipe with a sounding hammer would hear two tones where there had once been one and realize there was an inner sleeve still intact.

A widow repairing a cup would accidentally fire a clay patch in a steam vent and produce ceramic hard enough for valve teeth.

Dreams would help, but only after hands had begun the work.

The benevolent power would not pour knowledge into idle minds. It would lean toward attention. It would make the next correct experiment slightly more visible. It would place usefulness just inside the reach of patience.

Soon the plateau people would say, as they already said of all good fortune, that the land had left a tool half-buried and waited to see who noticed.

The sixth gift would be shade in emergencies.

Again, not comfort. Not ease.

But in the worst noon crossings, when a child misjudged distance or an elder’s knees failed between pump houses, a heat-hawk might circle where no prey moved. A ground bird might run strangely, drawing the eye toward a narrow shadow under a shelf. A loose condenser panel might fall at just the angle to cast a handspan of darkness over a face.

These interventions would be rare enough to become stories and common enough to become hope.

A boy once called Drinks-First, later renamed Carries-Two, would swear that a black stone rolled uphill to reveal a cool crack when his sister was near fainting.

No one would believe the uphill part.

Everyone would remember the cool crack.

The seventh gift would be restraint laid upon outsiders.

This would be the only act approaching severity.

Caravans would still come. Trade would continue. Cloth, grain, medicine, new metal: these were good. Exchange was one way life moved.

But those who came to measure the plateau for ownership would find their instruments unreliable. Compasses would tremble near the best cisterns. Survey stakes would split in the night. Maps would mildew though there was no moisture elsewhere. Men who marked condenser towers for seizure would develop a thirst no drinking relieved until they descended below the scarps.

An army could still climb, if it wished. The wise power would not make violence impossible. Nothing in the world is so blessed.

But greed would become confused there.

Greed depends on clean lines, predictable yield, transferable claim. The plateau would offer none. Rights belonged to moments. Water came from maintenance, memory, fog, timing, and permission. The place would grow increasingly illegible to those who wanted to take without entering its agreements.

Lowlanders would begin to say the upland was cursed.

The plateau people would hear this and smile with their mouths closed.

The eighth gift would be given to the dead.

Before dawn, when names were spoken at condensers and droplets gathered bright under the hard stars, a little more water would form on surfaces painted with the names of those who had repaired, saved, carried, warned, or taught.

Not much.

A finger wetting. A cup over many mornings. Enough to notice. Enough to argue about.

Families would begin repainting neglected names. Tool houses would be cleaned. Hand bones of old mechanics would be wrapped in fresh cloth. Children would ask what each elder had saved to deserve water after death, and the stories would lengthen.

This gift would not be about reward.

It would be about memory made practical.

A people who live by maintenance must also maintain gratitude.

The ninth gift would be for the unwelcome creatures.

Because a benevolent power, if it is wise, cannot love only the ones who speak.

The blind conduit things would find, deeper in the warm pipes, new mineral shelves where pale fungi grew after steamfall. Not enough to make them gentle. Enough to reduce their desperate raids after rain.

Pipe swarms would still hatch, but some would veer toward newly damp salt pans away from the covered tanks, if those pans had been prepared with waste and old brine. Beetle keepers would discover this and claim they had invented it. Perhaps they would have.

Salt-drunk animals, if led into a certain kind of shade and given bitter water in time, might sometimes return to themselves. Their meat would still not be eaten. Their survival would become unsettling. Children would watch a once-glazed ground bird shake itself, blink clear, and walk away as if embarrassed.

Mercy would widen the economy without abolishing teeth.

The tenth gift would be a question.

It would come differently to each person.

To a water counter, it would come as a tally that did not balance until she noticed an old widow had been quietly giving half her cup-time to a household with twins.

To a mechanic, it would come as a valve that would only turn if three rival families pulled together.

To a child, it would come as condensation forming on the wrong wall, spelling nothing, meaning everything: look again.

To a listener, it would come as a tremor beneath the whole basin, too deep to be pipe or vent, a slow pulse like an enormous sleeping heart.

The question would be:

What does enough mean, if fear is not allowed to answer first?

The plateau people would not become saints.

They would still quarrel over gutters. They would still curse lowlanders and each other. They would still abandon the dangerously foolish when saving them would crack a cistern. Children would still steal sips. Elders would still cling too long to rights they no longer used. Someone would still lick the wrong salt and call the resulting madness instruction.

But the question would remain.

It would enter jokes. Songs. Valve law. Marriage arguments. Funeral names.

Enough for thirst?

Enough for trade?

Enough for beetles?

Enough for strangers?

Enough for the dead?

Enough for the machine that gives only when fed?

Enough for the child who has not yet learned not to spill?

And because the power was wise, it would not answer for them.

It would only make certain answers more survivable.

Years later, no one would agree on when the change began.

Some would say it began the year the black lichen appeared on the north towers.

Some would say it began when Keeps-the-White-Tank heard a dry cistern asking and found the crack before dawn.

Some would say it began with the bitter salts, or the amber warning, or the first time a salt-drunk animal came back clear-eyed.

The listeners would say it began long before anyone noticed.

The mechanics would say nothing begins until someone repairs it.

Children would say the plateau had always been that way, because children are born into miracles and call them weather.

The benevolent power would accept all these accounts.

It would not need worship. Worship is often just ownership turned upward.

It would accept maintenance.

It would accept clean gutters, repaired mesh, shaded jars, fair cup-times, warnings shared before debts are counted. It would accept laughter after rain. It would accept a child lifting a lizard from a tank instead of letting it drown. It would accept a mechanic oiling an old valve and touching the metal once, briefly, in thanks.

It would remain mostly hidden.

A little in the fog.

A little in the salt.

A little in the pause before a foolish hand touches noon stone.

A little in the dreams of those who sleep beside humming ridges.

A little in every machine that fails kindly.

A little in every drop that gathers where someone has prepared a place for it.

And the plateau would continue: hot, crowded, mineral, exacting, alive.

Not saved from scarcity.

Not transformed into gentleness.

But accompanied.

And under that accompaniment the people would go on living as they had always lived, only with slightly more room inside the old agreement: room for one more cup, one more warning, one more repaired part, one more child brought safely through noon, one more dead name beading with dawn water beneath the hard and generous stars.
