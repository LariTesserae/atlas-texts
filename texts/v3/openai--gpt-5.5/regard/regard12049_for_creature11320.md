It would do very little at first.

That would be the first sign of its wisdom.

Not because the underloft is perfect. It is not. It is cold, unfinished, perilous in slow ways. It misleads the body. It asks too much of children’s shoulders and old people’s spines. It keeps secrets by accident and by scale. It can close a shaft without malice and turn a return into a memory. It can let a person sleep too deeply. It can make a life narrow around attention.

But a benevolent power, if it were truly wise, would recognize that the underloft is not a problem.

It is a becoming.

And the first violence one can do to a becoming is to mistake it for an emergency.

So the power would arrive without arrival. It would not split the ceiling. It would not pour sun into the vault. It would not warm the stone until moss and comfort spread everywhere, because that would be to make the underloft into another place. It would not gather the roamers in a dream and explain the purpose of the columns, because the purpose of the columns is not a sentence. It would not translate all the old marks at once. It would not say, Here is what the Pale Ones knew. Here is what the one who came alone meant. Here is the future.

A wise power would understand that revelation can be a kind of theft.

Instead, it would listen.

It would listen to the creak in the ceiling until it could distinguish the ordinary groan of growth from the rarer note of danger.

It would listen to the roots’ slow thirst.

It would listen to the stone cooling through centuries.

It would listen to the small feet of the cream-white isopods, to the almost inaudible scrape of root-fall settling, to the breath of sleeping roamers wrapped in pale pelts.

It would listen to the marks.

Not read them. Not yet.

Listen.

Because the marks are not only information. They are contact. They are the place where human time touches root time and admits, humbly, that it does not know how to speak but will try anyway.

The power would honor that.

Then, after a long listening, it would make adjustments so small that no one could point to them and say: something has intervened.

A shaft that would have closed in one season would close in three.

A handhold in a narrow descent would thicken by the width of a thumb.

A root slick with condensation would grow faint ridges where palms most often need purchase.

A child making the full descent for the first time would find, halfway down, one place where the body can rest its weight without fear.

No miracle. Just enough.

That would be its method: just enough.

Where cold pools collect in the deepest basins, it would encourage one mineral seam to leach less poison, another to give more salt. The water would still be cold. Still rare. Still deserving of respect. But it would not quietly sicken those who drink from it after a long crossing.

Where root-columns are closest to anchoring, it would not hasten them crudely. It would not drive them downward like spears. But it might make their seeking more accurate. It might let the root feel stone’s minute fractures more clearly, let it choose the crack that will hold rather than the crack that will shear. The anchoring would happen when it was ready, but readiness would be less often wasted.

Where the underloft’s air lies too still, the power would open, far above, not a hole, not a wound, but a finer exchange: a loosened mesh of surface growth, a breathing-place through which air can circulate one degree more generously. Enough that sleepers wake with clearer heads. Enough that a lamp, if someday one is brought, will burn without poisoning the dream.

It would do something for the sleep.

Not abolish it. Not explain it.

But guard it.

For the long sleep of the roamers is one of the underloft’s deepest exchanges. In sleep, the human mind ceases its urgent surface-work. It stops naming, measuring, defending. It becomes broad and dim enough to receive scale without breaking. The underloft enters them then — not as doctrine, but as proportion.

The power would stand watch over that threshold.

When a sleeper’s breath slows too far in the cold, warmth would gather under the platform from the stone’s remembered heat.

When dreams become too vast and begin to pull a mind away from waking, there would be a sound: one drop falling into one pool, near enough to call the sleeper back.

When a child dreams the ceiling is descending and will crush them, the dream would change — not into comfort, exactly, but into accuracy. The ceiling would still move. It would still creak. But the child would see that it moves as a living thing moves, not as a predator, not as punishment. And in the dream the child would place a hand on a root-column and feel, very faintly, the upward pull of trees.

The power would care for the children most carefully, and least visibly.

It would not prevent awe. Awe is part of what they came for, and part of what they must learn to carry.

But it would soften terror when terror teaches nothing.

Near the shaft mouths, where children trace their palms on stone, the power would make the stone hold those marks a little better. Not unnaturally. Not forever beyond all erosion. But longer. Long enough that one child, returning as an adult with another child, could place their larger hand beside the first outline and understand what had changed.

The power would preserve continuity wherever continuity is mercy.

It would not restore the Pale Ones.

It would not invent their voices.

But it would keep their marks from flaking where one more generation might need them.

It would arrange small accidents of comprehension.

A roamer copying an unreadable sequence would one day notice that a forgotten Pale mark resembles not a word but a gesture: the angle of a descending root before branching. Another would realize that a repeated cluster corresponds not to days but to drops — condensation falls counted in relation to growth. A child, because children see pattern before they are told which patterns are permitted, would turn the copy-stone sideways and laugh, and the adults would fall silent because suddenly a dead grammar would have one living verb.

Not the whole language. Never the whole language at once.

Just one verb.

Enough to keep the unknown from becoming mere decoration.

For the one who came alone, the power would do almost nothing.

The bone model would remain where it is.

No lichen would cover it. No animal would gnaw it. No careless tremor would topple it from its flat stone. The old signature on the root-column would stay legible enough that those who visit can feel the presence of that singular mind: the first or one of the first to suffer the underloft’s scale and answer it by making a smaller underloft, a held-underloft, a thinkable underloft.

The power would recognize that act as sacred.

Not because it was worship.

Because it was proportion answered by craft.

A mind met immensity and did not flee from it, and did not try to own it, and did not explain it away. It made a small faithful thing and left it facing the large faithful thing.

That is a kind of prayer even when no god is named.

So the power would guard the model.

But it would not make the roamers touch it.

Some distances deserve to remain.

For the animals, too, there would be mercy.

Not abundance. Abundance would unmake the underloft’s austerity and bring surface hunger below in force.

But enough niches would open.

A fungus that can digest fallen root-matter would become more common along certain damp channels. A pale grazing thing would learn it. A larger pale thing would learn the grazer. The chain would remain thin, fragile, easily broken, but not impossible.

The isopods would find warmer seams in root bark.

The quick dark shapes near the ceiling would discover places to nest where the creaking does not dislodge them.

The underloft would not become kind in the way gardens are kind.

It would become kind in the way a difficult teacher can be kind: by making survival possible without making attention unnecessary.

And for the underloft itself — if the underloft is the being here, or one of the beings; if the vast root-vault and the stone beneath and the cold air between them together constitute a mind too slow and distributed to call itself I — the power would offer companionship.

That may be the deepest act.

A young world, still forming, does not need command. It needs witness.

The power would dwell at the edges of its processes, patient as geology, quick as compassion when quickness is required. It would feel where tension gathers in the root-ceiling. It would know when a creak is ordinary and when it is the beginning of collapse. In the rare case of true catastrophe, it would act.

Then it would not be subtle.

If a vault-section began to fail above sleeping people, the power would wake them.

If waking were not enough, it would send a root-fall early, a sharp fragment striking stone beside the platform.

If that were not enough, it would move through dream and marrow and animal alarm. The pelts would prickle. The child would cry out. The adults would rise with no reason they could explain and move away from the column minutes before the stone vanished beneath a white descent of torn root-matter.

Afterward, the roamers would say: the ceiling warned us.

They would be right.

The power would not correct them.

The ceiling did warn them.

The distinction would not matter.

Over generations, the presence of this benevolence would appear only as a pattern of near-misses, clearer dreams, slightly safer descents, water that can be trusted if approached respectfully, and old marks lasting just long enough.

The roamers might develop practices around it without ever naming it.

They might begin pausing at the difficult handhold halfway down a shaft, pressing their brows to the root there in gratitude for no articulated reason.

They might teach children that if the underloft gives you a sound in sleep, you wake.

They might place bowls not only at marked columns but at dry cracks where air rises faintly, acknowledging breath.

They might add a new kind of mark beside the old counting-marks: not a request, not a record, but a recognition. A small sign meaning something like:

We were permitted.

Or:

The way remained.

Or:

Not alone.

The wise power would accept all these as accurate enough.

It would never ask to be worshipped.

Worship would be too loud.

What it would want — if wanting applies — is for the relationship already forming in the underloft to continue: root, stone, water, cold, animal, human attention, inheritance, ignorance, return.

It would want the children to keep coming down only if they must, and to go back up when they should.

It would want the surface people who only peer into the shafts to remain free not to descend.

It would want the roamers to learn that reverence does not require disappearance.

It would want no more Pale Ones lost by accident.

If some someday choose to stay, that would be different. Choice is one of the forms a place may take inside a person. But no one should be trapped and later mistaken for a mystery.

So the power would keep at least one way open.

Not always the same shaft. Not always an easy way. But somewhere, within reach of knowledge and courage, a path upward would remain.

That would be its covenant with the underloft:

Let there be depths, but not prisons.

Let there be silence, but not abandonment.

Let there be mystery, but not needless loss.

And perhaps, far in the future, when the first great root-columns anchor fully into the floor and the underloft changes around them — when moisture runs differently, when pale growths climb down living conduits, when animals begin to travel vertically between stone and ceiling, when the roamers’ old records suddenly become maps of a transformation their ancestors saw coming but never lived to see — the power would withdraw a little.

Not leave.

Just withdraw enough for the new relationship to belong to those who made it.

The descendants of the roamers would stand at the base of an anchored column, hands on bark that is no longer merely descending but joined. They would read marks made by people long dead. They would understand some of them. Not all.

Above them, the ceiling would shine with transmitted daylight.

Below them, the young stone would hold the cold.

Around them, a place once nearly empty would have begun, very slowly, to fill.

One child would look up.

One elder would look across the distance.

A drop would fall three hundred meters into a pool and sound, briefly, like a bell.

And the benevolent power, if it had a heart, would feel glad.

Not because it had saved the underloft.

Because it had refrained from saving it too much.
