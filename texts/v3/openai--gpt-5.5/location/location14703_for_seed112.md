At this altitude the world has forgotten rivers.

It is a continent-sized upland, lifted so high into the bright cool air that the horizon curves away in a pale blue-white glare. The ground is not bare rock, though it looks at first like a salt field seen through heatless sunlight: wide ivory plains, nacreous shelves, dim green seams, long terraces descending toward distances too large to read. There is no open water anywhere. No pools, no damp soil, no mist on stones. If moisture exists here, it is locked inside living tissue, bound into saps thick as resin, stored in glassy bulbs beneath bark, traded as vapor through sealed pores and mineral membranes. The land is dry in the way a machine room is dry: polished, taut, full of pressure, full of circulation, but with nothing spilled.

Everything is plant.

The plains are living crust. The cliffs are layered root-wood. The dunes are slow heaps of husk and pollen and silica leaf-scale. Even what seems like mineral—white plates, coppery ridges, black basalt ribs—is threaded with vascular filaments and photosynthetic skin. The continent is a botanical body spread thin across enormous distances. It does not grow lushly in the familiar sense. There are no dripping canopies, no crowded jungle shade. The vegetation is total but sparse: all surfaces belong to it, yet the forms stand far apart, giving the eye room to travel for kilometers between presences.

Here a grove may consist of seven plants spread across a valley the size of a nation.

They rise like towers, like chimneys, like cathedral organs grown from bone and jade. Some are hollow fluted columns two hundred meters tall, their skins pale green on the sunward side and ash-gray in shadow. Some are low radial mats, kilometers wide but no higher than a knee, patterned with copper veins and brittle silver hairs. Others are suspended between ground and air on lattices of root-struts, making enormous open cages through which the wind howls. Their leaves are not soft. They are blades, vanes, fins, scales, lenses, fans: stiff photosynthetic instruments tuned to brightness and gale.

The light is relentless. It pours over everything with alpine clarity, magnified by the pale ground and the reflective skins of plants. Shadows are hard-edged and blue. Surfaces flash. Miles away, a stand of mirror-leaves can ignite the horizon with such intensity that it looks as if a second sun has risen from the plain. Yet the air remains cool. The heat does not settle. It is torn apart by altitude, by constant motion, by the gigantic exchange of gases through the plant-body of the land.

The weather belongs to the vegetation.

There are no rainstorms. Instead there are pressure blooms.

At unpredictable intervals the plains begin to breathe. Billions of pores open across the living crust, and trapped gases rush upward in white roaring sheets. The sound arrives before the motion: a deep industrial thunder, as if a continent of furnaces has been opened at once. Then the air thickens with pollen, resin dust, dry spores, flakes of dead cuticle, glittering mineral chaff. Enormous leaf-vanes rotate into the wind. Stems flex. Root-arches hum. The whole plateau becomes an engine without builders.

From far off, the sound is like factories, shipyards, turbines, rail yards, grinding mills. Up close it is not like anything made by hands. It has too many layers. There is the bass concussion of gas vents opening under the crust. There is the shriek of wind through hollow trunks. There is the metallic ticking of seed-pods tightening. There is the slap and clatter of dry leaves the size of sails. There are percussion fields where thousands of jointed stalks knock against one another to shake loose spent husks. There are high electrical whines from the silver-haired mats when charge builds under the bright sky. And beneath all of it, almost always, the immense soft tearing sound of growth.

The land is old. Not ruined, not young, not in the middle of invasion, but settled into its own severe equilibrium. Its shapes have been worked by ages of wind, light, abrasion, and biological decision. The plant-cliffs show strata: green-black bands of ancient growth sealed under pale dry bark; ash layers from old lightning seasons; amber seams where resin once flooded wounds and hardened into translucent escarpments. Dead trunks do not rot in any wet sense. They erode into powder, fray into fibers, are rasped by airborne grit, then absorbed by creeping mats that advance over centuries like slow fire.

Valleys are carved not by water but by breath. Repeated gas releases have scoured channels through the living crust. Wind, carrying plant-glass dust, has polished root-buttresses into smooth ribs and cut window-holes through woody fins. Some canyons are lined with thousands of curled leaves, all facing the same direction, each one worn thin as horn by the passing air. They rattle continuously. Their sound fills the canyon so completely that silence there is unimaginable.

The fauna is abundant, though at first it hides inside the scale of things.

Small animals live in the vents and joints: pale jointed grazers with clamp-feet, bead-eyed pollinators that ride thermal bursts, flat translucent mites that scrape sugars from exposed veins. There are flocks of dry-winged creatures that look more like torn leaves than birds. They launch only during pressure blooms, letting the upward gas columns throw them kilometers high before they glide to another plant-tower. Their calls are sharp and mechanical, easily swallowed by the greater roar.

Larger animals move between the spaced groves. They are lean, high-ribbed, and dust-colored, built for distance rather than concealment. Some browse on mineralized leaves, cracking them with stone-like jaws. Some follow the migration of flowering vents, where certain plants extrude dry nectar crystals for a few hours before sealing themselves again. There are herd-beasts that carry forests of symbiotic moss-spines on their backs—not wet moss, but green filamentous growths that drink from breath and blood vapor. When they run, the spines whistle.

Predators are mostly patient. They shelter under root-arches or within the lee of old husk dunes, waiting for the deafening bloom-storms when prey loses orientation. Many hunt by vibration. Eyes are useful in the bright distance, but hearing is almost useless here; the world is too loud. So the hunters press long bones to the living ground and read the tremor-language passing through roots: hoof-click, wing-fall, vent-pulse, pod-split.

There are no buildings. Nothing in the landscape has been assembled from outside itself. But it is impossible not to feel industry here.

The plants mine.

Their roots descend through the plateau into mineral beds and draw up metals, salts, phosphorus, rare bright elements. Around certain old growths the ground is stained blue, orange, or black where excess chemistry has been exhaled and re-fixed into crust. Some towers grow copper bands around themselves to conduct lightning. Some spread arrays of black leaf-panels across ridgelines, angled with mathematical precision toward the sun. Some operate internal mills: seed embryos tumbled in hardened chambers until their coats are polished to aerodynamic smoothness. Others produce long chains of pods that advance along stalk-tracks with ratcheting clicks, each pod filled, sealed, charged, and fired by compressed gas into the upper winds.

It is industry without intention, production without design, mechanism as ecology. The whole continent manufactures itself continuously.

In the bright hours, which are most hours, the living plains appear almost bleached. Color hides in edges: green in the cuts of opened pores, red in resin wounds, violet under translucent bark, blue in the mineral exhaust around root vents. At low sun the place changes. The pale fields become gold. The tower plants cast shadows long enough to cover towns that do not exist. Leaf-mirrors tilt shut one by one, and their synchronized motion passes over the land like a shiver of armor. The sound does not stop, but it lowers. The great turbines of foliage slow. The venting becomes intermittent. Animals emerge from the white glare and cross the open spaces in dark processions.

Night is brief, cold, and electric.

Without the sun, stored charge moves through the vegetation. Root-lines glow faintly beneath the crust, revealing continent-scale networks: branching, converging, pulsing. Tall plants discharge into the upper air with violet streamers. Pollen clouds become visible as ghostly luminous veils, moving fast over the plains. Some flowers open only then—not petals, but geometric apertures in the bark, exposing crystalline reproductive structures that hum at different pitches. Pollinators gather in dense swarms, their bodies clicking, their wings making a sound like static poured through bone flutes.

The world has rules, and everything alive here obeys them.

Waste nothing fluid.

Open only under pressure.

Grow wide before growing tall.

Use wind as river, light as food, mineral as memory.

Do not shade what you cannot feed.

Do not soften.

The absence of water has not made the place dead. It has made life exacting. Every organism is sealed, armored, reflective, or hollow. Every exchange is timed. A wound is a crisis; evaporation is predation. Birth often happens inside capsules hard enough to survive years of abrasion. Death is slow dismantling by light and air. Even tenderness exists in dry forms: a parent plant shading its seedlings with mica leaves during their first exposed season; herd animals standing flank to flank around newborns to reduce wind shear; pollinators sleeping in the warm exhaust of a vent tower.

At continental scale, the turbulence becomes almost purposeful. Vast belts of vegetation enter active phase while others go dormant. Gas fronts roll for thousands of kilometers. Pollen storms collide with electrical bloom-lines. Whole plains change color in an afternoon as leaves invert from reflective white to absorptive green-black. A seed barrage from a western escarpment may darken the sky like volcanic ash, then spend weeks circling in high winds before settling in distant cracks. Some regions rise and sink subtly as root-masses inflate with stored gases or collapse after release, so that maps are never fully stable.

Yet it is not chaotic in the way of disaster. It is more like a long-established storm that has become a country. The turbulence is the balance. The deafening motion is the steady state.

If one could stand there safely—sealed against dryness, anchored against wind, shaded against the brilliance—the first impression would be emptiness: too much sky, too much distance, too few landmarks for so much living matter. Then the eye would adjust. The ground underfoot would be skin. The far ridges would be folded tissue. The white dust would be spent leaf. The towers would not be trees but organs of a planetary plant-field. The roar would separate into countless acts of breathing, feeding, signaling, grinding, launching, mating, defending, remembering.

And then the place would no longer seem empty at all.

It would seem vast because it is one thing happening in many places at once: a dry, cool, high, bright continent of vegetation, sparse in form but total in possession, old enough to have worn itself into elegance, loud enough to erase thought, alive enough to resemble industry, and turbulent enough that every horizon is always being remade.
