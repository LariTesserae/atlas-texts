It is a low city in the dark, built where the land has never quite decided whether to be shore, marsh, quarry, or dream.

Heat lies over it with the weight of a hand. Even in darkness the air feels sun-baked, as if the day has gone but the day’s fever has remained, stored in mud walls, in black stone, in the shallow water pooled in alleys. The atmosphere is wet enough to touch. It beads on any surface that rises above the ground; it gathers along edges; it trembles under lintels and on the undersides of broken arches. The rocks weep constantly. Not dramatically, not in cataracts, but in slow, patient slicks that make every surface seem alive with perspiration.

There is almost no sky, or else the sky is present but refuses to illuminate. Above the city is a dense, depthless dark, sometimes brown-black, sometimes blue like bruised metal, sometimes not a color at all. It is not the clean darkness of night. It is a dark made of humidity, suspended silt, old smoke without fire, and something like shadow leaking upward from the earth. Distances are visible only by inference: the low pale gleam of water between foundations, the dim shine on a sloped street, the repeating suggestion of walls fading outward into the murk.

The city spreads farther than it first appears. At a glance it seems like a handful of ruined blocks beside a marsh. Then, as the eye adjusts, more forms emerge: terraces, basins, causeways, courtyards, cistern rings, colonnades sunk to the waist in water, rooflines soft with erosion. It extends across a broad, shallow depression, a regional hollow in the land where water collects but never becomes a true lake. The settlement has the shape of long habitation: avenues that remember traffic, squares that remember gatherings, canals cut with practical regularity, retaining walls raised and repaired and raised again. Yet there are no tools here, no metal, no wheels, no lamps, no signs, no machinery, no made objects small enough to hold in a hand.

The built world seems to have grown rather than been constructed.

Its architecture is mineral and organic at once. Walls are not brick exactly, though they repeat in courses; not coral exactly, though they bear pores and branching seams; not bone exactly, though they curve around voids with a structural patience that suggests skeletons. Towers rise low and broad, more like termite mounds imagined by stone, but there are no termites, no insects, no animal life at all. Facades have windows arranged in civic order, but the windows open onto rooms whose dimensions do not agree with the outer walls. Doorways stand at human height in one district, knee-height in another, and in another they are tall, narrow slits that breathe warm vapor.

The materials are old. Nothing is sharp unless it has recently split, and recent splitting is rare. The city has been here long enough for erosion to become not destruction but style. Steps are worn into shallow troughs by vanished passage. Corners have rounded until they resemble river stones. Plaster-like surfaces have slumped, mineral skins have peeled, and the peeled portions have hardened again into new ledges. The whole place is in balance with its decay. It is not falling apart quickly. It has already undergone whatever catastrophe made it itself, and now it persists.

Water threads everywhere.

It sits in the seams between paving slabs. It fills gutters carved too carefully to be natural and too rootlike to be engineered. It emerges from the bases of walls, slides down into channels, disappears under thresholds, resurfaces in rooms that should be dry. In broad courts, shallow pools reflect darkness back at the darkness above. In narrower passages, the water is ankle-deep, hot, and opaque, filmed with mineral sheen. Sometimes bubbles rise from below, slow and widely spaced, each one swelling and breaking with a soft, private sound.

There are canals, but their banks are wrong. Some run uphill very slightly, their water holding a tilted surface as if obeying a local memory of level rather than level itself. Some divide at intersections and then rejoin before any visible turn, making loops that occupy less space than they should. A person walking beside one canal might see the same bridge ahead three times, each at a different distance, each wet with the same drip falling from the same missing cornice.

The land beneath the city is low and saturated. Beyond the outer structures lie flats of black mud, pale mineral crust, and thin mats of heat-stressed vegetation. Plants exist, but sparingly. A few reedlike stalks stand in open water, widely spaced, their blades narrow and dark, as though reluctant to spend themselves on leaves. Low, waxy growths cling to the shaded bases of buildings. Here and there a vine hangs from an upper ledge, but it is leaf-poor, mostly cord and node, drinking condensation from stone. In courtyards, isolated shrubs grow from cracks, each one alone in a wide emptiness of paving. Their foliage is sparse and thick, curled against heat, colored almost black in the dimness.

Nothing grazes them. Nothing nests in them. Nothing sings.

The absence of animals is not the ordinary quiet of a place after migration or drought. It is more complete than that. There are no birds stirring in the upper dark, no frogs in the standing water, no flies above the rot, no lizards pressed to warm stone. The city has moisture and heat and shelter, all the invitations of life, yet only the most minimal vegetation answers. Even decay seems selective. Fallen plant matter softens into dark paste, but it is not busy with larvae or beetles. It simply liquefies, stains, and becomes part of the wet ground.

The sounds are few and therefore enormous.

Drip.  
A distant settling of stone.  
Water slipping under a door.  
The faint hiss of condensation forming on a hot surface.  
Once in a while, from far away, a low concussive note, like a buried chamber exhaling.

The quiet does not feel empty. It feels listened into being.

There is weather, though no clear sky reveals its source. Heat gathers until the walls sweat more heavily. Vapor thickens in the avenues, making the city shorter and closer. Then a warm rain begins without warning, not falling so much as appearing throughout the air. Droplets bead simultaneously on paving, on water, on skin, on the underside of arches. The rain has no direction. It condenses out of the dark in a million small arrivals. Channels fill. Courtyards become mirrors. Stairs vanish under rippling black. The sparse plants uncurl by a fraction.

Afterward comes drainage. The city drinks itself down. Water levels lower through hidden throats. Basins empty in spirals. Thin waterfalls develop where none were before, pouring from window-openings into alleys, then slowing, then returning to drip. Steam rises from sun-heated stone though there is no sun visible. Mineral salts bloom white along edges and then dissolve again when touched by the next wet breath of air.

Many districts have their own rules.

In the western basins, reflections precede the objects that cast them. A column’s reflection may appear in a pool seconds before the column itself emerges from the dark. If a wall collapses there, its image falls first, breaking the water, and only afterward does the stone give way with a reluctant groan.

In the southern causeways, distance behaves like soaked cloth. A road can be stretched by looking too long down it. The farther arch remains far no matter how many steps are taken, until suddenly it is behind you, dripping onto your shoulders. Side streets open into courtyards larger than the districts that contain them. Some alleys end at the same square from opposite sides without turning.

In the central precinct, the buildings are arranged with urban intention: a plaza, surrounding halls, raised platforms, processional steps descending into a rectangular pool. But the pool is not horizontal. It stands upright in the air like a sheet of black glass, held between two stairways. Its surface ripples inward. Water leaks from its lower edge into a trough, yet the sheet never thins. Behind it, dim lights seem to move, but they are not lights from lamps or fires. They are pale stresses in reality, like veins in closed eyelids.

The central plaza is almost bare. Sparse vegetation marks only the cracks. The paving is broad, open, and darkly wet, with much space between each standing object. A single pillar leans at the plaza’s edge, thick and eroded, its surface covered in shallow depressions that fill one by one with condensation. When all depressions are full, the pillar turns slightly. Not enough to be seen directly. Only enough that its shadow, in a world without light, points somewhere new.

There are houses, if they can be called houses. Rows of cell-like chambers line certain streets, each with a raised threshold to keep out floodwater. Inside them are platforms grown from the walls, recesses like shelves, basins with smooth lips. They imply domestic life without supplying evidence of inhabitants. No cooking soot. No broken vessels. No woven scraps. No tools. Just spaces shaped around needs that may never have belonged to bodies.

There are also structures that resemble markets, with long aisles and repeating stalls. But the stalls hold only damp air. In one such place, the roof has sagged into a series of shallow domes, each dome collecting condensation until it drops all at once. The drops strike the floor in a pattern too regular to be random and too meaningless to be language. The sound travels down the aisle and returns from the far end before it should, softer, as though repeated by a mouth with no throat.

The city’s organization is undeniable. It has neighborhoods, water management, ceremonial voids, perimeter embankments, layered foundations. It has the density of settlement but not the clutter of habitation. Everything is spaced out, simplified by time, stripped to its durable intentions. Buildings stand apart with wet lanes between them. Courtyards open like pauses. There is room for crowds, but no crowd has ever arrived, or every crowd has been removed so completely that even the memory of bodies has been rinsed away.

The ground beyond the city is not wild exactly. It is a fringe of marsh-edge and eroded infrastructure, where streets dissolve into mudflats and canals become natural channels. Low levees extend into the darkness, their tops slick and narrow. Rectangular reservoirs have become reed pools. Stone-lined drainage cuts run through open sediment, then vanish beneath mats of black algae-like growth. Sparse shrubs stand on raised hummocks. Heat lightning sometimes flickers inside the clouds or inside the ground; it is hard to tell. The flash reveals no horizon, only more low wetness, more broken linear forms, more dark water reflecting a sky that gives nothing back.

Reality is weakest where water, heat, and old construction meet.

At certain corners, the mortar between stones shows moving depth, as if each joint contains a thin canal extending elsewhere. Reflections in puddles show buildings in better repair, or more ruined, or built in impossible materials: salt, skin, ash, moonlit paper. Occasionally an entire street appears to inhale. Walls draw closer by a handspan, water rises along their bases, and then everything relaxes. The motion is slow enough that one might blame dizziness until the tide marks fail to match their previous height.

Some arches open onto places that should not fit within the city. Through one may be seen a dry staircase descending through rain. Through another, a field of white reeds under a black sun. Through another, the same street you are standing on, but flooded to the rooftops, with the tops of towers protruding like stumps. These openings do not announce themselves. They are just part of the architecture until approached, and then the air inside them becomes cooler or hotter or older.

Still, the city is not chaotic. Its breakdown has rules, though they are not human rules. Water remembers paths more faithfully than space does. Stone keeps agreements that rooms violate. Heat makes surfaces honest: under the highest temperatures, false walls sweat differently and impossible corridors reveal seams. Darkness conceals distance but preserves direction. If one follows the flow of water, one eventually reaches a basin. If one follows a reflection, one returns to where one began, changed only in orientation. If one waits in complete stillness, the city resumes some process interrupted by attention.

That is the sense everywhere: processes continuing.

Condensation gathers and falls.  
Minerals dissolve and accrete.  
Mud receives water and releases gas.  
Walls sag by imperceptible degrees.  
Sparse plants open and close with the wet heat.  
Canals refill from unseen sources.  
Rooms exchange interiors when unobserved.  
The upright pool in the central plaza ripples toward an unseen shore.

No animals move through these cycles. No insects stitch the air. No voices call. The only living pace belongs to the few plants and perhaps to the city itself, if growth, sweating, healing, remembering, and reconfiguring can be called life.

The dominant smell is hot stone after rain, though rain may not have fallen. Beneath it: mineral water, wet clay, faint rot without sweetness, and the metallic tang of deep groundwater. In enclosed rooms the air tastes old and brackish. In open plazas it tastes of steam. Breathing feels like drinking from a warm vessel.

The darkness alters scale. A short wall may loom like a fortress until touched. A distant tower may suddenly reveal itself as only a stump nearby. The city opens and closes by increments. It never becomes fully visible. It must be assembled from fragments: a gleaming stair, a row of low arches, the wet back of a retaining wall, the pale roots of a lone plant gripping masonry, the black mirror of a canal, a roofline bending where geometry softens.

If there was once a people here, they left no image of themselves. No statues, no inscriptions, no graves. Or perhaps the city was the people. Perhaps its streets were organs of movement, its pools organs of memory, its courtyards organs of attention. Perhaps the sparse vegetation is not reclaiming it but being permitted, tolerated like lichen on a sleeping giant. Perhaps the absence of fauna is not loss but exclusion.

At times the whole low basin seems to tilt toward listening. Drips slow. Vapor hangs motionless. Water surfaces smooth themselves. In that pause, one can almost sense the city’s extent: district after district in the dark, wet and warm, spread across the marsh depression; an urban body without inhabitants; an eroded system in equilibrium with impossible laws. Then a bubble breaks in a canal, or a far wall sheds a sheet of water, and ordinary process resumes.

The place does not invite exploration, but neither does it forbid it. It receives footsteps as it receives rain: briefly, darkly, without surprise. Every mark fills with water. Every disturbance is rounded down. The city has endured long enough to make all arrivals temporary.

And all the while the rocks keep weeping, the hot rain keeps forming out of nowhere, and the black canals carry their shallow, patient water through streets that may never have been built, in a darkness that may never lift.
