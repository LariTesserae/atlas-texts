It is a garden with walls close enough to hear, if there were much to hear.

The place occupies the interior of a low, handmade enclosure: not quite a room, not quite a courtyard, roughly the size of a small chapel or the central square of a hamlet. Its boundaries are obvious but not simple. The wall folds inward and outward like a clay pot pinched by uncertain fingers, making alcoves, blind corners, narrow shelves, and little bays of still air. Everything is built. There is no exposed earth, no bedrock, no honest soil. Underfoot is a fitted mosaic of fired tiles, salt-brick, bone-white plaster, blackened resin, and compacted dust that has been pressed into slabs and sealed until it rings faintly beneath a step.

The whole place feels recently made. Edges remain sharp. Mortar has not yet softened. The tile ridges catch the sole. Where arches have been raised, the tool marks are still visible: thumb-smears in clay, cord impressions, the straight bites of stone blades. The garden has not had time to become old, but it has already become strange.

There is no water.

No basin, no dew, no moss-dark crack, no dampness under leaves. The air is warm and dry, almost medicinal, carrying the mineral sting of salt, heated ceramic, old ash, and crushed green things that are not truly green in any familiar botanical sense. Yet the place is lush.

The vegetation grows from sockets, jars, troughs, and hanging ribs of latticework. Every plant has been planted by hand, tied in place with fiber, wedged into mineral paste, or grafted onto scaffolds of reed and clay. There are no roots disappearing into a natural substrate; instead there are root-like grips wrapped around ceramic knobs, burrowing into pumice blocks, or feeding from pale cakes of salt and powdered shell. Some plants resemble succulents, but their swollen tissues are full not of water but of clear oil, volatile sap, or granular gel that ticks softly when warmed. Others grow as curtains of dry filaments, rustling only when touched. Broad black leaves overlap like scales of lacquer. Yellow blades rise in dense sheaves, each one edged with translucent crystal. A vine like braided copper wire winds around every vertical surface, tightening over weeks, never visibly moving.

The colors are saturated but dusty: green-black, ocher, bruised purple, ivory, cinnabar, smoky blue. Nothing looks wet. Leaves shine with wax rather than moisture. Flowers are common, though many are hard and faceted, more like carved beads or mineral eruptions than petals. They open slowly over many days, one hinge at a time, releasing pollen as a faint golden smoke that drifts downward rather than up. Some blossoms make no scent at all until shadow touches them; then they smell of hot stone, fennel, and extinguished lamps.

The garden is dense. It is difficult to see across it. Walls carry shelves, shelves carry pots, pots support frames, frames hold dangling nests of dry foliage and sleeping animals. Narrow paths turn between packed growths and handmade structures. A person walking through must turn sideways often, not because the garden is large, but because everything is near. Leaves brush shoulders. Clay pipes cross at knee height. Thin screens of woven grass divide one chamber of air from another. The ceiling, where there is one, is patched with stretched hide, translucent ceramic, and reed matting. In open places, normal daylight enters: soft, warm, and plain, as though from an ordinary afternoon. But it does not always fall from above.

Sometimes a patch of sun appears on the underside of a shelf.

Sometimes a shadow is cast by something that is not present.

The laws of the place are handmade too, and poorly joined.

A short stair climbs five steps and arrives half a step lower than where it began. A corridor behind a curtain is longer if entered while holding breath. A clay jar may contain the echo of a larger room: a cool, hollow acoustics with no matching volume. Seeds dropped on the floor occasionally bounce upward and remain hanging at ankle height until swept into a basket. The wall in the western corner has two faces, one visible to the left eye and another to the right; both are painted with the same red spiral, but the spirals turn in opposite directions.

These disturbances are not violent. They are woven into habit. The inhabitants have learned them as one learns drafts, loose stones, thorny shrubs. A child would know not to place a sleeping mat beneath the ladder that remembers being a tree. An old keeper would never prune the blue vine during the hour when lengths become negotiable. Reality here has not shattered in a dramatic way; it has softened, frayed, been patched, and been used.

The fauna are abundant, though quiet. Most are small. Dry beetles with lacquered backs move in processions along grooves in the tiles. Pale lizards cling upside down to the plaster, breathing slowly, their throats pulsing like little bellows. Moths with wings like ash sleep in the mineral flowers until warmth wakes them. There are many soft-bodied things too, improbable in a waterless place: air-slugs with translucent membranes, moving by contracting against invisible surfaces; coiled creatures that live inside hollow stems and taste the passage of time; tiny fox-faced animals with six feet and papery ears, nesting in the upper shelves among dried leaves.

The animals drink no water. They lick oils, chew salts, sip vapor from cracked seedpods, or bask beneath certain warm stones that seem to nourish them by contact alone. Some feed on sounds. Because of this, the garden is mostly quiet.

The silence is not empty. It is careful.

There is the faint rasp of mandibles on ceramic. A slow creak as a vine tightens around a reed frame. The powdery fall of pollen. The occasional click of a tile adjusting to heat. Wings open and close without beating. Far inside the wall, something knocks once every few hours, a patient and irregular sound like a person testing whether a door is still there. Voices, if someone speaks, are swallowed quickly by the plantings and by small listening mouths hidden in the leaves.

The warm air hardly moves. When it does, it travels along prescribed channels: through clay flues, under perforated benches, across trays of powdered mineral, then upward through the foliage. The circulation is manual and ancient in method. Bellows made of hide hang beside certain vents. Stone weights descend slowly to turn fans of woven reed. Shutters are opened with hooks. Nothing hums. Nothing glows with machinery. If there is technology here, it is the technology of hands, knots, jars, pulleys, levers, grafts, and memorized timings.

The garden must be tended constantly, but not urgently. Its processes are slow. A plant may take a year to turn one leaf toward a new angle. A salt-frond thickens grain by grain. The ceramic beds cure in warmth. Animals migrate from one side of the enclosure to the other according to cycles no clock can hold, gathering for weeks around a crack in the air, then abandoning it all at once. Shadows ripen. Empty niches become receptive. A doorway may spend a season deciding where it leads.

There are signs of caretakers everywhere, though none may be visible at first: pruning knives of chipped stone, copper wire hammered thin and reused many times, baskets of curled leaves, ladders polished by bare feet, clay tablets marked with pictorial instructions. Here is a drawing of a hand beside a crescent-shaped fruit, warning not to touch until the fruit has stopped remembering the moon. Here is a row of tally cuts recording the number of times a certain alcove has appeared. Here is a mask hung beside a bed of white reeds, perhaps for protection, perhaps as part of pollination.

The ground, being entirely made, has its own artificial geology. The lowest layer is a platform of interlocked stone blocks, but above it lie generations of repair: pale plaster poured into cracks, red clay spread and combed, black pitch used to seal seams, then crushed ceramic sprinkled over the surface to keep feet from sticking. Since erosion is young here, nothing has been rounded down. Breaks are fresh. Corners are acute. The constructed world has the tense brightness of something newly emerged from a kiln.

And yet the density of life makes it feel ancient in another way. Every ledge is occupied. Every vessel has sprouted or nested or warped. The walls are so thoroughly colonized by intended growth that the distinction between architecture and vegetation has become ceremonial rather than practical. A column is a trellis thickened with living plates. A bench is a root altar with a place left for sitting. A doorway is an animal trail through a decision made by builders long ago or perhaps next week.

At the center stands the driest thing of all: a fountain.

It is beautifully made, shallow, circular, and completely empty. Its basin is tiled in blue glass to imitate water, though the glass has cracked into a map of countries that do not stay still. From its middle rises a spout carved like a bundled reed. Nothing flows from it. Instead, the air above the spout bends. Dust motes circle there in slow ellipses. Tiny animals gather at the rim and extend their tongues into the invisible distortion, feeding on absence. Around the basin, the plants are lushest: thick leaves, spiraled vines, pendant pods, flower-heads like clustered eyes. They lean toward the dry fountain as if toward a spring.

Perhaps it once held water and the world has forgotten how.

Perhaps water is impossible here, and the fountain is a shrine to an extinct idea.

Perhaps the absence itself is fertile.

The garden does not answer. It continues its slow work: warming, tightening, flowering, listening. In its handmade enclosure, life grows densely without wetness, sound is eaten before it can gather, and reality peels back in small usable strips. Everything here has been constructed, but not everything has remained obedient to its makers. The place feels less like a building containing a garden than a rule someone built by hand — a low, warm, dry rule — inside which plants, animals, tools, and impossible distances have learned to live.
