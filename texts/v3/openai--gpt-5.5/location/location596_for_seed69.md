It is a high country without a visible sky.

Not roofed, exactly—not a cave, not a chamber—but a vast upland realm lying beneath a permanent dark canopy of vapor and soot, where the clouds press so low they feel like a second terrain suspended overhead. The world rises in enormous shelves and tilted basins, a continent of old stone lifted into warm air, its ridges softened by long weathering, its cliffs rounded and blackened, its valleys filled with breathing marsh. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even the scars are old. The mountains have accepted their cuts; the rivers have learned their channels; the machinery, where it stands, has rusted into the logic of the place.

Water is everywhere, though not always as streams. It sweats from stone. It beads on iron. It gathers in the leaf-cups of high plants and drips steadily from the cables strung between towers. Whole slopes seem to weep in the dark, each seam in the rock shining briefly when lightning passes behind the cloud ceiling. The ground is rarely dry. Underfoot there is black peat, warm mud, mats of fibrous root, plates of slick shale, puddles filmed with mineral color. In hollows, marshes spread out under dense growth: reed forests taller than buildings, soft islands of moss, floating mats of fern and fungus, pools whose surfaces tremble with bubbles rising from buried heat.

The temperature is warm and close. Not tropical in the bright, sun-burnished sense, but feverish, enclosed, full of condensation. Air moves heavily through the valleys, carrying the smell of wet leaves, hot metal, sulfur, algae, animal musk, and old oil. The darkness does not cool the place. Warmth comes up from below—from deep faults, buried engines, black-water vents, chemical bogs, and the slow respiration of so much life pressed together.

Vegetation grows thickly wherever it can grip. Since there is no daylight, or almost none, the plants are not green in any ordinary way. Some are black and lacquered, drinking warmth and minerals through enormous root-nets. Some are pale, translucent, veined with faint internal color. Some unfold only when the electrical storms begin, lifting fronds like listening instruments into the charged rain. Along the marsh-edge grow heavy, jointed reeds with coppery stems. On the higher terraces are groves of broad-leafed things that hang downward from cliff faces, their roots exposed like ropes, their leaves cupping condensation out of the air. Fungal shelves climb old masonry and dead trunks in overlapping armor. Mats of luminous lichen make dim maps on stone, not enough to illuminate the landscape, but enough to reveal edges: the curve of a stair, the jaw of a half-buried machine, the eyes of animals watching from the reeds.

The dark is nearly absolute between flashes and glows. There may be a sun above the cloud mass, but it has not been seen from here in generations, or epochs. The world is lit instead by intermittent things: furnace mouths in distant escarpments, blue-white arcs from exposed conductor lines, bioluminescent swarms, lightning diffused through vapor, the red pulse of warning lamps still obeying some forgotten schedule. Far away, on the rim of a plateau, a refinery stack exhales a column of sparks that vanish upward into black cloud. Closer by, the marsh gives off a faint green shimmer where disturbed water closes over swimming bodies.

Because the land is vast, the darkness has weather of its own. Storms breed in the basins and climb the slopes. Fog banks pour like slow rivers over saddles between ranges. Warm rain begins without warning, first as a hush of droplets on leaves, then as a roaring sheet that makes the whole forest bow and shiver. After rain, the rocks steam. Waterfalls wake along cliffs that had seemed dry an hour before. The marsh rises. Channels reverse. Hidden sluices open somewhere underground, and black water surges through culverts large enough for ships.

The terrain is layered, dense, crowded with presences. High ridges of basalt and old concrete run for hundreds of miles, their sides furred with vegetation. Between them lie industrial valleys partly drowned and partly reclaimed by root and silt. There are rail beds on embankments, but many end in open air where bridges have collapsed into ravines. There are pipeways as thick as tree trunks, still warm in places, crossing from hill to hill on skeletal trestles. There are pump houses sunk to their windows in reedbeds. There are isolated gantry towers standing on knolls like watchful insects. Some still move.

Built things are scattered, but the technology is not small. It is as if a vast industrial system once covered the highlands and then withdrew, leaving organs behind. The settlements are sparse—single blockhouses, maintenance shrines, operator cabins, stone platforms with signal masts, clusters of pressure tanks on ridgelines—but they connect to immense buried networks. You may walk for days through what seems like wild marsh and then hear, beneath the mud, the steady beat of pistons. A hillside may open at night with red service lights, revealing rows of intake vents breathing vapor. Far below a cliff, mostly hidden by ferns, turbines turn in a flood channel, grinding ancient water into power for unknown consumers.

The machines are not dead. They are weathered into habit. Some respond to rainfall. Some to animal movement. Some to the pressure of the marsh swelling above them. Once every few hours, a line of towers clicks awake across a whole valley, one after another, their relays snapping like metallic insects. Motors whine. Pumps thud. Floodgates drag themselves upward through mats of roots. Warm water pours from hidden ducts and changes the current in the marsh. Then everything shuts down again, leaving only dripping and the hiss of cooling metal.

Sound is constant and layered. Rain on leaves. Drips in thousands of pitches. Frogs or frog-like things pulsing from the pools in rhythmic choruses. Wingbeats under the canopy. The creak of huge plants turning slowly toward electrical charge. Distant engines. Near engines. Water hammer in buried pipes. The low animal bellow of something territorial in the reeds. Rocks ticking as they release heat. Mud sucking at the feet of larger creatures. In storm, the noise becomes overwhelming: thunder muffled but immense above the cloud ceiling, sirens winding up from abandoned plants, canopy leaves thrashing, overfull spillways roaring white in the dark.

Fauna is abundant because the place offers shelter, water, heat, and endless surfaces to feed upon. There are insects in dense mechanical swarms, attracted to voltage and mineral bloom. Some cluster around exposed wires until they glow faintly with stolen charge. Long-legged waders move through the marsh, feeling the mud with delicate toes, their throats flashing with dim internal lamps. Blind fish live in the flooded conduits and emerge during overflows into open pools. Arboreal mammals—or something close to mammals—travel along cable bundles and root bridges, chattering softly until machinery starts, when they fall silent all at once.

Predators are many. Thin eel-bodied creatures hunt in drainage ditches, able to slide over wet stone as easily as through water. Heavy-jawed marsh cats nest inside broken transformer housings. Larger things move only by implication: reeds parting in a long curve, a deep exhalation under a platform, the sudden disappearance of an entire chorus of smaller animals. In the high ravines, colonies of membranous fliers cling to the undersides of bridges and launch when storms ionize the air, feeding on airborne insects stunned by lightning.

The ecology has adapted to industry not as an interruption but as a season. Some plants flower only during turbine discharge, releasing spores into warm exhaust plumes. Certain animals migrate along maintenance rail lines because the ballast stays above flood level. Fungi digest rubber seals, insulation, old paint, and the tarry residues around valves. Mineral algae bloom in cooling ponds, forming dense orange slicks that nourish entire chains of grazers. The machines leak heat, metals, rhythm, and light; the living world has made these into food.

Strangeness is not rare here. It is structural. Gravity feels slightly inconsistent near the old pressure stations, where water climbs the inside of cracked pipes before falling upward into mist. Pools reflect lights that are not present on the shore. Some cliffs bear rows of circular erosion marks too regular to be natural, yet older than the oldest visible machines. In certain basins, sound arrives late: you see a flock scatter in silence, and only moments afterward hear the clap that startled them. There are plants whose leaves fold into diagrams when touched by rain. There are maintenance doors in bare rock that open onto warm corridors full of roots and moths, corridors that slope downward farther than the height of the mountain should allow.

Despite all this activity, the place feels settled. Not peaceful, but balanced in its own saturated way. The erosion is mature; water has negotiated with stone for a long time. Channels are polished. Iron railings have rounded under mineral deposition until they resemble bone. Concrete has cracked, been filled with moss, cracked again, and become habitat. Landslides are old enough to have forests on them. Collapsed factories are not ruins so much as reefs, dense with nests, vines, pipes, burrows, and dripping galleries.

On the highest plateaus, above the deepest marshes but still beneath the black vapor canopy, the land opens into broad wet barrens. Here the wind is stronger. Grasses grow low and metallic, whispering against each other. Pools collect in shallow basins of worn stone. Scattered across the flats are single structures: a switch house with one lit window and no road leading to it; a line of ceramic insulators marching into fog; a crane frozen with its hook submerged in a pond; a small shrine built from turbine blades, polished by rain and touch. Animals cross these plateaus in herds, their backs slick, their calls carrying for miles.

Below the plateaus, the valleys are almost overcrowded. Every vertical surface is occupied. Roots wrap pipes. Lichens coat walls. Snails graze on mineral crusts. Birds nest in valve wheels. Water threads through everything, making no distinction between nature and construction. A stairway may descend from a fern-choked ledge into a flooded generator hall where fish drift between columns and vines hang from the ceiling. A living tree may grow from the center of a rusted boiler, its roots descending through old fire tubes into black mud.

There are rules here, though they are not written in any surviving language.

Do not trust dry ground; it may only be water waiting for weight.

Do not stand near silent machinery during the first warm rain.

Do not follow lights seen under the surface of pools unless you know which ones are animals, which are lamps, and which are reflections from somewhere else.

When the reedbeds go quiet, climb.

When the towers begin clicking in sequence, leave the valley floor.

When the fog smells of copper, do not touch exposed metal.

And if, in the full darkness between lightning pulses, you hear a horn sounding from far inside the mountain, all creatures—winged, furred, scaled, rooted, and human if any remain—pause for a moment. The marsh stills. The engines seem to listen. Then some deep system shifts its load, and the landscape resumes: dripping, hunting, growing, pumping, eroding, breathing.

It is a wet highland the size of nations, hidden in darkness, warm as a living body, ancient in form and restless in process. It has factories like buried organs, forests like lungs, marshes like memory. Nothing in it is entirely natural anymore, and nothing artificial has remained merely artificial. Everything has entered the same cycle of rust, root, rain, heat, hunger, and return.
