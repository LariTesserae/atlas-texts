At the top of the old mountains there is a city that has almost no stone left in it.

From far below, it looks like a dark green stain spread across a high, pale tableland: a settlement draped over a nearly flattened summit, where the world has been worn down by ages of wind until even the cliffs seem tired. The approach is long and cool. The road rises through clouds, through slopes of exposed gray mineral and old scree, and then, abruptly, the mineral gives way to living matter. Not forest in the ordinary sense. Not jungle. The city itself is vegetation.

The ground underfoot is a mat of ancient rootwood, springy in some places, hard as fired brick in others. It has grown in plates and ribs, in lanes and terraces, in long vascular embankments that hold the shape of streets. The avenues are wide, often nearly empty, bordered by walls of woven trunk and lacquered bark. Bridges are braided from pale stems as thick as ships’ masts. Towers rise as bundles of vertical cane and dark-leaved buttresses, their upper floors flowering in slow, cold air. Windows are not cut but persuaded: oval gaps where the plant tissue has thinned into translucent membranes, veined like leaves and glowing softly when the daylight passes through.

Everything is plant, but not everything is lush. The place is open, high, austere. Vast squares lie between districts, surfaced with a low, mosslike skin the color of oxidized copper. Long distances separate the great living structures. There are boulevards where nothing moves except vapor and the ripple of leaves. The vegetation has taken the role of stone, brick, roof, pipe, stair, gutter, wall, rail. It fills the world as material rather than as thicket. There is space between its presences, and the spaces feel deliberate, as if the city has inhaled and held the breath for centuries.

Moisture comes and goes. It is not a wet place, not truly. Rain arrives in narrow bands that sweep across the plateau and are gone within minutes, leaving the bark blackened and shining. Condensation gathers on the undersides of bridges, on the tips of hanging tendrils, on the cold membranes of windows. Water collects in root-basins at intersections and in cistern-flowers the size of rooms, then slowly drains into the city’s vascular depths. In the morning, fog rises from cracks in the living streets, not thick enough to hide the city, only enough to make every distance uncertain.

The air is cool and thin. It smells of green sap, iron, old pollen, wet rope, engine oil, and the faint mineral bitterness of high places. Light is ordinary daylight, but it behaves poorly here. At noon the streets are clear and evenly lit, yet shadows sometimes fall in the wrong direction. A tower may cast a shadow uphill. A bridge may shade a square before it has been built, or after it has withered away. The sky above is bright, washed blue-white by altitude, but in puddles it reflects a different sky: darker, lower, crowded with immense leaves that are not overhead.

The city is ancient beyond civic memory. It was once built, perhaps, with plans and tools and scaffolds. It may once have had foundations of stone, copper conduits, retaining walls, warehouses, manufactories. Some of those remain, embedded like fossils in the plant mass. Here and there a steel girder protrudes from a trunk, swallowed halfway, its rust flaking into the bark. Old brick arches curve beneath root-vaults. Chimneys remain, but their stacks have been occupied by reedlike growths that exhale steam. Rails run through several districts, though the metal has long since been tightened into the woody surface, and railcars now move only when the roots beneath them pulse.

The industry of the place has not ended. It has become botanical.

There are factories in the eastern wards where the buildings breathe in rhythm. Their walls expand and contract with a slow muscular sound, drawing cool air through filters of fern and hairlike fiber. Inside, presses made of living timber squeeze resin from cultivated trunks. The resin runs in amber channels, thick and luminous, into vats warmed by fungal furnaces and ancient electric coils. Conveyor belts, patched with bark and rubber, carry seed-capsules, leaf-sheets, bundles of medicinal fiber, coils of root-cable. Pistons move. Pumps cough. Dynamo houses hum beneath green domes where enormous leaves track the sun like mechanical panels.

The city is loud.

Wind pours constantly over the plateau and breaks against the towers in a sustained, organlike roar. Leaf-vanes clatter on rooftops. Sap-pumps thud under the streets. Rain, when it comes, drums hard on stretched membranes and broad foliage, thousands of surfaces answering at once. In the industrial quarter the noise gathers into a layered pressure: grinding gears, woody creaks, hydraulic sighs, the slap of wet belts, the buzz of electrical lines wrapped in vine. Even in the empty plazas there is sound—distant machinery, the groan of living architecture shifting its weight, the dry applause of leaves high above.

Yet there are few animals. A pale moth may appear at a lamp-vein and vanish. Small wingless birds, if they are birds, nest deep in the hollow signs above unused stations and are seen only as brief triangular shadows. Once in a while something hoofed and extremely thin crosses a far terrace at dusk, stepping carefully between root seams before dissolving into green colonnades. There are insects, but not many, and their absence makes the vegetation seem less like habitat than sovereign matter. The plants do not support an ecosystem so much as replace it.

The old districts are arranged in rings around a central depression, a basin where the original summit has eroded almost flat. There stands the civic grove: a circle of immense trunks grown together into a single administrative body. Its bark is carved with records, though the carvings heal and rearrange themselves. Decrees appear as scars. Maps bud from the surface in raised relief, showing streets that sometimes do not exist until a week later. Residents come to read the bark, touching it with gloved hands, arguing softly beneath the great canopy while above them loudspeakers grown into seedpods broadcast weather reports, factory quotas, and warnings about unstable neighborhoods.

The people, if present, keep to covered walks and maintenance galleries. They wear layered coats, rubber boots, respirators during pollen shifts, and harnesses for the steep exterior stairs. They prune traffic signals. They oil root-switches. They tap walls to listen for rot. They do not appear numerous. The city seems too large for them, and perhaps it is. Many houses are inhabited only by slow vines and the memory of use. Balconies open onto empty air. Stairways climb into sealed blossoms. Whole apartment blocks have folded inward, not collapsed but reverted, each room becoming a chamber in a larger organism.

The rules of the place are unreliable, though not chaotic. They have settled into an ancient pattern of exceptions.

Streets remember footsteps. A lane heavily used will shorten over time, drawing its ends together like a healing cut. An abandoned road may lengthen until crossing it takes all afternoon. Doorways lead where they are most expected to lead, unless approached in grief, fever, or rain. Elevation is particularly unstable. The city is already high, perched on the worn roof of the mountains, but certain staircases climb higher than the plateau permits. From those upper walks one can see not only the surrounding valleys but previous versions of them: glaciers that no longer exist, seas that never reached this altitude, forests buried millions of years below.

In some plazas, erosion continues visibly and impossibly. A curb may age to dust over the course of an hour, while a fallen leaf beside it remains fresh for months. Statues of civic founders have weathered into smooth green humps, then sprouted. The oldest walls are the least solid; they shimmer at the edges, as if time has rubbed them thin. People avoid touching the pale places where reality has been worn nearly through. Tools left there return as seeds, or as rust, or as the sound of tools being used somewhere underground.

The plateau’s edge is never far, though the city’s openness makes distance deceptive. Beyond the outer districts, root-streets taper into terraces and windbreaks. The land falls away in long slopes of scrubbed stone and clinging vegetal infrastructure: aqueduct-vines, elevator trunks, cable roots descending into cloud. There are dry ravines where ancient rivers once cut deep channels, now bridged by living conduits that carry captured rainwater from one cistern grove to another. The mountains around are rounded, diminished, old as knuckles. Their high ridges have been softened almost to plains, but the valleys remain far below, blue with distance.

At dusk the factories brighten from within. Not with firelight, but with green-white electrical glow diffused through sap and membrane. The towers become silhouettes of leaves and pipes. Condensation thickens. The wind cools further. The city’s great vascular system changes pressure, and every wall gives a minute answering creak. Water released from the cistern-flowers runs beneath the streets, audible through grates as a sudden underground river. For an hour the place seems almost lively: pumps increasing, leaves rotating, transit roots flexing, bells sounding from high platforms to warn of fog.

Then the rain may arrive—brief, hard, silver in the ordinary light. It strikes the living roofs and runs down channels shaped like veins. It darkens the plazas. It beads on the industrial rails. It wakes a smell of raw green matter from every surface. The sound becomes enormous, a single body being played by weather.

And when the rain passes, the city remains: cool, elevated, ancient, half-empty, loud with hidden work. A metropolis of plant matter and old machinery, grown over the last stable bones of the mountain. It is not returning to wilderness. It is not preserving civilization. It is some third condition, old enough to have stopped choosing between them.
