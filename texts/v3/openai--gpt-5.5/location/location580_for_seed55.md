It is a drowned low country, so broad that no shore can be imagined from within it. The world is water from the overhead darkness down to the hand-laid floor, water in every room and street and orchard, water moving through doorways where air once passed, water settling in stairwells, wells, cisterns, courtyards, roofless halls, and the long straight beds of former canals. There is no dry horizon. There are only degrees of depth, degrees of blackness, and the slow pressure of cold water resting over everything.

The land beneath is almost flat, but not natural. Nothing here is bare rock, mud, sand, or reef. Every surface has been made. The floor is a continent of fitted stone slabs, clay brick, carved timber, packed ceramic tile, and basket-woven retaining mats hardened by age and mineral crust. Low walls run in mile-long lines, turning at measured angles. Causeways cross sunken basins. Circular cistern-mouths open into deeper dark. Terraces step downward so gradually that the drop is felt more as a change in pressure than a change in height.

It was built by simple hands. You can tell. There are no seamless alloys, no machine-smooth planes, no glass towers, no engines. Everything bears the scale of wrists, shoulders, rope, lever, chisel, wedge, fire, patience. Stones are dressed but not perfect. Mortar bulges between blocks. Wooden beams were cut with early blades and joined with pegs. Great walls are not clever so much as persistent: layer after layer, repair after repair, generations of additions made until the constructed surface became the only world. Even the seabed is a pavement. Even the planting grounds are framed. Even the channels of water were once guided, divided, slowed, offered a place to rest.

Now the water has taken those intentions and made them quiet.

The cold is deep and steady. It is not the sharp cold of wind but the slow cold of immersion, the cold of stone at the bottom of winter. It enters everything and remains there. Wood has blackened but not vanished. Ropes, where buried in silt, still keep the memory of twist. Clay jars sit upright in rows, filled with water the same as the water outside them. Door lintels wear collars of pale mineral. The iron, where there was any, is mostly gone, leaving rust-colored ghosts in the joints.

Light barely arrives. Far above, if there is a surface, it is more rumor than sky. At certain hours a dull grayness presses downward, not enough to illuminate the place, only enough to separate near from far. Edges appear first: the lip of a stair, the crest of a wall, the pale underside of a leaf. Distance is swallowed quickly. Whole avenues begin as a few upright shadows and end in black. The atmosphere of the place is not empty, but veiled, suspended, densely layered with cold water and fine age.

Vegetation has filled the drowned architecture with a slow, lush patience. It does not grow riotously; nothing here is warm enough or bright enough for riot. But it is everywhere. Long dark fronds stream from parapets and bridge rails, moving only when the water moves. Pale weed covers the paving in soft maps. Moss-like mats fur the steps. Filaments hang from windows in green-black curtains. Broad leaves, thin as soaked cloth, spread across courtyards where sediment has gathered in the corners. In the old planting terraces, beds of submerged growth lie thick and low, their surfaces dimpled by trapped bubbles that rise only rarely.

The plants have learned the architecture better than any inhabitant could. Roots enter the seams between stones. Algae polish the undersides of arches. Fine green threads stitch cracked tiles together. In cisterns, circular gardens form around the inner walls, descending ring by ring into darkness. In the long channels, ribbonweed lies in the direction of ancient current like combed hair. On roofs now become floors, mats of cold vegetation spread over hand-shaped clay, softening the geometry without erasing it.

There are no animals. No fish pass through the lanes. No crabs work at the corners. No shells cling to the walls. No birds cross the imagined surface. The absence is one of the first things the place teaches. Movement belongs to water and plants only. A leaf shifts. A strand of weed bends. A veil of silt lifts, drifts, and settles again. Nothing darts. Nothing hunts. Nothing startles. The silence has no hidden life in it.

Sound is scarce and greatly softened. A stone, if dislodged, does not crack sharply; it knocks once, low and round, then the noise is gone. Water touches water in muted folds. Somewhere in the dark, a beam relaxes after centuries and gives a single wooden complaint. Bubbles collect under a roof and release themselves one at a time with small, intimate taps. Far above, rain may be falling on the surface, but down here it arrives only as a dim pressure, a trembling too large and soft to be called sound.

The place has weather, but it is underwater weather. Slow currents replace wind. Cold layers slide over colder layers. At times a faint milky drift comes through the streets, mineral-rich and opaque, and the whole city-country disappears into itself for days. Then it clears by degrees, revealing the same walls, the same courtyards, the same weed-heavy gates. In some seasons the upper water darkens further and the plants go almost still. In others, a weak green cast enters from above and the submerged gardens thicken by a finger’s breadth.

Because the scale is vast, no single ruin defines it. There are districts of low domes where every roof is covered in black weed. There are fields of square basins, each one lined with tile, each one now an identical cold pool within the greater pool. There are avenues broad enough for processions that have become corridors of dim suspended growth. There are retaining walls that run beyond sight, straight as laws. There are hollow mounds made entirely of collapsed dwellings, their rooms compacted into layered masonry like sedimentary stone, though no natural sediment lies beneath.

Everything is old beyond drama. Catastrophe, if there was one, is no longer visible as catastrophe. The flood is not an event here; it is the condition. Erosion has done its long work and then slowed, as if the world has reached a final arrangement. Corners have rounded. Inscriptions have become shallow grooves. Steps are worn into troughs. Doorways have lost their doors and then their thresholds, leaving only openings with softened edges. The constructed land has been humbled, but not destroyed. It has settled into a final, patient shape.

There is density without crowding: walls near walls, roots in joints, hanging weed in doors, channels beneath terraces, basins beneath rooms, rooms beneath silt, silt beneath mats of green. The layers are close, but they do not press urgently. They have had too long to hurry. The whole place is saturated — with water, with age, with handiwork, with the quiet persistence of vegetation.

Its rules are simple.

Nothing burns.

Nothing dries.

Nothing moves quickly unless it is falling, and even falling is slowed.

Every surface was once touched by a maker.

Every maker is gone.

Plants may cover, bind, soften, and conceal, but they do not conquer in a season. They inherit by millimeters.

Water enters every chamber and makes all chambers equal.

Darkness is the common weather.

Cold preserves what warmth would have consumed.

If you travel through it, you do not cross a landscape so much as pass through an enormous submerged artifact. A made continent. A hand-built seabed. A gardened ruin without beasts or voices, lying low under black water, still receiving the faintest light, still growing, still wearing down, still keeping the shape of labor after all labor has ended.
