At this height the world should be stone and glare, wind and cold, but it is neither. It is warm, dim, green, and made.

The plateau is not a plateau exactly, because there is no exposed earth underfoot. There are no cliffs of mineral, no scree, no old bones of mountains visible except in rumor. Everything that can be stood on, climbed through, sheltered beneath, or crossed over has been woven, grafted, pressed, grown, cut, rejoined, and patiently persuaded into form. The whole high country is a constructed ecology: a continent-sized architecture of living plants suspended above cloudfields, rooted into itself, resting on pillars no one has seen the bottoms of.

The ground gives slightly with every step. It is a mat of layered stems as thick as roads, old bark plates polished by generations of bare feet and soft paws, fibrous decking grown flat by repeated trimming. In places the walking surfaces are broad and almost empty, green-brown causeways curving for miles through haze with nothing rising from them but occasional ribbed arches or the skeletal remains of older trellises. In other places the vegetation becomes total: walls of leaf, curtains of aerial roots, braided trunks grown into chambers, bridges, cisterns, watch platforms, animal runs. Even the hollows are plant-made. Even the drains are veins.

Water is everywhere but rarely in the form of open lakes. It beads, seeps, condenses, gathers under leaf-lips, slips down cords of moss, collects in cup-shaped bromeliad basins large enough to drown a house. The air is always newly post-rain. Surfaces shine without reflecting much light. On the underside of the great leaf roofs, droplets swell slowly until they fall in single heavy taps, each one swallowed by moss before it can echo. The rocks weep, though they are not rocks; they are petrified-looking knots of ancient rootwood, mineralized by sap and rain and time until they have the gravity of boulders. Water threads through them in clear lines, as if the wood remembers riverbeds.

The warmth is close and bodily. It does not blaze. There is little sun to speak of. Above, layer after layer of canopy and vapor turns daylight into a low green dusk, a submerged brightness. Morning and evening are almost indistinguishable except by smell: morning carries the peppery odor of new opening leaves; evening smells of bruised stems, damp fur, and the faint sourness of flowers that only bloom when the light fails. Sometimes the cloud below the plateau glows upward, and then the whole underside of the living world becomes faintly luminous, roots and hanging vines appearing as dark script against a milk-white depth.

The place is vast enough to have its own weather. Warm winds travel through the upper corridors for days before arriving. Rains are not storms so much as migrations: a darkening begins at one horizon, then a million leaf-cups turn their pale interiors upward. Condensation forms along miles of suspended tendrils. Water falls inside the structure, from canopy to platform to under-canopy to lower gardens, delayed and sorted by leaf shape and bark channel. A rain may take three days to pass from the topmost catching nets to the lower cistern forests. By then it has become less weather than inheritance.

There is emptiness here despite the excess of life. The distances are enormous. A bridge of braided liana may span a gorge of mist so wide that the opposite side appears only as a darker density in the green air. Between the built groves are long vacant reaches: silent decks, abandoned trellis fields, plazas of bark worn smooth and slick, chambers large enough to hold a village but occupied only by mist and one hanging fern. The plant-mass fills the world, but not evenly. It makes surfaces, membranes, edges, corridors — and between them are volumes of quiet air.

The builders use no engines. Perhaps they never did. Their technology is old hand-knowledge: bone awls, stone blades, wooden clamps, ladders of braided fiber, wedges, pulleys, waterweights, grafting needles, fire kept small and rare in ceramic-lined pits. Nothing is nailed if it can be grown together. Nothing is carried if it can be trained to bend. Villages are not built so much as edited out of growth. A dwelling begins as a ring of saplings tied inward, roofed by leaves selected for breadth, thickened over decades by graft and pruning. Granaries are swollen gourds hardened in place. Doors are living shutters that respond to touch and moisture. Stairs are root-flights, coaxed into regular knots. Bridges are raised like children.

Yet the made-ness is everywhere. The apparent wilderness has angles, repetitions, intentions. Rows of pale breathing flowers mark ventilation shafts. Spiral trunks twist at measured intervals to strengthen load-bearing groves. Vines descend in paired cords where someone once needed a lift. Water channels turn too neatly to be accidental. Even the so-called wild thickets grow on old frameworks: lattice-bones under moss, terraces beneath ferns, the geometry of forgotten hands softened but not erased.

It has lasted a long time. Erosion here is not collapse; it is accommodation. The old structures sag into new usefulness. A cracked cistern becomes a frog valley. A fallen bridge roots where it touches and becomes a ridge. Abandoned watchtowers thicken into fruiting columns. The living architecture has weathered into balance, each failure feeding another support. Nothing remains as first shaped, but nothing is simply ruined. Age is a slow green negotiation.

Sound is sparse and close. The moss drinks footsteps. Leaves rub without rustling much, too wet to chatter. The animals have learned the value of quiet in a world of suspended paths. There are soft throat-clicks from unseen climbing things, the membrane-hum of insects passing near the ear, the delicate scrape of claws on bark, the plop of water into hidden pools. Far away, perhaps miles away, a branch the size of a road shifts under its own weight and gives a low wooden sigh. Then silence seals over it.

Fauna is abundant, but much of it is glimpsed rather than heard. Pale arboreal deer move along side-bridges, their hooves split wide to grip wet bark. Wingless birds with long balancing tails run the vine rails in pairs. Glassy amphibians sleep in the water-cups, their organs visible as colored threads. Moths as broad as hands cling to the dim sides of dwellings, patterned like lichen maps. There are slow predators too: vine-cats with moss-dark fur, patient enough to remain part of a wall for half a day; hook-beaked gliders that drop silently through vertical shafts; root-snakes that hunt by sensing tremors in the living floor.

Many creatures are partly domestic and partly not. Colonies of wax insects seal seams in houses in exchange for flower access. Small black crabs patrol the cistern channels, cleaning rot. A kind of blind monkey harvests fungal blooms from high rafters and leaves the poisonous ones in neat piles, though no one knows who taught them. Children wear beetle-shell charms not as decoration but as treaties; certain insects will not cross a threshold marked with their own dead.

The world’s rules are not entirely dependable.

Distance changes in the fog. A familiar path may take an hour one day and an afternoon the next, not because it has moved, exactly, but because the space between its anchor-trees has lengthened in the damp. Verticality is also uncertain. Look down through a gap in the decking and the lower levels seem both near enough to touch and immeasurably far away, stacked in repeated green silhouettes until perspective loses confidence. Dropped objects sometimes fall quickly, sometimes drift, sometimes return days later embedded in fruit.

Memory has weight here. Old paths remain easier to walk even after they are overgrown. Frequently used rooms stay larger inside than their outer walls permit. Forgotten chambers shrink, filling with pith and pale root-flesh until only animals can pass through them. Names affect growth. A bridge that is still spoken of will hold longer than one ignored. A door called by the wrong name may open into a wet passage from another season.

There are places where reality has frayed so thoroughly that the inhabitants mark them with white leaves and avoid pruning nearby. In these zones, flowers bloom with shadows instead of petals. Rain falls upward from pools to clouds of suspended droplets. Ferns grow in loops, each frond emerging from its own tip. Animals crossing such places may return with their left and right sides subtly mismatched, or with the hunger of an ancestor, or with no reflection in standing water. The builders do not explain these things as magic or disease. They treat them as weather: dangerous, recurrent, requiring etiquette.

The dimness deepens toward the interior forests, where the plant architecture becomes cathedral-like. Great ribbed vaults of living wood rise hundreds of meters overhead, layered with hanging gardens. The air there is warmer, almost feverish, and smells of sap, wet pollen, and animal breath. Light comes from fungal crusts along old graft scars, from translucent seedpods, from the occasional opening in the canopy where a shaft of dull silver descends and makes every droplet visible. These interiors are nearly silent despite being full of life. A thousand organisms feed, mate, digest, grow, shed, and hunt in a hush so complete that the drip of water from one leaf to another seems ceremonial.

At the outer edges of the highland, the constructed world thins into vast open spans. Here the plant-substrate forms long suspended plains, flexible and sparsely inhabited, sloping gently toward curtains of cloud. The wind is stronger. Warm mist pours upward from unseen lowlands and condenses on the underside of the decks, feeding root-beards that trail into whiteness. The horizon is not a line but a fading: green structure, gray vapor, darker green, nothing. Sometimes distant portions of the living continent appear through breaks in the cloud — enormous, impossible masses of woven forest hanging at the same altitude, connected by bridges too far away to see clearly.

No one remembers the first makers. The present people maintain what they can with patient tools and inherited songs. They cut rot from load-bearing trunks, guide new shoots across old gaps, harvest rain from leaf-cisterns, and teach children which floors are young enough to flex and which old ones must be crossed softly. They do not think of themselves as living in nature, nor outside it. The distinction would be useless here. The world is artifact and organism, house and weather, garden and continent.

It breathes slowly.

At night — if the deeper dimness can be called night — the broad leaves fold partway down, and the empty spaces become larger. Warm fog moves through the corridors in visible pulses. Animals settle into cavities and woven nests. Somewhere far above, rain begins again, first as a change in pressure, then as the soft gathering of droplets on leaf-skins. Nothing roars. Nothing announces itself. The whole vast made forest listens inward while water descends through its green machinery, level by level, as it has for ages, as if the entire high world were one enormous hand-crafted instrument being played by mist.
