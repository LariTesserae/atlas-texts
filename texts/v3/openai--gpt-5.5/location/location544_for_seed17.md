At the top of the world there is an ocean with no shore.

It lies over a plateau so high that, if the water were ever taken away, the rock beneath would stand in the thin blue-black air above most weather. But the water is never taken away. It has been here long enough to seem original, though the stone still has the angular unfinished look of something recently broken from a planet’s interior. Ridges rise like the backs of buried knives. Basalt horns and shelves of pale feldspar cut upward into the water, sharp-edged, scarcely rounded. Scree fields hang where they fell, each block intact, not yet softened by time. The whole submerged continent feels young, but also arrested: created in violence, then held immediately in a cold preserving medium.

The light is ordinary in a way that feels impossible. Not bright, not dim, not enchanted—just the gray-white daylight of an overcast afternoon, spread evenly through kilometers of water. There is no visible sun. Above, far above, the surface is a continuous hammered ceiling, silver and flat, too distant to ripple distinctly. The water itself is clean, glass-cold, and faintly blue. It has the clarity of melted ice, but the scale of a sea. Looking across it, vision fails not because of silt or darkness but because distance finally becomes too large to understand. A cliff thirty kilometers away hangs in the water like a wall at the edge of thought.

Nothing green grows here. No kelp forests, no weeds clinging to stone, no mats of algae feathering the metalwork. The rocks are bare. The sediment is mineral powder, black and white and rust-red. The surfaces that in another world would be furred with life are clean enough to seem newly machined. Where biology exists, it does not form landscapes. It moves, passes, pulses, gathers in bodies and then leaves the surfaces naked behind it.

The emptiness is the first fact and the last. The ocean is full of volume and nearly empty of matter. Vast tracts contain only water, suspended grit, the occasional slow animal, the far-off geometry of a pylon or derrick. A person could drift for days between landmarks, hearing the world without seeing anything touchable. Distances are not measured by horizons here, but by how long a sound takes to become unbearable and then ordinary.

Because the place is loud beyond reason.

The sound is not a collection of noises but a continuous condition, like pressure or cold. It arrives through the bones, the teeth, the chambers of the chest. Somewhere across the drowned plateau, engines run in permanent synchronization: pumps, turbines, compressors, flywheels the size of towns, all locked into a single industrial chord. The water carries everything. Deep metallic hammering travels from unseen foundations. Chains groan in slow cycles. Valves release bass pulses that roll across the seabed like thunder trapped in stone. There are shrieks too—thin, blade-like, almost electrical—when current passes through old transmission spars or when ice plates high above grind against anchoring towers.

But nothing changes. That is the terror of it. The noise does not rise or fall with weather. It does not indicate an approaching storm, a failing machine, an animal migration, a collapse. It is simply the acoustic climate of the place. The same great note has been sounding for so long that the fauna have organs built around it.

They are abundant, though never enough to fill the water. They appear as sudden events in the emptiness.

Pale shoals cross the open spans in sheets so thin they seem like cracks in glass. Each creature is long and translucent, with a mineral spine visible as a silver thread inside it. They do not school by sight, but by vibration, aligning themselves to the standing waves thrown off by the buried engines. When the bass pulses pass through them, their bodies flash with internal frost-light—not bioluminescence exactly, more like stress in a crystal.

There are larger animals too, built for cold and pressure: slow wheel-shaped grazers with no plants to graze, scraping instead at chemical frost that forms on pipes and stone; blind ribbon predators that hang motionless in the open water until a shoal’s pattern falters; armored things like jointed anvils that walk upside down along the undersides of industrial platforms. Their shells are not camouflaged. There is almost nothing to hide among. They are white, black, nickel-blue, transparent, or the dull yellow of old machinery.

Many species gather near the scattered works.

The structures are rare but immense when found, not cities exactly, not ruins exactly. They stand miles apart across the drowned highland: pumping stations, skeletal towers, intake cathedrals, mooring frames, factories built to process something no longer named. Some are no larger than a house, a single pressure-sealed hut bolted to a ridge crest, its windows dark and its antennae combed backward by the unchanging current. Others are industrial reefs: lattices of pipe and strut rising from the basalt in tiers, chimneys venting no smoke but clear cold streams, platforms roofed in ice-glass, gantries bridging chasms wide enough to swallow weather systems.

They are not overgrown. Instead they are polished by passage. Animals have rubbed the corners smooth. The metal is pitted, but not decayed. There is corrosion in orange blooms around bolts and welds, yet it looks ceremonial, contained, as if rust here has a permitted geometry. Cables run from station to station across hundreds of kilometers, suspended in catenary arcs through the water. Some are thick as roads. Some hum visibly, making the water around them shimmer.

The machines still work.

No one tends them. Or if something tends them, it does so without leaving signs recognizable as maintenance. Indicator lamps burn behind thick glass in colors softened by depth: green, amber, red, a domestic palette made strange by scale. Rotors turn with patient exactness. Pistons rise and fall inside transparent housings filled with dark oil heavier than water. Conveyor belts crawl through sealed tunnels, carrying pellets of black stone, cylinders of compressed gas, blocks of blue ice, empty crates.

There is no hurry anywhere. Industrial production has become geology. The factories do not seem to be making progress toward completion or decline. They perform. They repeat. Their rhythm is the law that the animals, the currents, and the suspended mineral dust obey.

The cold is not dramatic. It does not bite in gusts or come and go. It is complete. Every surface has accepted it. The water is just above freezing, or below freezing by some permission of salt, pressure, and machine heat. Ice forms in unlikely places: not as sheets, but as rigid lace along cable knots, as clear collars around ladder rungs, as lens-shaped inclusions in the cracks of basalt. Above the highest towers, the underside of the surface ceiling carries dim, flattened icebergs embedded like thoughts that never descend.

There is weather here, but it is internal and almost stationary. Vertical veils of denser water hang in the distance, transparent curtains through which the light bends. Bands of tiny bubbles remain suspended for years in slow rotating columns above the vents. Snowfall occurs without falling: mineral flakes hover in layers, each layer fixed at its density, a white dust horizon in the middle of the sea. Currents exist, but they are stable corridors rather than wandering forces. One can cross from absolute stillness into a river of water moving eastward with the force of an avalanche, then back into stillness again, the boundary as precise as a wall.

Because the plateau is high, the pressure is not abyssal, yet the surrounding scale makes it feel deeper than any trench. The submerged mountains rise close to the upper water, but the basins between them are enormous and clean. Chasms open suddenly in the rock—fresh tectonic tears, their walls glassy and angular, descending into blue distance. From their depths comes more sound: the low structural singing of the planet cooling, or of machines anchored below the crust.

Some cliffs bear marks.

They are scattered, easy to miss amid the enormity: straight grooves cut into basalt; rows of sockets where beams once fitted; numerals stamped into ceramic plaques; ladders leading nowhere; observation domes facing blank water. On one ridge, a line of small shelters stands half-buried in mineral silt, each exactly two hundred meters from the next. Their doors are shut. Their exterior lights remain on. Around them, spindle-legged animals congregate in vast numbers, not entering, not feeding, merely aligning their bodies toward the same silent wall, as if receiving warmth, instruction, or memory.

In another basin, a factory floor lies exposed under water with no building around it: square kilometers of gridded metal, rails, cranes, empty vats. The cranes move once every forty-seven minutes, always along the same path, lowering hooks into nothing and lifting them again. Each motion sends a clang through the water so powerful it makes shoals scatter in expanding circles. They return before the next cycle. They have always returned.

The ecology is not lush. It is mathematical. Life occupies frequencies, pressure bands, thermal margins, maintenance shadows. The animals do not swarm randomly; they arrange themselves around outputs. Near warm pipes, soft-bodied things unfold like pale cloth and absorb chemical traces through their skins. Around cold brine leaks, armored larvae cluster in hexagonal mats, then detach and drift away when their shells harden. In the open water, predators wait at nodes where engine harmonics intersect, knowing prey will lose orientation there. Reproduction is timed not by seasons—there are none—but by machine cycles, by the opening of sluice gates, by the monthly reversal of a coolant river that has not missed its interval in centuries.

There are no storms in the human sense. No day of violence followed by calm. The place has achieved a frozen equilibrium of motion. Everything moves, but nothing develops. Every current returns to itself. Every animal migration traces a closed loop. Every sound is part of a chord that never resolves. Even damage seems incorporated. A broken tower leans at forty degrees over a ravine, but it has leaned so for longer than memory. Its guy cables hold. Its warning bell rings continuously, struck by a mechanical arm that cannot stop.

The deafening permanence gives the landscape a paradoxical stillness. You can watch a million fish pass, a turbine spin, bubbles rise in white columns, gantries crawl, predators unfold, ice thicken around a railing—and feel that nothing has happened. The world is not dead. It is too alive with repetition.

The absence of vegetation makes the stone more exposed, more articulate. You see every fracture, every cooled ripple of lava, every plane where the young crust split. The high plateau was born jagged and remains jagged. In places, black volcanic glass forms fields of blades, each edge sharp enough to cut the water into trembling optical seams. In others, pale intrusive rock stands in ribs and bosses, like bones not yet buried. There are impact pits too, or blast craters, circular and clean, their rims still raised. Some contain perfectly still pools within the greater ocean—denser liquid, darker, reflecting the overhead light like mercury.

The rules of the world are simple, though never explained.

Sound travels farther than sight.

Heat belongs to machines, not to sun or stone.

Growth is mobile; surfaces remain barren.

Distance is more real than direction.

Nothing rots unless given permission.

No process stops, but no process escapes its loop.

If a structure is lit, it is still part of the system.

If an animal is silent, it is listening.

And above all: the water is not an event. It is the medium of history. Whatever flood made this place has become indistinguishable from creation. The old category of land has no authority here. Mountains are underwater things. Roads are cables. Weather is resonance. Horizons are vertical as often as horizontal. A cliff can be a shoreline if a current breaks against it; a factory can be a reef if enough bodies circle it; an empty blue distance can be as solid a barrier as stone.

Sometimes, through the relentless noise, another vibration appears—not louder, but different. A low call from an animal vast enough to be mistaken for infrastructure. It moves beyond sight in the open basins, following the engine tone from station to station. Smaller creatures change alignment as it passes. Hanging cables tremble. Sediment lifts from the rock in a thin ghost and settles again exactly where it was.

Then the old chord resumes its dominance, or rather it is revealed to have never been interrupted.

The ocean remains bright, cold, mineral, immense. The plateau holds its unfinished edges. The machines keep their schedules. The animals move through the empty water like punctuation in an endless sentence. Nothing blooms. Nothing decays quickly. Nothing arrives from elsewhere.

And still the whole drowned height roars.
