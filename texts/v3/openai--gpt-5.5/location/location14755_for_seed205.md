At this height the heat feels improbable. The land should be cold, being so high above the low world that clouds drag their bellies along its rims, but the plateau stores warmth like a living kiln. The air is thin and bright, yet heavy with vegetal breath. Every exposed surface sweats. Stones, where they show at all, wear a skin of green-black gloss, and the trunks of plants are hot to the touch, almost fevered, as if the whole country is a body running a permanent temperature.

It is vast in a way that defeats ordinary distance. The plateau is not a field or forest but a raised continent of plant matter, miles upon miles of rooted mass spread over old eroded uplands. There are horizons inside it: horizons of leaf, of tendril, of ribbed canopies lying low against the land, of isolated towers of growth standing far apart like weathered monuments. The ground is rarely mineral. It gives slightly underfoot. It is a woven substrate of root, husk, dead fiber, living rind, fungal leather, seed-cases, and mats of broad, overlapping leaves hardened by sun into a springy crust. In places it cracks open to show older layers beneath: compressed centuries of stems, pollen, insect shell, woody foam, and black damp heat.

The terrain rolls in long, settled swells. Nothing here is young, even when it is growing. The forms have been worn into balance by ages of rain, sun, feeding, collapse, and regrowth. Valleys are shallow troughs where moisture gathers briefly after storms. Ridges are long-backed and armored in tough, pale grasses that stand only knee-high but go on for leagues. The great plants are scattered, not dense like a jungle, but each one commands an enormous domain. A single fan-tree may cover the area of a village, its leaves stretched on ribbed frames like green sails, its trunk a buttressed column corkscrewing upward before splitting into sunward plates. Between such giants lie open expanses of low plant-skin, red moss, heat-shimmering seed fields, and dry root plains where one can see far enough to feel exposed.

Water is present, but never generous. It comes in pulses and pockets. Afternoon clouds climb the plateau edges, unravel, and shed rain in narrow wandering curtains. A place may be drenched for ten minutes and then steam for three days. Condensation collects each dawn along the undersides of leaves and in the throats of flowers large enough to drown in. Some plants hoard water in translucent bladders strung along their stems; others grow wax basins at their crowns, guarded by spines and colonies of animals. There are streams, but many are seasonal and hidden, running through root tunnels beneath the surface, audible as a cool muttering under the hot mat. Open pools are rare and violently inhabited. Around them the ground is beaten smooth by hooves, claws, bellies, and dragging tails.

The light is ordinary only in the sense that there is a sun, a day, a shadow. It falls hard and white at midday, flattening the land into glare and blue shadow. Morning light turns the plateau gold-green and makes every hair and barb stand out. Evening brings a copper haze, because the plants release dust, spores, and aromatic smoke from sun-heated pores. The sky is wide, often empty overhead, though thunderheads breed around the far escarpments. When rain passes, it does not cool the land so much as wake it. Leaves unclench. Root vents open. The smell deepens from dry resin to pepper, rot, nectar, wet bark, hot animal hide.

The vegetation is nearly total, but it is not crowded. It is architectural, territorial. Here a grove of hollow reeds rises like organ pipes from a depression, each stem wide enough for a person to stand inside, singing whenever wind crosses its cut lips. There a slope is covered in overlapping succulent plates, each plate edged with tiny cilia that flex and taste the air. Farther on are forests of low, wandering vines that never rise above the waist but travel constantly, inch by inch, dragging their roots behind them and leaving pale scars where they have fed on older growth. Some plants grow downward from overhanging mats, forming curtains of fibrous rope into ravines. Others inflate at noon to lift their seed pods above browsing height, then deflate at dusk with long sighs.

The animals make the place overwhelming.

They are not always visible at first. The land appears open, spacious, even empty. Then the eye adjusts and the surface divides into motion. Beetles as large as bowls push through moss with clicking mandibles. Pale lizards bask on leaf plates until they vanish sideways into seams. Winged things hang beneath every broad frond, and when disturbed they lift in such numbers that the air darkens locally, not as a swarm but as weather. Herds move across the root plains in slow diagonals: tall, narrow grazers with split lips for stripping fungal rind; squat armored browsers that kneel to crack water bladders; delicate stilt-legged animals that step from leaf to leaf without disturbing the cilia.

Predators are everywhere, though many are patient. Some are animals. Some are almost plants. Heat-drunk cats with mottled bark-colored hides sleep in the ribs of fan-trees. Serpentine hunters live in the condensation channels and strike upward at drinking birds. There are mantis-like creatures that wear seed husks on their backs and remain still for days beside pollinator trails. There are flowers that close too quickly, and fruits that pulse with a warmth not their own.

Sound saturates the plateau. Silence is not natural here. Even at noon, when the heat presses everything low, the place thrums. Insects drone in layered pitches: a high silver whine near the ears, a wooden rasp from the grasses, a deep engine-note from buried colonies ventilating their nests. Leaves tick, click, and clap as they regulate themselves. Distant reed beds bellow when wind enters them. Animal calls carry absurd distances in the thin air: coughing roars, flute-like mating cries, alarm clatters, the drumming of hooves over root crust, the wet tearing sound of feeding. At dusk the noise rises to near-deafening. Pollinators pour from the shade, frogs begin hammering in hidden pools, night birds scream from the fan-tree crowns, and whole slopes buzz as flowers open and release heat.

The world has rules, and everything living knows them.

Do not cross a red moss plain after rain; the moss swells and becomes adhesive, trapping small animals until scavengers arrive.

Do not sleep beneath a water-tree unless you have watched it through a full afternoon; some drop fruit harmlessly, others drop larvae, and a few drop stones they have lifted slowly from the soil in root cages.

Do not trust shade that smells sweet.

Follow the stilt-birds to water, but never drink first. Wait for the armored browsers. They can smell poison blooms.

The plants here are not passive scenery. They are the land’s slow citizens, its masonry and machinery. They store the scarce moisture, pump vapor upward, feed cloudbanks with their breath, and anchor the eroded highlands against being carried away. Their roots knit cliffs. Their dead trunks become bridges. Their seed sails seed the lower valleys far beyond the plateau’s edge. Whole weather systems begin as exhalations from sunstruck fields of leaf.

The built traces are small and scattered, almost shy against the scale of the living country. A ring of stones half-swallowed by root-flesh on a ridge. A platform lashed high in a fan-tree with fiber rope, repaired so many times that it is more vine than craft. Low shelters made from cured leaf plates and bent reed, set on bare patches maintained with ash. Lines of carved stakes mark routes between water pockets, though many have been grown around, tilted, or incorporated into trunks. In one dry basin stand the remains of an old watch hut, its mud walls cracked, its roof replaced by a living mat of creeper that flowers only at night. Bone needles, obsidian flakes, woven collecting nets, gourds sealed with resin: the technology of those who pass here is hand-made, portable, respectful of weight. Metal is rare enough to be ceremonial or stolen from the sky.

People, if they live here, do so by negotiation. They burn little, because fire travels strangely across dry plant-skin and may wake seeds that should sleep. They cut with permission or with apology, taking only ribs from leaves already yellowing, only fibers from vines that regrow, only water from bladders after puncturing them in a way the plant can seal. They build above the ground when they can. The ground belongs to mouths.

There are strange phenomena that everyone accepts as weather. On certain hot mornings the plateau hums in unison, thousands of reed stems and insect chambers locking into one vast note that can be felt in the teeth. During sporefall, distances blur; far fan-trees seem to walk, and animals mistake their own shadows for rivals. In the old troughs, where root layers are deepest, the surface sometimes rises and falls slowly as if something beneath is breathing. Travelers say these are not creatures but buried forests digesting themselves into soil.

At the edges of the plateau, the land drops away through eroded escarpments draped in hanging vegetation. Clouds form there, torn upward by heat. Rainbows appear in the vapor below one’s feet. The lowlands are visible only in clear weather, a dim blue distance under the green rim. From the edge, the plateau can be heard behind you: the insect thunder, the reed calls, the endless chewing and flowering, the leaf mechanisms clicking in the sun.

It is a hot high world of sparse giants and total growth, dry until it is suddenly wet, open until it moves, ancient without being still. Everything is established, adapted, interlocked. Nothing wastes water. Nothing wastes shade. Nothing dies unused. The landscape is less a place covered by plants than a plant-made continent, inhabited by animals in such number that the air, ground, and roots all seem crowded with appetite. Even the empty spaces feel alive, held open deliberately, waiting for rain, migration, seed, or prey.
