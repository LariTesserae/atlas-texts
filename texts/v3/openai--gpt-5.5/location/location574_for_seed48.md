It is a low industrial basin at the edge of a warm, shallow sea, though the sea itself is usually hidden by walls, cranes, heat haze, and a long shelf of gray vapor lying over the horizon.

The ground is almost flat. Not naturally flat in the clean way of a salt pan, but worked flat, settled flat, pressed and repressed over many decades by machinery, tide, runoff, and repair. Roads cross it in long, straight lines, then buckle slightly where the wet subsoil has lifted them. Concrete aprons tilt toward drainage channels. Rail spurs vanish into standing water. Old loading yards spread out in enormous open rectangles, their painted markings faded to ghosts beneath a skin of silt and oily rainwater.

Everything here is low: low warehouses, low pump houses, low retaining walls, low bridges over black canals. Even the skyline keeps its head down. Stacks and distillation towers rise in clusters, but the constant dimness makes them seem less vertical than they are, like parts of some half-submerged machine. The city is not dense with people or buildings. It is dense with systems. Pipes, culverts, pumps, sluice gates, catwalks, tanks, vents, conveyors, switchyards, service alleys, locked yards, and concrete drains occupy the place where a living city might have had gardens, trees, courtyards, storefronts. The built fabric stretches for miles, but much of it is open space: asphalt basins, gravel lots, bare mud, puddled yards, skeletal gantries, and unused platforms steaming after rain.

There is no plant life to soften it. No grass at the cracks, no reeds in the ditches, no algae green on the canal walls. The absence becomes conspicuous after a while. The water is there, and the heat is there, and the ground is broken and full of seams where life should have taken hold, but nothing leafed or rooted has answered. The surfaces are mineral, metallic, synthetic: concrete, brick, rust, slag, tar, glass, ceramic, compacted ash, salt-stained stone. In the older districts the bricks have sweated white mineral blooms. In the newer ones, poured slabs have cured into pale expanses webbed with hairline cracks and patched with darker repairs.

The wetness is continual but not lush. It is the wetness of seepage, condensation, overflow, leak, tide push, stormwater with nowhere to go. The air beads on railings. Water gathers under loading docks and in the depressions between slabs. It weeps from the faces of cut embankments, from the joints of retaining walls, from conduits that were sealed years ago and have begun to breathe again. Even indoors, in the empty sorting sheds and disused compressor halls, moisture slicks the floors and darkens the lower courses of walls. Drips fall from roof beams at irregular intervals, though no rain is falling.

When rain does come, it arrives hard and warm. It drums briefly on sheet metal roofs, turns yards into mirrors, fills the concrete gutters until they run brown and silver, then stops. Afterward the whole basin exhales. Steam rises from pavement. Tank shells glisten. Water boils softly off hot pipe casings. The air smells of salt, iron, wet dust, fuel, old rubber, and the faint animal musk of the creatures that have claimed the city’s margins.

The heat is heavy, almost architectural. It fills the open spaces like another material. By noon, even under a dim sky, metal cannot be touched with a bare hand. The dimness does not mean coolness. It comes from cloud, haze, industrial vapor, sea mist, and suspended dust, all layered together into a low ceiling of light. The sun is usually a pale disk, visible only as a brighter wound behind the overcast. Shadows are weak. Colors flatten: ocher rust, gray concrete, black standing water, ivory salt crust, red warning paint bleached to pink. At night the basin does not become dark so much as amber and dull blue, lit by security lamps, sodium yards, flare stacks, and the slow red blinking of aviation lights on towers.

The place is old enough to have reached a kind of equilibrium. It is not freshly ruined, not collapsing dramatically. Its decay is methodical. Corners have rounded. Drainage paths have stabilized. Flood lines repeat themselves at the same height on walls. Rust advances in familiar territories. Concrete broken by subsidence has been patched, then patched again, and the patches have become part of the pattern. Some sections are active, some abandoned, but all of it belongs to one long weathered system. The basin has learned where it sinks, where it leaks, where the tide presses hardest, where salt grows overnight, where animals roost, where machines still wake at dawn.

Despite the scale of construction, it often feels empty. Wide roads carry no traffic for long minutes. A row of loading bays may extend for half a kilometer without a person in sight. Catwalks cross the canals like lines drawn in the air, unused except by birds. The distance between things matters here: between refinery blocks, between storage farms, between power substations, between the low workers’ housing on one dry rise and the freight terminals on another. The city has volume but little crowding. Much of it is made of spaces intended for turning, storing, flooding, venting, waiting.

Then the animals appear, and the emptiness proves false.

They are everywhere.

Not fantastic animals, not strange ones, but too many of them, concentrated in a place that otherwise seems hostile to life. Birds first: gulls, terns, cormorants, pigeons, black kites, egrets where the water is shallow enough, herons standing on concrete lips as if they were mangrove roots. They line the pipes and railings in dense ranks. They nest under gantries, on ledges, inside broken ventilation housings, atop the warm roofs of substations. Their droppings stripe the tanks and whiten the bollards. When one flock lifts, it is like a sheet being shaken loose from the city.

Rats move in the service trenches and along the dry edges beneath loading platforms. They are sleek from heat and abundant food, feeding on fish wash, grain dust from old silos, waste from the docks, eggs, carrion, anything that settles out of the industrial flow. Feral dogs sleep in the shade of parked machinery and patrol the empty roads in small, silent groups. Cats occupy the upper interiors of warehouses and the ledges of administration buildings. Bats fill expansion joints and culvert mouths. Crabs work the canal edges, picking through the trash racks. Fish gather thickly around warm-water outfalls where oxygen and discharge make uncertain gradients. Insects cloud wherever water stands long enough, though the lack of vegetation changes their habits; they breed in sumps, drains, buckets, tire ruts, bilge pools, and forgotten cisterns.

The overwhelming quality of the fauna is not always loud. Often it is a pressure of presence. Eyeshine under a stair. Wings shifting inside a roof. The sudden coordinated turn of hundreds of birds on a wire. Small prints in the wet dust over every threshold. Gnawed insulation. Feathers stuck to tar. Molted crab shells in the corners of machine rooms. The animals use the city better than its builders did. Pipes are branches. Drainage ditches are creeks. Tanks are cliffs. Conveyor housings are burrows. Abandoned offices are dens. The warm, wet, plantless basin has become a scavenger ecology, running on fish, refuse, heat, shelter, and water.

Sound carries strangely. For an industrial place full of animals, it is mostly quiet. The damp air absorbs sharp edges. The broad empty yards give noises room to thin out before they reach you. There is always some low mechanical presence: a transformer hum, a pump turning beneath a grate, the distant pulse of compressors, the faint tremor of fans inside a cooling tower. Sometimes a pressure valve releases with a sigh. Sometimes a chain knocks against a mast. Water ticks from roofs into puddles. Tires hiss on wet concrete far away and then are gone.

The animals make sound in bursts rather than continuously. A gull cry cuts across the basin and vanishes. Pigeons clatter up from a warehouse beam. Dogs bark once or twice from behind a stack of shipping containers, then fall silent. Rats rustle in dry packaging. Crabs scrape faintly on concrete at the canal margin. Mosquitoes whine near the ear in sheltered places. At dusk the birds become briefly immense, all wings and calls, as they exchange the hot roofs for ledges, wires, and bridge undersides. Then the sound drops away again into water, machinery, and the breathing of the humid city.

The canals are the main streets of the nonhuman world. They are straight-sided and dark, built originally for drainage, cooling, freight, and flood control. Some are narrow and black, squeezed between brick walls furred with salt. Others are broad basins reflecting the dim sky, with floating mats of debris but no weed, no green skin, no reeds. Sluice gates divide them into levels. At low tide they smell of mud, rust, and exposed shell. At high tide the sea pushes inland through them, lifting trash and oil films, bringing fish and crabs, stirring the rats from lower tunnels. The water is never clear. It is brown, gray, or metallic, sometimes with a rainbow sheen, sometimes hot enough near outlets to steam in the rain.

There are districts within the basin.

The tank fields occupy the eastern flats, hundreds of round steel tanks standing in bunded compounds. The bund walls hold shallow rainwater where birds bathe and insects breed. Ladders climb the tank sides. Walkways circle their rims. Many tanks still work; others are sealed and numbered but silent. Their paint has peeled in layers, revealing older company colors beneath newer ones. The ground there is gravel and stained clay, crunching underfoot unless it has flooded into paste.

South of the tanks are the fabrication sheds, long buildings with corrugated roofs and enormous sliding doors. Some still contain active lines: sparks behind welding curtains, the smell of hot metal, slow cranes moving beams through gloom. Others are empty except for nesting birds, water-filled inspection pits, and drifts of dust turned to mud near leaks. The roofs tick all afternoon as they expand and cool under passing cloud.

The western edge is older, made of brick, stone quay walls, and narrow canals. Here the buildings are lower and closer, though still not crowded. Former warehouses have been converted into workshops, depots, dormitories, storage, or nothing at all. Their lower floors flood during storms. Their upper floors hold cats, bats, and the last administrative offices with flickering fans and damp paper. The brick has been worn smooth by salt air. Doorsteps are hollowed by generations of boots. Iron rings remain set in the quay stones for barges that no longer come.

Beyond that is the residential strip, if it can be called residential. Rows of concrete housing blocks stand on a slight rise only a few meters higher than the rest, enough to keep most floodwater at the curb. Laundry dries poorly in the damp heat. Windows are shaded with metal awnings. Stairwells smell of warm plaster and mildew. Children know which yards belong to dogs, which canals have crabs, which fences are electrified, which roofs are safe enough to climb at evening when the heat loosens its grip. People live here, but they seem provisional compared with the systems and the animals. Human life threads through the basin rather than filling it.

At the lowest points, after storms, the city becomes a temporary archipelago. Roads disappear under shallow brown water. Painted lane lines waver beneath the surface. Fish enter the underpasses. Rats climb cable trays. Workers move on raised catwalks and in high-wheeled trucks. Pumps begin everywhere, their synchronized thudding turning the quiet basin into a subdued engine. Water slides through grates, culverts, sumps, basins, canals, outfalls. For hours the whole landscape is visibly performing the task it was built and rebuilt to perform: receiving water, holding it, dirtying it, heating it, moving it on.

Then the flood drops. A scum line remains on every wall. Crabs are stranded in wheel ruts. Birds descend to feed. The concrete steams. The city resumes its low, damp silence.

The rules of the place are simple and old.

Nothing stays dry unless it is actively kept dry.

Nothing stays cool unless shaded, buried, or wet.

Anything abandoned becomes shelter.

Anything warm draws animals.

Anything lower than the tide will eventually belong to the tide.

The lack of plants gives the basin an unfinished, exposed feeling, as if all the usual veils have been removed. There is no canopy, no undergrowth, no seasonal flowering, no soft succession reclaiming the built world. Time marks itself instead through corrosion, sediment, animal occupation, and repeated repair. A handrail thins year by year. A drain clogs, is cleared, clogs again. A colony of birds shifts from one warehouse to another after a roof panel fails. A road is lifted, repoured, and slowly sinks back toward the water table. The landscape changes, but in increments, through maintenance and pressure.

At evening the dimness deepens without much color. Heat lingers in the slabs and walls. The canals turn black except where lamps tremble on them. Birds settle in such numbers that some roofs seem furred. Dogs come out onto the roads. Insects gather under lights in trembling columns. From far off comes the muted horn of a ship or a shift siren softened by vapor. Pumps continue below the grates. Water continues to bead, fall, collect, and move.

The city feels both active and deserted, hostile and heavily inhabited. It is a wet, hot, low industrial plain where the human order has not vanished but has thinned, leaving its frames, channels, heat, and waste to support a dense animal life in the absence of any green world. It is not wild in the usual sense. It is not reclaimed. It is occupied.
