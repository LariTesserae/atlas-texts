At this height the world has been stripped down to its mineral decisions.

The plateau lies above most weather, above the blue-brown haze of lower air, in a clarity so dry it seems polished. There is no water in sight and no memory of water in the usual sense: no channels, no deltas, no stains of seepage, no darkening where moisture might have gathered. The ground is pale with salts that were never sea-salts, crusts precipitated from hot gases and ancient sublimations, layered in brittle plates over a deeper red-black stone. In places the crust has curled like old paint. In others it has fused into glassy pans, milky and opalescent, reflecting the sky with a hard, shallow brightness.

The light is almost ordinary. That is part of what makes the place difficult to understand. The sun appears where a sun should appear, not too large, not too dim. Shadows fall in reasonable directions. The horizon is visible. One could, at first glance, mistake the plateau for a terrestrial desert lifted to impossible altitude and abandoned.

But the ordinary light reveals too much.

The land extends for many leagues in open shelves and descending basins, a country of broad mineral floors interrupted by slow escarpments, leaning mesas, and ribs of darker rock exposed by ages of dry abrasion. Nothing grows. There is no brush, no lichen, no tough little plant negotiating with heat. The slopes are bare down to the grain. Even dust seems reluctant to gather except in long, low drifts at the bases of cliffs, where it arranges itself into crescent ridges and then remains there for years, perhaps centuries, moving only by the width of a hand when the high winds return.

The heat is enormous, but not lush or stormy. It is a high, clean furnace heat. Stones ring faintly as they expand. The air trembles, yet thinly, so the shimmer has a precise, metallic character. Distance does not blur so much as fracture. Far mesas separate into duplicate outlines. A ridge may show three summits from one angle, then one, then none, as if the landscape is being rendered by an impatient law. The heat does not make the world soft. It makes it exact, unbearable, and suspect.

There are no buildings. No towers, no pipes, no roads, no foundations, no camps. Yet the plateau is industrial.

It is industrial the way an organ might be industrial if magnified to continental size and freed from flesh. Under the salt crust, deep engines turn.

They are not machines built by hands. They are systems of pressure, crystal, animal, static, and heat. The dark ridges are gear-lines. The basins are production floors. The escarpments expose repeating mineral assemblies: hexagonal columns nested into toothed rings, coppery veins passing through them like busbars, black ceramic seams pulsing with dull internal redness. At intervals, vents open in the ground with the symmetry of intake valves. They do not smoke; there is too little volatile matter for smoke. Instead they exhale shimmering columns of hot, dry gas that smell of alkali, iron filings, scorched bone, and ozone.

These vents pulse slowly. One breath every hour. One deep stroke in the hidden engine.

When a vent exhales, the sound rolls across the plateau like a factory starting somewhere beyond the horizon. It is not a single explosion or gust, but a layered mechanical chorus: bass throbs, grinding harmonics, high whines that pass through the teeth, rhythmic clacking from rock faces as thermal joints open and close. The whole country answers. Crusts tick. Glass flats sing. Hollow mesas boom internally. The noise is loud enough to seem inhabited, organized, purposeful.

Then it stops, leaving a ringing absence.

Into that absence come the animals.

They are everywhere, though the land remains empty. That is the paradox of the place: vast space between presences, and yet fauna beyond counting. They do not crowd the ground like herds on a plain. They occupy strata, intervals, seams, and mirages. They hang in the air at different heights. They travel through the rock. They unfold from heat shimmer. They appear as flocks, swarms, caravans, and processions, but never with the wet urgency of living things from gentler worlds. Their abundance is dry, mineral, patient, overwhelming.

Nearest the ground move the clinker-beasts: low, many-limbed animals with bodies like slag cooled in motion. Their skins are black and blue and rust-red, cracked into plates that glow faintly along the seams. They cross the salt pans in wide, sparse migrations, each individual separated from the next by hundreds of meters, yet the migration may continue for days. Their feet are soft pads of asbestos-white fiber. They leave no tracks unless the crust is thin, and then the impressions smoke without flame.

They feed on charge.

At noon, when the light stands almost vertical, the salt fields accumulate a brittle electrical tension. The clinker-beasts lower their heads and spread branching mouthparts over the crust. There is a sound like paper tearing in another room. Blue sparks crawl upward into them. Their back-plates rise and fall once. Then they continue on.

Above them, in the wavering air, are the vane-swarms.

From a distance they look like ash, but there is no ash. They are flat, translucent animals, each no larger than a hand, with stiff radial fins and a dark central bead. They ride the thermal columns that emerge from vents and fissures. Millions may occupy one column, turning edge-on and face-on in coordinated flashes, so the air becomes a cylinder of intermittent silver. They make a constant slicing sound, a dry applause, loud when the swarm lowers itself into denser air. They do not flap. They adjust. Their movement is the movement of valves, shutters, regulator fins.

When the vents inhale — if inhalation is what follows the exhalation, a long reduction of pressure beneath the crust — the vane-swarms descend in spirals and press themselves flat against the ground. The entire plain may glitter with their bodies. Then, slowly, they stand on edge again and lift.

There are larger animals too, rare in any given acre but numerous across the open reach of the plateau. The horizon often carries their silhouettes: walking furnaces, ribbed balloons, crane-necked things with no heads, only arrays of black sensory pits. Some are lighter than air, buoyed by internal gases too hot and dry to condense. They drift at the speed of continental weather, casting long, webbed shadows over the flats. Their undersides carry clusters of smaller creatures, entire dependent faunas clinging like toolheads beneath a great machine.

One such animal may take half a day to pass overhead.

Its arrival is announced not by wingbeat but by tone. A subsonic hum presses into the salt crust; the ground answers with sympathetic vibration. Small stones arrange themselves into rings. The vane-swarms climb toward it, not in panic but in service or hunger. Filaments lower from the drifter’s belly, hundreds of black cables made of living keratin and conductive mineral. Where they touch the plateau, little white fires crawl along the ground. The animal harvests the charge stored in the crust and gives off a smell like hot metal cleaned with acid.

All of this happens slowly. Nothing hurries. Even predation has the patience of geology.

There are hunters whose bodies are almost indistinguishable from tilted slabs of stone. They wait among old erosion terraces for decades. A clinker-beast passes within reach; the slab shifts a degree. A seam opens. The prey animal continues walking, unaware that one of its legs now belongs to the predator. Hours later the rest of the body is drawn in, plate by plate, through a mouth that is also a fault. The sound is a low, methodical crushing. By sunset, the hunter has become part of the ridge again.

The erosion here is old and settled. It is not the fresh violence of flood or glacier. It is the result of heat, gas, abrasion, crystal growth, animal passage, and the patient collapse of impossible geometries. Cliffs are rounded where particles have blown against them for eras, but cut sharp where reality has sheared. Some slopes display strata that cannot agree on their own order. A lower layer may be visibly younger than the layer above it, not by fossil or color but by behavior: one still shifts at the edges, trying out positions, while the older stone rests with the authority of something that has already happened.

In the basins, the salt crust forms polygons many meters across. Their borders are raised, dark, and slightly warm. If one watches long enough, the polygons change adjacency. Not quickly. A traveler could sleep, wake, and find that the same three plates now meet at a four-way junction. No crack opens. No plate slides. The map simply revises itself. The animals take this into account. Their routes are not paths across space but negotiations with the plateau’s changing grammar.

Sound rules the place more than motion does.

The loudness is not frantic. It is cyclical, layered, and immense. Every few minutes some distant escarpment releases stored heat with a cannonlike report. Every hour the vents breathe. Every afternoon the glass pans begin to sing, each at a pitch determined by thickness and stress, until whole basins become orchestras of sustained, painful tones. The vane-swarms add their dry hiss. The ground animals clatter and scrape. Far below, beneath the crust, something rotates or reciprocates with the steadiness of a buried mill.

There are moments when the sounds align into rhythms too regular to dismiss. The plateau becomes an engine house without walls: pistons of gas, belts of migrating animals, resonant floors, conductive herds, living regulators. No intelligence is visible, unless persistence is a kind of intelligence. No one built it. No one tends it. Yet it produces something.

Not objects. Not goods. Not smoke.

It produces phase.

At certain times — most often in late afternoon, when the heat is strongest but the light begins to tilt — reality loosens over the basin floors. A dry clicking begins at the edges of the salt polygons. The air flattens. Shadows detach slightly from the things that cast them. A mesa may appear closer than a nearby stone. A swarm may pass behind the horizon and in front of one’s face at the same time. The animal calls, already strange, gain extra endings.

Then the breakdown opens.

It is not colorful. There are no auroras, no theatrical rips. Instead the world becomes over-specified. Every grain of salt acquires a visible border. Every border contains smaller borders. The space between two stones grows deep enough to hold weather, though there is no weather here. One sees alternate positions of animals layered around them like transparent shells: where they were yesterday, where they will be after the next venting, where they would have been if the basin had chosen another geometry. The plateau does not dissolve into chaos. It offers too many versions of order at once.

The fauna depends on these periods.

During the breakdown, creatures emerge that cannot exist at other times. Thread-thin grazers extend from cracks in the air itself, browsing on angles. Bell-shaped animals descend through the sky without falling, their rims chiming against invisible surfaces. Slow, enormous torsos move beneath the transparent ground, not buried but adjacent, as if the plateau has neighboring plateaus occupying the same altitude. Some animals are only sounds with shadows. Others are shadows with digestive systems.

They come in overwhelming numbers.

For an hour the emptiness is full past comprehension. Every void contains migration. Every shimmer contains a mouth. The air above the salt flats stacks with living layers: vanes, bells, wires, rings, transparent eel-forms, hinged black triangles, dustlike spores that are not spores because there are no plants and perhaps no water-based reproduction here. The noise rises until it seems the sky must crack. The hidden engines below answer with deeper pulses, and the animals arrange themselves to the rhythm, a vast factory of bodies processing heat, charge, pressure, and possibility.

Then, slowly, the extra orders withdraw.

The plateau resumes being a high dry expanse of salt and stone. The sun lowers. Shadows lengthen correctly again. A few clinker-beasts continue their patient transit. Vane-swarms settle in dark lines along warm cracks. The far drifters become vague bags of heat against the light. The vents close, or seem to. The buried machinery drops to a murmur.

At night, if night can be trusted, the heat leaves the surface only reluctantly. Stones glow dull red in their interiors. The sky is crowded with hard stars, and between them are gaps that look less like darkness than unrendered space. There is no dew. There is never dew. The salt remains dry enough to whisper under its own contraction.

Some of the animals become luminous after sunset, but not beautifully. Their glow has the utilitarian severity of indicator lamps: amber points along a ridge, green dashes moving across a basin, white pulses under the translucent wings of vane-swarms. From far away, these lights resemble the control panels of a factory spread over a region the size of a small country.

Still no walls. No chimneys. No rails. No hands.

Only the old high plateau, weathered into equilibrium with its impossible processes. It has had time to become stable. Its breakdowns are part of its climate. Its animals are part of its machinery. Its machinery is part of its geology. The lack of water is not absence here but law: no softening, no rot, no leaf, no mud, no mercy of dissolution. Everything is done by heat, by charge, by abrasion, by pressure, by the slow correction of forms.

If one were to stand there long enough, one might notice that the loud cycles do not measure time.

They manufacture it.

Each vent pulse lays down a little more duration in the salt. Each migration draws a usable path through the competing versions of the basin. Each drifter’s hum stabilizes the sky it passes beneath. Without the animals, perhaps the plateau would fly apart into all its possible shapes. Without the hidden engine, perhaps the animals would remain unassembled, scattered as potentials in the hot bright air.

So the place continues: empty and crowded, natural and industrial, silent in its distances and deafening in its operations, barren of plants and water yet violently alive. A high desert factory with no builder, an ecosystem of furnaces, an old eroded plain whose most important mineral is causality, mined slowly by innumerable beasts beneath ordinary light.
