Below the surface, far below any weather that would call itself sky, there is a hollow continent of warm stone.

It is not a cave in the familiar sense. It has no dripping ceiling, no patient stalactites, no black pools answering the footfall. It is too dry for that. It is an underworld emptied of water so completely that even the idea of dampness feels foreign here. The air, if it is air, is warm and mineral-thick, tasting of kiln dust, old salt, and the faint bitterness of crushed metals. Breathing it dries the mouth at once. Cloth stiffens. Skin tightens. Every surface seems to be drinking from you, though nothing here has ever known thirst.

The vault stretches beyond sight in every direction. Its ceiling is lost in darkness, not because it is infinitely high, but because light fails early here. A lamp shows only the nearest ridges, the nearest pillars, the nearest leaning faces of stone; beyond them the vastness continues as a pressure rather than an image. You feel its scale in the delay before an echo returns, or in the way it never quite returns at all. Sound is absorbed, caught among crowded ribs of mineral and strange porous formations, until a shout becomes a private thing.

The ground is young. It has the rawness of something recently opened, recently cooled, recently fractured. Edges are sharp. Ridges break like chipped obsidian. Sheets of pale mineral overlap one another as if the floor were made of enormous scales. There are fields of cracked white crust, salt or something like salt, spreading flat between black upthrusts of volcanic glass. There are amber plates, translucent and brittle, under which darker shapes are sealed. There are furrows where the material of the world folded while still soft and then hardened immediately, preserving every wrinkle. Nothing here has been rounded by rain or softened by moss. There are no old riverbeds, no silt, no mud. The landscape has wounds, but not scars.

It is dense without being lush. Nearly every space is occupied. The floor bristles with mineral growths: blade-thin fins, clustered knobs, hollow tubes, nodules like knuckles, pale combs that rise knee-high and continue for leagues. The walls are layered with shelves and dark seams. Pillars stand close together in places, not smooth cave columns but jagged stacks of fused fragments, as if a collapsed city had been compressed and reheated into geology. Even the empty spaces feel crowded, filled with suspended dust so fine it moves only when disturbed and then resettles almost at once.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green. Nothing leafed, rooted, or flowering. Yet the place is not dead.

Fauna is abundant, though much of it does not announce itself. Life here has learned quietness, or perhaps quietness is the original law from which life emerged. Small bodies cling to the undersides of ledges: shell-backed things no larger than a thumb, matte gray, arranged in regular rows like beads on a loom. They do not crawl unless touched by a change in heat. Then, slowly, a whole patch of stone may seem to loosen and rearrange itself.

In the salt flats, long narrow animals lie half-buried under the crust, their backs indistinguishable from mineral seams. They have no eyes visible, only shallow sensory pits along their sides. Their mouths are not mouths but grinding plates that rasp at the ground. They feed on chemical skins, on dry films, on the invisible accretions left where warm gases seep upward through hairline cracks. When they move, they make a sound like paper being folded underground.

Higher up, in the fissures, live the glass moths. They are not insects, though one wants to call them that. Their wings are transparent chips grown from their own bodies, too rigid for true flight. They launch themselves from ledges and glide in brief, silent descents, steering by tilting their thin bodies through the warm air. In a lamp beam they flash once and vanish. Their bodies are segmented and hollow. If crushed, they break rather than bleed.

There are larger creatures too, but they are seldom seen whole. A traveler might notice a line of shallow dents crossing a mineral plain, each one broad as a shield and spaced far apart. Or a row of polished grooves where something heavy has dragged its underside along the rock for centuries without seeming to hurry. In the deeper dark, colonies of heat-eaters mass around vents no wider than a hand. They resemble heaps of black seeds until they turn, all at once, toward the warmth of a living body.

The warmth of the place is steady. Not hot, not searing, but held at the temperature of something incubated. The stone radiates it. The air carries it. There are no cold drafts. No seasonal shift. No storm front. No meaningful morning or evening. Time does not pass here in weather. It passes, if at all, in abrasion so slight it may as well be ceremonial.

This is one of the unnerving rules of the hollow continent: it does not change unless entered.

Dust remains in the same drifts for generations. Loose flakes of mineral lean at impossible angles and do not fall. Warm vapors gather in pockets and stay there, unmixed, like invisible lakes. A track left in the pale crust may persist until something living deliberately crosses it. The bodies of dead creatures do not rot in any recognizable way. They dry, lighten, and become part of the general mineral grammar: shell, hinge, rib, shard, powder.

Even the fauna obeys a kind of static rhythm. Many animals here live not by constant hunger but by intervals of almost total suspension. They wait in cracks, under plates, inside hollow stones. They wake for pressure, heat, vibration, scentless chemistry. A colony may spend years arranged in the same pattern, then shift one body-length to the left and settle again. A predator may hold its strike for a decade. A clutch of eggs may remain eggs until a particular mineral seam opens near them, releasing the gas they require to begin.

The darkness is not empty. It has texture. In some places it is matte and close; in others it seems deep and granular, full of faint, non-visual motion. There are occasional lights, but they do not illuminate so much as mark. A blue crust glows weakly where rubbed. Certain animals carry dull internal embers along their bellies, visible only when they turn over. Far away, on no regular schedule, a seam in the wall may show a red line like a closed eyelid. But the landscape itself remains largely unlit. It is a world of touch, heat, weight, nearness.

The scattered built things are easy to miss because they are made from the same materials as the terrain. There are low shelters tucked under overhangs, assembled from flat mineral plates and sealed with a clay-like dust that contains no clay. There are windless cairns, though nothing here blows hard enough to require windbreaks. There are notched stones set in circles around vents. There are ladders of bone and glass descending into cracks, hand-knotted with dry sinew. There are walls no higher than the waist, built not to keep animals out but to persuade them to pass on one side rather than the other.

The builders used simple tools. Their cuts are patient but irregular. No metal gleams except in rare hammered strips, soft and dark, pinned into stone as markers. Most implements are chipped glass, sharpened shell, twisted fiber from some animal source, ceramic blades fired in the natural heat of the vents. Their craft is intimate and local. They did not conquer the place; they annotated it.

In certain chambers, the walls are covered with shallow carvings: rows of dots, heat-maps of vents, silhouettes of creatures seen from below, handprints made not in pigment but in abrasion, the surface polished by repeated contact. Some marks appear instructional. Others are devotional. Others may be warnings, though warning against what is unclear. One recurring symbol shows a line descending from a ceiling into a circle of small bodies. Another shows a human-like figure lying flat among mineral blades, surrounded by listening animals.

There are no large settlements. Perhaps the hollow continent cannot support gathering. Perhaps sound, heat, and breath attract too much attention. Perhaps the people who came here, or were born here, understood that permanence belonged to the stone alone. Their traces appear as pauses in the terrain: a sleeping niche worn smooth; a rack of tools left beside a salt plain; a kiln pit still warm though unused; a string of shell tokens hanging in a fissure, motionless in air that never stirs.

The ecology has no plants at its base. Instead, it is built on seepage and surface chemistry, on warmth rising through fresh stone, on minerals that bloom invisibly and are grazed before they can become crust. The smallest lives are films, powders, colonies of granules that darken when fed by certain gases. The grazers scrape them. The hunters take the grazers. The larger animals harvest heat, pressure, and the slow electrical irritations that pass between mineral layers. Some creatures seem less alive than negotiated into being: arrangements of shell, nerve, dust, and crystal maintaining themselves against stillness.

Because there is no water, bodies here solve themselves differently. Blood is thick or absent. Joints are dry hinges. Eggs are capsules like fired beads. Wounds seal with powder. Many animals carry internal salts that would kill surface life. Others have no fluids at all in the ordinary sense, only greases, gels, resins, or charged grains moving through channels by pressure and heat. Death is not wet. Birth is not wet. Decay is a quiet conversion from animal to artifact.

The terrain encourages careful movement. A step may snap a crust that has not been broken since it formed. A hand placed on a wall may come away silver with reactive dust. The sharp fins sing faintly if struck, but the sound dies at once. There are pits hidden under membranes of pale mineral. There are fields where the ground is so brittle that a dropped tool vanishes into a widening lace of cracks. There are corridors lined with black glass that hold body heat like memory; pass through them and, hours later, heat-seeking things may arrive to inspect your outline.

Yet there is little drama. The place does not erupt. It does not flood. It does not quake often enough to be called restless. Its strangeness is not spectacle but equilibrium. Everything is poised. Everything waits at the edge of action without completing it. A blade of stone remains half-detached. A creature remains half-awake. A settlement remains half-abandoned. A sound remains half-heard.

In the largest chambers, the ceiling lowers into darkness in immense terraces. The floor beneath is crowded with pale cones, each one about the height of a person, each one hollow. They may be geological vents. They may be the old husks of colony animals. They may be both, because categories blur here. Warm air rises from some of them, too faint to move dust. Around their bases are rings of tiny footprints. At intervals among the cones stand small built platforms, just large enough for a person to sit cross-legged. Their surfaces are polished smooth.

One can imagine watchers there, wrapped in cured hide or woven shell-fiber, listening to the almost-silence: the minute ticking of cooling glass, the dry rasp of a grazer under the crust, the distant shift of a heavy body in a passage too far away to see. They would have known the meanings of these sounds. They would have known which silences were safe.

For there are different silences in the hollow continent.

There is the ordinary silence of stone.

There is the feeding silence, when small creatures stop moving because something larger has entered the chamber.

There is the vent silence, when the warm seams close and the air loses its bitter taste.

There is the artificial silence near old structures, where animals avoid a place for reasons that may no longer exist.

And there is the total silence found in certain basins, where even footsteps seem to fail. The mineral there is dark violet and soft-looking, though hard underfoot. No tracks remain on it. No animals cross it. The primitive markers around such basins are always placed at a distance: three stones stacked, then a gap, then a fourth stone laid flat. Whatever the mark means, it is repeated with remarkable consistency.

Perhaps those basins are older than the rest of the young world. Perhaps they are not part of the world at all, but openings into a deeper stillness. Perhaps they are where change goes when it is refused elsewhere.

The deeper one travels, the less the landscape resembles a cave and the more it resembles an interior organ of a planet that never developed blood. Strata curve like muscle. Mineral plates overlap like cartilage. The warm air moves through hidden channels with the faintest suggestion of breath, though there is no wind. The animals become paler, flatter, more patient. Built traces become rarer but more deliberate: a single carved pillar beside an abyss; a hand tool placed on a ledge and weighted with a stone; a row of masks made from animal carapace facing a wall of unbroken black glass.

The abyssal regions are still dry. Even there, at the lowest accessible shelves, where one might expect pressure and heat to squeeze moisture from stone, there is only powder, salt, vapor, and darkness. The warmth increases slightly, enough to bead sweat on a traveler’s body before the air steals it away. The sound of one’s own breathing becomes indecently loud. Fauna gathers close but not visibly. Shells click once, then stop. Something brushes the ground and becomes motionless when listened for.

In this place, life is not an eruption against death. It is a set of agreements with mineral stillness.

Move rarely.

Waste nothing.

Keep close to warmth.

Do not depend on water.

Do not expect light.

Leave marks only where marks can last.

And above all: do not assume that what is still is inert.

The hollow continent remains, warm and dark, crowded with patient bodies and fresh stone. Its surfaces look newly born, but its quiet feels ancient. Its structures suggest hands, but not dominion. Its creatures are everywhere, but most of them wait so perfectly that the landscape itself seems to be the animal, and the animals only moments when it chooses to articulate a limb, a mouth, a listening shell.

Stand still long enough and the place begins to assemble around you. A ridge becomes a spine. A salt field becomes a hide. The scattered shelters become old scabs. The vents become nostrils too slow to breathe. In the almost-dark, with no water running and no leaves stirring and no sky to measure time by, the world below the surface does not seem dead or alive.

It seems unfinished.

And yet, somehow, impossible to alter.
