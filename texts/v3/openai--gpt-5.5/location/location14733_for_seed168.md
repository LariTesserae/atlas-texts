At this height the world is broad, pale, and almost unused.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense, not yet. The land has risen, but it has not been worn. There are no old gullies, no river scars, no softened valleys, no landslides crumbled into habit. The plateau seems to have come upward all at once from beneath the planet’s skin and then stopped: raw shelves of mineral, tilted slabs, basaltic ridges still bearing the blunt geometry of pressure rather than weather. Edges are clean. Cliffs stand with the unfinished look of broken ceramic. Plains stretch between them, smooth as fired clay, dusted with salt and metallic oxides.

There is no water anywhere.

Not hidden in hollows, not frozen under shade, not breathing in mist. The air is warm and thin and dry enough that a shadow feels like another substance laid over the ground. When the wind moves, which is seldom, it does not smell of rain or soil. It smells faintly of heated stone, alkali, powdered glass, iron, resin, and something electrical, like the air around old machinery.

Yet the plateau is lush.

The life here is not green in the familiar, wet-leafed way. It grows in thickets of mineral-fed forms that do not transpire, do not drink, do not soften. Acres of tall, jointed vegetation stand rooted in cracks and seams, feeding from salts, heat, and the slow exchange of gases between the ground and sky. Their trunks are fibrous but hard, the color of copper verdigris, bruised purple, old brass, and ash-white wax. Some rise as fluted columns, hollow inside, ribbed like organ pipes. Others branch into racks of flat black leaves, matte and heat-absorbing, arranged to catch the sun without losing anything to the air.

There are broad fields of filament grass, each strand a dry, glassy tube. They do not rustle unless touched directly. Their tips carry pale crystalline fans that open at sunrise and remain open all day, not fluttering, not growing perceptibly, only holding their angles with the patience of instruments. In places the vegetation is so dense it forms low forests, but these forests have no shade-coolness, no damp floor, no birds, no insects, no soft decomposition. Fallen stems do not rot. They delaminate into shining fibers and become part of the mineral mat, slowly reabsorbed by chemistry older than appetite.

Between these lush zones lie enormous emptinesses.

The landscape is open in every direction: a high region of basins, rims, and exposed foundations under a steady sky. The inhabited patches are far apart, like islands in a dry sea. You can see one growth-field from another across miles of bare ground, a dark green-black stain on a copper plain, a band of vertical reeds along the base of a cliff, a cluster of silver fronds gathered around a fissure vent. Nothing crosses between them. There are no trails, no tracks, no burrows, no paths made by hooves or feet or use. The spaces remain purely spatial, unclaimed.

The plateau’s rawness gives it an embryonic dignity. It feels less like a ruin than a beginning that has not learned to age. Rock skins are unpitted. Crater rims remain crisp. Slopes descend in simple planes, as if drawn rather than carved. The dust lies evenly and thinly, undisturbed except where vents have sifted up new mineral flour. Some basins are floored with salt mirrors—not wet, not reflective like water, but pale, laminated crusts that catch the day in a flat, dead brightness. Across them stand isolated growths: single black stalks twenty meters high, rooted in nothing visible, casting long immobile shadows.

There is no animal life at all.

The absence is not merely quiet. It is structural. No buzzing, no calls, no wingbeats, no hidden stirring under leaves. No small collapses in the underbrush, no scraping, no chewing, no sudden startled motion. The vegetation has no flowers designed for visitors. Its spore-plates, when it has them, release only in response to heat thresholds and electrical charge. Its seed bodies are heavy mineral beads that fall straight down and wait for the ground to crack. Pollination, migration, predation, nesting—these ideas do not belong here. Ecology is conducted by contact, pressure, vapor, charge, and time.

And time, here, has almost stopped.

The days pass, but the place does not seem to change from one to the next. Light arrives, holds, withdraws. Warmth deepens and releases. Shadows rotate around the trunks and blade-fields with mathematical calm. The vegetation opens and closes by tiny degrees, but so slowly and so repeatably that it feels less like behavior than calibration. A person could sleep for a month beside one of the glass grasses and wake to find every stem in the same relation to every other, every salt pan unmarked, every distant ridge still sharp against the sky.

The industrial quality of the place has no builders behind it.

Nothing here has been constructed, but much of it operates. The plateau breathes like a factory sealed under stone. Deep below, geothermal engines turn without wheels. Pressure chambers fill and empty. Fissures exhale warm gases in measured intervals through fields of mineral reeds, and the reeds respond like pipework, condensing trace elements on their inner walls. Certain groves click once a day as their resinous plates realign. Others hum at frequencies too low to hear except through the bones of the feet. The ground is veined with conductive ore, and in the long afternoons whole slopes gather static until a faint blue crawling appears along the edges of leaves.

There are places that resemble refineries grown from geology. Not buildings: no walls, no beams, no design imposed from outside. Rather, terraces of natural condenser-stone step down from vents, each lip crusted with different colors—sulfur yellow, cobalt, tin white, hematite red. Around them grow tubular shrubs with valves at their nodes. Warm gas passes through them, is filtered, altered, exhausted. Deposits accumulate at their roots in regular heaps. Seen from a distance, these groves have the orderliness of machinery, but close up every part is alive in the dry, unanimal way of the plateau: metabolism without softness, industry without labor.

The silence is deep but not absolute.

Most of the time there is only the faint ticking of thermal contraction in the rocks. Sometimes a reed-stem gives a small ceramic ping. Sometimes a vent exhales, not with a roar but with a long, almost polite sigh, and the nearby leaves turn their black surfaces toward it. In the salt basins, minute crystalline growth can produce a whispering sound like paper being folded very far away. When the wind does come over the high rim, it crosses the empty plains without finding much to move. It slides around the groves, thin and warm, and departs.

The sky is ordinary in brightness, which makes the land stranger. There is no eclipse, no permanent twilight, no supernatural color to explain what is wrong. Daylight lies evenly over the plateau. It shows everything clearly: the dry forests, the new cliffs, the broad bare distances, the absence of water, the absence of animals, the absence of history. Under such normal light, the place cannot hide inside atmosphere. Its rules are exposed.

One rule: nothing wastes moisture, because moisture is not part of the bargain.

Another: growth must be structural. Every living thing here is also support, conduit, condenser, insulator, archive.

Another: movement is expensive and therefore almost unknown.

Another: the land is young, but not restless. It emerged into equilibrium, as if the violent phase happened before the first witness and then was sealed.

In the highest basins, where the air thins further and the ground turns a soft gray-green with powdered nickel salts, the vegetation becomes unexpectedly extravagant. Tall fans rise in overlapping ranks, each blade translucent amber at the edge and dark at the core. They are lush by abundance rather than softness: thousands of blades, thousands of stems, immense mats of filament and root, all packed around invisible chemical gradients in the rock. The forest canopy there is not leafy but architectural, a dry vault of interlocking planes. Light passes through it in warm bands. The floor is carpeted with brittle scales shed from older growth, still holding faint charge. If touched, they cling to skin like mica.

But even in these dense places, the emptiness remains. There is no companionship of species in the animal sense. No suddenness. No weather drama. No decay-funk, no mud, no hatchings, no pursuit. The living systems stand apart from one another, each grove a silent apparatus tuned to its fissure, each field an engine of patient conversion.

At sunset the plateau becomes bronze.

The salt flats dim first, then the raw cliffs, then the black leaves. Heat stored in the stones lifts upward in visible tremors. The vegetation’s conductive veins glow faintly for a few minutes—not enough to light the land, only enough to reveal that the groves are internally organized, that their stems carry charge along routes as deliberate as circuitry. The glow moves nowhere. It pulses once, stabilizes, and fades.

Night is warm for a long time. Stars appear with hard clarity. In the dark, the absence of sound enlarges the place until the open basins feel planetary. Somewhere far below, beneath the new crust, the natural engines continue: compression, exhalation, deposition, charge. Above them, the dry forests stand unmoving, lush and silent, thriving without water in a world that has barely begun to be shaped.
