It is a high wet country, too broad to be a valley and too intricate to be called a plain.

From a distance it looks almost urban: a dim continent of terraces, basins, walls, lanes, courtyards, and long rectilinear shelves climbing away under a cool sky. But nothing here has been cut or raised by tool. There are no engines, no roads in the human sense, no lamps, no doors, no metal, no glass. The city-shape belongs to water and stone. Springs have issued from the high ground for so long that they have built with their own patience, laying mineral skin over mineral skin, making parapets of pale travertine, steps of wet limestone, alleys where runnels have deepened into channels. Frost has widened cracks. Rain has polished lintels that were never lintels. Gravity has settled everything into a long-established order.

The land is elevated enough that the air is cool even in the brighter part of the day. Light arrives cleanly, without drama: a normal sky, pearled by thin cloud, with occasional blue openings that make every wet surface flare. There is no tropical abundance here. Vegetation is sparse and disciplined. Low sedges stand in seams where water pauses. Moss darkens the shaded lips of pools. Lichens scale the drier stone in pale maps, green-gray, rust, bone-white. Here and there a tough shrub leans out of a crack, small-leaved and wind-pruned, its roots gripping the mineral city like fingers.

Water is not everywhere in sheets, but it is everywhere near. It seeps, beads, condenses, gathers, slips under ledges, emerges from pores in rock. The ground has the after-rain feeling even when no rain is falling. Walk any distance and your boots would darken; put your hand to a wall and it would come away cold and damp. Shallow pools lie in rectangular hollows, not because they were planned, but because the stone has fractured in long straight tendencies. Some basins are clear enough to show submerged steps furred with algae. Others are cloudy with suspended lime, milky green, holding the sky as a dull reflection.

The terrain has streets, but they are water-streets. Narrow channels thread between raised shelves, no wider than a body in places, then opening into broad plazas of still water where mineral rims have grown thick and rounded. Rivulets descend from level to level in near silence, a chain of soft overflows: not waterfalls, but lips spilling into lips. On colder mornings, thin ice forms along the edges, transparent and fragile, making the whole place seem edged in glass. By noon the ice loosens and vanishes, leaving only a colder glitter.

The scale is difficult to understand from inside it. Every terrace leads to another terrace, every basin to a larger basin, every wall to a farther wall. The plateau extends beyond sight in all directions, a wet architecture on the back of a mountain range. Weather forms over it as if over an inland sea. Fog rises from the saturated ground and moves through the stone corridors in slow bodies. Clouds catch on the uplands and empty themselves in fine rain. Wind comes rarely in strong gusts; more often it is a broad pressure passing over the open levels, making the sedges lean all at once.

The place is dense, but not crowded in the way of a forest. Its density is structural. Surfaces are close to surfaces. Each stone edge has a second edge beneath it. Channels cross under shelves and reappear yards away. Pools sit inside larger basins, which sit inside amphitheaters, which are nested inside the greater plateau. The ground is layered with past waters: old flow lines, abandoned spillways, dry ledges now slick again after rain, mineral curtains hanging over older stone. Nothing feels empty, even where vegetation is nearly absent. The materials themselves are populous — stone, water, vapor, lichen, silt, cold air, root hair, mineral crust.

Sound is subdued. The first impression is silence. Then, slowly, the ear learns the small inventory: a drip from an overhang into a hidden pool; a thread of water dragging across stone; the occasional tick of cooling rock; a distant, breath-like murmur where many channels converge below a terrace lip. Birds are rare. Insects are seasonal and few, appearing as small dark motions above the damp sedges during the warmer hours. Once in a long while, a highland fox or lean marsh-cat moves along a dry rim, careful-footed, vanishing before it seems fully present. Amphibians live in the shallow basins, but quietly, mostly under stones, their eggs clinging to submerged mineral shelves like beads of clear resin.

The ecology is modest but exact. Algae grow where the water is slow and sunlit. Moss occupies the shaded seams. Sedges take the silt traps. Lichens claim the wind-dried faces. Small grazing snails rasp films from the stone, leaving faint clean arcs behind them. Tiny crustaceans drift in some of the pools, transparent until they catch light. Predators are rare because food is rare; life here is measured, economical, adapted to coolness and wet mineral scarcity. Nothing wastes height, warmth, or shelter.

Yet the landscape is active. It is not still ruin, not dead stone. It is making and unmaking itself all the time. Rain alters the circulation. Channels overflow and braid. A pool clears over days, then clouds in an hour when a spring pulses from below. Edges accrete mineral skins so slowly they seem permanent, then a freeze cracks them and a crescent of pale stone falls away. Silt gathers behind a sedge root and makes a new landing. Moss lifts the water fractionally, holding it in green sponge. Fog condenses on every cold vertical face and returns as droplets to the runnels. The city breathes through capillary motion.

There are districts, though no names for them.

There are the pale upper shelves, exposed to wind, where the stone is almost white and the pools are shallow, bright, and cold. There are the sunken courts where fog stays late, where black moss grows thick on the inward walls and the water is tea-colored from old plant matter. There are long causeway ridges only a few handspans above the surrounding marsh, dry in appearance but sweating through hairline cracks. There are collapsed neighborhoods of stone, where old terraces have slumped and fused into broken stairs, still navigable by water. There are spring mouths: round, dark, quiet openings at the base of walls, each one releasing a steady lens of colder water into the basin before it.

The rules of the place are simple and strict.

Water chooses the path, but stone remembers the choice.

Height is never dry for long.

Any hollow becomes a pool; any pool becomes a mirror; any mirror slowly fills with mineral, silt, eggs, leaves, or sky.

Life must stay low, hold fast, and accept interruption.

Nothing moves loudly unless it is falling.

Nothing grows tall unless it can survive being shaved by wind, frost, and hunger.

Nothing built remains only built; everything becomes drainage, shelter, crust, or ledge.

At evening the light does not redden much. It thins. The terraces lose their hard edges and become bands of gray and silver. The water darkens first in the channels, then in the basins. Condensation thickens on the cold stone. The whole plateau seems to exhale upward, giving back the day’s slight warmth as mist. A rare bird may cross overhead, silent-winged, using the plateau as a map of pale lines and dark pools. Then it is gone, and the place returns to its enormous quiet labor: seeping, settling, cooling, overflowing, remembering.
