At the top of the world there is a country so old it has almost forgotten how to be mountainous.

It lies high above the lower clouds, not in sharp peaks but in immense worn shelves and backs, a broken upland of flattened stone. The land rises by stages: one pale escarpment after another, each so broad and wind-planed that from a distance they seem like the steps of some extinct giant stair. The highest surfaces are not jagged. They have been ground down, softened, unshouldered by ages of rain, heat, frost, dust, and the slow rasping of plants. What remains is elevation without youth: a plateau-world of bald summits, shallow basins, stone pavements, and cliffs whose faces are rubbed smooth as old bone.

The air is warm, thin, and restless. It does not sit still for even a breath.

Wind pours over the open tablelands in hard, constant sheets. It comes with a sound like ocean surf magnified through caverns, though no ocean is visible. It combs the grasses flat, tears vapor from the mouths of ravines, snaps the broad leaves of upland shrubs until they clatter like shaken hide shields. In the cuts and gullies it becomes a roar. In the stone bowls it circles and drums. At the cliff edges it screams so cleanly that it seems almost metallic, though there is no metal here except perhaps a few dull fragments hammered into tools and lost generations ago.

The place is never quiet. Even when the sky is clear, the land is loud.

There are falls without rivers: narrow streams that appear suddenly from the stone after rain, leap from a ledge in white ropes, and vanish into moss before they reach the next terrace. There are reed-choked pools cupped in hollows of black rock, their surfaces trembling constantly under falling grit and wind-shaken seeds. Condensation gathers at night on lichen, grass, and the velvet undersides of leaves; by morning it runs in shining threads down the plateau skin, feeding tiny gardens no wider than a hand. Moisture here is not abundant, but it is cunning. It hides in cracks, in shaded mouths of caves, under mats of roots, inside the porous stone. It beads on everything before dawn and disappears by midday.

The land is lush in pieces rather than evenly green. From afar the uplands look empty: vast tawny planes, olive smears, dark seams where the ravines cut deep. But up close, wherever the rock gives shelter, vegetation surges. Ferns crowd the shaded cliff bases in feathered banks. Vines drop into sinkholes and climb out again, gripping the stone with pale root-fingers. Low trees grow twisted by wind, their crowns flattened into long green banners pointing leeward. Thick grasses fill the shallow basins, bright and wet at their roots, dry and whispering at their tips. There are shrubs with waxy leaves broad enough to catch mist, and bulbous plants that split the stone with slow patience, storing whole seasons of water in swollen underground hearts.

Between these lush pockets lie great intervals of openness. You can walk for an hour across a high pavement and pass nothing taller than ankle moss. The horizon remains enormous, trembling slightly in heat. Distant ridges appear and disappear behind veils of dust, pollen, and torn cloud. The scale is too large for intimacy but too broken for emptiness to become simple. Every feature suggests another beyond it: a basin leading to a cliff, a cliff to a ravine, a ravine to a suspended meadow, a meadow to another stone back rising in the warm glare.

The ground itself is ancient and complicated underfoot. Some surfaces are smooth pale limestone, polished by blown sand until they shine faintly. Others are dark basalt plates fractured into hexagonal scales, each crack furred with moss. There are fields of red lateritic gravel that rattle in the wind, thin soils full of powdered shell and old root ash, banks of clay so weathered they crumble into colored dust at a touch. In many places erosion has written the land in parallel lines: ribs, channels, terraces, shelves, all softened by age. Nothing here looks recently broken. Even the cliffs seem tired of falling. Their collapses happened long ago, and the fallen blocks have been dressed with lichen, softened at the corners, rooted through, made part of the general settlement of the world.

And yet the present is violently alive.

Storms are born quickly on the high plains. A blue morning can become a black noon. Clouds gather not from one direction but from several, rising out of ravines, boiling upward from the warm lowlands, sliding across the plateau in torn masses. They drag their bellies over the high grasses and burst. Rain falls in short, hard assaults, each drop striking dust into scent. The whole land changes in minutes. Dry channels become roaring bronze torrents. Pools overflow their rims. Water spreads across stone in thin shining sheets, reflecting the sky so completely that walkers seem to cross fragments of cloud. Then the rain ends. Sun returns. Steam lifts from the earth. The torrents shrink to threads. Within an hour, the land has closed most of its mouths again.

During these storms the sound becomes almost unbearable. Wind, rain, water, leaf, stone: everything speaks at once. Pebbles skitter across the flats in rattling swarms. Branches whip and crack. Cascades appear by the hundred, each shouting from a different height. In the deeper ravines, floodwater booms against hidden chambers and sends up pulses through the ground that can be felt in the ribs. Thunder rolls strangely here, not as single strikes but as long traveling detonations, passed from plateau wall to plateau wall until the whole upland seems to be remembering an avalanche.

There are few animals. The land has room for multitudes, but does not host them. Occasionally a dark bird rides the updrafts near the cliffs, motionless for a long time, then gone behind vapor. Small lizards appear on warm stones after rain, throats pulsing, skins patterned like cracked clay. In the reedy pools something moves at night, but seldom shows itself: a soft plunk, a widening ring, the bending of a reed. Insects are more often heard than seen, and even they come in fits—after rain, in brief clicking eruptions from the grasses, then silence beneath the louder weather. There may be larger creatures in the ravines, but if so they keep to the green depths and leave only occasional signs: a pressed tunnel through fern, a muddy print in drying silt, a tuft of coarse hair caught on thorn.

Human presence, or something near to it, is sparse and weathered almost into geology.

On certain sheltered shelves stand low huts of stacked stone and woven grass, built against boulders to escape the wind. Most are empty. Their roofs are weighted with slabs. Their doorways face away from the cliffs. Smoke marks stain a few interior stones, but no fresh ash remains. Nearby are shallow pits lined with clay, perhaps once used to store tubers or rainwater. There are windbreak walls reduced to knee height, cairns with no obvious purpose, paths only visible where many bare feet once polished the stone darker than its surroundings.

In one basin, a ring of upright slabs surrounds a pool that fills only during storms. The slabs are carved with simple notches: counts, moons, rainfall marks, or prayers to something that lives in the clouds. The carvings are shallow, nearly erased, and the lichen has colonized them with slow green insistence. In another place, a long row of stones crosses an exposed saddle, each stone set by hand yet now so worn and settled that it seems to have grown there. Travelers once followed these lines in fog. Or offerings were placed along them. Or the stones were meant to hold the wind back, which they do not.

Tools found here are blunt and practical: chipped blades of dark glassy rock, bone awls, grinding stones, digging sticks hardened by fire. There is evidence of baskets in the form of clay impressions, rope in the form of twisted fibers preserved in dry crevices, simple shelters repaired many times by people who did not stay long. The upland is not a place of towns. It permits camps, crossings, seasonal returns. It does not encourage possession. The weather takes ownership of everything left unattended.

The strange quality of the region is subtle at first, because the obvious violence of wind and storm explains too much. But after time, the mind begins to notice patterns that do not belong entirely to weather.

Certain pools ripple before the gust reaches them. Some ravines answer thunder before thunder sounds. Mist sometimes climbs downward from the plateau edge, flowing into chasms as if poured from below. In the largest basins, the grasses all lean in different directions, arranged in spirals too precise to be accidental, though they shift after every storm and never repeat. Stones hum faintly when wet, each with its own pitch; after heavy rain, whole slopes produce low chords under the roar of runoff. The sound might be water moving through hidden pores, or pressure in the rock, or some ancient resonance left from when the plateau was higher and younger and still breaking itself apart.

The people who once came here seem to have made rules for survival, and some of those rules remain legible in their abandoned arrangements.

Do not sleep in the first hollow that offers shelter; water remembers it before you do.

Do not trust a dry channel with polished stones.

Tie everything down before sunset, even in calm.

Drink from shaded seeps, not from the bright pools that gather on open rock.

When the stones begin to sing, leave the ravine.

When birds fly below you, descend no farther.

When the wind stops, kneel.

The last rule matters because stillness is rare and terrible. Perhaps once every few weeks, between storm systems, the air drops suddenly. The grasses lift upright. The leaves stop clattering. The waterfalls, if any remain, continue in distant threads, but the great body of sound withdraws. Then the scale of the place becomes almost unbearable. You hear your own pulse in the high warmth. You hear droplets falling from leaf tips into pools. You hear small grains of loosened stone ticking down slopes. The emptiness, formerly hidden by noise, opens around you.

In that brief quiet the landscape seems not peaceful but watchful. The wide eroded backs of the mountains hold their positions under the normal sun. The lush pockets steam gently. The ravines darken. Condensation gathers where it should have already burned away. Far across the plateau, a single standing stone may appear to move because heat bends the air around it. Nothing living shows itself. The old huts crouch among their boulders. The paths fade and resume.

Then wind returns all at once.

It comes over the western escarpment like an invisible flood, flattening grass in a visible line. Leaves flash their pale undersides. Dust lifts. Water shivers. The first loose pebbles begin to run. The whole upland inhales, or roars, or remembers how to be alive, and the quiet is erased so completely it feels imagined.

This is a landscape of remnants and eruptions: ancient surfaces, temporary waters, lushness clinging where it can, scattered human marks nearly reabsorbed, rare animals moving through intervals of shelter. It is high, warm, open, and deeply worn. It is empty in the way a drum is empty—hollow enough to become thunderous. It has settled into an old form, but that form is never still. Every storm redraws the small things. Every season strips and feeds the green pockets. Every gust rehearses the long erosion that made the place and is still, patiently, making it less.
