Below the surface there is a town that has forgotten stone.

It occupies a single cavern, not vast in the way of underworlds in stories, but large enough to hold a neighborhood, a market, a power house, a few bridges, and the suggestion of streets. Its ceiling hangs low in some places and rises in others like the underside of an enormous root-bound hill. Everything has been worn into agreement over time. Nothing looks newly made. Nothing looks abandoned either. The place is established in the way a tide pool is established: every surface claimed, every niche used, every process repeating with small deviations.

The cavern walls are not bare rock. They are buried under plant matter so completely that geology appears only as a rumor: a dark mineral glint behind torn bark, a seam of limestone visible where roots have pulled away, a wet ledge white with old salts. The main material of the town is vegetation—trunks trained into columns, woven root lattices stiffened into walls, thick mats of moss functioning as pavement, broad waxy leaves layered into roofs. Even the air seems partly vegetable, warm and green and faintly sour, full of spores, pollen, sap vapor, and the breath of unseen things.

The ground is soft but not wild. Paths have been made by pruning rather than paving. They curve between living structures whose walls slowly pulse with sap. Doorways are grown into oval openings. Windows are slits where translucent leaves have been stretched thin and veined like skin. Bridges cross shallow channels on braided roots as thick as cables. Balconies are shelf fungi bolted to trellises of woody vine. The town is urban, but its order is botanical: blocks follow moisture lines, alleys follow root pressure, and public squares form wherever the canopy has been trained not to close.

There is little open water. Moisture appears in interruptions.

Condensation gathers on the ceiling and falls in fat, irregular drops. Each drop strikes a leaf, a roof, a pipe, an animal shell, and joins the general percussion. Along the lowest street, a narrow black stream appears for several hours each cycle, fed by seepage from the northern wall, then sinks away through grates of root and bronze. Basins carved from old machine housings collect rain that never came from the sky. The air is damp rather than wet; the town sweats, exhales, dries, and sweats again. Cloth never fully dries. Metal carries a permanent bloom of green corrosion. Moss grows on the handles of doors that are opened every day.

The warmth is steady, not tropical, but subterranean and bodily. It comes from deep engines, fungal compost beds, slow fermentation chambers, and vents that breathe up from below the cavern floor. In some alleys the heat is mild and almost comforting. Near the factories it becomes oily and close, making the vines gleam and the animals restless.

The factories are not large, but they are loud.

At the center of the cavern stands the old pressing house: a many-level structure of iron frames overgrown by root and creeper, its smokestacks useless because there is no sky to smoke into. Instead, vapor is drawn through fluted plant chimneys—hollow trunks bred for draft—and expelled into cooling nets high in the canopy. Belts turn. Pistons bow and rise. Flywheels, half-swallowed by moss, rotate with a patient, wet thunder. Pumps lift sap, water, nutrient slurry, and something pale that may be latex or milk. Cogs are packed with greased leaf-fiber. Copper wires run along living branches, held in place by knots of trained vine, carrying a low industrial current that makes certain flowers glow dimly at their edges.

The machinery does not oppose the vegetation. It has become part of it.

Roots enter pump housings deliberately. Vines wrap pressure gauges without obscuring their faces. Ferns grow in the heat above boiler casings, their fronds trembling with every mechanical stroke. The power plant uses falling condensation, fungal methane, and the muscular contraction of huge cultivated pods suspended in frames like sleeping animals. Every few minutes one pod tightens with a sound like a sail taking wind, pulling a chain, turning a crank, feeding force into the town’s network.

The light is dim and layered. There are no bright lamps except in workshops, where shielded industrial bulbs glow amber behind cages slick with moisture. Most illumination comes from biological arrangements: pale lichens along stair edges, lantern fruit hung at intersections, blue-green bacterial pans under bridges, moth-attracting blossoms that open and close around small glass filaments. Shadows are numerous and soft. Movement is often perceived before form. A passerby is first a disturbance in fernlight, then a silhouette, then a face greened by reflected glow.

Because of the dimness, the town feels larger than it is. Streets continue farther in the imagination than in fact. Corners seem to lead to other districts but often end in a wall of roots, a locked service gate, or a cul-de-sac where beetles nest in the remains of a municipal fountain.

The vegetation is nearly total, yet the space is strangely sparse. There are not many buildings crowded together; instead there are intervals, breathing gaps, chambers of shadow between the living structures. A single vine-column may occupy the center of a square with nothing around it for twenty paces except low moss, scattered machines, and animal tracks. The emptiness is not barren. It is waiting. It allows sound to travel. It allows wings to cross. It gives the larger plants room to lean and adjust themselves over decades.

The whole place has been eroded into habit. Edges are rounded. Steps sag where generations of feet and paws and claws have passed. Railings are polished smooth by hands, tendrils, ropes, rain, and the sides of animals squeezing past. The original plans of the town are still visible in places—straight service corridors, numbered pipe junctions, brick foundations—but everything rigid has been softened. Iron beams curve under root pressure. Masonry has cracked along lines now occupied by moss. Old painted signs remain, but lichen has eaten the letters into a script of stains.

The fauna is overwhelming.

At first it seems like background motion: gnats in light shafts, small frogs along pipes, pale worms looping in compost gutters. Then the eye adjusts and realizes that every surface is inhabited. Beetles the size of fists cling under leaves, lacquered black and gold. Blind cave birds roost upside down from the roof-vines, chattering in bursts so sharp they sound like metal filings poured into a pan. Long-legged rodents race along cable runs. Transparent lizards hunt them from the warm walls. Moths gather around factory vents in living curtains, their wings opening and closing like many soft mouths.

The larger animals use the town as confidently as citizens.

Root-pigs with shovel faces nose through refuse yards, turning compost and machinery scraps into ridges. Slow, antlered salamanders haul themselves through the lower channels when the seep-stream runs, their backs carrying gardens of algae and young ferns. Pale dogs with too many joints sleep in doorways and wake all at once when the pumps change rhythm. In the rafters, something arboreal and social moves in groups: dark hands, ringed tails, quick eyes catching lanternlight. They steal copper washers, fruit, eggs, and sometimes keys.

Predation is constant but not dramatic. A vine snaps shut on a roach. A bird drops onto a frog. A centipede slides from a drain and the nearby mice vanish as if erased. A child-sized mantis, camouflaged as a bundle of dead leaves, waits beside an electrical junction where heat draws moths. The town accepts this. It has rules for it. Certain alleys are avoided during hatchings. Bells are rung before the waste doors open. Red fungus painted on a threshold means something inside is breeding and should not be disturbed.

Sound fills the cavern until silence seems like a superstition.

There is the industrial body-sound: pumps knocking, belts slapping, chains dragging, boilers breathing, pressure valves coughing steam into plant chimneys. There is the water-sound: drips, trickles, sump gurgles, the periodic rush of the temporary stream entering its grates. There is the animal-sound: shrieks, clicks, wingbeats, chewing, mating calls, warning calls, the dry scrape of carapaces across bark. There is also the plant-sound, if one stays long enough to hear it: leaves shifting without wind, pods tightening, roots creaking in masonry, the faint fibrous tear of growth.

The loudness is not chaotic. It has cycles. When the pressing house accelerates, the birds rise in complaint. When condensation thickens and begins to fall harder from the ceiling, frogs answer from the drains. When the heat vents open, insects pour toward them, and the predators follow. At certain hours the entire cavern seems to inhale: machines slow, flowers close, animals pause, and from beneath the floor comes a deep thermal rumble like a distant train passing under the world.

The strangeness of the place lies not in any single impossible thing, but in the way categories have worn through.

A street may also be a root, a vein, a conveyor, and a migration path. A building may flower once a year and require evacuation. A factory may be pollinated. A public garden may digest waste, generate power, and serve as a court of law because disputes are settled there beneath witness-vines that respond to certain tones of voice. The oldest pipes have become hollow habitats for singing insects whose rhythms regulate work shifts better than clocks. Some doors open only when breathed upon. Some machines must be fed chopped leaves and bone meal before they will start.

The inhabitants—human or nearly human, if one glimpses them through the dimness—move with practiced caution. They wear waterproof aprons, fungal leather boots, copper ear-caps, and veils against biting insects. Their tools are pruning hooks, wrenches, sap needles, voltage rods, and little bells used to warn animals rather than people. They do not speak loudly unless competing with the factory floor. Much of their communication is gesture: hand signs in green gloom, knocks on pipe, colored spores dusted onto posts.

The town has ordinances, though they are less like laws than agreements with forces that cannot be arrested.

Do not cut a white root unless the root has first been sung to and marked for seven days.

Do not run current through flowering circuits.

Do not store salt near the nursery walls.

Do not kill the blind birds; they hear pressure changes before the gauges do.

Do not sleep in the open squares during warm-drip season.

Do not follow a child’s voice beyond the west culvert, because the mimics nest there and have learned the old lullabies.

The boundaries of the place are clear. On all sides, the cavern narrows into service tunnels, root chokes, maintenance shafts, or flooded squeezes. Beyond them may be other chambers, other towns, perhaps dry stone or deep water, but this landscape is held tight: a warm, dim, living industrial pocket under the earth. It has the intimacy of a room and the complexity of a city. One can cross it in an hour, but not understand it in a lifetime.

Near the southern wall, where the old brick is most visible, there is a small plaza kept mostly empty. A single tree grows there upside down from the ceiling, its roots descending like chandeliers and its leaves brushing the mossy ground. Condensation forms on every leaf tip and falls into metal bowls arranged beneath. The bowls ring when struck by drops, each with a different tone. Around them insects gather, and frogs gather for the insects, and the pale dogs gather for the frogs, and workers pass through carrying tools under their arms, listening without looking up.

The music is accidental, civic, and ancient.

Above it all the engines turn, the plants thicken, the animals multiply, and the town continues—not thriving exactly, not decaying exactly, but living in the balanced unrest of something that has been here long enough to become natural.
