At the top of a very high ridge, where the air has thinned almost to a taste, there is a small basin cupped between new black peaks.

It is not large. You can see most of it from one standing place: a hollow perhaps the size of a ruined hamlet, enclosed by raw stone walls and snow-bright shoulders of mountain. The surrounding summits are jagged in a way that feels unfinished, as if they have only recently been forced upward from inside the earth and have not yet learned the rounded patience of old ranges. Their edges are sharp. Their talus slopes are fresh and angular. Fallen stones still show pale broken faces, unweathered, glinting under the fierce white light.

The light is enormous here. It has very little atmosphere to soften it. Even when the sun is low, it arrives like metal: hard, clear, almost audible in its brightness. Shadows are blue-black and exact. Ice crystals on rock, fur, moss, and rope flash sharply. The sky is not merely blue but deepened toward violet, and clouds, when they pass, seem close enough to strike with a thrown stone.

The cold is constant. It is not the deep buried cold of polar night, but the exposed cold of height: windless one moment and then suddenly needling through seams of clothing; dry on the lips, stinging in the nose, numbing the fingertips. Breath appears and vanishes. Water does not linger long in open bowls unless the sun is on it. Yet the basin is damp in a secretive, intermittent way. Moisture gathers where it can. It beads on the undersides of overhanging stone. It runs in hair-thin threads beneath mats of vegetation. It freezes overnight in shallow lenses, then melts into silver slicks by noon. Rain comes rarely but intensely, blown upward from lower valleys in brief gray veils, leaving everything jeweled before the cold takes hold again.

At the center of the basin lies a chain of shallow pools, not quite a stream and not quite separate water. They occupy depressions in the young rock like thumbprints. Their surfaces are often skinned with ice at dawn, but beneath the ice are green-black depths, surprisingly alive. Meltwater slips from one pool to another through narrow cuts, vanishing under stones and reappearing several paces away with a soft, private sound. In places the water steams faintly, not from warmth exactly, but from the difference between hidden earth-breath and the cutting air.

Around these pools the vegetation is unexpectedly lush.

It does not form a forest. There are no tall trees, nothing that could stand against the altitude and exposure. The lushness is low, dense, close to the ground: cushions, mats, ropes, tufts, rosettes. Moss swells over stone like green wool. Lichens bloom in orange, chalk-white, rust, and luminous yellow. Dark sedges grow in thick little ranks where water seeps. There are tiny alpine flowers with waxen petals, some translucent as frost, some an improbable blue. Their stems are short and strong, furred against the cold. Fernlike plants curl inside cracks where no soil seems to exist. Something like dwarf willow creeps flat along the ground, its leaves no larger than fingernails, silver-backed and trembling.

The basin is empty in the large sense. There is far more air than matter, far more open rock than shelter, far more brightness than shade. Presences are widely spaced and distinct: one boulder standing alone; one cluster of moss; one cairn; one pool; one patch of flowers on a ledge. Everything has room around it. Nothing crowds the eye. Even the lush vegetation is confined to favored pockets, gathered where condensation, melt, and mineral dust make a temporary bargain.

The materials of the place are simple and severe: fresh volcanic stone, pale fractured granite, ice, wet moss, bone, hide, wind-polished wood carried from lower slopes. The rock still smells faintly mineral after rain, sharp and metallic, like struck flint. Some surfaces are dark and glassy, others grainy and pale. The basin floor is uneven, cracked in recent lines, and in a few places the ground sounds hollow underfoot. Thin crusts of lichen bridge fissures that descend into darkness.

Because the land is young, it is active.

Pebbles fall often from the surrounding walls, ticking once or twice before silence absorbs them. Frost opens hairline cracks during the night and the sun pries them wider by day. Sheets of old snow detach from high ledges and slide down in soft collapses. Water appears, disappears, re-routes itself. A pool that was full one morning may be half-empty by dusk, while another, dry for weeks, may suddenly brim with black meltwater and swimming larvae. Small fans of gravel form at the mouths of gullies. New scratches scar the slopes after every freeze.

Yet despite all this movement, the place is quiet.

Not silent, exactly. Silence here has texture. You hear the faint ticking of thawing ice. The threadlike drip of water under stone. A wingbeat. The dry scrape of claws on shale. A distant, dull crack from a snow cornice far above. Occasionally a stone falls, and the sound seems indecently loud, ricocheting once around the basin before dying into the open air. There is no continuous roar, no forest murmur, no human bustle. Even the animals have learned to keep their sounds small.

There are many animals.

At first the basin seems empty of them, because most are scaled to its cracks, shadows, and tufts. Then the eye adjusts. Tiny dark birds flick from rock to rock, almost soundless, probing moss with needle beaks. Pale moths cling to sun-warmed stone, wings folded like scraps of old parchment. Beetles, glossy and slow from the cold, move under lichen shelves. In the pools are translucent shrimp, black tadpole-like things, flat worms clinging to submerged rock. Flies hatch in brief bursts whenever sunlight touches still water, rising in silver columns so quiet they seem more like weather than life.

Larger creatures pass through rather than dwell here. White-gray hares with short ears graze the moss edges, their bodies nearly invisible until they move. Small hoofed animals — goatlike, narrow-faced, sure-footed — descend from the upper rocks in the bright hours to lick minerals from wet stone. Their hooves make delicate clicks. A russet fox, compact and thick-furred, hunts the basin in looping paths, pausing with one paw lifted, listening to motions beneath frost-crust. High above, dark birds circle in the vast light, patient and almost ceremonial.

Predation happens quietly. A bird vanishes under a stoop of shadow. A hare kicks once in the snow-bright grass and is still. Beetles are plucked from moss. Larvae consume one another in the pools. Life here is abundant but economical, compressed into brief feeding windows and narrow refuges. Nothing wastes movement. Even the flowers open only during the most reliable hours of light, then shut themselves tight, holding their color inside like a coal.

The human marks are few.

Near the eastern wall of the basin stands a shelter made from flat stones stacked without mortar. It is low, half-sunk into a fold of rock, roofed with slabs and weighted with antlers. From a distance it resembles a natural pile, but up close its intention appears: a doorway just large enough to crawl through, a smoke-blackened ceiling, a ring of stones inside where a small fire once burned. There is no wood here except what has been carried up with effort. The fire would have been precious, brief, fed with twisted roots, dried dung, and scraps of resinous branch hauled from below.

A little higher on the slope, three poles lean together in a tripod, weathered silver and bound with rawhide. From them hang narrow strips of tanned skin, stiffened by cold, clicking faintly when wind enters the basin. They may be offerings, warnings, drying racks, or markers for traps now gone. Nearby are flakes of worked stone: black obsidian or some glassy local rock, chipped into scrapers, points, and blades. The craft is old and direct. Hands shaped these tools by percussion and pressure; no furnace, no wheel, no elaborate joinery. A bone needle lies half-buried in moss. A shallow groove worn into a boulder suggests repeated sharpening.

There are cairns too, but not many. Some mark a safe path through crusted ground. Some hold down scraps of hide. One small cairn stands beside the largest pool and contains, in its center, the skull of a mountain animal, placed facing the water. Lichen has begun to take it. Tiny yellow cups grow around the horn bases. Melt drips through the empty eyes in the afternoon.

The people who came here did not settle in numbers. This is not a village site. It is a seasonal place, a watching place, a hunting place, perhaps a place for rites tied to thaw, migration, or the first opening of the high passes. The basin offers water, animals, mineral stone, and a view of the sky unobstructed by trees. It does not offer comfort. Anyone staying here would count fuel, watch weather, and sleep lightly.

The rules of the world are clear but not gentle.

Stay low when the wind rises. Do not step on smooth white crust unless you know what lies beneath it. Trust moss less than rock, rock less than ice, ice least of all in sunlight. Drink from running seams, not still pools after the birds have fed. Leave fat for the small ones if you want them to return. Stack stones where the path disappears; the mountain changes its mind after rain. If clouds climb from below and the light turns flat, descend or shelter before the basin vanishes around you.

There is a slight strangeness to the place, nothing grand enough to overturn its reality. It is more like a persistent error in expectation. Sounds sometimes arrive late. A falling stone may be seen before it is heard, though it drops only a few body-lengths away. Reflections in the pools show clouds that have not yet crossed the sun. On windless days the hanging hide strips twist as if remembering weather. The dwarf willows all lean toward the largest pool, though the sun travels elsewhere.

The animals seem to accept this. Birds drink from the future-colored reflections. The goats avoid one particular slab of stone without urgency, simply flowing around it as water does. In the coldest hour before dawn, when frost grows audibly along the edges of the pools, faint pale filaments rise from the moss and withdraw again at the first touch of direct light. They might be fungal threads. They might be vapor. They might be something the old visitors knew by name and marked with the skull cairn.

By noon the basin is brilliant.

The pools flash. The moss darkens and swells with melt. Insects lift from the wet places. Hoofed animals step down from impossible ledges. Flowers open flat to the light, exposing orange throats and black pollen. The stone shelter casts a hard blue shadow. The air smells of cold minerals, wet green growth, animal fur, and distant snow.

By evening the brightness withdraws all at once behind the western teeth of rock. The temperature drops with startling speed. Water skins over. Flowers close. Birds tuck themselves into cracks. The fox appears again, almost noiseless, its whiskers white with frost. The basin becomes a room of blue stone and small lives holding still.

Then night comes, clear and high, and the stars are savage in their nearness. The little pools reflect them one by one through their forming ice, as if the mountain has opened small windows downward into another sky.
