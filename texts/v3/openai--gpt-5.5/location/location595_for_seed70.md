At a height where there should be thin air and hard sunlight, there is water.

It lies over the mountains in a single warm, transparent mass, as if a sea has been poured into the sky and held there by some patient law older than weather. The summits do not break it. They remain submerged: black young ridges, glassy slopes of basalt and pale stone, narrow fins and knife-edged saddles still too fresh to have been softened by time. The whole upland is drowned, but not in darkness. Daylight enters from above in long green-gold shafts, wavering only slightly, so the place is neither ocean nor lake nor cloud, but something between all three — an elevated water-world suspended over steep country.

The terrain continues for many miles. Ranges pass into terraces, terraces into bowls, bowls into steep-walled valleys filled from rim to rim. There are no shores in the ordinary sense, only changes in density: places where the water grows milky with mineral breath, places where submerged forest thickens until sight shortens to a few arm-lengths, places where open blue-green corridors run between ridges like drowned passes. Far off, the light fades not because of depth exactly, but because there is so much life layered into it.

The vegetation is lush and close. It grows from cracks in new rock, from shelves of pumice, from mats of mineral silt that have barely settled. There are forests here, but they are buoyant forests: tall ribbon-stems rooted in stone and rising upward in slow spirals; broad translucent leaves that open like lungs to the light; curtains of fine red and amber fronds hanging from overhangs; floating canopies of thick, waxy pads caught beneath the water’s upper skin. Some plants have the look of kelp, others of ferns, others of moss magnified into architecture. Many do not choose between forms. A stalk may begin as a dark reed, branch into feathery coral, and end in a cluster of soft green cups holding trapped bubbles.

Everything is crowded, though not hurried. Growth presses against growth. Vines loop through submerged gullies. Root-fans spread across raw stone like dark hands. Filaments knot into veils so fine they turn the water pearlescent. The landscape is dense in layers: stone, silt, algae, leaf, suspended pollen, drifting strands, slow bubbles, the faint snow of organic matter descending and rising at once. Yet the scale remains open. One can look through breaks in the vegetation and see an entire drowned valley dropping away, its slopes furred green, its central ravine filled with blue light.

The warmth is constant. Not tropical in the manner of hot air, but bodily, enveloping, a gentle heat held in the water and in the black stone beneath it. In some hollows the rock exhales. Threads of clearer water shimmer upward from vents, carrying the scentless memory of minerals. Around these places the vegetation grows more intensely: pale leaves, almost white; red-brown fans; nodules like closed flowers; soft black mats that ripple without current. The mountains are young enough that they still seem undecided. Their fractures are sharp, their ledges abrupt, their mineral faces bright where slides have recently opened them. The water has begun its work, but only begun. It polishes edges grain by grain. It gathers silt in the lee of outcrops. It teaches the new land to blur.

Sound is scarce. The whole region is wrapped in a muffled quiet, not silence but restraint. There is the faint tick of settling stone. The soft contact of leaves touching leaves. A distant, low tremor from somewhere under the ridge, more felt than heard. Occasionally a bubble escapes from a fissure and rises with a silver wobble, brushing through stems, breaking against the upper water-ceiling in a tiny, private click. When something larger moves, the sound travels strangely: a single pulse through the ribs of the place, then nothing.

Animals are rare. A small pale thing may appear between fronds and vanish before it has fully become visible — a finless swimmer with wide membrane-wings, or a narrow-bodied grazer trailing threads from its mouth. In the open passes, shadows sometimes cross far above: broad, slow shapes like leaves that learned intention. They do not hunt often. There is not enough urgency here for abundance of flesh. Life belongs mostly to rooted things, drifting things, colonies, films, patient mouths too small to see. The few animals seem like visitors in a country governed by plants and stone.

The strangeness of the place is not violent. It accumulates.

Upward and downward are mostly trustworthy, but not entirely. In certain basins, fallen leaves sink halfway and stop, forming suspended meadows in midwater. Fine roots grow from them both toward the rock below and toward the light above. In narrow clefts, beads of water denser than the surrounding water crawl along the stone like transparent slugs. Some cliffs shed flakes that do not fall, but hang beside the parent wall for months, slowly turning green. There are hollows where light pools as if it has weight, brightening the silt long after the sun has shifted. There are groves whose leaves all turn at once toward something under the mountain rather than toward the sky.

No constructed thing interrupts it. There are no paths, no cuts, no signs of hands, no tool marks, no remnants of habitation. Nothing has been arranged except by pressure, growth, buoyancy, fracture, settling, hunger, mineral seep, and time. The world’s rules are simple but unfamiliar: stone rises young and sharp; water receives it; plants take hold before soil is fully born; motion is expensive; change is slow. A landslide may take a season to finish falling, its debris caught in vegetation and descending by inches. A new vent may cloud a valley for years before clearing. A single root may split a ledge over the course of generations.

The light remains ordinary enough to make the impossibility more convincing. Morning enters slantwise, pale and clean, turning the upper leaves yellow. Midday opens the distances, revealing ridge behind ridge behind ridge through blue water. Evening warms everything to bronze, and the black stone seems briefly red, as though still remembering fire. At night, if night comes here as it does elsewhere, the place does not become empty. The plants hold dim green afterlight in their tissues. Mineral vents glow faintly in cracks. Rare animals pass like thoughts between them.

It is a high drowned wilderness, young and overgrown, quiet not because it is dead but because almost everything here lives slowly. The mountains are still forming their faces. The water is still learning its basins. The forests are still deciding what a forest can be when there is no air among the leaves.
