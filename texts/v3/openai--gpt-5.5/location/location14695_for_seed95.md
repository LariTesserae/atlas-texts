At this height the world has been worn almost level, not into smoothness but into patience.

The land is a broad upland table of old stone, a plateau so ancient that mountains have passed through it and gone. What remains is not dramatic in the young way: no sharp peaks, no fresh cliffs, no roaring cascades. Instead there are long backs of rock rising and sinking by degrees, shallow basins, low rims, terraces like the memory of steps. The horizon is open and far, but the ground nearby is crowded with detail. Every surface is occupied. Every crack has a tenant.

The stone is dark in some places, honey-pale in others, all of it polished by ages of weak rain, warm wind, and the slow chemical chewing of mist. It has the look of bone left too long in the sun: hardened, porous, dignified, almost final. Here and there the plateau is paved with natural slabs, fitted so closely they resemble an abandoned road, though no hand set them. Between the slabs lie seams of ocher dust, black lichen, shed insect casings, tiny feathers, pellets, dried skins, and the beadlike droppings of animals too numerous to count.

Water is present but never abundant. It arrives in interruptions.

At night, condensation gathers thickly on the cool stone and strings the low vegetation with bright drops. In the morning it slides into hairline fissures, vanishing before the warmth fully rises. Rain comes as brief, tilted curtains from clouds that do not linger. The water darkens the plateau, wakes mineral smells, fills shallow bowls, then retreats into pockets and underlayers. Some basins hold greenish pools for days at a time, each no larger than a room, each ringed with tracks and larvae and the fragile signatures of drinking mouths. Other hollows contain only damp mud beneath a crust of salt and dust, waiting.

The vegetation is sparse if seen from afar. The plateau may appear mostly bare: a high, warm, stony openness with little shade. But close to the ground the life is intricate. There are wiry shrubs no taller than a knee, their branches twisted into defensive knots. There are gray mats of cushion plants pressed flat against the stone, absorbing fog through felted leaves. There are red-stemmed succulents growing in crevices, their skins waxy and scarred. There are lichens like old maps: orange continents, black rivers, pale islands. Mosses appear only where the rock sweats. They flare green after moisture and then darken, folding into themselves, not dead but withdrawn.

Nothing here grows quickly upward. Life spreads sideways, downward, inward. Roots probe cracks older than species. Seeds wait years in dust. A shrub may take a century to become large enough to cast a hand-sized shadow.

Yet the animals are overwhelming.

They are not loud, not generally. The place remains mostly quiet, as if sound has difficulty crossing so much exposed stone. But the quiet is dense with small motions. The plateau is inhabited in layers: beneath slabs, inside hollow stems, under crusts, in shallow burrows, in cliff-splits, in the mud beneath temporary pools, in the air just above the warm ground.

There are insects in astonishing numbers, many of them armored and slow. Glossy beetles push through mineral grit. Pale termites build low, ribbed galleries between stones, their structures looking like melted clay roots. Ants move in black and copper streams that split and rejoin at fissures. Winged things rise at dusk in soft clouds, so many that the distance seems to grain and shimmer. Moths the color of dust cling to the undersides of ledges. Transparent flies stand on the surfaces of the pools, each one mirrored beneath itself.

Larger creatures live by stealth and economy. Thin lizards lie flat against heated rock, indistinguishable until they blink. Soft-footed mammals with oversized ears emerge from cavities at twilight, harvesting seeds and beetles, pausing often to listen. There are birds, many more than the sparse vegetation should allow: ground-nesting runners, cliff-pickers, small hooked-beaked hunters, flocks that arrive suddenly and depart without pattern. They rarely sing. Their calls are chips, clicks, low whistles, the scrape of claw on stone, the brief alarm that vanishes almost as soon as it begins.

In the temporary pools, life erupts with obscene speed. Eggs sealed in mud split open when rainwater touches them. Tadpoles, translucent worms, red branch-legged crustaceans, and soft-bodied things with too many cilia crowd the water until it seems thicker than water should be. They feed, molt, mate, and seed the mud again before the pool shrinks. Birds know these basins. So do snakes, whose bodies are the color of wet basalt. So do beetles that fly at night from one dark mirror to another, guided by some rule older than sight.

The fauna does not dominate by size but by presence. Every pause reveals more of it. A stone apparently empty becomes a city of joints, antennae, palps, folded legs. A shrub contains nests, husks, eggs, sleeping bodies, parasites of those bodies, and smaller parasites still. The air above a damp hollow is a vertical pasture. The ground itself seems to breathe through animals.

Scattered across the plateau are structures, though whether they are built or grown is difficult to say. Low rings of stone encircle certain basins, too regular to be dismissed at first glance, but perhaps made by frost heave, root pressure, generations of burrowing animals. Some ridges are pierced by small doorlike openings leading into chambers lined with mud and silk. Pale towers stand here and there, waist-high, fluted and windowed, made by colonial insects from chewed mineral and plant fiber. In the distance they resemble the ruins of a vanished settlement. Up close they are warm, faintly pulsing, crowded with blind workers.

There are also old tracks hardened into the stone: not footprints exactly, but lanes. Animals have crossed the same saddles and descended to the same water pockets for so many thousands of years that their routes have become shallow grooves, polished and dark. These are the roads of the plateau, made without intention yet kept with devotion. They converge at certain damp places, avoid certain bare flats, circle certain boulders that may contain nests or old scent or some mineral taste.

The world here has rules, and they are slow rules.

Water must not be wasted. Shade is a currency. Stillness is a form of hunting and a form of prayer. Nothing assumes permanence except stone, and even stone is understood to be moving, though too slowly for the eye. Life survives by waiting until the instant when waiting is no longer wise. Eggs sleep. Seeds sleep. Spores sleep. Reptiles sleep through heat inside cracks. Moss sleeps through drought. The plateau itself seems asleep most of the time, but it is not empty sleep. It is the sleep of a body conserving enormous memory.

Warmth lies over everything, especially after midday, when the stone releases a dry mineral heat. The air is thin because of the height, and the sun has a clear, unsentimental force. Shadows are sharp but rare. The wind comes lightly, often as a broad pressure rather than a gust, carrying dust, resin, old salt, guano, damp clay from some basin not visible from where one stands. At times cloud passes over and the whole country dims without cooling much. The light is ordinary in strength, neither blazing nor dim, yet it reveals too much: the pitting of stone, the scales on a lizard’s throat, the delicate serrations of grass stems, the crowded life in a spoonful of rainwater.

The strangeness of the place is not theatrical. It does not announce itself with impossible colors or floating stones. It comes from proportion and age. The land is open, but the open space is packed. It is barren, but never vacant. It is quiet, but never still. It is ancient, yet full of hatching. Its ruins are alive; its cities are made by insects; its roads are worn by thirst.

In some areas the plateau drops gently into shallow valleys that are less like valleys than broad depressions, their floors holding more dust and therefore more life. Here the shrubs stand closer together, though still low, and the animal density becomes almost feverish. Burrows perforate the banks. Termite towers cluster like eroded villages. Birds patrol the edges. During rare rains, water threads through these basins in braided sheets, no deeper than a finger, gliding over stone and vanishing into gravel. For a few hours the land reflects the sky in fragments. Then the fragments shrink, cloud over with larvae, and become mud.

There may be bones, but they do not last long. They are gnawed, bored, dissolved, taken into shells and eggs and mandibles. Death here is rapidly subdivided. A carcass becomes a weather event for the small inhabitants. Beetles arrive. Flies arrive. Ants make decisions by the thousand. Vultures or their lean equivalents wait at a distance, silent, patient, formal. In a day, what was a body is a stain, a scatter, a change in the soil beneath a thornbush.

At night the plateau cools, but not harshly. Moisture condenses. The stones darken. The sparse plants shine at their edges. Sounds become clearer: tiny chewing, the ticking movement of beetles, the soft collapse of dust as something digs, the dry whisper of wings, an occasional bird call from far off, like a pebble dropped into a deep well. Stars appear with highland clarity when clouds are absent. Under them, the ground comes alive more visibly. Eyes catch faint light and disappear. Pale bodies cross black stone. The temporary pools ripple without wind.

Nothing changes quickly here, except the very small. The shape of the land has already endured its great violences and settled into an old equilibrium. Erosion continues, but as a final polishing. Ridges lower by grains. Basins deepen by breaths. A crack widens enough in a century to admit one more root. The living world, meanwhile, cycles through frantic miniature generations upon this almost motionless back of stone.

It is a place near the end of geology and the beginning of innumerable lives. High, warm, damp only in secret, sparsely green, densely inhabited. A plateau of old rock and brief water, where silence is never empty and every hollow is waiting for rain.
