The region is not quite underwater and not quite under rain. It is under a pressure.

A warm, dim, green-black world lies spread across a low basin that may once have been a coastal plain, or may have risen yesterday from a sea that has not yet agreed to leave. Water fills everything: not as a lake fills a hollow, but as weather fills a sky, as blood fills tissue. It hangs in curtains, lenses, vertical rivers, slow sheets, hovering globes, sudden avalanches. It stands chest-deep in the streets of ruined service roads. It pours upward from cracks in new basalt. It drifts sideways through groves. It occupies the air so completely that distance is seen through wavering membranes, one behind another, each bending the world a little differently.

The ground is low and young. Sharp black ridges spear up through the flood-muck in places, their edges still glassy, unrounded by time. Newly cooled stone forms blistered terraces beneath the water, bright with orange mineral veins and pale seams of salt. There are fresh gullies, but they do not look carved; they look torn open. Banks of clay slump into channels and expose roots that have already grown into the wound. The land feels unfinished, as though it is still being extruded, poured, kneaded from below by some enormous subterranean engine.

Everywhere is dense growth.

Not old forest, not patient forest, not canopy built over centuries, but lushness in a state of outbreak. Mangrove-like trunks rise from steaming shallows, their bark green with slick membranes. Ferns grow in stacked tiers, fronds layered so tightly they resemble feathers soaked in oil. Vines hang into the water and continue growing beneath its surface, splitting into translucent ribbons that pulse faintly with sap. Broad leaves collect rain that is not falling from clouds but condensing directly out of the air in heavy beads; the leaves bend, spill, spring back, and fill again. Moss crawls over pipes, ladders, stranded machinery, the sides of tanks. Reeds crowd every open channel until the channels become breathing green throats.

The vegetation is too quick. Shoots emerge from mud while you watch. A broken stem lengthens, knots, flowers, and collapses into rot in an hour. Seedpods inflate under water like bladders, burst without sound beneath the larger sound, and release filaments that immediately snag on metal and stone. Roots do not search so much as invade. They enter bolt-holes, gauges, vents, eyelets, the hollow ribs of collapsed gantries. Where concrete has cracked, root-masses have thickened into muscular ropes, prying slabs apart with a slow industrial insistence.

There are no animals.

No insects in the wet leaves. No birds crossing the dimness. No fish beneath the skin of the flood. No frogs calling from the inundated machinery. The absence is not quiet, because nothing here is quiet, but it is complete. The green world produces no chirr, no wingbeat, no scuttle, no mating cry, no alarm. Flowers open to no pollinators. Fruit splits and falls for no mouths. The water is disturbed by impact, pressure, eruption, rain, collapse, machinery, but never by a living body fleeing or feeding.

The sound is immense.

It is not one sound but a stack of sounds so crowded they lose their borders. Water hammers on water. Water pours through grates, down shafts, over roofs, through broken windows, along half-submerged corridors. Somewhere in the dim region, turbines are still turning, or something has learned to imitate turbines: a deep rotary thunder that climbs and drops without pattern. Pumps cough, seize, restart, scream in metal casings drowned to their gauges. Chains clatter though no chain is visible. Sirens howl from scattered towers, some submerged to their horns, so their warnings emerge as bubbling moans. Steam vents explode in white columns. Vast sheets of suspended water collapse without warning, striking the flooded ground with the force of buildings falling.

The air itself roars. It seems to have become granular, thick with impacts. To speak here would be to place a small object into a furnace.

The sky, if it is sky, is low and dim. Not night. Not storm-dark exactly. A greenish bruise-light filters through layers of vapor and floating water, through suspended silt, through veils of rain that fall sideways and then upward. The sun is a rumor diffused into a pale smear that migrates unpredictably, sometimes appearing below the horizon, sometimes glinting from puddles overhead. Shadows do not point consistently. At times, the flooded plain is lit from within the water itself, by cold industrial glows leaking from submerged equipment: cyan strips, red hazard lamps, amber panels blinking under algae, entire drowned rooms pulsing behind a wall of wavering brown-green liquid.

The built things are scattered, but they matter.

A pumping station stands on short concrete stilts, half-swallowed by vines and foam. Its roof has folded inward under the weight of collected water, yet the central flywheel inside keeps rotating, slow and huge, throwing off spray with every revolution. Farther out, low refinery stacks protrude from the floodplain like black reeds. Some release steam. Some release rain. Some release ropes of dark fluid that rise, twist, become glossy vines before touching down again as roots.

There are catwalks, though they rarely connect anything now. They begin at the side of a settling tank, cross forty meters of steaming water, then end in midair where the rest has been sheared away. Railings hum under constant vibration. Warning placards remain bolted to posts, their symbols warped by mineral deposits and plant growth: pressure, caustic, electrical hazard, no entry, eye protection required. Many are written in languages whose letters appear to rearrange when seen through rain.

A line of utility poles crosses the basin, leaning at different angles, wires sagging into the flood and rising out again, wrapped in wet creepers. They still carry current. Blue-white arcs crawl along them during the heavier downpours, branching into the water, into the leaves, into the air. The electricity does not kill anything visibly, because there is nothing here to kill except machines and plants, and both seem to accept it as weather. Leaves nearest the wires have grown metallic edges. Root mats around junction boxes twitch in repeated pulses.

In the distance, perhaps several kilometers away, an industrial complex occupies a low ridge, or is being occupied by it. The ridge is new stone, black and red and wet, pushed up through the factory floor from below. Buildings sit tilted on its flanks: warehouses, settling sheds, cylindrical tanks, rectangular processing halls with windows blown outward by pressure from within. Conveyor belts hang like dead tongues between structures, though some still move, carrying nothing but water, fern fronds, stones, and occasionally pieces of themselves.

The factory never stops operating. It no longer has a product. Or its product is the place.

Machines scoop mud and pour it back into channels where it becomes soil before dispersing. Presses stamp sheets of rain into brief translucent panels that flex, crack, and dissolve. Pipes inhale floodwater and exhale warm mist fragrant with crushed leaves, rust, salt, and something like ozone. Kilns glow underwater, red mouths dimmed by depth. Cooling towers birth clouds that immediately condense and fall back on them in brutal vertical columns. Every mechanism is overgrown, flooded, corroded, misaligned, but not inert. Their labor has become ecological. Their failure has become a climate.

Reality here is not broken cleanly. It is softening.

Edges disobey themselves first. The line where water meets air trembles, doubles, rises a few centimeters away from the true surface. A stairway descending into a flooded basement may, when viewed from the side, also ascend into the belly of a cloud. Reflections do not always reflect the thing above them; sometimes they show what the structure looked like before the flood, sometimes what it will look like after the vines finish with it, sometimes a bare unbuilt marsh under a copper sky.

Distances swell and contract with the rain. A pump-house visible beyond three channels may require a day to reach, while a tower at the horizon may appear abruptly close enough to touch, its wet ladder clanging in the spray. Walkways repeat. The same warning light can be seen ahead, behind, overhead, and submerged underfoot, blinking at slightly different intervals. Some pools are deeper than the landscape allows. Drop a metal rod into one and it will ring against something far below, as though striking the hull of a sunken city.

The water carries fragments of impossible weather. Warm currents flow past like invisible animals, though there are no animals. Cold disks of depth drift through the air, flattening leaves as they pass. Rain sometimes falls in the shape of rooms: four walls, a ceiling, a doorway made entirely of descending water, remaining coherent for several minutes before shattering into ordinary storm. In certain hollows, water piles into transparent mounds taller than trees, held by no container, trembling with trapped lights and the distorted silhouettes of machines that are not physically present.

New land forms constantly and badly.

Mud boils up from below, thick with shell-like mineral plates, though no shells live here. It hardens into ridged causeways that split under the pressure of roots. Sandbars appear in channels and are immediately colonized by grasses, then overturned by a surge, then replaced by dark stone that steams in the rain. The banks of canals calve into the water with explosive slaps. Concrete slabs tilt, settle, sink, rise again wearing curtains of weed. Fresh ravines open where drainage systems below have collapsed or awakened. A shallow field may become a lagoon between one siren-cycle and the next.

There is an organizing principle, but it is not stable enough to be called order. The whole basin behaves like a living industrial process: intake, compression, overflow, growth, rupture, condensation, ignition, drowning, regrowth. It cycles, but the cycles mutate. Every repetition produces a new side effect. Every side effect becomes infrastructure. A leak becomes a stream. A stream becomes a canal. A canal grows reeds dense enough to support a rail. The rail hums with power and attracts lightning. The lightning vitrifies the mud. The glassy mud sprouts ferns with transparent stems. Their roots pry open a buried pipe. The pipe begins to sing.

The smell is warm and saturated: rain on hot stone, bruised chlorophyll, iron oxide, wet concrete, diesel long diluted, fungal sweetness, brine, electrical burning. At times the scent of fresh-cut timber passes through, though there are no saws cutting wood. At other times the smell of a hospital corridor leaks from a flooded doorway in the side of a hill. Some structures exhale air that is older and drier than anything outside, and for a moment the lungs remember dust.

The vegetation has learned the machinery’s shapes. There are trees with bolted flanges embedded in their trunks. Ferns grow in perfect spirals matching turbine vanes. Vines wrap pipes so precisely they seem manufactured as insulation. Mats of algae form grid patterns over standing pools, dividing them into luminous cells. Flowers open with the clicking rhythm of relays. Seedheads hang in bunches like corroded lightbulbs, each full of warm rainwater. When they burst, the seeds scatter upward, drawn by some reverse gravity into the dim, roaring air.

Yet the plants are not peaceful. They are exuberant to the point of violence. Leaves slap against metal in the windless storm. Roots crack ceramic tiles with sounds like rifle shots. Fast-growing cane spears through corrugated roofing from below, then splits into tassels that clog gutters and vents. A vine entering a control room through a broken window may cross the floor, climb the console, press its tendrils into every switch, and flower from the emergency stop button in a single afternoon. The machines respond by starting, stopping, flooding, heating, sparking. The plant responds by growing harder.

The water has depth even where it should not. Channels between buildings are only waist-wide but descend into blue-black vertical abysses filled with drifting leaves. The flooded fields beyond the refinery appear shallow because grass-tips protrude, but the stems lengthen downward into darkness without soil, suspended in columns of liquid kilometers deep. Ladders bolted to low walls continue below the waterline far beyond their possible length. In some places, walking on a submerged road, one can see rooftops beneath one’s feet, then the underside of clouds beneath those rooftops, then another drowned road with another surface above it.

The place is low, but it contains verticality as pressure, not elevation. Depth replaces height. Rain replaces sky. Towers rise only to vanish into wet haze, while pits, tanks, basements, sumps, shafts, culverts, and canals seem to multiply beneath the surface. The basin is a flat mouth swallowing layers.

Rules exist, though they must be learned by injury.

Do not trust still water; it may be falling very fast in another direction.

Do not shelter under broad leaves during a siren rise; they collect weight from several skies.

Do not step through a doorway if its reflection shows the door closed.

Do not touch yellow moss on energized metal; it remembers current and releases it later.

Do not follow voices from pipes. There are no voices here, only pressure choosing familiar shapes.

Do not assume a bridge remains connected because it was connected when you began crossing.

Do not drink water that casts a shadow.

At intervals, the whole region convulses. The deafening background becomes a single solid impact, as though an ocean has been dropped onto the basin from above. Water leaps from every surface. The floating lenses burst. Rain reverses. Trees bend flat, then upright, then continue growing at new angles. Factory stacks flare with internal lightning. The submerged lights all brighten at once. The mud ridges crack open and expose raw, glowing seams beneath. For a few minutes the world is nearly legible: every flow visible, every machine outlined, every root lit from within, every canal showing its true impossible depth.

Then the convulsion passes, and density returns. Dimness folds back over the plain. The sounds separate again into many unbearable sounds. The new cracks fill with warm water. The plants lean into the fresh minerals. Pumps drag breath through drowned throats. Somewhere a conveyor resumes its empty labor.

From a distance, if distance can be kept, the basin looks like a storm that has rooted itself. A drowned industrial district, a young delta, a greenhouse built inside a thunderhead, an unfinished continent submerged under its own birth-fluid. It extends beyond immediate sight but not beyond influence: every horizon is busy with motion, every low ridge crawling with vegetation, every structure either sinking, growing, operating, or becoming rain.

Nothing watches from the leaves. Nothing nests in the girders. Nothing hunts in the flood. The absence of animals gives the place an unfinished cruelty, as if it has prepared too much habitat for a world that has not arrived, or has already rejected it. There is food everywhere and no mouths. Flowers everywhere and no visitors. Shelter everywhere and no bodies. Sound everywhere and no calls.

Only water, heat, machinery, young earth, and rampant green.

Only the basin making and unmaking itself.

Only the flood learning how to be a factory, and the factory remembering how to be a weather system, and the plants taking both lessons faster than either can survive.
