At the top of the world, where altitude has thinned not only the air but the agreement of matter, there is a city without people.

It stands across a high basin of pale stone and black salt, miles wide, ringed by cliffs that rise higher still in layered walls like the exposed ribs of an old planet. The basin is not flat, but it gives the impression of having once been hammered level by some immense, patient force. Its floor is wind-polished mineral: ash-gray feldspar, blue shale, dry nacreous crusts, salt so old it has turned granular and opaque. There is no water. Not hidden in gullies, not frozen in shadow, not preserved in jars, not promised by cloud. The air is cool and extremely dry, with the taste of chalk, iron, and unlit stone.

The city occupies the basin unevenly, as if it settled there rather than being built. Streets run in long empty grids that bend where perspective bends. Towers are cut from the same material as the cliffs, or appear to have weathered out of them: narrow, faceted, windowless shafts with terraced flanks, colonnades of stone too thin to support themselves, archways leading through entire neighborhoods of shadow. The architecture has the order of a settlement and the patience of geology. There are plazas, causeways, stairways, retaining walls, hollow courtyards, amphitheaters open to a dim sky. But there are no signs, no tools, no metal, no glass shaped by craft, no wheels, no lamps, no evidence that hands ever labored here.

Still, it is urban. The place has districts. It has edges and centers. It has avenues designed for processions no one remembers, and tight alleys that admit only a single file of wind. It has cisterns that never held water, wells that descend into dry brightness, bridges over ravines filled with nothing but altitude. It has a civic geometry, an intention almost legible, though the reader has not been invented.

The city may have formed by erosion. The basin’s rock fractures in right angles and sheds itself in slabs, and over millions of years the wind may have carved towers, rooms, thresholds, and steps. Salt expands in cracks, pries surfaces apart, makes cornices, galleries, balconies. Dust collects in hollows and hardens into floors. The cliffs exfoliate in rectangular sheets. The cold contracts stone until it rings faintly at night, and the morning loosens it again. Every block has been shaped by the slow work of dryness.

Or perhaps erosion came later and merely edited what was already there.

The light is dim, though not dark. The sky is a low violet-gray, too close and too deep at once, bruised around the horizon. The sun, when visible, is small and blurred, as if seen through a membrane of powdered mica. Shadows are long but indistinct. They pool in doorways and then lift, rearranging themselves without reference to the sun’s position. At certain hours the city appears carved from bone; at others from cooled smoke. Distance is unreliable. A tower half a mile away may show individual grains of stone, while a wall at arm’s length may blur into a concept of wall.

The air almost never moves quickly. There is wind, but it is spare and careful. It travels down the avenues in thin streams, carrying dust finer than flour. The dust does not billow; it threads. It draws pale lines over pavements, erases footprints before they can claim to have existed, accumulates in the corners of staircases, and then, without warning, lifts in sheets and crosses a plaza like a school of silent fish.

Sound is scarce. The basin absorbs it. A falling pebble can be heard for a long time if it chooses the correct staircase, tapping downward through a hundred turns until it becomes indistinguishable from memory. In the towers there are muted clicks: thermal fractures opening and closing. Salt grains hiss faintly when the temperature changes. Sometimes a long stone façade gives a single, low note, not quite a groan and not quite music, as internal stress redistributes through its mass. There are no running streams, no leaves, no insect whine, no engines, no voices. Silence is not empty here. It is built up in layers.

The terrain beyond the city is open and severe. Long salt flats slope toward broken shelves of basalt. Dry ravines, ancient and elaborate, cut down from the surrounding heights, though nothing liquid seems capable of having made them. They may be the paths of former winds so dense they flowed like rivers, or of powdered crystal avalanches, or of something that took the shape of erosion without the substance. The ravines are smooth-sided, branching, mature. Their banks show strata of white, ocher, black, green-gray, and translucent amber stone. The land has endured itself for a very long time. Nothing is raw. Even the fractures look ancestral.

There are mountains beyond the ring-cliffs, visible only when the dimness thins: serrated planes of blue mineral rising into a sky that does not successfully recede. Their peaks seem impossibly high, not because they pierce clouds—there are almost none—but because they appear to stand in different scales at once. A ridge may be twenty miles away and also just behind the next wall. On certain days the mountains duplicate. On others they are absent, and the basin sits beneath a blank dome of mineral dusk.

Vegetation is nearly nonexistent. In some of the deepest shade, where salt has not burned everything clean, there are crusts on the stone: black, brittle, circular stains that might be lichen if lichen had forgotten softness. They grow by subtraction, leaving etched halos in the rock. They do not green. They do not flower. They seem less alive than persistent. When touched, they release a smell like cold ash and old mushrooms. In abandoned courtyards, pale filaments sometimes bridge cracks between flagstones, but they withdraw from direct attention, becoming scratches, then mineral veins, then nothing. Whether these are organisms, salts, or habits of the stone is unclear.

Fauna is rare, and most of it is doubtful. High overhead, at irregular intervals, something like a bird crosses the dim sun: too far up, too slow-winged, with a body that vanishes between beats. Its shadow passes over plazas several minutes before or after it does. Among the salt flats live small clicking animals, no larger than a hand, with translucent shells and jointed legs made for silence. They emerge only when the temperature drops. They do not drink. They scrape mineral films from shaded stone and retreat into crevices when watched.

There are also the tall ones.

They are seldom seen directly. At the ends of avenues, where distance has begun to fail, a vertical shape will detach from a colonnade and cross an open square with the slowness of a sundial. It may be an animal, or a pressure in the air, or a misaligned piece of architecture briefly migrating. Its body is narrow and faceted. It bends in too many places. It leaves no track, but after it passes, nearby walls show fresh weathering, as if years have rubbed against them in a moment. The clicking creatures avoid those routes for many days.

The city is active, though quietly so. It is full of processes. Each dusk, frost does not form—there is no moisture for frost—but the stones whiten anyway, exhaling a powdery bloom from within. By morning the bloom has arranged itself into delicate maps across plazas and thresholds. These maps correspond to no known terrain. Wind erases them by afternoon, and the next evening the stone produces another set.

Gravity varies by district. In the lower markets, if they are markets, fallen blocks lie heavily embedded in pavement, and walking requires effort, as if one climbs while standing still. In the upper terraces, where stairs lead to terraces lead to more stairs, pebbles descend reluctantly and sometimes pause in midair before deciding on a direction. Dust in those neighborhoods gathers in vertical drifts against nothing. A person crossing there would feel their bones listening.

Time is also not uniformly distributed. Some streets are newer at one end than the other. A wall may be sharp, freshly broken, and unweathered on its eastern face, while its western face has been rounded by ten thousand years of wind. Doorways open onto rooms filled with afternoon light from a century that has not touched the street outside. In certain plazas, footsteps arrive before the walker. In others, echoes remain after the sound has been forgotten by the air.

Yet the place is not chaotic. Its breakdown has rules, even if they are not human rules.

No water may persist. This is the first law. Anything resembling moisture is converted almost instantly into salt, light, or distance. Breath becomes a faint crust on cloth and skin. Tears would crystallize before falling. Blood, if spilled, would darken, stiffen, and flake into mineral script. The city rejects liquidity as a body rejects poison.

No flame burns cleanly. Fire, if brought here, would flatten and dim, becoming a red stain on the air. Heat is tolerated only briefly. The coolness of the basin is not merely climate but ordinance.

Nothing grows upward unless it is stone, shadow, or error.

Names do not remain attached to things. A plaza named once will not be the same plaza when named again. A street counted by its arches will acquire or lose arches during counting. The city does not resist measurement violently; it simply outlives each measurement as it is made.

And nothing truly echoes unless it has happened before.

The buildings themselves seem to participate in cycles. At dawn, narrow seams open along the tower walls and reveal interiors glowing faintly with a color like buried moonlight. By midday the seams close. At dusk, staircases rearrange their destination by a small but consequential degree. Whole districts subside by less than an inch, then rise again over the course of months. Salt pushes through joints, lifts pavements, buckles courtyards, is abraded away by wind, returns. The city breathes in mineral time, but in some neighborhoods that breath has accelerated enough to be perceptible.

There are empty squares where the paving stones are arranged in concentric rings. At their centers are pits descending into dry dark. No air rises from them. Drop a stone and no sound returns; lean too near and the pit reflects not a face but an aerial view of the city, seen from impossible height. Sometimes those pits show the basin as it was before the city, bare and cracked. Sometimes they show the city filled with processions of tall, antlered shadows. Sometimes they show nothing but a white salt plain extending forever.

The streets bear marks like traffic, but not from wheels or feet. Long parallel grooves run along major avenues, polished smooth and slightly luminous. They curve around plazas, split at intersections, pass through archways with exact clearance. Perhaps heavy bodies once moved there. Perhaps the avenues themselves flow at night, carrying pieces of the city from district to district. In a few places, towers have been dragged sideways, leaving foundations exposed like roots of stone.

There are no interiors in the ordinary sense. Rooms open into rooms, but depth behaves badly. A doorway in a ground-level wall may lead to a balcony overlooking the city from above. A cellar may contain a sky. A corridor may stretch for hours and return to the threshold before the walker enters it. Many chambers are empty except for dust arranged in undisturbed cones. Others contain stone furniture grown seamlessly from floor and wall: benches too tall, tables too thin, basins without hollows, shelves holding slabs of blank mineral. The forms suggest use, but not bodies.

The absence of inhabitants is one of the city’s strongest presences. Everything seems prepared for arrival or recently abandoned, though nothing decays in the way abandoned things decay. There is no rot. No rust. No cloth. No ash heaps. No bones, except for mineral formations that imitate ribs and skulls without ever fully committing. The city is empty per volume, empty per street, empty per room. Its vastness is not crowded by history. It has structure without habitation.

At the outer edges, where the urban grid loosens, causeways extend across the salt flats toward isolated towers. These towers stand miles apart, each on a pedestal of eroded mineral, connected by roads so straight they seem drawn onto the world rather than built from it. Between them lies almost nothing: white crust, black stones, dry wind, rare clicking life under the surface. The openness there is immense. The city becomes a constellation of architecture, with silence as the dark between stars.

Storms come, but not with rain.

They begin as a darkening of the upper air, a slow inward folding of the sky. The temperature drops. Shadows sharpen. Then the wind arrives, carrying not sand but flakes of mica, salt needles, and thin black plates of exfoliated shale. The storm moves through the streets without roaring. It whispers against corners. It streams through colonnades, polishes walls, engraves lines into exposed surfaces. Visibility falls to a few steps, but the city’s outlines remain visible in the mind with unnatural clarity. During these storms, distant buildings appear nearby, and nearby walls become memories of walls.

Afterward the city is subtly changed. A staircase has gained three steps. A tower has lost its shadow. A plaza has rotated relative to the mountains. New cracks shine in the pavement. The crust-lichens expand by a hand’s breadth. The clicking animals emerge to harvest whatever the storm has uncovered. The tall ones are more numerous then, or more nearly visible, standing in intersections as if listening to the altered geometry.

Night is not much darker than day. The sky deepens to a smoky indigo, and stars appear in excessive numbers, clustered so thickly in some regions that they resemble salt spilled upward. But the stars do not hold their positions. They drift in architectural patterns, forming streets, courtyards, towers, then dissolving. Some match the city below too precisely. Looking up can give the impression that the basin is reflected overhead, or that the city on the ground is the reflection and the true one hangs beyond reach.

In the coldest hours, the stone speaks very softly. Not in language. Not even in voice. It ticks, settles, contracts, releases. Across miles of empty avenues, one can hear a sequence of minute adjustments passing through the city like thought through a sleeping mind. The sound is quiet enough that it might be imagined, but it has direction. It begins in the high terraces, travels through the central plazas, descends into the dry wells, and vanishes beneath the salt flats.

By morning, the city has endured another night of being itself.

It is a place of dryness so complete it has become metaphysical. A high, cool, dim basin where erosion has made architecture or architecture has consented to erosion. A settlement without technology, without inhabitants, almost without life, yet not still. It changes by mineral impulse, by wind, by salt pressure, by distortions in distance and time. It is empty in the way a mouth is empty just before speaking.

And always, beyond the last avenue, the cliffs stand in weathered tiers, holding the basin up against the thin violet sky. They are old beyond ruin. Their faces are carved with dry channels, abandoned roads, and openings that may be caves or windows. In the dim light they seem less like mountains than the outer walls of a much larger city, one in which this entire basin is only a courtyard, long unused, still active, and waiting without expectation.
