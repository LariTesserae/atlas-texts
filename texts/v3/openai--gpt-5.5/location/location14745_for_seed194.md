In a crack beneath an old salt flat, there is a city no wider than a buried room.

From above, nothing announces it. The surface is a pale, cold crust, nearly level in every direction, polished by ages of wind until it has become less a landscape than a decision the world made long ago and never revised. No stream has crossed it in geological memory. No rain has softened it. The salt lies in plates, shallow ridges, and blind polygonal scars. At night — and it is almost always night here, under cloud, ash, or stone — the flat gives back no gleam.

The entrance is a seam, hidden under a lip of mineral crust. It exhales no dampness. It smells of dust, resin, old paper, bitter roots, and something like extinguished incense.

Below is the greenless garden-city.

It is entirely plant, but not lush in the familiar way. Nothing here is wet, leafy, or tender. The vegetation is black, grey, bone-yellow, and deep matte violet, adapted to a world where water is a rumor from another age. Its tissues are not filled with sap so much as slow oils, waxes, mineral gels, and volatile salts. Its cells hold their shape with silica and lignin; its veins carry charge, scent, and powdered nutrients rather than flowing liquid. It feeds on stone, on trapped gases, on the faint chemistry of the salt, on the temperature difference between the upper crust and the deeper earth. It grows without sunlight. It does not photosynthesize so much as remember fire.

The city fills the chamber, yet the chamber feels empty.

That is the first contradiction of the place. Vegetation is everywhere: underfoot, overhead, in walls, bridges, towers, screens, ladders, shrines, gutters, doorways, niches, courtyards. But it occupies space as a skeleton does. Long ribs of root span the dark. Hollow trunks rise like minarets. Fibrous causeways cross open wells of air. Curtains of root-hair hang from vaults, sparse as old cobweb, each thread alive and listening. The living mass has learned thrift so completely that it builds with absence. Rooms are outlined rather than filled. Streets are the gaps between woody tendons. Plazas are not paved surfaces but resonant voids held in place by arches of patient growth.

It is urban, unmistakably. There are districts. There are avenues. There are little stacked chambers like dwellings, their thresholds grown into regular shapes by hands or by an intelligence close enough to hands. There are terraces, ramps, lookout holes, storage alcoves, signal posts, knots of public architecture. Some walls are woven from thousands of dry stems trained into bricklike courses. Others are slabs of fungal-dark bark pressed flat over centuries until they resemble leather masonry. At intersections stand posts carved with simple marks: scratches, dots, spiral grooves, thorn-script. Nothing is metal except rare black flakes of meteoric iron bound into tool edges and ceremonial hinges. Most craft here is older: bone-hard thorn awls, stone polishers, salt knives, cordage braided from root-fiber, baskets grown directly into shape and cut free when mature.

No animals live here. No insects worry the bark. No birds nest in the vaults. No worm has turned the dust. Decay is slow, chemical, almost legalistic. When a structure dies, it does not rot; it dries further, tightens, rings when struck, and is eventually absorbed by neighboring roots that rasp mineral from it grain by grain. The ecology is closed and severe. Nothing grazes, nothing hunts. There are no eyes, except the false eyes of seed pods and the polished sockets where tools have worn handholds into wood.

And yet the place is loud.

Not continuously loud in the way of machinery or crowds, but loud because every sound has nowhere soft to go. The dry city is an instrument. Wind from the upper seam moves through ducts grown for it, and the whole chamber hums. Hollow towers moan in different pitches. Seed capsules click and clatter against their cages. Tensile roots creak as they contract in the cool. When the temperature shifts by a fraction, entire neighborhoods answer with knocks, pops, groans, rattling cascades — slow architecture adjusting its bones.

Some avenues have chimes made of split stalks. Others have suspended plates of salt and bark that boom when touched by air. The loudest places are the old civic hollows, where the plant-builders shaped amphitheaters of root and resin around central emptiness. Stand there in the dark and the city seems to speak from all directions at once: a dry thunder of fibers, a chorus of wooden throats, a language too slow to be heard as language unless one waits for days.

Light is absent. Not dim — absent. The darkness has texture. It presses close around the face and fingers. Navigation is by touch, by vibration, by memory of surfaces. The floor-veins differ underfoot: smooth for main ways, ridged for side passages, thorned at the edges of forbidden chambers. The city’s oldest roads are polished not by feet, perhaps, but by centuries of palms, tendrils, ropes, and dragged tools.

The temperature is cool and stable. Nothing steams. Nothing sweats. The air is thin, dry, and faintly charged. Breath, if one brings breath into it, sounds indecently wet. The living structures seem to recoil from moisture, sealing pores, tightening membranes. Water here is not life but disaster. A spilled drop would travel farther than expected, chased by thirsty salts and alarmed root-hairs. Around any dampness, the city would wake defensively: wax would bloom, valves would shut, powdery spores would retreat into armored pods. This is a plant world that has survived by refusing the oldest bargain of plants.

Its cycles are almost still.

A gate may take forty years to close. A bridge thickens by the width of a fingernail in a human lifetime. Seed vaults open only when a particular mineral wind blows down from the salt flat, perhaps once in several centuries. There are calendars here, but they are grown into the walls as bands of density, colorless rings, arrangements of scars. Time is not counted in days. It is counted in contractions, husk-falls, the dulling of a resonance, the migration of salts from one root district to another.

The city is ancient beyond usefulness. Whatever founded it — gardeners, refugees, thinking plants, or some vanished dry civilization — has been eroded into the same final grammar. Sharp corners have become rounded. Decorative carvings have softened into tactile ghosts. Towers have slumped and re-rooted. Streets that once led somewhere now terminate in sealed bark. There are public squares too fragile to enter, their floors made of suspended fiber lace over black hollows. There are neighborhoods so old they have become geological: plant tissue mineralized into a kind of dark woodstone, still faintly alive at the edges.

Because it is low and buried, the place has no horizon. Its scale is intimate, hidden, almost pocketlike; yet inside, it suggests a whole metropolis miniaturized by pressure and time. One can cross from one boundary to another in minutes, but the layers make it feel endless. Every wall has another wall inside it. Every trunk is a tower containing stairs no wider than fingers. Every plaza has a lower plaza beneath, accessible through a throatlike opening. The city is not broad; it is folded.

There are laws here, though no one announces them.

Do not bring flame: the oils in the living walls remember combustion too well.  
Do not bring water: the city will seal against you.  
Do not cut a sounding-root unless another is already grown to take its pitch.  
Do not speak loudly in the seed districts, where vibration is inheritance.  
Do not trust an open doorway; some are mouths in the civic sense, meant to admit air, not bodies.  
Do not assume death has ended obligation.

At the center is the oldest empty place: a round chamber called, perhaps by later visitors, the Dry Well. It is not a well and never held water. It descends only a little, a shallow bowl lined with black root enamel. Around it, the entire city bends inward. Tubes, streets, bridges, acoustic horns, and root-canals all orient toward this central hollow. Here the sound is immense. A whisper becomes a bark. A footstep becomes a drum. When the wind passes overhead, the Dry Well answers with a sustained note so deep it is felt in the teeth.

In that darkness, surrounded by total vegetation that has become architecture, surrounded by emptiness shaped with civic care, it is possible to understand the place as a city built not for movement but for endurance. It does not flourish. It persists. It has taken the absence of water, sunlight, animals, and haste, and made from those absences a living urban mineral, a dry garden of rooms, instruments, and waiting roots.

It is not dead.

It is listening very slowly.
