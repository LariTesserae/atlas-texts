The place is a high, enclosed basin, not much larger than a ruined chapel, set into the shoulder of a black mountain. Its rim is a broken ring of basalt and pale saltstone, high enough to catch thin air, yet the hollow itself holds heat like a kiln. Darkness collects there even when the outer world is bright. The surrounding cliffs lean inward, and their undersides are glassy, soot-colored, reluctant to reflect light. What illumination exists comes as a dull mineral glow from seams in the ground: red at the cracks, violet in the salt, a faint bruise-colored sheen along the old walls.

There is no water. Not hidden, not seasonal, not remembered by the stones. The basin has the dryness of a sealed vessel left too long near a fire. Every surface is powdered, filmed, crusted, or polished by heat and dust. The floor is a packed mosaic of salt plates, black clinker, bone-colored mineral scales, and drifts of metallic grit that move slightly under pressure but never quite blow away. The air tastes alkaline and burnt. It pulls at the mouth and eyes. Any softness brought into this place would be claimed quickly: stiffened, salted, emptied.

No plants grow here. There are no roots probing cracks, no lichen, no moss, no tough little leaves crouched in shade. The ecology has found another foundation. The stones themselves wear dim living films, not green but gray, amber, and black-purple, as if the mineral surfaces have bruised. These films are not vegetation in any familiar sense. They are heat-fed skins, slow chemistries, patient stains that thicken where sulfur breath leaks from below. They grow in seams and under ledges, and much of the animal life grazes them down to a shine.

The place is crowded almost beyond belief.

At first, in the darkness, it seems empty because nearly everything here knows how to be still. Then the eye adjusts and the basin resolves into bodies. They occupy every ledge, crack, roof-shadow, salt ridge, fallen block, and hollow. Small plated things are fitted together like living gravel. Blind skitterers cling to vertical stone in overlapping layers. Pale, jointed animals hang under the basalt shelves, their limbs folded close to preserve whatever their bodies cannot afford to lose. Long, flat predators lie flush with the ground, indistinguishable from flakes of black glass until they move.

There is fauna in excess, but not in the riotous way of a forest. It is a compressed, withholding abundance. The animals here are dry-made: shells, plates, membranes, waxy hides, sealed eyes, mouthparts like needles or scrapers. Many are small enough to live between salt crystals. Others are cathedral-large only by comparison, the size of a kneeling person, but in this confined basin they feel immense. Their movements are economical. A leg unfolds, a jaw opens, a row of feelers trembles, and then stillness returns.

Sound is rare and precise. There is no water-noise, no leaves, no birdsong, no broad wind. The basin mostly listens to itself. Fine claws tick on salt. A shell scrapes softly against stone. Somewhere under a slab, many bodies shift at once with the faint sound of dry seeds being poured. The largest animals breathe through side-slits, a hushed accordioning. Occasionally the heat splits a mineral plate with a sharp, delicate crack, and every creature pauses as if the whole place has spoken.

The terrain is old enough to have settled into its own logic. Nothing looks fresh except the breaks made by heat. The cliffs have been gnawed smooth by thermal stress and dry abrasion. Salt has climbed and reclimbed the same edges until they are rounded like worn bone. The basin floor has sunk slightly in the center, forming a shallow bowl packed with life and mineral residue. Around the edges stand scattered structures: low rings of fitted stone, a few upright slabs, a collapsed arch that leads nowhere, cairns so fused by heat they might be natural. They do not dominate the place. They are interruptions, almost fossils of intention. No devices remain, no metal, no sign of working machinery or sustained craft. Only stones placed together long ago, or by something with a habit of arranging shelter.

The heat has cycles. It is not constant. From below come pulses, slow and deep, warming the floor until the salt plates exhale a bitter vapor. During these pulses the animals change position. Grazers rise from cracks and spread over the mineral films. Needle-mouthed hunters climb the low walls and wait above them. Thin winged things, too brittle-looking to fly in open air, loosen themselves from the shadowed ceiling and drift on convection, barely beating their translucent vanes. They do not sing. Their wings make a dry paper whisper.

When the heat drops, the basin contracts. Plates close. Burrows seal with living plugs. The larger predators slide into wall-gaps, leaving only their sensory filaments exposed. The films darken and harden. A different population emerges then: white threadlike crawlers, bead-bodied mites, blunt-headed scavengers that gather around shed skins, cracked shells, and the bodies of the unlucky. Nothing remains unoccupied for long. A bare patch of stone is an event, a vacancy that will be filled.

The rules of this world are strict.

Nothing wet survives openly. Any spilled fluid becomes salt, crust, food, signal. To be soft is to be temporary. To move too much is to announce oneself. To cast heat is to be found. Shade is not cool, only less exposed. Darkness is not emptiness, only cover. The ground is not inert; it feeds, records, and punishes. The animals seem to know the shape of every crack and the timing of every thermal breath. They navigate without sight, reading vibration, chemical trace, pressure, and the faint electrical moods of heated stone.

The basin has boundaries that matter. Beyond the rim the mountain continues upward into dark scree and thin, scorching air, but the life is concentrated inside the hollow, packed as if poured there and unable or unwilling to leave. The ring of cliffs traps mineral fumes. The old stonework creates ledges and pockets. The floor holds the deepest warmth. Every niche has been claimed for generations beyond counting. It feels less like a landscape one crosses than a single dense organ, a dry heart of the mountain, beating in heat instead of blood.

And despite the crowding, despite the constant hunting and grazing and hatching and molting, the place remains mostly quiet. Its violence is intimate. A grazer disappears beneath a hinged plate without a cry. A swarm changes direction as one body, silently avoiding a predator’s chemical shadow. Eggs glued under a salt lip split open in a neat row, releasing tiny armored lives already ready to hide.

The darkness makes the basin feel larger than it is. The heat makes it feel lower, buried, even though it sits high on the mountain. The absence of water and plants makes it seem dead at first, but that impression cannot last. Life is everywhere here, only stripped of lushness, stripped of generosity. It has become mineral, patient, sealed, and exact.
