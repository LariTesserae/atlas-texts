At the top of the manufactured mountain there is a room without a roof.

It is not a room in the ordinary sense, because its walls are not straight and its floor is not level, and because clouds pass through it slowly enough to touch. But it has boundaries. A person can walk its full length in a few minutes, from the west grate to the east parapet, from the hot pipe-bank to the hanging cisterns, and understand that beyond those limits the place stops being this place. Around it rises the larger machinery of the summit: black pylons, scaffolded ribs, pressure towers, stacked conduits, maintenance gantries bolted into one another like the bones of an unfinished animal. But here, within the small high enclosure, the world gathers itself into a wet, green, warm hollow.

Everything is made.

There is no soil that was not mixed, no stone that was not cast, no ledge that was not poured into a mold or extruded from a kiln-mouth below. The apparent cliff along the northern side, streaked with moss and dripping amber water, is a ceramic façade over a condensation wall. Its cracks are intentional, placed to accept roots. Its weeping is scheduled, though the schedule has become generous and inaccurate. The floor looks like dark basalt interrupted by seams of pale mineral, but the basalt is sintered slag, still faintly glossy in its sheltered hollows, and the mineral veins are capillary channels carrying nutrient brine. Even the little pools in the depressions have underlids: shallow basins of enamel and mesh, with valves at their throats.

The place is young. Not young as a garden replanted this season, but young as land before weather has learned what to do with it. Edges remain sharp under the moss. The steps have not been hollowed by feet. The artificial rocks carry no long memory of rain; their surfaces bead water too cleanly, too eagerly. In some corners the construction marks are still visible: blue chalk, stenciled numbers, a forgotten measuring rod sealed under a skin of algae. The terraces have not slumped. The little channels have not yet chosen their own routes. Water follows the paths given to it, for now.

It is warm at this height because the mountain is warm. Heat rises through it from boilers, turbines, fermentation vats, buried reaction chambers, and some deeper industrial heart whose nature is not visible from the summit garden. The warmth does not come as a wind but as a presence underfoot and behind walls. Metal railings sweat. Pipes exhale. The grates breathe upward, and where the breath meets the high air, mist forms in slow white folds.

Clouds arrive already tired from the slopes below. They drag themselves over the parapets and unravel among the pipework. Moisture condenses on every surface: on bronze valves, on black rubber collars, on the undersides of leaves, on the webbing between scaffold joints. Drops gather, tremble, and fall. They strike leaves with small soft sounds, tap on steel, tick into basins, slip down ceramic stone. The place is never loud, but it is never still. Its quiet is full of minor mechanisms and patient water.

A pump turns somewhere behind the wall with a slow, almost thoughtful pulse.

Hollow pipes click as they cool.

A hidden relay snaps once, then not again for a long time.

The vegetation has taken to the artificial mountain with unsettling confidence. It is lush, but not wild in the old sense. It grows from racks, pockets, suspended troughs, vertical seams, rotating drums, and gridded beds fed by translucent tubes. Ferns spill from the cracks of fabricated schist. Broad waxy leaves, bred for cloud capture, tilt upward to comb moisture from the passing vapor. Vines hang from the overhead catwalks and descend in curtains, each stem clipped at intervals to copper guides. Pale orchids root in ceramic sockets. Liverworts form dark wet maps along the lower panels. Moss thickens around bolt heads until the metal seems to have sprouted green knuckles.

There are dwarf trees too, though they are not old. Their trunks twist dramatically because they have been trained that way by wires now half swallowed into bark. Their canopies press close to the warm walls, leaves overlapping leaves, a crowding of green bodies in a chamber not quite large enough for them. The density makes the place feel larger than it is. One cannot see from one side to the other without looking through layers: leaf, mist, pipe, frond, hanging root, ladder, water thread, shadow, leaf again.

Small lamps are set into the structure, but in daylight they are mostly unnecessary. The light here is ordinary, or appears ordinary after one has accepted everything else. Sun reaches the enclosure through a high gap in the machinery and falls in square-edged patches, interrupted by rails and cable runs. It is a warm mountain sun, bright but softened by vapor. In the morning it turns the mist white-gold. At noon it makes the wet leaves glare. By late afternoon the whole room becomes greenish, as if submerged just below the surface of a pond.

The water has many forms.

There is the steady sweating of the condensation wall. There are the bead-strings along the vine wires. There are narrow runnels in the floor, clear enough to show the tiny regulating screws beneath them. There are two shallow pools, one round and one irregular, both no deeper than a hand, each with floating mats of duckweed bred to tolerate mineral heat. There are overhead gutters that release a brief rain every few hours, not a downpour but a rehearsed weather: first a shiver through the leaves as pressure changes, then a scatter of drops, then a minute of dense vertical falling, then steam where the water strikes warmer grates.

After each rain the room smells newly assembled.

Wet metal. Crushed fern. Hot ceramic. Mineral broth. Flowering vine. Ozone from the relay boxes. A faint sweetness from the fermentation vents below.

The ecology is young enough that it still shows its instructions. Plants are arranged by function as much as beauty. The upper leaves harvest cloud-water. The middle vines shade the basins. The mosses bind the artificial substrate. The root-mats clean overflow. Blue-green films digest residues along the warm channels. Pale fungal fans, thin as paper, grow behind grated access panels where the air stays damp and dark. Nothing is quite ornamental, yet the result is beautiful in the way of complicated survival.

Fauna is rare. Not absent, but sparse and secretive, as though animals have not yet received permission to live here.

Now and then a single black gnat moves in an erratic script above a pool. A small pale lizard, almost translucent, appears on the hot side of a pipe and vanishes into a seam. Once in a while a pollinating moth comes up from the lower greenhouses, battered by fans, and spends a day among the flowers before disappearing into the machinery. There are snails, but very few; their shells are tagged with tiny flecks of colored lacquer, and they seem less like inhabitants than test instruments. No birds nest here. The height and the metal discourage them. Sometimes one crosses overhead, a swift dark mark against the open sky, and its cry, if it gives one, sounds startlingly foreign.

Beyond the parapet the world falls away.

Not into wilderness, exactly, but into tiers of construction descending the mountain’s flank: more platforms, skeletal towers, steam plumes, crane arms, ribbed roofs, cableways, greenhouses clinging to the slope, reservoirs bright as mirrors. Far below, where clouds break, there may be a valley, but from this enclosure it is more rumor than view. The summit room feels suspended above its own foundation, a small wet organ grown at the top of an enormous engineered body.

The rules of the place are practical and peculiar.

Nothing may dry completely.

No root may enter an unlined conduit.

Overflow belongs first to the mosses, then to the polishing pools, then to the lower cisterns.

Mist is harvested before rain is requested.

Heat must never be vented all at once.

Flowers open according to pressure, not hour.

Decay is permitted only in trays.

The garden does not hide these rules. It displays them in gauges, color bands, moisture flags, transparent tubes where brown-green liquid moves with a slow intestinal patience. A red wheel near the west wall is marked EMERGENCY DRAIN, though lichen has softened the letters. Beside the round pool, a brass plaque lists the first planting sequence. Several names have corroded away, though the plaque cannot be more than a few years old. Corrosion here is fast, memory slow.

There is a sense that the place is still becoming real.

Each day water tests the seams. Each day roots find the difference between designed crack and accidental gap. The moss spreads past its assigned panels. Fern spores appear inside gauge housings. A vine has crossed from its copper guide to a steam pipe and wrapped it three times, tightening as it grows. The maintenance crew has tied a cloth around the pipe in surrender rather than cut the stem. In the irregular pool, mineral crust has begun to form along one edge, making a tiny white shelf no engineer drew. It is the first gesture of geology, delicate and premature.

Erosion has barely begun. The manufactured rocks have not softened, but they are listening. Water repeats itself against them. Algae darkens the corners. Fine sediment collects in seams, made from dust, pollen, powdered ceramic, insect bodies, decayed leaves, and the slow abrasion of the place by its own weather. Given enough time, the floor will forget its casting. The channels will clog and overflow. The wall will stain beyond its planned pattern. Roots will pry panels apart. The mountain will acquire accidents, then habits, then history.

For now it remains raw under its greenness: a new landscape wearing the first convincing layer of life.

The industrial mechanisms are not dominant in volume, but they are dominant in authority. They sit behind everything. A fern may hide a valve, but the valve decides when the fern drinks. A pool may reflect the sky, but its depth is governed by a float switch. Warmth comes from below; rainfall from above; fertility from side-fed tubes; drainage from grates; mist from the mountain’s breath. The vegetation is lush because the machine has made lushness possible, and because life, given warmth and wetness and a surface to grip, answers with excess.

Still, there are moments when the machine seems to be learning from the garden.

Condensation follows leaf edges better than pipe gradients. Moss seals hairline fractures more delicately than resin. Roots distribute water through a wall the way no schematic predicted. The valves have been adjusted to accommodate growth; the rails cut back; the walking path narrowed. The made world has begun to negotiate with the living one. Neither has won.

In the quiet after the scheduled rain, the whole enclosure steams gently. Drops fall less often. Leaves lift again. The lizard returns to its pipe. A gauge needle trembles and steadies. From below comes a low vibration, felt more in the teeth than heard. A cloud enters over the eastern parapet and loosens itself among the trees.

Standing there, one feels not that one is in nature, nor that one is outside it, but inside a draft of nature: a prototype of a marsh, a summit bog, a greenhouse, a shrine, a factory organ, a small high weather-engine teaching itself how to become a place.
