At the top of a warm, high plateau there is a hollow no larger than a ruined hamlet, a basin cupped in ocher stone and black volcanic glass. It has edges you can walk in an hour: a ring of low cliffs, a few tilted pillars, a broken stair cut by hand into the mineral slope. Beyond the rim the world falls away into dim yellow distance, all dust and height, but inside the hollow something like a forest persists.

There is no water here.

Not hidden water, not dew, not underground springs, not mist. The stones are dry through to their bones. If you split one, it gives off a powdery smell like old salt and heated clay. The air does not carry rain. It carries static, pollen, ash, powdered mica, and the metallic tang of sun-warmed minerals. The ground is crusted with pale salts and dark resinous stains. Every surface seems thirsty, yet nothing is withered.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost argumentative. It rises from cracks, bowls, ledges, and dry basins in extravagant sprays: jointed reed-things made of translucent horn, fan-shaped fronds like beaten copper, soft mats of violet filaments that never feel damp, only faintly oily. There are low shrubs armored in overlapping green-black scales. There are pale bladder-vines, swollen not with water but with warm gases, tugging upward against their roots and making the whole plant tremble. Some growths are not green at all: cinnabar coils, ivory combs, blue-black paddles, amber curtains of thread.

They feed on heat, salt, breath, and vibration. Their roots do not seek moisture but grip seams of mineral, dissolving stone with slow acids and drawing up metals in hair-thin channels. Their leaves are tuned membranes. When the wind crosses the hollow, the grove does not merely rustle; it plays. It shrieks, hums, clicks, drones, and whistles through a thousand dry throats. Seed-pods rattle like bones in baskets. Tall gas reeds boom softly, then suddenly bark. Flat fronds clap together in irregular applause. Even when nothing moves, the place murmurs with internal pressures: bladders expanding, fibers tightening, shells splitting, roots grinding deeper into rock.

The light is dim though the air is warm. A permanent veil hangs overhead, not cloud but dust and spore and electric grit, moving in slow bruised bands across the sky. The sun appears as a copper coin behind gauze. Shadows are soft-edged and uncertain. Colors do not blaze; they smolder. The whole hollow seems lit from the side by a failing furnace.

The terrain has been here a long time. It is not young chaos, not fresh ruin. The stone is rounded where feet, claws, wind, and root-acid have worked it for generations. The basin floor is worn into channels, though nothing liquid runs there. Instead, dry flows move through it: avalanches of husk, salt, pollen, powdered shell, shed skin. Low dunes of organic dust gather against stones and are later consumed by root-mats. The cliffs bear rippled scars where wind has planed them. Old terraces, perhaps made by hands, perhaps by the plants themselves, step around the inner wall.

There are structures, but they do not dominate. Three small huts lean beneath a shelf of rock, built from stacked slate, rib-bones, and woven reed-shells. Their roofs are weighted with stones against the constant gusts. A line of posts marks an old boundary, each post carved with simple signs: spirals, open palms, hooked birds, warnings shaped like mouths. There are shallow pits lined with ceramic, dry kilns perhaps, or offering basins. A low wall encloses nothing visible. A ladder made of knotted root descends into a fissure where warm air exhales with a smell of pepper and iron.

Whoever lives or once lived here uses hand tools. Obsidian scrapers, bone awls, stone hammers, cordage braided from plant-fiber, bowls fired in covered pits. Metal is rare: a few beaten copper hooks, a dull bronze bell hung from a frame, green with age. The bell rings often, though no hand touches it. The wind rings it, or insects strike it, or the ground itself shivers.

Fauna is abundant, too abundant for such dryness, but it obeys the same strange economy. There are little lacquer-backed animals that skate across salt crusts on many legs, scraping mineral bloom into their mouths. Glass-winged beetles gather on the horn-reeds and tune them by chewing tiny holes in their flanks. Long-bodied lizards with feathered nostrils bask on black stones, then leap at passing moths with startling violence. Pale rodents, almost translucent, live inside the root mats and drink nothing; they gnaw resin and lick alkali from stones. Their eyes are huge in the dimness.

Overhead, narrow birds circle within the basin, never rising far above the rim. Their wings are ragged and almost leaflike. They dive through curtains of pollen and emerge dusted gold. When they call, the sound is not birdsong but a rasping metallic laugh. At dusk they cling upside down to the bladder-vines, puncturing them delicately and inhaling the gas. Then they become buoyant and drift in their sleep, tethered by their claws.

There are larger things as well, though the hollow is small. A slow ungulate with a head like a shovel moves between the growths, cracking salt plates with its hooves. Its hide is patched with symbiotic moss that is not moss, a dry velvet of mineral-eating threads. Swarms of tiny red mites follow it, cleaning the velvet and singing in a high, almost electrical hiss. Sometimes something beneath the ground knocks back at their song.

The place is loud because everything here communicates by impact, pressure, scrape, resonance. There is no soft water-noise to cover or soothe it. Sound travels hard through dry air and harder through stone. The basin is a bowl, and the bowl amplifies everything. A falling husk cracks like pottery. A lizard’s claws are a rapid ticking. The wind turns the plant-growth into an orchestra of alarms. Insects pulse in dense waves. Burrowing things drum messages through the roots. The cliffs answer with echoes until even a footstep seems to become part of a larger argument.

And the place is turbulent. Not in the way of rainstorms or rivers, but in ceaseless dry convulsion.

Warm updrafts climb from vents in the basin floor. Cold dust spills over the rim and is shredded by rising heat. Static gathers on the fronds until sparks jump from plant to plant, blue-white and silent for an instant before the grove erupts in popping seed-fire. Pollen storms begin without warning. One moment the air is merely dim; the next it is thick with ocher grains, spinning in columns, tasting bitter on the tongue. Animals vanish into burrows and crevices. Gas-vines swell, lift, tear free, and tumble upward like living balloons until hooked birds slash them open. Their ruptures make a sound like drums splitting.

Some plants grow visibly during these events. A patch of violet filament will thicken, knot, and raise little stalks in the span of an afternoon, then dry into brittle lace by evening. Horn-reeds shed their outer skins in sheets that flutter across the basin and snag on the huts. Resin flowers open only when the static is strongest, exposing dark throats lined with crystal teeth. They do not receive bees. They receive sparks. Each spark triggers a puff of spores and a smell like burnt honey.

The rules of this hollow are strict, though not obvious at first.

Do not bring open flame near the amber curtains; they remember heat and return it violently.

Do not sleep on bare salt, because the salt blooms upward at night and seals around warm flesh.

Do not cut a gas-vine below its third knot; it will scream through the wound, and every burrowed thing in the basin will answer.

Do not speak loudly beneath the copper fans when the sky is red. They imitate voices badly, then continue using them after the speaker has gone.

Do not break the black stones with white rings. They are eggs, or graves, or both.

At the center of the hollow stands the oldest growth: a sparse grove of seven tall mineral trees, widely spaced, each one twisted as if shaped by centuries of wind. Their trunks are matte silver-gray, not bark but layered ceramic. From their crowns hang dry green streamers and clusters of hollow pods. The ground beneath them is swept clean by constant vibration. Nothing small remains there long; seeds, dust, bones, tools, and shed skins all tremble outward in slow circles.

The people, if they are still people, seem to have organized their lives around these seven trees. There are handprints on the trunks, made in red ocher and black ash. Small bundles hang from lower branches: carved stones, knotted cords, lizard skulls, folded fans of plant hide. A circle of sitting stones faces inward. The stones are polished by use. In the middle is a shallow depression filled with pale powder that shifts even when the air is still.

At certain hours the seven trees resonate together. It begins as a pressure behind the teeth. Then the pods answer one another in tones too low to be called music, and the entire basin changes behavior. Insects freeze. Birds land. The shovel-headed grazer kneels. The bladder-vines deflate slightly, as though listening. The huts creak. Dust lifts from the ground in delicate standing waves. For a few minutes the loudness becomes order: drone, tremor, click, pulse, hiss, all aligned.

Then one pod cracks.

A new animal may fall out, wet with resin but not water. Or a bundle of seeds. Or a stone tool already shaped. Or a scrap of something like skin bearing symbols no one carved. The basin accepts these things without surprise. They are taken up, eaten, planted, worn, feared, or worshiped according to their behavior.

Nothing here is stable in the short term, yet the whole has the composure of an ancient system. It is turbulent the way a hive is turbulent, or a market, or a throat speaking continuously in a language older than its speakers. Things burst, molt, harden, drift, burrow, and sing themselves apart. But the cliffs remain. The dry basin remains. The seven trees remain. The air remains warm and dim and full of motion.

From the rim, looking down, it might seem sparse: a small hollow, a few structures, wide spaces of pale mineral ground between clusters of growth. But inside those spaces, every grain is involved. The emptiness is not absence. It is room for sound to travel, for spores to spin, for animals to dart, for shadows to detach and become wings.

It is a lush place without softness, a garden without water, a dry lung high above the world, breathing dust and heat and noise.
