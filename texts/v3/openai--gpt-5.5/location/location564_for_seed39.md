At this height the world has not yet learned how to be old.

It is a high, open basin cupped between black shoulders of unfinished mountains, so elevated that the air feels planed thin. Breath comes with a small metallic taste. The sky sits close, not bright but not fully overcast either: a dim, cold lid of moving vapor, silver-gray in the daytime and blue-black at evening. Light arrives strained through cloud and suspended moisture, spread thinly over stone, snow patches, and shallow water. Nothing casts a strong shadow for long.

The terrain is raw. Not ruined, not heavily eroded, but newly exposed: a landscape just assembled from frost, uplift, ice, and fracture. Ridges stand with their edges still sharp. Slabs of dark volcanic rock jut at angles as if they have recently cooled, though they are ancient enough to hold lichen on their windward faces. Pale seams of quartz and feldspar run through them like unfinished wiring. Gravel fans lie at the mouths of narrow gullies, but the gullies are short, tentative, not yet carved into confident channels. Everywhere the ground has the feeling of first draft: cracked plates, fresh scree, rounded glacial lumps, raw clay, stone flour, moraine ridges not yet softened by grass.

Water is everywhere without making the place lush. It sits near the surface, glints in hoofprints, trembles under skins of new ice, threads through moss-dark cracks, and seeps from between stones with a constant patience. Snowfields cling to the shaded hollows above the basin, and their melt descends in many small ways rather than one grand river: rills, trickles, wet stains, hidden under-gravel flows, drips from overhanging rock lips. The rocks weep even when the sky is clear. After rain, the whole basin appears varnished. After frost, every wet edge carries a white crust of needles.

There are pools too shallow to be called lakes, cold tarns cupped in clay and stone, their surfaces dim as lead. Some are clear enough to show silt slowly shifting underneath. Others are cloudy with glacial flour, a suspended blue-white opacity that makes them look deeper than they are. Around their margins the mud is soft but not fertile, gray and granular, pressed by thousands of animal tracks that fill and refill with water.

Vegetation survives in fragments. It does not cover the land; it signs its name where it can. Low mats of sedge occupy the wettest rims. Black-green moss thickens in seeps and beside the warmest stones. Lichens make coin-sized colonies on boulders, orange, ash, sulfur-yellow. Dwarf willow creeps flat against the ground, its stems no higher than a hand, leaves small and hard as if hammered from dull metal. In summer, if the season can be called summer, tiny flowers appear with startling color—purple saxifrage, white stars, a few yellow cups—then vanish quickly into seed and frost.

The emptiness is immense. Not barren exactly, because much is happening, but spacious to the point of austerity. A person standing here would feel very visible and very small. Between one boulder and the next there may be a hundred paces of wet gravel. Between one tuft of grass and the next, open stone. The basin extends beyond the immediate eye, folding into adjacent shelves and saddles, each one similar but not identical: pools, snow tongues, rock ribs, seep-lines, bare ground. It is a regional emptiness, large enough to walk for days without finding shelter, road, wall, fire ring, tool mark, or cut branch.

There is no construction at all. No hut. No cable. No fence. No cairn placed by a hand. No track except those made by hooves, claws, pads, bellies, and wings. If there is industry here, it is not human.

Yet the place has an industrial character, not because it contains machines, but because it operates like one. The mountain is a cold engine. Ice stores weight all night and releases water by day. Meltwater disappears beneath scree and reemerges under pressure, pulsing from vents in the rock. Freeze and thaw work like pistons, widening cracks grain by grain. Wind combs the basin in steady passes, sorting dust, drying ridges, polishing exposed stone. Gravity moves loose material downslope in small deliveries: a pebble clicking, a sheet of scree settling, a snow cornice slumping with a low internal boom.

At certain hours, especially when the temperature hovers around thaw, the whole basin seems to run. Not loudly, but with many processes layered together: dripping, ticking ice, the fine hiss of meltwater under stones, the faint grinding of suspended sediment in channels too small to see. Fumarolic breath may rise from a few warm seams along the upper slopes, not dramatic, just pale vapor feathering into the cold air with a smell of minerals and wet iron. The sound is mostly quiet, but never fully still. It is the quiet of a great facility operating behind thick walls.

Animals are unexpectedly abundant. They do not fill the air with noise, but they are everywhere if one learns how to look. Herds of mountain goats or wild sheep cross the high shoulders, pale bodies moving slowly over black rock. Their paths are the oldest lines in the basin, older than any human road would have been. Marmots whistle from stone piles, then vanish into crevices. Pikas harvest the sparse plants into hidden haystacks beneath boulders. Ptarmigan, mottled gray and white, crouch so still they seem like loose stones until they erupt at one’s feet.

Along the wet margins, life gathers densely for brief periods. Midges rise in wavering columns whenever the wind drops. Beetles crawl under flat rocks slick with condensation. Long-legged shorebirds pick through the mud, their calls thin and widely spaced. Ravens travel the basin in pairs, inspecting tracks, carcasses, exposed nests, whatever the cold has preserved. High above, raptors use the dim updrafts along the cliffs, patient as weather.

Predation is part of the movement here, but it is not theatrical. A fox follows the sedge-line with its nose low. An eagle’s shadow passes once over a pool and every small bird flattens into silence. Something dead beneath a snowbank feeds ravens, insects, and meltwater all at once. In the cold, nothing rots quickly; bodies become temporary structures, nutrient islands in a land with little soil. A carcass may matter for years.

The atmosphere is saturated and cold enough that moisture behaves almost like another mineral. Fog condenses on stone. Rime grows on sedge blades. Breath hangs low. The undersides of overhangs glitter with tiny ice beads even while water drips from their lips. Sound is softened by damp air and open distance. A falling stone can be heard far away, a single clean strike followed by smaller clicks. Wingbeats over water sound close, then vanish into the basin’s breadth.

The rules of this world are simple and severe.

Water is wealth, but warmth is scarce.  
Soil is beginning, not established.  
Shelter exists only where rock happens to lean.  
Every living thing must stay low, move seasonally, or borrow heat from the bodies of others.  
Decay is slow; erosion is slower still; but freezing works constantly.  
Nothing grows tall because the wind will not permit height.  
Nothing remains dry for long because the mountain sweats from within.

By afternoon the basin darkens without becoming night. Cloud descends until the upper ridges are erased. The snowfields fade into the sky, and the pools become the brightest things in the land, dull mirrors holding the last light. Animals move more confidently in the dimness. Hooves tap on stone. A marmot gives one sharp alarm and is answered by nothing. Water continues its hidden work.

Then cold reasserts itself. Thin ice forms first at the pool edges, then over hoofprints, then across the smallest runnels. The wet ground stiffens. Drips slow. Vapor thickens in the hollows. The natural engine does not stop, but its rhythm changes: from flow to pressure, from seep to crystal, from liquid motion to the silent expansion of ice inside stone.

By morning, a few more grains will have loosened. A crack will be wider by less than the width of a hair. A sedge root will have held one pinch of soil against being washed away. A bird will have taken an insect from the mud. A seep will have opened under a new lip of frost.

The place is still being made, and almost everything here is either waiting, feeding, freezing, or beginning.
