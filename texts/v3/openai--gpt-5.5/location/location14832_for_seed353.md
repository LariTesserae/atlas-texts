At this height the air has almost nothing left in it.

It is not thin in the clean alpine sense, not blue, not sharp with snow or distance. It is thin like an exhausted breath, hot and mineral, stripped of moisture so completely that exposed metal never rusts, only powders; that cut surfaces do not rot, only seal; that anything once wet has become legend, sacrament, engineering error. The plateau lies under a sky without stars, a dark copper-black vault pressed low by heat. Day and night have lost their useful meanings. There is only the long dimness, the baked dark, the dull interior glow of furnaces hidden behind leaves.

The whole region is plant.

Not forest, not jungle, not meadow. Those are water-words. This is something older and drier: a vegetation that learned to become architecture, industry, road, wall, scaffold, archive. It covers the land so completely that there is no visible soil. There may be stone far beneath, some original highland of basalt or salt or dead reef lifted into the sky before memory, but no one could point to it. Every surface is root, bark, fiber, husk, resin, pith, thorn, plate, woven tube, seed-casing, lignified membrane. The ground flexes faintly underfoot because it is not ground but the backs of enormous flattened stems, centuries layered over centuries, compressed into a living pavement.

The plants are black, umber, ocher, iron-red, smoke-green where there is younger growth, and silver-white where salts have bloomed from them. Their leaves are not leaves in any tender sense. They are shutters, scales, radiators, kiln-vanes. They overlap in dense ranks, angled to drink heat instead of light, opening and closing with the slow precision of factory louvers. Some are translucent and show inner lattices like stained horn. Some are matte and soft-looking until touched, when they ring faintly like ceramic. Most have no visible pores. They do not transpire. They hoard.

Nothing here wastes vapor because there is no vapor to waste.

Their chemistry is sealed inside them: bitter oils, molten waxes, volatile solvents, brines locked into cells so tight they might as well be gemstones. Where other worlds have sap, this place has resin under pressure, dark and hot enough to smoke when exposed. The ancient plants feed on heat gradients, on minerals drawn upward through capillary roots miles deep, on the electric unrest of the plateau, on carbon dust and sulfurous air. In the darkest hollows, they grow pale panels that catch infrared from the buried machinery. In the open expanses, they raise forests of thermal fins.

The place appears built because it is built. It has been built for so long that construction and growth are no longer separate acts.

There are avenues, although no animals walk them. There are towers, although nothing inhabits them in the animal way. There are bridges, ramps, viaducts, cistern-like vaults that hold no water, chimneys that exhale no flame but waves of odorless heat. The entire plateau is an industrial botanic metropolis, assembled by slow directives encoded in root and cambium and reinforced by engines swallowed into the living mass. Some engines are still recognizable: flywheels half-buried in bark, piston-housings webbed with tendrils, rotary presses made of bronze-green vine and blackened ceramic. Others are no longer machines in any human sense. They are organs of production: pulse-columns, compression groves, seed foundries, resin refineries, thorn extruders.

At intervals, tremendous trunks rise like factory stacks, fluted and ribbed, their sides plated with grown armor. They are not round. They have corners, seams, service ridges, balconies grown for vanished workers or for no workers at all. Around their bases cluster buttresses and pipe-roots so densely interlocked that the eye cannot find an empty shadow between them. Every gap has been claimed by some filament, brace, seed-chain, tendril-cable, or fan of dark foliage. Space is scarce. Even the air seems occupied by drifting strands of dry pollen, by suspended threads, by heat mirage, by the invisible architecture of scent and static.

Yet the region is open. It extends outward in every direction, town-sized, valley-sized, too broad to take in from any one height. From a ridge of petrified root one can look across miles of crowded plant-factory and see it rolling away in tiers, courts, black orchards, ribbed roofs, thornfields, latticed towers, conveyor-vines, heat-sails, all under the lightless sky. The terrain was once mountainous, perhaps. Erosion has made it patient. Peaks have been shaved down to long shoulders. Cliffs have softened into terraces. Ancient ravines are filled with woven vegetable masonry, their old courses visible only as darker lanes where the growth sinks and thickens. Everything has settled into a final, deeply aged form, then refused finality.

Because it is turbulent.

The motion is constant, though rarely loud. Whole districts rearrange themselves without wind. A boulevard of bark may buckle upward over an hour, become a wall, split into panels, expose a red inner framework, and then close again as if nothing happened. Root-rails creep along their sockets. Thorn racks rotate. Seed pods pass from one cradle-vine to another in long chains, each transfer exact and nearly silent. Far off, a tower sheds its outer skin in black curls the size of sails; before the pieces reach the ground, smaller plants have caught them, shredded them, sorted them, fed them into grinders of living toothless wood.

The quiet makes the activity more unsettling.

There is no birdsong, no insect buzz, no scurry in the dry litter. No animal trails, no nests, no bones except perhaps the oldest ones, mineralized and absorbed into wall panels. The only sounds are structural: clicks from contracting pods, the leathery clap of heat-vanes closing, faint crackles inside resin conduits, low subaudible hums from buried dynamos, a distant shudder when a pressure trunk vents into another chamber. Sometimes an entire acre trembles with silent labor, and the dust above it lifts a finger-width from the ground, hangs, and settles.

The darkness is not empty. It has grain. It is full of near-forms, hot silhouettes, veined surfaces barely outlined by a red glow seeping through fissures. There are furnace flowers far below the canopy, blooms the size of rooms whose petals are dull carbon outside and incandescent within. They open only into enclosed courts, never to the sky. Their light shines upward through slatted leaves and woven rafters, cutting the gloom into bars. In some districts the plants have grown mirrors from mineral skin to redirect this buried glow, and the reflections move slowly over the walls like exhausted searchlights.

Heat gathers everywhere. It radiates from the pavement, from the trunks, from sealed tanks where resin ferments under pressure. The air tastes of pepper, tar, alkali, scorched rope, old dust. A hand placed on any surface comes away warm. Some surfaces are too hot to touch. Others are strangely cool, not because they contain water, but because their internal chemistry eats heat while changing phase, storing it for later mechanical work. Leaves snap open after absorbing enough thermal load and drive small turbines with sudden dry sighs. Seed capsules cook for decades before splitting.

When they split, the plateau changes.

A pod the size of a kiln will open soundlessly along pre-cut seams, revealing rows of hard black seeds nested in asbestos-like fiber. Mechanical vines descend from overhead racks and select among them. Some seeds are carried into drill-mouths that bore through existing plant-masonry. Some are pressed into cracks and welded in place with resin. Some are rejected and ground into fuel. Others are flung upward by spring-stems into the hot dark, not to travel on wind, for the air barely moves, but to arc across courtyards and land in assigned receptors waiting open like mouths.

There is intention here, but not haste. Or rather, there are many hastes nested inside one immense patience.

The plateau is ancient beyond ruin. It has outlasted rain. It has outlasted the species that built the first machines, if they were species at all and not the plants themselves in some earlier, more exploratory phase. It has outlasted rivers whose beds are now corridors packed with root-cable. Time has abraded all ornament down to function, and function has grown ornament again by repetition. A valve repeated ten thousand times becomes a frieze. A cooling fin becomes a grove. A waste chute becomes a canyon lined in glossy black bark. Old industry has been digested and re-expressed as botany; old botany has become infrastructure.

Nothing looks new, but everything is being made.

There are fields of vertical blades that turn without wind, their edges rasping so softly the sound is felt in the teeth. There are low basins full of curled plant-metal shavings, where young shoots sort mineral dust by charge. There are dry canals carrying not water but chains of pellets, spores, capsules, powdered salts, and bricklike wafers of compressed fiber. There are warehouses grown as ribcages, their interiors dense with hanging sacks that inflate and deflate according to obscure schedules. There are plazas completely roofed over by interlocking leaves, where the temperature climbs high enough to blur vision and where red resin crawls in gutters like slow lava.

At the plateau’s outer margins, the constructed vegetation meets the eroded highlands and overtakes them. The old slopes are low and rounded, their stone hidden under a hide of braided root. Salt flats lie beyond, pale under the dark sky, but even there the plant has advanced in mats and hexagonal plates, anchoring itself in mineral crust. It does not need streams. It sends taproots through fractures deep enough to reach ancient trapped chemistries, or it manufactures what it needs from heat, dust, and pressure. In places, the salt has been harvested into white columns, bundled with black vine, and built into pale towers that glow faintly from stored charge.

There is weather, but not as weather is usually understood. No rain comes. Clouds, if they exist, are high smears of metallic vapor, invisible until a red furnace-bloom catches them from beneath. Storms are electrical and dry. They crawl over the plateau as fields of tension. Before one arrives, the plant-city tightens. Leaves fold. Spines align. Tall stacks lower their crowns. The air becomes rigid. Then blue-black sparks leap across the higher vanes, almost silent in the thin air, and whole districts rearrange their circuits. Growth surges. Factories flower. Old sections die back in minutes, carbonizing into useful panels. New conduits push through them while they are still hot.

The dead are not removed. They are incorporated.

This is one of the rules of the place. There is no decay in the moist, animal, fungal sense. There is only conversion. A broken stem becomes beam. A failed tower becomes quarry. A spent leaf becomes insulation. A misgrown root is not waste unless it cannot be burned, powdered, laminated, or used to teach the next root where not to go. Memory is structural. History is load-bearing. Mistakes remain visible as knots, scars, obsolete junctions, blind stairways, sealed hatches, bridges leading into walls of later growth.

Another rule: no open interior remains open for long.

Any hollow larger than a hand will be measured by tendrils. Any corridor not heated, used, or defended will be filled with ribbing. Any abandoned chamber will sprout sorting hairs, then storage bladders, then a lattice, then become inaccessible except as a denser patch in the architecture. The landscape hates vacancy. It answers emptiness with manufacture. Even darkness is thickened here, crosshatched by branches and cables until it feels woven.

And another rule: sound is expensive.

Machines do not roar unless they are failing. Vents muffle themselves through fiber baffles. Gears turn in resinous sleeves. Presses close slowly, with the gravity of jaws in a dream. When something makes a loud noise, the whole district responds. Louvers snap shut. Needles rise. Heat-flowers dim. The silence afterwards is attentive and predatory, though there are no predators.

No animal could live here for long. There is nothing soft enough, wet enough, forgiving enough. The air would desiccate lungs. The heat would cook blood. The chemistry would poison hunger. The vegetation has no need to lure, feed, frighten, or collaborate with fauna. Its flowers are signal organs, not invitations. Its fruits are components. Its seeds are machine parts with embryos inside, or embryos that have learned to behave like machine parts.

Still, it is not dead.

It is extravagantly alive, but alive in a way that gives no comfort. Life here is pressure, design, repetition, repair. Life is a black root gripping a gear until the gear becomes a joint. Life is a leaf array computing the night’s heat. Life is a kilometer of thorned cable tightening in unison to rotate a tower three degrees toward a warmer darkness. Life is the continual refusal of desolation by something that has made desolation its medium.

At times the plateau seems to breathe. Not with lungs, not with vapor, but with coordinated expansion. The packed surface rises imperceptibly. Panels tilt. Shafts open. Warm air passes through hidden flues, carrying dust and the bitter scent of awakened resin. Then everything contracts. Far away, a line of towers bows inward like grass under wind, though no wind is present. The motion travels across the region in a slow wave. Under the dark sky, in the hot thin air, the entire constructed plant-world flexes its ancient body and continues its work.

If there is a center, it is not obvious. The landscape offers many false centers: a ring of tallest stacks around a red-lit pit; a dry canal hub where pellet-streams converge; a terrace of huge sealed blossoms facing one another like council chambers; a mountain stump wrapped in root and engine, humming with buried charge. But every center feeds another. Every tower is both organ and scaffold. Every road is both vein and conveyor. The place is less a city than a single plant that learned urbanism, or an industry that learned to grow, or the remains of a civilization whose infrastructure kept obeying instructions after all citizens became unnecessary.

The darkness never lifts. The heat never breaks. The plateau does not sleep.

It continues to build itself out of itself, quietly, densely, turbulently, upon the ancient flattened heights where water is gone and space is nearly gone and yet life has filled everything.
