At low elevation, where the land has almost forgotten how to rise, there is a wide drowned plain of stone.

It is not a marsh in the green sense. Nothing here flowers, roots, seeds, or leafs. The ground is mineral and old beyond storytelling: slate rubbed flat as worn bone, black shelves of basalt, low plates of limestone polished by ages of water moving back and forth over them. The whole region seems planed down to its final patience. Ridges are rare and humbled. What passes for a hill is only a long, shallow swelling under the skin of the plain, like a buried animal too large to uncover.

Water is everywhere, though seldom deep. It lies in seams, mirror-pools, ankle channels, cold pans, and long trembling gutters between stone ribs. It seeps upward from hairline fractures. It sweats from the undersides of overhangs. It collects in the dimples of ancient tool marks and the hollows of vanished shells. After rain — and rain comes often, fine and needling — the entire surface gleams as if glazed. Even when the sky clears, condensation keeps forming on the rocks, beading and slipping, so that the place appears to weep continuously without grief.

The light is low and uncertain. Not night, not proper day. A ceiling of cloud hangs close, bruised gray and blue-black, thick enough to muffle the sun into a pale coin. Distance loses color early. Far shelves of stone fade into silver vapor. The horizon is not sharp but dissolved, as though the world has been soaked too long and its edges have begun to loosen.

There are no trees to measure scale, no grass to show wind, no moss to soften the old fractures. The openness is therefore strange: broad, exposed, and yet difficult to read. You can see a long way across the flats, past scattered pools and ridges and the dark mouths of sinkholes, but there are few landmarks. Here and there stand small structures, all low to the ground, built from what the plain allows: stacked slabs, whale-like ribs of drifted bone or mineralized cartilage, walls mortared with gray clay, conical cairns with thumb-sized holes left between stones. Some are shelters. Some are traps. Some are older than any current hand and may never have been meant for human bodies at all.

The workmanship is simple but exact. No metal shines except occasional dull copper-green stains on stone. The tools used here are chipped flint, basalt hammers, bone awls, woven gut-cord, fired-clay weights. Low pens have been made in tidal channels, crescent walls that guide small armored creatures into holding pools when the water withdraws. Flat stones are propped into listening screens. Shell bowls are wedged under dripping rock to catch mineral water that forms a pale crust around their rims. There are marks cut into certain slabs — tally lines, weather signs, migration counts, warnings — but the rain has blurred many of them into soft scars.

The place is sparse in objects, but not empty.

It is loud.

At first the sound seems like weather: a vast, continuous rush, as if an unseen sea is breaking just beyond the fog. Then the layers separate. Water clicks and slaps in thousands of shallow basins. Thin waterfalls fall from ledges into stone bowls with hard, bell-like notes. Wind moves through holes in the cairns and abandoned traps, making them moan, whistle, and drone. Beneath that is the living noise: scraping, clacking, coughing, piping, shell-rattling, throat-bellowing, the hiss of many bodies dragging themselves over wet rock.

The fauna here is abundant because the stone feeds them, though not in any green way. Life clings to chemistry. In the warmest cracks — warm only by comparison — pale films grow slick over iron-rich seepage: not plants, not quite fungi, more like living tarnish or bacterial skin. The animals graze them with rasps, tongues, comb-feet, abrasive lips. There are flat gray mollusk-things the size of shields, each one patterned like a map of dried riverbeds. There are translucent eels in the fissure pools, their organs visible as slow blue sparks. There are jointed mud-runners that move in sudden synchronized bursts, hundreds at a time, like spilled beads finding a slope. There are blind, heavy-headed amphibians that breathe through frilled collars and sing so loudly at pressure changes that the very puddles tremble.

Predators move among them. Long-backed skulkers, slick as wet rope, wait under shelf edges and strike at the grazing herds. Hook-beaked birds — if they are birds — nest in the holes of the old cairns, though they have no feathers, only layered scales and ragged throat sacs. They scream constantly, disputing territory, warning of tides, announcing carrion. On dim afternoons their cries gather into a tearing canopy of sound.

There are larger presences too, seldom seen clearly. Out on the flatter reaches, the pools sometimes empty all at once as something passes beneath the surface-stone, drawing water down through hidden veins. The ground knocks. Rings spread across puddles. The shell-grazers freeze, then begin their clattering flight toward higher slabs. Local people, when there are people here, do not speak during these passages. They crouch on stone and keep their hands out of the water.

The world has rules, and they are learned by listening.

When the cairns begin to hum in a low register, fog is coming inland.

When the blue eels rise to the surface and knot together, the seep-water will turn bitter by morning.

When the blind singers go silent, something large is hunting.

When the rock sweats warm rather than cold, no fire should be lit; gases gather under the flats and will bloom into pale flame if invited.

Rain does not simply fall here. It activates the plain. Dry channels become silver cords. Inert-looking crusts swell and darken. Eggs hidden in mineral cracks soften open. Buried animals push up through slurry, unfold, feed, mate, vanish. The whole ecosystem moves in pulses governed by water pressure, moon-drag, and the slow breathing of underground aquifers. A pool that is empty at dawn may be boiling with life by dusk. A channel safe to cross in the morning may, by evening, be a throat.

Despite all this motion, the age of the place dominates. Everything active seems temporary compared with the stone. Bones are ground to powder. Shelters sink. Marks weather away. Species arrive in swarms and disappear into the cracks. The plain remains, nearly flat, deeply worn, receiving rain after rain.

At the center of one broad basin stands a ring of short pillars, each no taller than a crouching person. They are not natural, though time has tried to make them so. Their tops are cupped from long use. Water gathers there, black and reflective. Around them lie discarded tools: snapped scrapers, perforated stones, cracked bowls, old cords turned stiff with mineral deposits. The ring is positioned where seven channels meet, and when the water runs high, each channel pours into the basin at a different pitch. Together they produce a deafening chord, a steady cold roar that vibrates in the chest and teeth.

No one knows whether the pillars were built to make that sound, measure it, worship it, or survive it.

In the dimness, with the animals calling and the pools shivering and the stone dripping from every edge, the place feels less like a landscape than an exposed organ of the earth: ancient, wet, mostly mineral, but not dead. It has outlived forests. It has outlived names. It continues by seep, scrape, echo, hunger, and rain.
