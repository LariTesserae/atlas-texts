Below the surface there is a city too old to look ruined.

It lies in a hollowed stratum of pale stone, a buried basin the size of several valleys laid end to end. The ceiling is not low, not even visible in many places; it withdraws upward into brightness, a white mineral glare that seems less like sunlight than like memory of sunlight stored in the rock. Long seams of translucent crystal run overhead and through the far walls, lit from within by slow chemical fires. They give the underworld a cold, even radiance. There are almost no shadows except in holes and doorways, and even those are grey rather than black.

The air is cool and damp in pauses. It does not rain often, but moisture is always finding a way to appear. Condensation gathers on lintels, on the undersides of bridges, on the smooth backs of statues worn nearly faceless. Water beads along ropes of calcite and falls in widely spaced drops. In certain districts it collects in shallow basins cut into the floor, greenless pools rimmed with salt and white mineral crust. Elsewhere narrow channels run through the streets, not flowing continuously but waking in pulses when some hidden reservoir above exhales.

There are no plants. Nothing rooted, nothing leafed, nothing softening the masonry. The city has no gardens, no moss in cracks, no grass reclaiming old courts. Its surfaces are mineral, bone, hide, clay, shell, soot, and worked stone. Color is sparse: chalk white, old ivory, grey-blue slate, damp black, rust-red stains where primitive metal has bled into the walls. The absence of vegetation gives every space a stripped clarity. Streets open into plazas that feel like dried cisterns. Towers rise like eroded stalagmites, their windows small and irregular, as if chipped out by patient animals rather than planned by architects.

Yet the place is inhabited everywhere.

The fauna here is abundant, but it is a quiet abundance. Pale jointed creatures move along walls with the care of priests. Blind pack-beasts, low and seal-skinned, haul sledges over stone runners polished by centuries of use. Thin-legged scavengers pick through shell heaps and refuse pits without cries. Moths without wings cling to warm chimneys. Eels live in cisterns that are dry half the year and wake when the water returns. There are hand-sized animals like soft stones that unfold only when touched by condensation. Larger things sleep in public squares, ignored by the city’s people, their ribs rising and falling beneath hides patterned like sedimentary layers.

Sound travels strangely here. The whole undercity should be noisy: it is broad, inhabited, always changing, full of work and motion. But the stone drinks sound. Voices fall flat after a few paces. Footsteps arrive as faint clicks. The beasts do not bellow. Tools strike with muted taps, as if wrapped in cloth. Even collapses are muffled; a wall gives way in a distant quarter, and the report is less a crash than a pressure change, a breath passing through the cavern.

The turbulence is mostly visual, spatial, structural. Nothing stays where it was for long.

The city is built on ancient ground that still shifts in small, constant acts. Floors buckle by finger-widths overnight. Whole lanes become impassable when mineral curtains grow down from the ceiling and fuse with the street. Arches sag and are shored up with bone poles, stone wedges, braided gut, crude timber imported from some unreachable surface or perhaps preserved from a lost age. Houses are dismantled and rebuilt by hand using the same blocks in new arrangements. A wall that was a dwelling becomes a retaining terrace; a terrace becomes a market ledge; a market ledge splits and is sealed as a shrine to its own fracture.

This is an urban place, but not a dense one. Its emptiness is part of its design. Buildings gather in islands around cisterns, warm vents, salt kitchens, shell yards, and communal ovens. Between these districts lie wide stone flats, abandoned avenues, unclaimed basins, and plazas too large for their current population. Some were once reservoirs. Some were ceremonial spaces. Some may simply be places where the rock refused to carry weight. The city extends far beyond sight, but in intervals: habitation, emptiness, habitation, emptiness. A person walking through it passes from a clustered neighborhood of low domes and scaffolded towers into a bare expanse where only drip-pits and animal tracks mark the floor.

Its age is not ornamental. It is geological.

Everything has been softened by time except the light. Stairways have lost the memory of individual steps, worn into ramps by feet, hooves, bellies, and water. Carved signs have become smooth tablets with a few surviving grooves. Defensive walls slump into long ridges. Drainage channels have been recut over older channels, which were themselves cut over cracks in the original cavern floor. In some places the city has sunk into its own dust and calcite, so that only upper windows remain open at ankle height. Elsewhere erosion has pared buildings down to their internal stones, exposing rooms like diagrams.

The inhabitants possess only simple tools, or only choose to use simple tools. Their technology is patient, manual, intimate with material. They cut with stone blades and soft metal chisels. They lash, wedge, stack, braid, fire, scrape. Wheels are rare because roads deform too quickly; sledges and shoulder poles are more reliable. They measure vertical movement by hanging strings from ceilings and watching clay weights shift against marked boards. They predict condensation by tasting walls. Their maps are not flat drawings but bundles of knotted cord, each knot a cistern, a vent, a collapse, a beast path, a district whose name has changed many times.

The brightness gives the place an almost outdoor feeling, but the ceiling, when visible, denies it. Far above, the luminous seams cross like frozen lightning. Sometimes they flare. Sometimes they dim in ripples, and the city responds at once. Animals turn their heads upward. Workers pause with stones in their hands. A market silently folds its awnings. Children are carried inside. A few minutes later, a storm may occur without wind: sudden condensation, sheets of droplets forming on every surface at once, falling not from clouds but from architecture, from statues, roofs, ropes, ribs, and crystal. The streets shine. Hidden cisterns overflow. Pale crabs emerge from cracks. Then the moisture vanishes into channels and thirsty stone, leaving salt rings and the smell of cold clay.

In other hours the turbulence comes from below. The floor hums too quietly to hear, but water in bowls trembles. Hairline fractures open across plazas. People mark them with chalk and wait. Sometimes the crack seals itself with a glassy mineral scar. Sometimes it widens, revealing a lower brightness, another street beneath the street, another buried layer of the same city or an older one. Then crews gather with hand tools and rope. They salvage blocks, bones, vessels, sleeping animals, carved thresholds, door lintels with names no one can read. The city consumes its past gently and continuously.

There are rules here, learned rather than written.

Never build where the blind beetles refuse to cross.

Never speak loudly under a crystal seam during a flare.

Do not drain a basin completely; something in the stone must always believe water remains.

A doorway that narrows by itself belongs to the rock again.

If an animal sleeps in a road, the road has moved, not the animal.

These rules are obeyed with practical reverence. The people do not seem to think of the cavern as hostile. It is unstable, but not malicious. It is ancient enough to have moods instead of intentions. They live by yielding to it, by moving lightly despite their city-scale works. Their urbanism is a negotiation with mineral weather.

The markets are among the strangest places because they are busy without clamor. Traders sit on low platforms with shell bowls of salt, dried pale meat, pigments ground from stone, tool blanks, wick-fat, cordage, lamp fungus perhaps—though if it is fungus, it is more like a film of living mineral than a plant. Beasts pass through in loose herds, noses close to the floor, licking invisible nutrients from damp patches. Children chase them silently, not laughing aloud but grinning, communicating with hand signs. Bargains are made by touch, tally, and gesture. The bright cavern-light renders every object exact: a chipped blade, a wet rope, a slab of smoked hide, a cup made from the skull of something long extinct.

Beyond the inhabited districts are the flattened outskirts: immense eroded avenues where the city thins into foundations and low ridges. Here the fauna becomes bolder. White lizards bask on luminous stone. Long-bodied things coil in dry aqueducts. Flocks of small, soft, nearly soundless animals move across the open floor like spilled ash. There are old towers reduced to stumps, old bridges leading nowhere, old wells capped with heavy stones that tremble from time to time. The brightness persists even there, though colder, more remote.

The deepest impression is of contradiction held in balance: a bright underground; a city without machinery but full of infrastructure; a place nearly silent yet always in upheaval; a mineral world without vegetation yet crowded with animal life; an ancient landscape still unable to become still.

It feels less like a ruin than a living cross-section of time. The city is not built upon the cavern so much as braided through its erosion. Every wall is temporary, every pool recurrent, every district a phase. The people, the beasts, the water, and the rock all participate in the same slow catastrophe, which has been going on for so long that it has become a form of peace.
