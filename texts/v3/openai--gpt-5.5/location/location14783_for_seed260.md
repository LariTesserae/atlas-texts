At the top of the world, above the old weather and below a sky that has not decided how far away it is, there is a plateau of black salt and pale glass.

It is not flat. Nothing here has finished becoming flat. The land lifts in enormous unfinished gestures: half-ridgelines, blunt ribs of stone, terraces that begin as if they meant to become cliffs and then lose conviction, basins without lakes, valleys without rivers, passes that open into other passes and then into vertical emptiness. The elevation is extreme enough that distance behaves differently. Horizons are stacked. You can see across a whole province of raw geology, and yet each far escarpment seems to lean near when the light changes, as though scale is only a habit the eye keeps trying to impose.

There is no water.

Not hidden under frost, not locked in streams beneath stone, not waiting in clouds. The cold here is dry and mineral. The air feels sifted. Breath comes thin, abrasive, faintly metallic, carrying the taste of ground feldspar and old smoke. The basins are crusted with white and blue salts, but they have never known seas. The salts grow directly from the rock in slow efflorescent blooms, branching outward over centuries in brittle fans. In some hollows the ground is lacquered with opaline skins that look wet until touched; then they fracture into dust with a sound like paper tearing miles away.

Yet the place is lush.

Not with leaves in the ordinary sense. Not with wet green abundance. The vegetation here has learned other arrangements with matter.

Across the plateau stand groves of dry growth: pale, ribbed columns like bundled reeds, each one hollow and cool to the touch, rooted not in soil but in seams of mineral vapor. Their crowns are made of filamentous plates, thin as mica, overlapping in thousands, angled to catch dim light and whatever invisible nourishment descends from the broken sky. They do not sway. There is almost no wind at ground level. Instead they slowly rotate over weeks, turning their translucent fronds toward regions of pressure, toward sounds, toward mathematics.

Between the groves spread low carpets of dark lichen-like forms, though they are not lichens and have no need of moisture. They grow as velvet-black geometries on salt crust and basalt: hexagonal mats, feathered spirals, long scriptlike bands following fault lines. Their color deepens where the rock is youngest. In the cracks, glassy tubers swell like sealed lanterns, full of amber resins and powdered metals. Some plants are all root and no stem, exposing braided cords over the surface like the musculature of the land itself. Others resemble shrubs made from green ash, each twig jointed and brittle, bearing clusters of dry pods that never open, only hum.

The lushness is not dense. There is space everywhere. Groves stand far apart, each surrounded by a cleared radius of bare stone, as if the plants require solitude. A traveler crossing the plateau might walk an hour between clusters, then come upon a sudden field of blade-grass made from flexible ceramic, acres of it knee-high and moon-pale, every blade edged with faint blue fire. Beyond that: nothing but black ground, a stone cairn, and the dim outline of another vegetative island half a day away.

The light is poor.

There may be a sun, but it is usually behind a high lid of ash-colored luminosity, less a sky than a membrane. It glows from within, bruised violet near the zenith, brown at the horizons. Sometimes great rectangular shadows pass across it, too straight to be clouds, and the entire plateau dims in pulses. At other times, the sky opens into impossible depth: not stars, but the undersides of other landscapes, inverted and silent, their mountain roots hanging like chandeliers in a void of cold red.

The world is breaking here, but slowly.

Its breakdown is not a storm, not an explosion. It is patient, structural, almost ceremonial. A boulder may take a decade to remember that it belongs three meters to the left, then shudder across the ground in a single night, leaving no track. A ridge may appear twice on the horizon, one version eroded by time that has not happened yet, the other still soft and steaming from creation. The same grove may be visible from two directions at once, but not from between them. Footprints occasionally lead ahead of the walker, pressed into dust by feet that have not arrived.

The geology is embryonic. The mountains are still under construction. Strata lie exposed in fresh, clean bands, unworn by rain, unsoftened by rivers. The slopes do not show the graceful carving of erosion; they show fracture, extrusion, abrupt decisions. Dark stone rises in folds like cooling wax. Pale sedimentary layers end midair, their edges feathered into suspended grains, as if deposition paused before reaching the ground. There are cones of pumice with no craters, ravines with no causes, arches of basalt whose keystones have not yet hardened.

At the highest places, the rock is porous and warm inside, though the air remains cold. Put a hand to a fissure and one feels not heat exactly, but intention: a deep upward pressure, mountains thinking themselves into further height. The plateau is young in the way an egg is young, not empty but packed with future anatomy.

And over all of it, the sound.

It is deafening.

Not loud in the simple way of thunder or machinery, though at times it contains both. It is the continuous sound of a world assembling and failing to assemble. A colossal low chord moves through the ground without pause, felt in teeth and knees before it is heard. Above it are layers: mineral shrieks from growing salt fans; organ tones from hollow plant-columns; the granular roar of dust lifting and settling though no wind is visible; distant impacts that never produce visible collapse; the slow bell-like peal of cliffs recognizing gravity.

The plants contribute much of the noise. The groves sing by pressure and fracture. Their mica crowns tremble microscopically, producing vast shimmering drones that seem too large for their bodies. In the blade-fields, each ceramic grass-stem clicks against the next at intervals so precise that the field becomes a clock, then a chant, then an argument in a language older than mouths. The black mats on the rocks emit subsonic pulses. The sealed pods hum continuously, some at pitches that blur vision. There are no animals to call, no insects, no wingbeats, no burrowing life, yet the place is never quiet. It is more animate for lacking animals, as if all vitality has been taken up by stone, salt, and plant.

Silence exists only inside certain ruins.

They are scattered, small, and rare: low shelters built from stacked slate, roofed with ribs of plant-column and mats of woven mineral fiber; windbreak circles on exposed saddles; cairns marking routes that no longer connect; a handful of terraces cut into slopes for cultivation, though the crops have become wild and geometric since abandonment. The technology is simple. There is no metal beyond hammered native copper and soft sky-iron gathered from fallen black nodules. Tools are chipped glass, bone-colored ceramic, braided root-cord, stone weights, resin lamps that burn without flame. Doorways are low. Walls lean inward against the sound.

Whoever built here did so carefully and by hand. Their marks remain modest against the scale of the plateau: a line of drilled holes along a cliff face, perhaps for pegs; shallow bowls carved into stone to collect falling dust, not rain; poles set in circles around groves, hung with cracked tablets incised with warning spirals. Some structures are unfinished, or perhaps still being built by processes slower than human labor. A foundation may have one new stone each century. A stairway climbs thirty steps up a slope and then continues as a shadow, usable only under certain dim sky conditions.

In these shelters the sound drops abruptly, as though cut with a blade. Step across the threshold and the deafening world becomes a pressure outside the skull. The interiors smell of ash, resin, and cold clay. There are sleeping niches too short for comfort, storage jars sealed with salt, handprints in green pigment made from ground plant tissue and alkali. On some walls are diagrams of impossible seasons: not spring and winter, but Nearness, Splitting, Reverse Dawn, Root Eclipse, The Month When Hills Repeat.

The rules of the place are known only partially, and mostly as cautions.

Do not look at the same horizon through two openings.

Do not answer a sound that resembles your name, because the plateau has no animals and therefore no caller.

Do not cut the pale column-plants during their listening phase; their hollows will remember the wound and teach it to nearby stone.

Do not sleep on fresh rock.

Do not cross a salt basin when its crust reflects a sky different from the one above you.

Do not count the groves aloud. Numbers attract correction.

There are paths, but they are not roads. They are agreements renewed by walking: faint lines of compacted dust, rows of palm-sized stones, strips where the black growth has withdrawn. Because there is no rain, nothing washes away. Because reality is unstable, nothing remains entirely reliable. A path may persist for a thousand years and then, in an afternoon of dim violet light, decide it has always gone somewhere else.

The coolness deepens toward evening, though evening is not guaranteed to follow day. Temperature changes slowly. Shadows accumulate rather than fall. The plant-columns close their mica crowns over the course of many hours, each plate settling with a tiny click multiplied across miles until the plateau rattles like a field of bones. The black mats absorb what little light remains and begin to glow at their edges, outlining the cracks in the land. Far off, unfinished mountains pulse from within, dull red behind their skins.

At night, if it is night, the sky lowers.

It may come close enough to touch the tallest ridges. Then the high peaks disappear into it, not hidden by cloud but inserted into another medium. Their silhouettes blur, reappear inverted, split into multiple versions. The deafening chord sinks lower. Structures creak. Salt fans grow audibly, branching with sharp crystalline reports. In the groves, the sealed pods brighten and dim as if breathing, though there is no breath in them.

The absence of water becomes most palpable then. No dripping. No frost. No damp in the stone. No scent of rot. Nothing softening, dissolving, carrying, cleansing. Life here does not drink; it accretes. It listens to minerals, eats light reluctantly, metabolizes pressure, stores time in dry tissues. Death does not decay but delaminates. Fallen fronds shatter into flakes. Old roots become cords of colored dust. A dead grove may stand for centuries as a pale acoustic ruin, still singing when the ground-tone passes through it.

And because erosion is only beginning, the dead are not yet buried. Everything remains exposed: first attempts, failed mountains, abandoned tools, cracked seed-pods, the edges of laws. The whole region feels provisional, like a draft of a continent laid out at impossible altitude before weather, animals, and rivers have been invited in. Yet it is not empty. It is abundant in a severe way, lavish with space, growth, contradiction, and unfinished form.

To cross it is to move through a world that has not settled on being one world.

A grove stands to your left, and also ahead, and also in memory behind you. A basin of salt opens like a white eye, dry as bone. A slate hut waits under a leaning escarpment, its doorway black and mercifully quiet. The air trembles with an immense note. The plants listen. The mountains rise by fractions. The sky misaligns.

Nothing runs. Nothing flies. Nothing drinks.

Still, everywhere, slowly, impossibly, life is growing.
