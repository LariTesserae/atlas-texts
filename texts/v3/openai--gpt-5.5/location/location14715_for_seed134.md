At the summit of a drowned mountain there is a hollow no larger than a ruined chapel, though no hand has ever shaped it. It sits high above the abyssal plain, lifted on a young black ridge whose flanks are still sharp, unsoftened by age. The place is entirely underwater, but not dark. Light comes down in a broad, clean column from far above, sifted by a thousand meters of cold blue, and in this column everything appears too clear, too exposed, as if the sea here has forgotten how to hide its contents.

The hollow is a bowl of new stone.

Its rim is made of broken basalt, glassy in places, matte in others, a rough crescent of mineral edges and recent fractures. The rock has not yet been rounded. It still holds the violence of its making: ribs, splinters, congealed ropes, collapsed bubbles, knife-like fins of lava frozen mid-fold. Pale mineral crusts grow over the black in patches, not like lichen or coral, but like chemical weather: chalky blooms, silver skins, amber stains, green-black varnish. There is no vegetation. Nothing rooted, nothing leafed, nothing that sways with the simple obedience of a plant. The surfaces are bare mineral or occupied by animals, films, eggs, husks, slime, mineral precipitate, and the slow fur of microbes that are not quite visible until they move as a single dull sheen.

Water fills everything.

Not merely surrounds it, but seems to be the main substance of the world. The hollow is dense with it, layered with it. Cool currents slide over one another without mixing, and each layer has its own clarity, its own smell, its own faint color: iron-yellow, sulfur-white, blue-black, glass-green. Sometimes the water looks thick enough to climb. Sometimes it looks like air with a memory of weight. Curtains of colder brine fall from cracks in the upper rim, descending slowly like rain in a room where rain has forgotten gravity. They strike the floor of the bowl and spread outward as crawling transparent fog.

The place is small, but crowded beyond comfort.

Every surface is near another surface. Every crevice contains movement. The basalt walls overhang inward, and from the ceiling of stone hang mineral teeth, fresh stalactites of carbonate and silica, growing grain by grain from the chemical breath of vents. They are not old enough to be grand. They are young, crowded, eager. Some are no longer than fingers; others have fused into screens and lattices, making narrow passages through which the water pulses.

In the center of the hollow is the vent-field.

It is not built, though it gives the impression of machinery. There are no pipes, no engines, no metal casings, no deliberate angles. Yet the earth has arranged itself into something industrial. Chimneys of black sulfide rise from the floor, squat and crowded, hissing through their porous throats. Some are broken off and already resealed with new mineral lips. Some lean against each other like overheated furnaces. Some breathe steadily; others pulse in long intervals, exhaling dark water in slow, heavy bursts that roll upward like smoke.

The sound is immense.

Underwater sound has no mercy. It enters bone, shell, stone. The hollow roars with a deep, continuous pressure: the venting of hot mineral water into the cold sea, the crackle of cooling rock, the grinding of pebbles in unseen currents, the far drumming of collapses inside the young mountain. There are knocks like valves opening. There are whistles like steam escaping, though no air exists here. There are low mechanical groans from the basalt as it settles by fractions of a millimeter. The whole place sounds like a factory operating in its sleep.

Yet almost nothing happens quickly.

The vents breathe. The currents fold. Mineral skins thicken. A crack widens by a hair. A chimney grows a pale rim. A wall sheds a flake that falls with solemn slowness through the water and lands among the animals. Changes occur as accumulations rather than events. Even violence here is patient. The mountain is young, but it will take centuries learning how to be old.

Fauna is abundant.

Not plants, not reefs in the usual sense, but animal bodies layered over mineral bodies until the distinction becomes uncertain. Pale crabs stand on the black stone in clusters, legs touching, claws lifted into the flow. Translucent shrimp hang in the water like sparks held still, their backs marked by dim internal organs and cold opal eyes. Tubeworms, white and red and gray, protrude from mineral seams in dense bunches, though they resemble instruments more than vegetation: organ pipes, pressure gauges, clustered probes. Their plumes tremble in the chemical current.

There are snails with iron-black shells, their surfaces rough as forged slag. There are limpets arranged along heat gradients so precise that each animal seems to occupy a law rather than a place. There are pale eels coiled in cracks, opening and closing their mouths to the roar. There are soft-bodied things like folded gloves, like tongues of glass, like pieces of the water made temporarily muscular. Some animals are only seen when the light catches their edges: clear bells pulsing between chimneys, ribbed ribbons turning in the current, threadlike predators stretching and contracting across impossible distances.

The density of life is not lush. It is congested.

There is no greenery, no softness of meadow or forest. This is abundance without comfort. Bodies attach to hot stone because the stone leaks food. Bodies crowd where chemistry is favorable and avoid neighboring inches as if they were lethal. The world is divided by invisible borders of temperature, acidity, mineral concentration, pressure, and current speed. A crab may live its whole life between two stones because beyond the first lies water too cold to feed it, and beyond the second a plume too hot to cross.

The rules here are exact and strange.

Heat is territory.

Chemistry is weather.

Light is incidental.

The animals do not graze; they attend. They face vents like congregations facing an altar, but the altar is a wound in the planet. Many carry their food inside them as colonies of bacteria. Others scrape mineral films from rocks where no plant has ever grown. Some hunt by waiting for the current to misplace a weaker body. Some are blind, yet turn toward minute changes in dissolved metal with the assurance of migrating birds.

Because the hollow is high, perched on the submerged ridge, the water above it is clearer and brighter than the surrounding depths. A slanting daylight enters from a crack in the overlying blue, catching suspended mineral particles and making them shine like dust in a sunlit room. At times the whole vent-field appears theatrical: black chimneys, white crusts, red plumes, chrome shells, glass animals, and slow smoke rising through a cathedral of water.

Then the current shifts, and the smoke fills everything.

The light becomes milk. The animals vanish into noise. The hollow turns into an industrial cloud, loud and cool at its edges, hot and poisonous at its throats. A shrimp may be inches away and invisible. The basalt rim becomes a rumor. The water presses against itself in layered sheets, and the only certainty is vibration: the low engine-sound of the mountain, the hiss of minerals being born, the distant hammering of stone adjusting to its own recent existence.

There are no constructed structures here, but the place repeatedly imitates construction.

A chimney resembles a smokestack.

A field of tube-worms resembles a bank of sensors.

A row of mineral vents resembles boilers.

Crab shells clatter like loose hardware.

The vents pulse like pistons, not because anything intends production, but because the mountain cannot help manufacturing: sulfides, heat, bacterial food, shells, poison, habitat, sound. It is industry before invention. It is machinery without design, labor without workers, output without purpose.

At the edges of the hollow, the terrain falls away abruptly. Beyond the bowl the ridge drops into colder blue, and the density thins. Animals become fewer; mineral crust gives way to bare black slopes; the roar softens into a distant pressure. The boundary is not a wall, but it is definite. Inside: light, sound, bodies, chimneys, chemical rain. Outside: the long, empty descent of the sea mountain.

The coolness is constant except near the vents. Most of the hollow is cold enough to numb flesh, a clean high-sea cold, but threaded through it are narrow ribbons of warmth and scalding seams no wider than a hand. The animals arrange themselves around these gradients with exquisite discipline. There is no season in any ordinary sense. There is only the slow wandering of cracks, the birth and death of vents, the thickening of crusts, the collapse of a chimney after years of exhalation, the sudden crowding of larvae into a new warm fracture.

The youngness of the place is everywhere.

Edges have not learned smoothness. Newly broken rock flashes gray beneath black skin. Mineral towers grow too fast, brittle and extravagant, then shear off under their own weight. The floor is littered with fragments: chimney shards, shell pieces, flakes of glass, pellets of precipitated metal. But even this debris is quickly occupied. A fallen stone becomes a platform. A dead shell becomes a shelter. A cooling crack becomes a border. Nothing remains empty for long.

The weirdness is not in one spectacle, but in the way ordinary categories fail.

The rain falls underwater.

The smoke is water.

The factory has no builder.

The animals eat the planet’s breath.

The mountain is still forming while being inhabited.

And the light, strangely normal, makes it all harder to dismiss. This is not a hidden nightmare glimpsed by lightning. It is plainly visible. The blue daylight lays itself across every shell and chimney and pale moving leg. It shows the mineral snow drifting upward and downward at once. It shows the crowded lives arranged around invisible laws. It shows the hollow as a small, loud, cold, submerged room at the top of a mountain, where the earth is fresh, the sea is heavy, and life has gathered densely around the slow machinery of stone.
