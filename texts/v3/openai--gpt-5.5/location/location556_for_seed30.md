It is a small place, not a region so much as a held breath: a high warm basin cupped near the shoulder of a mountain that does not yet seem finished becoming stone.

The basin is only a few hundred paces across. Its boundaries are clear without being walls. On three sides, pale ribs of new rock lift from the ground in smooth, swollen arcs, like something grown under skin and not yet exposed to weather. They are not cliffs exactly; they have no talus, no fractured aprons, no long history of collapse. Their surfaces are soft-looking, unscarred, faintly translucent in places, with beads of water forming from inside them and sliding downward in slow, trembling strings. The fourth side falls away into dim cloud, but even there the drop feels recent, as if the world has only lately decided there should be an edge.

Everything is damp. Not flooded, not lush, but pervasively wet. Water sits just below the surface of the ground and announces itself through dark patches, sudden sucking sounds underfoot, cold breath rising from cracks, droplets gathering on metal and stone. After every gust, the air condenses into brief silver veils. The soil is not soil yet; it is a warm, dark paste of mineral powder, fungal thread, and fine grit, slick on top and springy below. In shallow depressions, clear pools quiver with a skin of amber oil that is not oil but some living film, folding and unfolding in response to vibration.

Vegetation is sparse. A few reedlike stems stand apart from one another, each with only two or three black-green blades, their bases sunk into wet mineral seams. Low mats of copper moss cling to the shaded sides of stones. Here and there grow stunted trees no taller than a person, with pale bark and roots exposed like fingers refusing to go deeper. Their leaves are small, widely spaced, and glossy, designed less to gather sunlight than to drink mist.

The light is dim though the air is warm. Above the basin hangs a permanent ceiling of bruised vapor, lit from within by a weak, diffuse glow. There is no visible sun, only a direction from which the clouds are less dark. Colors are muted until water touches them: then rock flashes lavender, moss burns orange, the backs of insects gleam blue-black. The place feels like late afternoon inside a storm that has forgotten to arrive.

Sound fills it. For such a small and sparsely furnished basin, it is astonishingly loud.

Water ticks, drips, runs, gulps, sighs. The new stone makes faint ceramic pops as it cools or grows. The reed-stems click together though there is seldom enough wind to move them. From the pools comes a steady fizzing murmur, like rain heard through walls. Animals call constantly: chirps, knocks, wet whistles, wing-buzz, soft barks from things hidden under ledges. At intervals a deep tone passes through the ground, not heard so much as absorbed by the teeth and joints. When it comes, every creature pauses for one breath, then resumes louder than before.

The fauna is everywhere, much more abundant than the plants. Warm dampness has given the basin over to motion.

Small translucent frogs cling to the vertical rock faces with too many toes. Their throats pulse with green light when they call. Blind salamander-like animals move beneath the mud, their paths visible as raised lines that collapse slowly behind them. Finger-length crustaceans climb the reed stems at dusk and wave paddle-shaped antennae at the clouded sky. Moths with glass wings gather on wet stone, drinking mineral seepage. There are beetles whose shells are patterned like circuit boards, and pale, hopping things that resemble seeds until they open their mouths.

Predators are present but discreet. Slender ribbon-snakes live in the warm cracks, emerging only when the ground-tone sounds. A birdlike animal, no larger than a hand, hunts by hanging upside down from the ribs of rock and dropping straight into pools. Something larger moves beyond the cloud-edge at night; its steps are slow, soft, and too evenly spaced to be entirely animal.

Scattered through the basin are structures, though none dominates it. They are small, partial, and strangely careful.

Near the center stands a ring of twelve black posts, each no higher than a person’s chest. They are sunk into the mud without disturbing it. Their surfaces drink light. Water climbs them upward in thin reverse rivulets and gathers at their blunt tops, where it forms hovering droplets that never fall unless touched by a living thing. When insects pass between the posts, their wingbeats change pitch. When a person stands inside the ring, sounds arrive out of order: a splash before the foot enters water, a frog-call before the throat swells.

At the eastern rim, half-buried in fresh rock, is a shelter or machine or shrine. It has the size of a small hut, but no door, only overlapping plates of a white ceramic material veined with gold. The plates open and close very slowly over the course of the day, breathing vapor. Inside, one can glimpse a darkness pricked with tiny moving stars. The animals avoid entering it, but many gather around its base, where warmth leaks into the mud.

There are other marks: a line of polished stones arranged along no obvious path; three metal bowls catching condensation from the underside of an overhang; a ladder made of one continuous piece of transparent material leaning against nothing; a patch of ground where footprints remain raised instead of impressed, as if the mud remembers pressure by swelling.

The technology here is not abandoned exactly. It is scattered, quiet, and active in ways that make abandonment difficult to define. Nothing flashes with purpose. Nothing announces itself as a device. Yet the basin is being maintained, or grown, or calculated.

The black posts tune the weather.

The buried white structure persuades the rock to rise.

The bowls collect not water but moments of rainfall from other days.

The transparent ladder only becomes solid during thunder.

There are rules, but they are local rules. They do not seem to extend beyond the basin’s edge.

Water prefers surfaces that have been named aloud. If a stone is spoken to, condensation gathers there first.

Fresh rock will not break under force, but it can be shaped by repeated sound. The frogs know this and sing new hollows into the cliffs every season.

No shadow remains still. Even under steady cloud, shadows tremble faintly, drifting toward the central ring as if drawn by gravity.

Living creatures cast warmer reflections than their bodies. In the pools, their reflected forms glow softly and often move a fraction of a second ahead.

Metal left overnight grows a thin fur of mineral needles, each aligned toward the clouded drop at the basin’s open side.

Nothing dead decays quickly here. Bodies are taken apart by small mouths, yes, but also by light, by mist, by the patient curiosity of the mud. Bones soften into chalky spirals. Wings become transparent scales in the pools. Shells are incorporated into the growing rock, where they remain visible like thoughts not fully submerged.

Despite the strangeness, the place is not hostile. It is too young to be hostile in the old ways. It has not learned drought, avalanche, forest, or ruin. Erosion has barely begun its grammar. There are no gullies, no worn paths, no ancient stains. Water does not yet know where it wants to go, so it tries many routes: down the stone ribs, across the mud, upward along the black posts, sideways through the air as mist. The mountain itself seems undecided, warm from its making, pushing new surfaces into the basin whenever the deep tone passes.

The animals adapt faster than the geology. They have mapped the sounds, the heat leaks, the safe intervals between ground-pulses. They know when the ceramic hut will exhale and when mineral rain will fall from a cloudless patch of air. They gather under the reed-stems before the pools reverse. They scatter when the shadows lean too sharply.

At times the whole basin becomes active at once.

The vapor ceiling lowers. Droplets fatten on every exposed surface. The black posts hum in twelve slightly different notes. The mud shivers with hidden bodies. Frogs light the rock faces green. The reed-blades rattle though there is no wind. Warm rain begins upward from the pools, rising in strings and beads until it reaches the cloud layer and vanishes into it. In response, the white buried structure opens all its plates, revealing inside not machinery but a depth of star-cold darkness impossible within so small a shell.

Then the new rock grows.

It does not rise dramatically. It swells by the width of a fingernail. A ledge becomes more definite. A rib of stone lifts a little higher. A crack seals. A shallow basin appears where there was flat mud. The landscape revises itself, quietly but unmistakably.

By morning — if morning can be used in a place without a sun — the basin is changed. A pool has shifted closer to the ring of posts. One of the stunted trees has gained a root above ground and lost one below. The ladder leans at a new angle, still against nothing. A fresh stone, smooth and damp as an egg, sits near the western rim, already dotted with insects drinking from it.

The place remains small. It remains sparse. There is much open mud, much dim air, much room between stems and structures. But emptiness here is not absence. It is workspace. It is unassigned material. It is where the next rule may appear.
