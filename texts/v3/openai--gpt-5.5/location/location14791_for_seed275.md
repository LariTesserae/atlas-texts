Below the surface, where a city ought to be stone, the world is plant.

It lies in a cavern wide enough to have districts, horizons, weather of its own, and the faint illusion of a sky. The roof is not always visible. In some quarters it hangs low and ribbed with rootwork, pale as bone; in others it rises into a cool green vault crossed by veins of mineral glass and luminous fungal panels, so evenly bright that the undercity keeps a kind of noon. There is no sun, but light arrives anyway: refracted through crystal shafts bored from higher strata, amplified by mirror-leaves, diffused by broad translucent membranes stretched between towers of living wood. The light is steady, practical, almost municipal.

There is no water.

Not in streams, not in pools, not dripping from the ceiling, not hidden in cisterns. The air is dry and cool, with the dusty clarity of salt flats and sealed museums. The plants here have not solved thirst by finding water; they have replaced the need for it with stranger economies. Their sap is resin, wax, volatile oil, slow-moving mineral gel. Their leaves do not drink mist. They sift ions from stone, breathe trace gases from vents, crack salts with enzyme-light, and trade pressure through networks of fiber and hollow pith. A cut vine does not bleed but exhales: a soft, aromatic breath of camphor, metal, pepper, and old paper.

The ground is not soil in the familiar sense. It is a compacted mat of rootfelt and powdered mineral, springy in some places, brittle in others, overlaid with pavements grown rather than laid. Streets are long strips of lacquered bark, dark green and amber, polished by traffic. Curbs rise as braided woody lips. At intervals, pale root-arches cross the avenues, carrying bundles of living cable, seed-pipes, and humming industrial tendons. Buildings do not stand on foundations; they descend into them. They are grown downward and upward at once, anchored in dense root bulbs sunk into the cavern floor, their upper chambers swelling like husks, pods, trunks, silos, and ribbed seedcases.

The city is open, but not crowded. It extends far beyond the first visible neighborhoods, spreading across the basin in boroughs separated by quiet spaces: bare mineral flats, nurseries, processing yards, pale fungal commons, groves of columnar stems. You can look down a boulevard and see, far away, another cluster of towers glowing through the cool air, then beyond it the green-black hints of yet another. Between the districts there is room — long pauses of emptiness where the cavern floor has only begun to be claimed.

This is a young place. It has the organization of a city but the unfinished skin of an embryo. Many streets end abruptly at fresh walls of cambium, still soft and stippled with growth pores. Some towers are only scaffolds of root and rib, their chambers not yet inflated. Raw mineral shows through everywhere: chalky shelves, blue-gray basalt humps, salt blisters, seams of mica like dull stars. Erosion has barely begun to give anything age. Edges are sharp. Cut faces of stone still remember the tools that opened them. The plantwork is spreading over them now, not covering ruins but inventing the first version of architecture.

The industrial quarter occupies the lower terraces, where the cavern floor dips toward a line of dry vents. There are factories here, though they are half botanical organs, half machines. Huge pistons made from bundled stem-muscle flex slowly inside glass-and-bark housings. Flywheels turn without haste. Conveyor vines pass mineral nodules from one processing mouth to another. Seed presses thump at long intervals. Kilns burn without flame, using catalytic heat and powdered salts. The machinery is mostly quiet: not silent, but restrained, as if sound is expensive.

A low mechanical respiration moves through the quarter.

Hush of belts.  
Click of seed valves.  
A distant compression sigh.  
The dry scrape of chitin feet on bark pavement.  
Once in a while, the hollow knock of a pod being sealed.

The city makes things from what the dry underground gives it: insulating fiber, resin plates, salt-glass, mineral pigments, coil-root cable, aromatic solvents, hard wax tiles, protein cakes extruded from nitrogen-fixing nodules. It manufactures replacement organs for itself. A broken bridge is not repaired with beams but grafted, splinted, fed, and instructed. A factory wall may flower diagnostic flags. A chimney may close for a week and reopen as a spiral of breathing vents. Infrastructure here is not inert. It is disciplined life.

Yet despite the vegetation’s total claim, the place is sparse. The plants are everywhere, but they do not form jungle. There are broad clear avenues, plazas of bare root-polish, empty lots marked by slow growth rings, long promenades under high vine-catenaries. The living architecture seems to require intervals, perhaps for airflow, perhaps for light, perhaps because each organism-city needs room to think through its roots. This gives the undercity a ceremonial quiet. It feels less like a forest than like a capital planned by trees that dislike crowds.

Fauna moves through it in abundance, though much of it keeps to edges.

Small animals with lacquer shells graze on mineral films along the curbs. Pale, jointed browsers climb the sides of buildings, shaving lichen from ventilation ribs with delicate mouthparts. Flocks of membranous things drift in the light wells, not birds exactly, but seed-moths with transparent wings and weighted tails. They do not sing. Their wings make a papery tremor when they pass overhead. In the factory wards, long-backed scavengers with masked faces wait for extruded waste cakes, each animal marked by colored pollen from the district it frequents.

Predators are present but discreet. A vine may tighten around an unwary grazer with the patience of law. Thin, foxlike creatures move along pipeways, stepping over sensor blossoms without touching them. There are ambush flowers in service alleys, their petals folded into the exact color of shadow. At night — or during the municipal dimming that serves as night — small hunting bulbs detach from ceiling roots and float downward on gas sacs, trailing hairlike snares.

The rules of the world are dry rules.

Nothing rots quickly. Decay is a profession, not an accident. Dead matter is collected, registered by scent, and passed into cracking chambers where specialized fungi, beetles, and acid roots dismantle it grain by grain. Fire is rare and feared, but heat is common, carefully caged. Open liquid is impossible; any free solvent is immediately absorbed, taxed, or quarantined. Breath matters. Exhalations feed wall gardens. Factories are arranged according to gas appetite. Some streets are planted with carbon-hungry fronds; others with sulfur shrubs or nitrogen lace. To walk across districts is to pass through different invisible cuisines.

The inhabitants — if they are people, or something people-like — have built with the plants by surrendering the old distinction between building and cultivation. Their homes are chambers inside trunks, cooled by mineral fins and lit by photosynthetic glass. Doorways iris open when touched with the correct chemical signature. Elevators are slow peristaltic shafts. Markets occupy the undersides of giant leaves held flat by tensile ribs, where goods hang in dry nets: salt loaves, folded textiles, cured resin, carved tools, dormant seeds, insect armor, polished lens-stones.

There are signs, but many are grown. A public warning appears as a band of red thorns along a railing. A transit route is indicated by blue-veined moss. Civic announcements bloom on notice-trees in repeating patterns of color and scent. Some walls carry old mechanical displays too: segmented amber lights, pressure gauges, enamel number plates, rotating drums. The industrial age has not been replaced by the botanical one. They have intermarried.

Because the city is below surface, it has boundaries that are not walls but geology. The cavern rim rises in shelves and shadowed inclines. Beyond the inhabited basin, tunnels lead outward into unlit substrate mines, root farms, animal warrens, gas wells, and growth fronts where the city is still becoming. These frontiers are quiet and tense. Surveyor vines probe cracks in the stone. Young rootlets advance behind ceramic shields. Machines on caterpillar treads score grooves into mineral faces so spores can grip. Teams of pale insects carry charged dust. Every new block begins as a negotiation between stone pressure, gas chemistry, civic need, and the appetite of the founding plant.

In those raw districts, the undercity seems almost vulnerable. The architecture has not hardened. Translucent walls pulse faintly with new sap. Root beams are wrapped in splints. Light panels flicker as their chloroplast lattices align. The streets smell sharper, greener, more chemical. There are no old stains, no softened corners, no historical soot. It is all first draft. Even the silence there feels unworn.

Elsewhere, in the mature central boroughs, the city has begun to acquire rituals of maintenance. At regular intervals, usually without any bell, shutters along the avenue open and release streams of powder-fine pollen. The air turns gold for five minutes, then clears as intake fronds along the roofs drink it back. Once a day, mirror-leaves on the ceiling rotate a few degrees, and all shadows shift at once. Every few hours, the root pavements tighten, smoothing cracks and lifting debris toward collection mouths. Herds of cleaning beetles emerge from municipal hives, cross the plazas in slow black fans, and vanish beneath gratings.

The sound remains low. Even activity is muffled by living material. Bark absorbs footsteps. Rootfelt swallows vibration. Leaves baffle echoes. A whole factory can operate behind a wall and announce itself only as a warmth against the palm, a faint pulse in the floor. The loudest events are biological: the sudden clap of a seed pod opening, the brittle rain of husks from a ceiling vine, the alarm rattle of a thousand tiny shells when a predator passes.

The coolness is constant. Not cold enough for frost, but enough to keep every surface firm and every odor precise. Resin hardens cleanly here. Breath clouds only in the deepest service tunnels, where chilled gases seep upward from old strata. Workers wear layered fiber coats, not against weather but against the slow extraction of heat by stone and plant alike. The city itself seems to prefer this temperature: measured, dry, unsentimental.

There is strangeness, but it is not chaos. The weirdness has zoning laws.

A plaza may be paved entirely with living eyes — not animal eyes, but glossy lens-organs that track the movement of light and redirect it underground. A library may consist of seed vaults whose contents must be germinated to be read. A power station may be a grove of black stems that flex in rhythm with subterranean pressure tides. In one district, buildings communicate through slow color changes, so the skyline blushes, pales, and darkens over the course of a workday. In another, streets migrate by a few centimeters each month, following better gas flow, so maps are printed on elastic membrane.

There are rumors of older roots beneath the city, though the city is young. Something vast may have been planted before the cavern was opened. Sometimes machines at the lowest vents stop together, all gauges trembling. Sometimes the pavement carries a pulse too slow to be industrial. In certain silent courts, the towers lean inward as if listening to a voice rising through the rootmass from below.

Still, the place is not ominous so much as intent. It is busy becoming habitable on its own terms. It has rejected water, or lost it, or never learned it. It has made dryness fertile. It has made a forest that is also a municipality, a mine, a factory, a respiratory system, a nursery, and a map of future streets.

From a high balcony grown into the side of a central trunk, the view opens across the whole cavern basin: green architecture under artificial noon, broad empty avenues, pale mineral gaps, distant industrial terraces, animal specks crossing plazas, light wells descending like solid columns, and beyond all that the unfinished margins where root and machine continue their patient advance into raw stone.

Nothing flows here.

Everything grows.
