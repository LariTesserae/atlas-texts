The town sits low in a shallow basin of stone and soot, not quite underground and not open country either. Its boundary is clear: a ring of black retaining walls, waist-high in some places and building-high in others, enclosing perhaps a few streets, a few yards, a canal cut, several worksheds, and the old engine hall. Beyond the ring the land rises only slightly, a bare mineral plain of grey clay, clinker, and weathered slag. Nothing green grows there. Nothing green grows anywhere.

The ground is damp without being wet. Moisture collects in seams, in bolt holes, in the hollows of worn paving stones. It beads on iron railings and on the backs of pipes. It darkens the brickwork in patches and leaves white mineral blooms where it evaporates. There is no steady rain, only an intermittent mist that condenses out of the cool air as if the town itself exhales it. Gutters carry almost nothing, but they are never dry. Narrow channels run beside the streets, lined with glazed ceramic, containing a finger’s depth of brown water that does not seem to flow and yet never stagnates.

The town is old enough to have stopped looking abandoned. Its ruin has become its architecture.

Brick factories stand with their upper windows bricked shut and their lower windows fitted with thick glass, cloudy from within. Rooflines sag, but only to a certain settled curve, as if they reached that shape generations ago and then refused further collapse. Sheet-metal awnings are mottled with rust in stable constellations. Iron ladders climb walls and end at sealed hatches. Chimneys rise everywhere, short and square, taller and round, paired, clustered, solitary; none smokes, but all hum faintly through their masonry.

There are streets, though they are too narrow for carts and too regular to be accidental. Rails run down the center of some of them, sunk into stone, polished on top by use. At intervals, small platforms stand beside the rails: cast-iron waiting posts, numbered with enamel plaques. No vehicle arrives. No vehicle has arrived for as long as anyone could measure. Still, the rails vibrate.

The scale is intimate, almost roomlike despite the industrial mass. You can see from one end of the settlement to the other if you stand on the old loading dock. The engine hall dominates everything, but even it is not vast—more like a municipal cathedral built by people who worshipped compression, heat exchange, and tolerance. Its façade is ribbed with buttresses of dark brick. Above the entrance, a wheel twenty feet across turns inside a circular recess. It turns slowly, once every nine minutes, and has always done so. The wheel drives belts that enter the wall through slots and vanish into the interior, where pistons knock, governors spin, flywheels breathe, valves clack open and shut.

The sound is immense.

It is not a sound that rises or falls. It is a condition of the place, like gravity. Engines thump in patient sequence beneath the streets. Pipes moan with pressure. Chains rattle somewhere unseen, always with the same interval between rattles. Metal plates flex and settle with a booming note. From the chimneys comes a low organ tone, not produced by smoke or flame but by air passing through baffled shafts. Every wall participates. Every surface has learned its pitch.

And over this mechanical thunder is the life of the animals.

They are everywhere.

They crowd the wires and the gutter lips, nest beneath the eaves, hang from archways, cling to warm engine casings, drift in the canal water, crawl through the pipe galleries, pack themselves into cracks no finger could enter. Most are pale, cool-bodied things: blind rooks with translucent eyelids; ratlike animals with mineral-colored fur and too many incisors; flat insects the size of coins, armored like stamped tin; white crabs in the drains; soft-bodied lamprey-things that knot around valves and feed on condensation. There are moths with no wings, birds that never fly, beetles that live entirely on rust, and long-tailed creatures that move in loops along the underside of railings, passing each other without conflict.

They make more noise than seems physically possible. Scratching, clicking, chittering, coughing, squealing, drumming. Wings that cannot lift bodies still vibrate constantly. Beaks tap glass in steady patterns. Claws run over brick. Shells scrape pipe. Something in the drains sings a wet two-note call, again and again, without variation. The animals are overwhelming not because they surge or swarm unpredictably, but because their abundance is absolute and permanent. Every niche is occupied. Every ledge has its appointed occupants. Every warm seam has its colony.

Yet the place does not change.

This is the town’s strangest rule, and after a while it becomes impossible to ignore. A blind rook drops a white pellet onto the paving; later, the pellet is gone, but the stain beneath it is exactly the stain that has always been there. A crab loses a claw in a gutter dispute; by the next circuit of the engine wheel it has two claws again, or perhaps it was never the same crab. Rust advances nowhere. Brick flakes do not accumulate. Water does not rise, drain, freeze, or foul. The animals eat, breed, die, shed, and cry out, but their number remains fixed—not statistically, not approximately, but with the precision of a gauge needle.

Even the damp has an equilibrium. Touch a wall and your fingers come away wet. Return an hour later, a year later, and the print you left has already been absorbed into the same mottled sheen. Puddles hold the reflections of pipes and chimneys with a tarnished steadiness. No ripple crosses them unless one of the pale drain animals breaks the surface, and when the rings fade, the water resumes its former expression exactly.

There is no vegetation, no moss softening mortar, no weed prying up a stone, no lichen whitening the north side of a wall. In its place are mineral skins and simpler lives. Fungal crusts grow in some corners, if growth is the word: matte grey films on ceramic, black velvet under valve housings, amber nodules in pipe shadows. They do not spread. They occupy territories with surveyed borders. Some pulse faintly when the pressure in the main line changes. Some emit a smell like wet pennies and old flour. They may be alive, or they may be another kind of industrial residue that has learned the posture of life.

The air is cool, heavy with metal, animal breath, wet brick, machine oil, and the faint sourness of enclosed water. Light arrives normally enough from above, though the sky is often a pale undecided grey. It falls into the basin and strikes the upper windows, the rails, the glazed channels. There are shadows, but not deep ones. The town is visible, legible, almost plain. Its strangeness does not come from darkness. It comes from repetition, from excess held motionless.

Inside the engine hall, the temperature is only slightly warmer. The floor is tiled in cream and black, cracked but clean. The engines stand in rows, not enormous but numerous, each mounted on stone plinths. Brass labels name functions no longer understood: RETURN BREATHER, THIRD BALANCE, MILK OF PRESSURE, LOW ANIMAL DRIVE. Belts move overhead. Rods pass through walls into adjacent buildings. Gauges tremble at their marks. None of the needles wander.

Animals occupy the machinery without interfering with it. White moths cluster on pressure dials, leaving the numerals visible. Rats sleep in the pauses between reciprocating arms, their ribs rising and falling in perfect safety. Beetles cross belts at exact moments when seams pass beneath them. A bird nests inside the open mouth of a speaking tube; its chicks peep in rhythm with the engine knock. There is no accident. The town has made room for every body, or the bodies have been shaped by the town’s tolerances.

The canal is the quietest place, though even there quiet is only relative. It runs along the northern wall, straight as a cut ruler, filled with dark water to the same level along its whole length. There are mooring rings but no boats. Iron stairs descend into the water at three points, each step furred with pale shellfish. Under the surface, thick cords hang from submerged outlets and sway in the faintest current. The cords are living chains of small eel-like things, mouth to tail, tail to mouth, feeding on whatever the outlets release. They never detach. They never lengthen. They glimmer when the light reaches them.

At the southern edge are the yards: open rectangles of cinder and stone, sparse after the crowded walls. Here the emptiness of the place is most apparent. Despite the mass of animals, much of the town remains bare. A single crane stands in one yard, its hook suspended three feet above the ground. A stack of ceramic pipes occupies another, arranged in pyramids, each pipe hosting one sleeping animal in its hollow. There are loading bays with nothing to load, sheds with wide doors open onto swept floors, bins full of identical black pellets that are not coal and not seed.

The town feels inhabited but not by people. Human scale remains everywhere—door handles, stair widths, benches, signs, cups in a canteen, a coat hook with a cracked rubber rain cape still hanging from it—but humans are absent in a way that does not feel recent. Their absence has been incorporated. The systems they built did not fail. Instead, they closed around other tenants.

Perhaps the engines maintain the animals. Perhaps the animals maintain the engines. Perhaps both are consequences of a deeper mechanism below the basin, something that converts damp, pressure, and cool air into duration. The rules are never announced, but they can be learned:

Nothing accumulates beyond its place.

Nothing decays beyond its appointed weathering.

No population exceeds its number.

No machine stops.

No water dries.

No new plant enters.

No silence is permitted.

At certain intervals, all the town’s sounds align. The engines knock, the drain-song calls, the rook beaks tap, the belts slap, the pipe organ chimneys groan, the beetles rasp beneath the stones. For a few seconds the separate noises become one immense chord, loud enough to make the damp tremble on the walls. Then the chord separates again into its components, not fading, not growing, simply returning to the pattern it has always had.

Standing there, you may feel that you have arrived at a moment. But the town has no moments. It has only state.

Cool air, wet iron, pale animals, brick, vibration, water held in channels, machines held in motion, a small industrial basin at the bottom of a shallow grey world—complete, loud, crowded, sparse, and fixed.
