At the bottom of a low, cold basin stands a city that no one built.

It is not large. You can see from one end of it to the other in the dimness: perhaps the size of a ruined hamlet, or a cluster of chapels gathered around an empty square. Yet it has streets, thresholds, towers, courtyards, alleys narrow enough to suggest intention. From a distance it looks urban — a settlement of pale walls and black apertures — but up close the likeness wavers. The walls are not stone laid by hand. They are mineral ribs grown upward in sheets, folded by pressure, split by cooling. The doorways are places where two crystals failed to meet. The windows are gas-holes. The plazas are flat polygons of salt-glass, newly hardened, still sharp at their margins.

There is no water here. Not hidden under the ground, not frozen in cracks, not carried in clouds. The basin has never known rain. Its chemistry belongs to powders, vapors, static, and slow mineral hunger. Surfaces are dry in a way that feels absolute: not merely arid, but unwettable, as if moisture would be a foreign element with no grammar here. Dust does not clump. It slides. It gathers in knife-thin drifts along the bases of walls and then, without wind you can feel, rearranges itself into new angles.

The air is cool and dim. A high veil of suspended mineral ash turns the sky the color of old pewter. Light enters the city sideways, weak and diffuse, and never seems to strike anything fully. Shadows are soft at their edges but very deep inside the openings. The tallest structures — no taller than a few cathedral columns — are pale, fluted spires with translucent skins. They glow faintly for a while after dusk, not with warmth, but with stored pressure, the way certain stones remember being struck.

The terrain around the city is low and almost level: a young salt-field not yet worn smooth by age. It has the freshness of something recently exposed. Ridges are crisp. Fractures still fit together. The ground is a mosaic of plates, each plate slightly cupped, as though the basin is trying to curl inward. There are no softened gullies, no rounded hills, no evidence of long weather. Everything looks newly broken, newly extruded, newly abandoned by the force that made it.

And yet the place is active.

The city changes constantly, but almost without sound. At intervals, a wall tightens in the cold and opens a hairline fissure from roof to floor. Dust slips into the seam. A crystal cornice lengthens by the width of a fingernail. In the central square, pale mineral foam rises from vents in the ground, hardens, and collapses into lacework arches. Whole neighborhoods may be born this way: first a bubbling of dry salts and reactive gases, then a crust, then columns, then enclosed chambers, then a brittle avenue between them.

The processes are architectural but not technological. There are no tools, no mechanisms, no wires, no lamps, no machines sleeping in the walls. The city is a behavior of the basin. It builds because pressure, cold, chemistry, and charged dust happen to build in rectilinear habits here. It organizes itself because the minerals prefer edges, grids, repeating voids. Streets form where incompatible salts repel each other. Courtyards open where gas collects and bursts. Towers rise above places where the earth exhales.

Vegetation is sparse, though not absent. In the lee of certain walls grow black-green crusts, thin as soot and tough as enamel. They are not lush; they do not soften the place. They spread in patient stains across shaded mineral, feeding on static charge and trace gases. Some form upright fronds like singed paper, no higher than a hand, each one furred with silver dust. In the faint light they flex almost imperceptibly, turning their dark faces toward differences in temperature rather than toward the sun.

There are also the glass reeds.

They grow only in alleys where the air is most still. Each reed is hollow, transparent, and dry, rooted not in soil but in mineral seams. They do not drink. They conduct. During the coldest hours they gather charge from the ground and release it into the air through branching tips. Sometimes a whole alley of them brightens at once with a violet internal thread, then fades. No crackle follows. The discharge is silent, absorbed by the mineral haze.

Fauna is rare. You may cross the city for hours and see nothing move except dust and the slow flexing of crust-plants. Then, in a doorway, a pale creature may detach itself from the wall.

It is flat, jointed, and nearly transparent, like a shard of mica taught to walk. It has no call. Its feet make no audible step. It grazes on the black crusts with a mouth too small to see, leaving clean arcs behind it. If startled, it does not run; it folds along pre-existing lines and becomes indistinguishable from broken mineral. There are larger animals too, though seldom seen: narrow-bodied things that pass through the upper openings of the towers at dusk, gliding on membranes stiffened by cold. They hunt the mica-grazers by shadow, not sound.

The silence is one of the laws of the place.

Not a peaceful silence, exactly. More like a rule enforced by the materials themselves. Nothing rings here. Nothing echoes. The salt-glass absorbs impact. Dust muffles fracture. Even collapse happens softly: a balcony of mineral lace gives way, descends in pieces, and becomes a pale heap without a crash. If you place your ear against a wall, you may feel activity inside it — tiny shifts, grain-slides, chemical pulses — but the air carries almost nothing.

The city seems empty because its life is thinly distributed. A courtyard large enough for a market may contain only three crust patches, one reed, and a slow widening crack. A street may wait all day before anything crosses it. Space is abundant. Presence is rare. The buildings stand with wide gaps between them, as if the idea of a city arrived before the need for inhabitants.

Still, there is order.

The outer ring is low and jagged, made of fresh extrusions like broken teeth. Inside it, the avenues straighten. They converge on the central square, where the ground is smooth, dark, and faintly concave. At the square’s center is a dry well — or something shaped like a well. It descends only a few body-lengths before ending in a sealed, glossy floor. No water ever lay there. Instead, it is the basin’s main vent, now dormant in the cool hours. Around its rim grow the densest black crusts in the city, arranged in radial bands like script.

When the temperature drops further, the square begins its nightly work. Gas trapped below the plates contracts. The mineral skin tightens. Thin seams open across the ground in straight lines, dividing the plaza into a map of future streets. The reeds brighten. The crust-plants flatten themselves. Far overhead, the ash veil thickens and the dim light becomes almost blue.

Then the city exhales.

Not wind. Not breath. A slow, silent loosening. Vapor without moisture seeps from grates that are not grates, from gaps that resemble cellar mouths, from slits beneath walls. The vapor is metallic and faintly luminous. It does not rise far; it pools at knee height, filling alleys with a low ghost-fog made of dry chemistry. Where it touches certain minerals, new skins begin to form. By morning there will be another arch across a lane, another blind window, another room sealed off from every other room.

Nothing here decays in the ordinary way. Without water, rot has no empire. Instead, things become more angular, more divided, more specialized. A fallen spire does not soften into soil. It fractures into plates, plates into scales, scales into powders that migrate and eventually join new walls. The city consumes its own ruins immediately, but very quietly.

It is young. That is important. It has not yet learned erosion. Its shapes are too sharp, its avenues too clean, its towers too eager. Given ages, perhaps the mineral haze will sand it down into dunes of pale dust. Given pressure, perhaps it will seal itself into a single mass, all streets and chambers erased. But now it is in its brief urban phase: open, intricate, almost habitable, full of entrances leading into dry dark.

The strangest thing is that the city appears to anticipate bodies.

Steps occur where no feet require them. Niches form at shoulder height. Rooms gather around empty centers. Walls curve inward as if to shelter conversation. Yet no people have lived here, and no people are expected. These forms are coincidences of crystallization, but the coincidences repeat too often. The basin keeps making places to wait, places to gather, places to look out from.

Perhaps that is the world’s rule: matter here dreams socially before life does.

So the dry city stands in its cool basin, dim under the ash sky, building and unbuilding itself without tools, without voices, without rain. Sparse black growths listen to charges in the stone. Rare transparent animals pass like thoughts through its alleys. Dust shifts from threshold to threshold. New walls rise in the night where empty air had been.

By morning, the map will be different.

Not much. A doorway narrowed. A corridor sealed. A tower grown another pale ring higher.

Enough to prove the city is awake.
