At the top of the world there is a country of stone cities.

It is not a single city, though from far enough away it looks like one: a continuous roughness spread across a high plateau, filling basins, saddles, ridges, cliff shelves, and the rims of old glacial bowls. Its streets climb and fall over miles of broken volcanic ground. Its terraces step along escarpments. Its walls run from horizon to horizon, then vanish into cloud, then reappear much farther away as a gray line stitched across snow-black rock.

The land is young. The mountains have not yet been softened into gentleness. Ridges keep their knife-edges. Gullies cut straight and hard through the slopes, as if made yesterday by some sudden rush of meltwater. Cliffs fracture in clean planes. Talus lies in bright, angular heaps below them, the stones still sharp enough to split sandals and hooves. There are few deep soils. Most of the ground is mineral: slate, basalt, pale granite, frost-shattered scree, pumice, gravel, ribs of exposed bedrock polished by ice and foot traffic.

Everything is high. The air is thin enough to make carrying water a serious task and climbing stairs a slow negotiation with the lungs. Smoke rises reluctantly. Voices do not carry far unless the wind chooses to take them. On clear days the sky appears not blue but almost black above the white glare of distant peaks. Clouds are lower than they should be. Sometimes they come through the streets rather than over them, filling alleys with cold vapor, beading on hair, eyelashes, wool, stone lintels, the backs of pack animals.

Water is scarce but never entirely absent. It appears in pockets, as if the land remembers moisture in fragments. There are cistern courts below every district, round-mouthed and covered with slabs. There are roof channels cut by hand into stone gutters, leading rain and melted frost into jars, underground tanks, hollowed logs, skin-lined pits. In the mornings, condensation slicks the inner faces of walls. Children scrape it into cups with bone blades. Women hang coarse cloths overnight between poles; by dawn they are stiff with captured fog, and the water is wrung out carefully into clay vessels.

There are streams, but they are unreliable, born from hidden ice or afternoon thaw. They run white and bitter for a season, then sink beneath rubble. Their channels remain: dry braids of pale stone crossing the plateau, bridged by low arches and stepping blocks. Where water persists, the whole city gathers tightly around it. A spring no wider than a wrist may support a neighborhood of several hundred people, two terraces of barley, a line of tethered goats, and a shrine built from stacked river stones.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Nothing grows tall unless protected by walls. The wind will not allow it. Plants keep close to the earth: gray cushion mosses in cracks, black-green lichens on north-facing stones, bitter herbs no higher than a thumb, wiry grasses with roots deep in gravel. In sheltered courtyards there are small plots edged with flat rocks, where people coax cold-tolerant grains, onions, medicinal roots, and dark-leaved cabbages from thin soil enriched with ash and dung. These gardens are precious, fenced not only against animals but against blowing grit.

The city is dense because the habitable places are few. Houses press together for warmth, wall sharing wall, roof overlapping roof. They are built from what the plateau offers: dry-laid stone, rammed earth where clay can be found, turf blocks, timber carried up from distant valleys and used sparingly, bone and horn in hinges and pegs, hide stretched over frames, slate shingles weighted with rocks. Mortar is rare. Most structures survive by mass, compression, and habit.

From above, the settlements resemble mineral growths: clusters of rectangular rooms, towers, pens, storehouses, stairways, retaining walls, kiln pits, granaries, watch platforms, and long processional ramps fitted together with little empty space. Streets are narrow, sometimes no more than shoulder-width, roofed in places with slabs to stop snowdrift and wind. Larger avenues are paved with rough stone and grooved by sled runners. Bridges span ravines, built from stacked stone piers and lashed wooden beams, their railings furred with ice.

The people here do not have much metal. A few bronze knives, copper pins, and iron fragments are prized and repaired until they become heirlooms. Most work is done with stone, antler, bone, wood, leather, fire, patience, and many hands. Quarries ring the city like wounds in the hills. The sound of toolmaking is common but not loud: the tap of hammerstone on chisel, the scrape of hide being softened, the hollow knock of wooden mallets setting blocks, the rasp of bone needles through wool.

Infrastructure is everywhere, though simple. Water stairs descend into cisterns. Windbreak walls run along exposed streets. Smoke holes are shielded by stone cowls. Food stores are raised on piers against damp and rodents. Dung is collected, dried, stacked, and burned. Snow fences are built before winter and partly dismantled in spring. Signal cairns stand on high points, each topped with a basket for smoke or flame. Paths between districts are marked by stone teeth protruding from the ground so travelers can follow them when fog erases the world.

Despite the cold and the sparse vegetation, animals are abundant. The city is full of them.

Goats move in disciplined streams through morning streets, hooves clicking on stone. Their coats are thick, their horns scarred from rubbing against walls. Small shaggy cattle stand in steam-breathing groups below terraces, patient as boulders. Dogs sleep in doorways, rise silently, and follow anyone carrying food. Pack animals—sure-footed, long-haired, bred for cliffs and thin air—kneel to be loaded with jars, stone blocks, hides, salt, grain, and fuel.

Wild creatures live in the urban fabric as confidently as citizens. Rock doves nest in wall holes and under eaves. Ravens patrol rooftops and refuse heaps, speaking in low, private croaks. Pale foxes slip between storage yards at dusk. Hares graze on the protected edges of garden terraces. Marmots whistle from stone piles beyond the last houses. Snow finches scatter like ash when a gate opens. High above, lammergeiers and eagles circle the updrafts, watching the slaughter courts and the cliff paths.

There are insects too, though few in winter: dark beetles under warm stones, moths in wool stores, tiny midges rising from damp cistern mouths during brief thaws. In summer, if it can be called summer, the wet hollows suddenly tremble with life. Frogs appear in melt pools that did not exist a week before. Water birds land in shallow basins on their migrations, rest among the stones, and leave before the pools shrink.

The atmosphere is mostly quiet. Not silent—the place is too inhabited for silence—but quiet in the way of cold, high settlements where breath is expensive and sound is swallowed by open air. Morning begins with muffled movement: doors scraping, animals snorting, ladles against pots, the cough of fires taking hold. Bells are rare; wood is too precious for large frames and metal too precious for bells. Instead, people signal with horns, clappers, whistles, smoke, and patterned flagging from roof to roof.

The loudest sounds are weather and stone. Wind combs the wall tops with a dry hiss. Ice cracks in cistern rims at night. Loose scree shifts after freeze-thaw, pouring down a slope in a brief whispering rush. Far away, avalanches occur with a delayed, padded thunder, as if the mountains are turning in their sleep. Rain, when it comes, is a major event: a cold slanting rain that darkens every wall and sends people running to uncover channels, clear gutters, open cistern mouths, set out jars, stretch cloths, shout across roofs. Then it passes. The city drips for hours afterward.

Light is ordinary but clean. There is no permanent gloom, no supernatural glare. Days are bright when the sky clears, with hard-edged shadows and dazzling snowfields on the surrounding peaks. In cloud, everything becomes pewter: walls, faces, animals, distant ridges, even smoke. At sunset the stone briefly warms to rose and ocher, revealing the many hands that have shaped it—chisel marks, stacked courses, patched cracks, old blocked windows, new parapets rising over older roofs.

The scale changes the mind. A single district can take a day to cross because of elevation and switchbacks. The greater urban field extends beyond what anyone can see at once. There are inhabited cliffs separated by glaciers, market basins divided by ridges, road systems that descend into fog and climb out two days later into another quarter of the same civilization. Some neighborhoods have never heard the same dialect as others. Some measure the year by the thawing of eastern channels; others by the arrival of salt caravans or the nesting of high birds.

Yet the place changes slowly. Its cycles are patient and repetitive. Walls grow by one layer at a time. A terrace is widened over three generations. A cistern deepens whenever labor is spare. A path becomes a road because enough feet insist on it. Frost breaks a stone; someone turns the broken face inward and builds with it. A dry channel becomes a street, then after an unusually wet decade becomes a channel again, forcing the street to climb to one side. Whole neighborhoods migrate upslope or downslope not in a season but over centuries, following water, avoiding shadow, seeking shelter from new wind patterns.

The rules of the world are practical and severe.

Water belongs first to storage, then to bodies, then to animals, then to plants, then to washing. No roof may shed rain uselessly into the street. No one may foul a melt channel above a cistern mouth. Dung is fuel, not waste. Bones are tools before they are refuse. A wall that blocks wind for one house must not create a drift that buries another. Travelers may sleep in gate shelters, but they must leave a measure of fuel or labor behind. Every child learns the difference between safe snow, crust snow, rotten ice, and cloud so dense it becomes a cliff.

The city has no great monuments in the lowland sense. Its monument is endurance: the stacked habitation of an entire high world, a civilization built from cold stone, animal warmth, caught water, and memory. Beauty appears in small economies—a perfectly fitted threshold, a row of frost-white prayer stones, red wool drying against gray walls, steam rising from a herd at dawn, tiny green shoots under panes of scraped horn, a raven walking along a parapet with the seriousness of an official.

Beyond the outermost neighborhoods, the plateau remains vast and unfinished. Fresh ridges stand where earthquakes have lifted the land. New cracks open after winter. Glaciers grind slowly in high cirques, feeding temporary rivers that flash through empty ravines. There are abandoned construction sites where a spring failed, half-built terraces now used by foxes and nesting birds. There are new settlements beginning wherever dampness proves reliable enough: a ring of stones around a seep, a storehouse, three houses, a wall, a path, a cistern, another roof, another family.

From a distant pass, the whole landscape looks almost natural, as if the city were only another form of scree, another mineral texture of the young mountains. But closer in, there is breath in every crevice. Smoke threads upward. Animals shift in pens. Hands move behind hide curtains. Water ticks into jars. Frost grows on lintels. Somewhere, very far away across the plateau-city, a horn sounds once and is answered after a long delay by another, softer, nearly lost in the cold air.
