At the shoulder of a cold mountain, just below the line where snow stops melting altogether, there is a small city made entirely of plants.

It is not large. From above, if anything could fly high enough through the dim cloud to see it, the place would look like a dark green knot caught in a notch of stone: a compact quarter, a crowded grove, a settlement no wider than a few fields. Its edges are clear. On one side, young gray cliffs rise in broken slabs, still sharp from their recent birth, their faces not yet softened by age. On the other, a steep marshy drop falls away into vapor. Between them, in a shallow basin of cold seep-water and black soil, the plant-city has thickened until there is almost no space left that is not alive.

The ground is not really ground. It is a resilient mat of roots, moss, braided runners, old leaf-felt, and wet bark plates grown over buried stone. Step anywhere and the surface gives slightly, as though the whole place is breathing underfoot. Water lies close beneath it. It trembles in root-hollows, wells up through seams, gathers in thumb-wide channels that shine like pewter in the low light. Every wall weeps. Every stair sweats. Droplets bead along tendrils and the undersides of leaves, then fall in slow, irregular taps into hidden pools.

The air is cold enough to sting the inside of the nose. Breath turns pale immediately. Frost gathers where the mist hesitates, silvering the edges of fern-fans, the lips of pitcher leaves, the roofs of woven boughs. Yet the city is not deadened by the cold. It has learned to hold heat in dark masses: peat-thick avenues, composting cores inside trunk-pillars, dense cushions of lichen and moss that steam faintly at dawn. The warmth is never comfortable, only suggestive — a low biological persistence under the freezing air.

There are streets, though no one paved them. They are corridors left by the pressure of growth, narrow and deliberate, curving between living facades. Hedges have grown into walls, roots into curbs, arched branches into covered passages. Some lanes are so tight that a shoulder would brush wet leaves on both sides. Some pass under vaults of interlocked stems, ribbed like the inside of a chest. Others open suddenly into little chambers: round courts carpeted in moss, dark cistern-gardens, root balconies overlooking lower tangles of green.

The city has levels. Nothing here is flat for long. It climbs the basin in terraces of living matter, each terrace held by woven roots and stiff woody ribs. There are upper walks made of fused branches, slick with condensation. There are underways between root-buttresses where the light turns blue-black and the air smells of loam, resin, and cold water. There are towers, if towers can be grown: columnar shrubs and clustered trunks braided around cores of stone, rising two or three stories above the rest before flowering into dense crowns that catch cloud-water.

Because the settlement is young, its forms are still fresh and urgent. The roots have not yet rounded themselves into comfort. New growth spears into passages, pale and sharp. Bark seams gape where trunks are fusing. Stone recently lifted from the mountain shows through in places, angular and raw, with plants already gripping it. The city feels recently emerged, as though it pushed up after a season of thaw and has been rapidly deciding what it is: grove, nest, ruin, market, organ.

There is no metal here, no cut timber, no fired brick, no lamps, no wheels, no tool marks. The order is urban, but not technological. It is the order of repeated growth, of pathways maintained because leaves avoid certain currents of air, of chambers kept open by dripping water, of walls thickened where wind presses hardest. The city has infrastructure, but it is all vegetal. Rain travels along leaf-gutters into cistern blooms. Meltwater descends through root-pipes into storage hollows lined with waxy membranes. Waste disappears into fungal beds beneath the streets, where it becomes heat, then soil, then leaf. Bridges repair themselves by callusing. Doors are curtains of hanging moss that withdraw slowly when warmed by a body, or stiffen shut in frost.

Light enters with difficulty. The sky above is usually a low ceiling of cloud, and the canopy takes most of what little arrives. The whole place exists in dim greens, blacks, wet browns, and the occasional ghost-pale underside of a leaf. In the deeper lanes, visibility is less a matter of seeing than of recognizing textures: glossy leaf, matte bark, white frost, black water, red-brown root. At midday the city brightens only slightly. At evening it does not so much darken as condense.

There is sound, but it is restrained. The most constant noise is water: dripping from leaf to leaf, ticking into puddles, seeping behind root walls, running in narrow channels under the paths. Sometimes a sheet of accumulated droplets releases all at once from a canopy and patters down like a brief, private rain. The wind rarely enters directly. It moves above the city, making the crown layer groan softly, while below there are only shivers and creaks. Now and then a branch adjusts under its own weight with a muted wooden knock. Somewhere unseen, a hollow stem exhales.

Animals are rare. The cold and density leave little room for them. A few pale insects move slowly in the moss, hard to distinguish from seeds or beads of ice. Slender dark birds visit the upper crowns when the mist thins, but they do not sing much; they click, peck, vanish. Under the rootwalks live small amphibious things with transparent skin and black eyes, tucked into the warm seams where water passes through rot. Their movement is more rumor than presence. No large creature could easily enter the inner lanes. The city is too tight, too full of itself.

And yet it is active. Growth here is slow by the standards of summer lowlands, but relentless. Leaves pivot over hours to catch the weakest light. Tendrils explore gaps. Moss climbs walls grain by grain. New roots descend into water, taste minerals, thicken, and become supports. Fungi pulse through the underside of the settlement, pale networks rearranging nourishment from one quarter to another. After rain, the whole city changes shape slightly. Swollen leaves lower over alleys. Water corridors open. Certain floors become soft and impassable. After a freeze, membranes tighten, passages widen, and frost makes handholds where before there was only slickness.

The place has rules, though they are not written. Warmth rises; therefore the highest chambers are the most fragile and the most coveted by flowering vines. Water owns the lowest routes; nothing permanent grows across them unless it can bend. Open space is temporary and must be defended by shade, cold, or flow. Anything abandoned is swallowed quickly. A courtyard left unused for a week becomes a thicket. A gap in a wall becomes a window, then an eye-shaped scar, then a sealed seam of bark.

At the center is the densest part: a round, sunken plaza filled with shallow black water, surrounded by leaning trunks grown together into a continuous ring. Their roots descend into the pool like pillars in a flooded crypt. Above, branches lace so tightly that only a dim circular glow shows where the sky should be. Droplets fall from the canopy into the pool one at a time, each ripple touching root, wall, root, wall. The water is clear near the surface, but beneath it are layers of submerged leaves and pale root fibers, moving slowly in currents too delicate to see.

This central basin seems to be the reason for the city, or its memory. All lanes eventually bend toward it. All water eventually arrives there, pauses, darkens, and slips away underground. In deep cold, a skin of ice forms across the pool, but it never freezes solid. Something beneath — compost, spring, breath, mineral warmth — keeps it alive.

The city does not feel abandoned, though there are few animals and no people. It feels occupied by growth itself. Every surface is claimed. Every opening is negotiated. The settlement is not a ruin of builders, but a builder without hands: a cold, wet, crowded intelligence of roots and leaves, making streets because flow requires them, making rooms because density needs pockets of air, making towers because cloud-water can be harvested from height.

From the cliffs above, fog descends in slow folds. It enters the upper crowns first, then beads into silver along needles and leaf-tips. The city drinks it silently. For a while the whole place becomes indistinct: walls, paths, chambers, and bridges softened into one dark living mass. Then the mist gathers weight and begins to fall inside the canopy as fine rain.

Nothing dramatic happens. No roar, no swarm, no sudden transformation. Only the continuing processes: seep, drip, root, frost, thaw, rot, bloom. The young stone under the city cracks a little more. Moss finds the crack. A tendril follows. Water remembers the route. And the plant-city, small and packed and cold at the edge of the mountain, becomes fractionally more complete.
