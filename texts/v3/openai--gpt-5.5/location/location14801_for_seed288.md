Below the surface, the land opens rather than closes.

You enter expecting a cave, a narrowing, a throat of stone, but the descent gives way to a broad underplain: a buried basin several miles across, roofed by limestone and old salt, dimly lit by fissures high above. The light that arrives is weak and indirect, strained through cracks, dust, root-fibers, and mineral veils until it becomes a gray-green twilight. Nothing shines. Nothing glitters except the occasional dull plane of salt where an ancient wall has broken open.

There is no water here.

No streams, no pools, no dripping from the ceiling. The air is cool and dry enough that sound seems to scrape through it. Dust lies undisturbed in shallow drifts. The floor is an old evaporated bed, flat in some places as a plaza, warped in others into low ribs and terraces where mineral layers have slumped, split, and settled over enormous time. Underfoot are salt crust, chalky clay, gypsum plates, powdered bone, and old limestone worn smooth not by flowing water but by pressure, breath, claws, passage, and ages of tiny collapses.

The place feels ancient beyond ruin. Not ruined in the dramatic sense — no broken towers, no fresh fall, no violence — but ancient in the way of something that has already spent all its possible catastrophes. The ceiling has finished deciding where it will hold. The pillars have finished thickening. The floor has finished lowering. Everything has been abraded into its final patience.

And yet it is not empty.

Across the basin stands a city without tools.

Its avenues are not built from cut stone, but they are avenues all the same: long pale corridors between buttresses of mineral and packed earth, winding through districts of column, hive, root, and nest. Low walls divide broad courts. Chimneys rise in clusters, some no taller than a person, some stacked twenty or thirty meters high against the cavern roof, made of hardened pellets, salt-cement, chewed fiber, and dust. Galleries lace their sides. Ramps spiral up them. Arches occur where roots have been plastered over by generations of insects until they become ribs of architecture.

From a distance it resembles an abandoned town swallowed underground. From within, it is clear nothing is abandoned. The builders are simply not human.

The vegetation is sparse in arrangement but lush where it takes hold. Life gathers in islands, and each island is dense, fibrous, layered. Pale root-curtains descend from cracks in the roof, braided like old hair, some dry and brittle, others fat with stored life. Around their bases grow mats of gray lichen, waxy rosettes, threadlike fungi, and low, folded plants the color of sage ash and bone. They do not look fresh, but they look determined. Their leaves are narrow, curled inward, furred with dust. Some are translucent at the edges. Some grow in cushions so compact they seem carved.

There are groves here, if one accepts that a grove can be made of roots hanging from above rather than trunks rising from below. There are gardens, too, though no hand tends them: chambers where beetles cultivate dry fungal felt, terraces where pale shoots grow from old dung, hollows where seeds fallen from the surface wait years for the faintest breath of usable damp. The plants live slowly. They open by degrees. They flower rarely, and when they do, the blossoms are small, powdery, and scentless except to the creatures that need them.

The rule of the place is conservation.

Nothing flows freely. Everything is stored, recycled, hoarded, passed mouth to mouth, body to soil, soil to root, root to insect, insect to winged hunter, hunter to dust. Moisture is not seen; it is hidden in tissue, in wax, in eggs, in the cool breath of stone before dawn. A dead animal vanishes quickly, not into rot but into division. Chitin remains. Hair remains. Fine white bones remain for a while, then become part of the floor.

The fauna is everywhere.

Bats crowd the high vaults in black, breathing masses, so numerous they seem like another ceiling. Their noise is immense. Even when they are still, they are not silent: squeaking, rustling, grooming, arguing, shifting claw by claw along the stone. At intervals a whole colony lifts, and the cavern becomes a storm of wings. The air strikes the face in pulses. The sound is deafening, not a single roar but countless dry impacts layered together: leather wings, clicking teeth, chirping echoes, the rattle of beetle shells, the hiss of sand disturbed by running feet.

In the streets below, insects move in organized rivers. Pale ants pour through grooves worn into the salt. Termites maintain their towers with slow ceremonial industry, carrying grains, sealing cracks, opening vents. Crickets sing from every crevice until the stone itself seems to be vibrating. Blind lizards hunt them with patient, mechanical movements. Long-legged cave hares browse the root-falls and leave chains of pellets along the avenues. Small foxlike mammals, thin and large-eared, sleep in abandoned wall pockets and emerge to raid nests. Moths cling in drifts to the shaded faces of pillars, their wings patterned like mineral stains.

The city has neighborhoods defined by species and material.

There is the Root Quarter, where the ceiling lowers and the hanging vegetation forms pale curtains. The ground there is soft with old fiber and husk. The sound is muffled but constant: chewing, scraping, the minute rainfall of dry fragments. Animals move through it as through a market, browsing, hunting, stealing, pollinating.

There is the Salt Ward, open and almost empty, a wide white flat crossed by dark trails. Nothing grows except crusts of black-green lichen in cracks, but the bats come down at night to lick minerals from the floor. Their feet leave thousands of tiny marks. Their calls bounce so sharply across the flatness that distance becomes confusing.

There is the Tower Field, the most urban part of the basin, where insect mounds stand in blocks and clusters, joined by bridges made from roots and hardened earth. Warm air rises from some towers; cool air sinks through others. No mechanism drives this. The architecture breathes because shape permits it. Each mound is repaired constantly, yet the whole district changes almost imperceptibly, a skyline revised grain by grain.

There are old plazas, too — broad depressions where the floor has compacted into smooth clay. Herds of small hoofed cave animals gather there to sleep, their bodies close together for warmth. Predators circle the edges. Overhead, bats pass in such numbers that their shadows thicken the already dim light.

Despite the noise, the landscape itself is slow. The deafening life within it moves quickly in small ways, but the place as a whole changes by eras. A tower leans for centuries before falling. A root dies and remains standing, plastered over by insects until it becomes a column. A crack widens, letting in one more blade of surface light, and an entire new garden begins below it over the course of generations. Salt creeps. Dust settles. Bones whiten. Trails deepen by the width of a claw.

There is little color. The palette is cool and mineral: ash green, gray, white, tan, umber, the faint violet of shadow. The animals bring brief darkness and motion. The plants bring subdued softness. The built forms bring order. Nothing here is extravagant, yet everything is elaborate when looked at closely. A wall is not a wall but thousands of mouthfuls placed by insects. A street is not a street but a consensus of feet. A plaza is not planned but inherited.

The air smells of salt, dust, guano, dry fur, old clay, and the faint peppery scent of crushed lichens. It is not foul except near the roosting vaults, where the ammoniac sharpness can sting the eyes. Elsewhere the air is clean in a desiccated way, like a sealed jar opened after centuries.

There are no machines, no lamps, no wheels, no metal, no fire. The city functions through appetite, instinct, temperature, and habit. Ventilation is made by mound and chimney. Roads are made by migration. Storage is made in seeds, fat, husks, and eggs. Waste is currency. Death is infrastructure.

At the far end of the basin, the ground rises into a long collapsed shelf where the ancient surface has sagged downward. There, the dim roof opens into a chain of narrow chimneys. During certain hours, a colder light seeps through them and catches the hanging roots from above. The roots glow faintly, not with magic, but with the thinness of their skins and the paleness of their stored starch. All the insects in that district alter their routes. The bats grow restless. The crickets lift their volume until the entire cavern becomes a single shrill body of sound.

Then, slowly, the light withdraws.

The city remains: dry, cool, crowded with life, spacious with emptiness, older than memory, still being built by creatures that do not know they are building.
