At the top of the world there is a plateau so broad that from within it no edge can be imagined.

It is not a mountain summit in the ordinary sense, not a peak or chain of peaks, but an elevated continent: a high, cold tableland lifted above the lower atmosphere, planed and opened by ages of wind. Its horizons are far and severe. The sky is enormous, pale blue-white at noon, darkening toward indigo even while the sun is high. Light arrives without softness. It strikes every surface cleanly: salt crust, mica planes, frost-pale stone, the waxed backs of leaves. Shadows are blue and sharp. Distance does not haze so much as shimmer with mineral brightness.

There is almost no water.

No streams cut the land. No ponds gather in hollows. No rain-dark stains mark the cliff faces. The old channels that cross the plateau are not riverbeds but windbeds: long polished troughs where air has moved for thousands of years along the same invisible paths. Basins that might once have held lakes are now white, hard, and hollow-sounding, crusted in salt and borate and pale carbonate. In some places the ground rings faintly under pressure, as if the surface were a lid over a sealed emptiness.

And yet the place is green.

Not everywhere. Never everywhere. The vastness remains mostly open: miles of stone pavement, pale gravel, wind-combed dust, black ribs of exposed basalt, fields of cracked salt like old porcelain. But across this emptiness stand islands of vegetation so vivid they seem improbable, almost ceremonial. Groves appear at long intervals, separated by distances large enough for their own weather. They gather in lee pockets, on mineral seams, along pressure fractures, on the shaded sides of tilted escarpments, wherever the world’s dry chemistry has left something usable.

The plants are lush without being soft.

They do not sprawl in wet abundance. They rise armored, lacquered, intricate. Some are like low forests of jade antler and silver thorn, each branch flattened into blades that turn throughout the day to face or evade the sun. Some grow as thick cushions the size of houses, dark green on the outside and coppery within, breathing through thousands of pinhole mouths that open only when the air cools. Others are tall, reedlike columns with translucent ribs, storing not water but volatile salts and cold-trapped gases in their tissues. When backlit, they glow with internal amber veins.

Their leaves are not tender leaves. They are skins, plates, sails, scales. Many are furred with crystalline hairs that pull trace frost and mineral vapor from the night air. Before dawn, the groves glitter—not with dew exactly, but with a dry rime, a dusting of sublimed brightness that vanishes into the plant surfaces as soon as the sun lifts. The vegetation feeds on light, on wind-borne dust, on salts migrating upward through the fractured plateau. Its roots are not moist threads searching for soil water; they are pale, branching mineral tongues, dissolving stone grain by grain with patient acid and exchanging ions with ancient strata.

The soil, where it exists, is thin and old. It smells faintly metallic, faintly bitter. Much of it is not soil in the lowland sense but a living crust: black-green, ocher, violet-gray, composed of lichenous mats, bacterial films, and mineral membranes that seal the ground against the theft of the wind. Step on it, and it would break like burnt paper. Left undisturbed, it grows over centuries into a tough, patterned skin that holds the plateau together.

The terrain has been settled into itself for a very long time.

Nothing here looks young. Even the sharp rocks have been sharpened slowly. Ridges stand in long parallel files, their windward faces polished and their lee sides feathered with scree. Mesas rise from the plain with undercut bases and broad sunstruck crowns. Their walls are banded: red stone, gray ash, white salt, black glass, greenish mineral seams exposed by ancient uplift and patient erosion. Arches occur, but they are low and thick, more like worn knuckles than delicate spans. Ventifacts lie everywhere, stones carved by blown crystals into blades, flutes, and hollow-eyed shapes.

There are valleys, but they are dry valleys: cold corridors of moving air. They carry dust instead of water, sound instead of current. The floors are smooth with accumulated fines, patterned into ripples and braided streaks by alternating winds. In the mornings, downslope air pours through them like invisible liquid, bringing a faint smell of alkali and leaf resin. By afternoon, the sun warms the pale flats and the wind reverses, climbing upward in long, steady breaths. The plants lean and flex with it. Their blades click softly. Their seed vanes loosen and turn.

Weather here is active but nearly silent.

Clouds form high and thin, not rain clouds but bright mineral veils, sheets of ice-crystal and dust that race across the sky without dimming it much. Sometimes the whole plateau enters a white hour: not fog, not storm, but a suspended brilliance as fine salt particles rise from the basins and fill the air with glitter. During these hours the groves become silhouettes, black-green and motionless, and the sun is a diffuse burning disk. Then the wind shifts, and the whiteness streams away in banners, revealing the land again piece by piece.

At night the cold comes quickly. Heat leaves the stones. The salt flats contract with tiny ticks and pings. The plant mouths open. The groves breathe.

This is when the place is most alive.

From a distance, a grove at night gives off a faint, low radiance, not enough to light the ground but enough to separate its forms from the dark. Pale filaments unfold from the larger plants and extend into the air like root systems turned upward. They comb the passing wind, catching crystals, spores, microscopic mineral grains, the rare trace of frozen vapor. Some plants exhale slowly through vents at their bases, and the cold gases crawl downslope, visible under starlight as a ground-hugging shimmer. By morning these exhalations have left pale halos around the groves, delicate circles of precipitated salts.

The world has rules, and they are strict.

Nothing wastes moisture because there is almost none to waste. Nothing grows quickly unless it grows only briefly. Most life here is conservative, armored, cyclic. It waits through glare and wind, then acts in narrow windows: at dawn, at dusk, during the white hours, under certain moons, when the pressure falls, when the salt crust cracks, when a seam of old mineral breath opens from below.

The lushness is therefore not abundance everywhere, but intensity where life has learned the exact terms of survival.

A single grove may contain hundreds of species layered in strange companionship. At its outer edge grow low mats of dark lichen, forming a windbreak no higher than a hand. Behind them stand thorn-fans with reflective leaves, angled to scatter light downward. Under those fans, in the cooler shadow, grow folded rosettes of blue-black tissue, thick as leather, their centers full of dry crystalline powder. Taller columns rise in the middle, some branching, some smooth, all scarred by wind. Around their bases are tangles of rootlike cords exposed above the ground, braided with fungal sheaths and salt tubes.

The groves make their own microclimates. Within them the air is stiller by a fraction, cooler by a fraction, chemically different. Dust falls out of suspension there. Seeds accumulate. Dead leaves do not rot so much as delaminate, becoming thin flakes that are broken by frost and worked into the crust by microbes. Every grove is a slow engine, harvesting light and stone and wind, building a small green complexity from a nearly empty world.

Between groves lies openness.

The distances matter. A traveler crossing this plateau—if such a traveler existed—would spend most of the time in exposure: bright ground, open sky, silence, the sensation of moving across a planet not made for bodies. The vegetation would appear first as a dark grain at the horizon, then a low stain, then an island of articulated forms. There would be no path to it. No sign of hands. No cairns, no cuts, no smoke, no metal, no shaped object except those shaped by erosion and growth.

Fauna is rare, but not absent.

Small things live in the crust: translucent mites, mineral-shelled worms, slow jointed grazers no longer than a fingernail. They emerge at night to rasp films from stone or drink the chemical exudates around plant roots. Their movements make almost no sound. In some groves, pale mothlike creatures cling to the undersides of leaves during the day, wings folded like flakes of mica. At dusk they lift briefly, drifting rather than flying, transferring pollen between plants that may be separated by hundreds of body lengths but only a few breaths of wind.

Larger animals are seldom seen. There are long-legged herbivores adapted to the emptiness, ash-gray and narrow-bodied, with wide padded feet for crossing salt crusts. They travel alone or in pairs, browsing only the outer growth of a grove before moving on, never staying long enough to exhaust it. Their metabolism is slow. Their eyes are mirrored against the light. They can stand motionless for hours, indistinguishable from weathered branches.

Predators exist as patience.

One kind lies half-buried in gravel on the windward side of groves, its hide patterned like stone, waiting for the rare grazer or the smaller burrowing animals that come to feed on fallen plant scales. Another hunts by scent in the cold hours, moving with such delicacy that its feet leave only shallow stars in the dust. Encounters are infrequent. Hunger here is not dramatic. It is distributed across time, measured in long intervals and careful expenditures.

The loudest events are geological.

A salt pan may split under thermal stress with a crack that runs for miles, a single dry report followed by a whispering collapse as plates settle against each other. A cliff face, undercut for centuries by abrasive wind, may finally shed a slab; the fall booms once, rolls, scatters, then leaves the silence larger than before. During seasonal pressure shifts, deep fissures exhale from the plateau’s interior. The air emerging from them is cold and mineral-rich, and vegetation around such vents grows strange: pale, tall, almost glassy, with leaves like frozen flames.

These vent-fields are among the most unusual places on the plateau.

From afar they look like mirages of forests, vertical and colorless, rising out of broken black stone. The plants there do not photosynthesize in the usual way alone. They also feed on the chemistry of the exhaled gases, their root systems descending into cracks warm with hidden reactions. Around the vents, the ground is stained yellow, green, and violet by mineral blooms. There is a faint odor of struck flint and bitter resin. The air trembles slightly, though it remains cold.

No water flows there either. The life is built on other exchanges.

The plateau’s seasons are defined less by rain and drought than by light angle, wind direction, and atmospheric thickness. In the bright season, the sun rides high and the plants armor themselves, reflecting much of the glare. Their colors become pale: sage, silver, blue-green, white-edged. Growth slows aboveground while roots work downward into mineral seams. In the long cool season, when the sun lowers and nights lengthen, the groves darken and become more active. Spores lift from them in faint clouds at dawn. Seeds detach on spiral vanes and skate across the hardpan for miles, waiting to lodge against a stone, a crack, the skeleton of an old plant.

Death is visible but clean. There is little decay in the wet sense. Dead plants remain standing for years, bleached and hollow, their tissues converted into lattices of salt, cellulose, and mineral glass. Wind plays through them. They become nurseries for lichens, shelters for insects, traps for dust. Eventually they fracture and fall, and the grove grows over them, incorporating their minerals back into the living crust.

The atmosphere of the place is one of brightness and restraint.

Nothing gushes. Nothing crowds. Life is present but spaced, deliberate, exact. The land feels untouched not because it is pristine in a fragile, decorative way, but because it has never admitted interruption. It has its own balances: wind against crust, light against pigment, salt against root, cold against metabolism, emptiness against green intensity.

Sound travels far. Most of the time there is only the thin movement of air over stone. Sometimes the dry leaves of a grove make a faint clicking, like distant insects or cooling metal. Sometimes seed vanes rattle across salt flats. Sometimes, in the predawn, an unseen animal gives one short call—hollow, questioning—and the plateau absorbs it without echo.

The vastness remains.

Beyond one grove are flats, beyond the flats a line of mesas, beyond the mesas another basin, another escarpment, another scattering of green islands under the white sun. The same processes repeat, but never identically. Some basins are nearly sterile, pure glare and salt. Some ridges are black with lichen on their shadowed sides. Some vent-fields glow faintly at night. Some groves are ancient, their central plants wider than towers, though no tower has ever been built here. Some are young stains around a crack no wider than a hand.

From above, the plateau might look sparse: a pale high continent stippled with dark living constellations.

From within, each constellation is a world.
