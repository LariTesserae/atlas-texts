At this height the world is too bright.

The plateau rises above most weather, not as a flat table but as a continent of new stone, a lifted skin of black basalt and pale green mineral glass still showing the violence of its birth. Ridges stand sharp enough to look unfinished. Cliffs break in clean planes. Scree fields glitter with fresh fractures, as if the mountains have only recently cooled and have not yet learned to slump. The horizon is very far away, yet never empty: it is crowded with serrations, ice-bright shelves, vapor columns, forests, animal movement, and the hard white blaze of a sun that seems closer here than it should.

Cold rules everything. It is not a dead cold, not the stillness of a polar desert, but a cutting, active cold, full of breath and condensation. Every surface smokes faintly when struck by light. The rocks wear rims of frost in the shadows and slick skins of thaw where the sun reaches them. Water exists in interruptions: not lakes, not broad rivers, but seams, droplets, runnels, sudden wet chambers under mats of vegetation, meltwater threads falling down black grooves and vanishing into roots. Rain comes rarely and sideways, more like a decision in the air than a storm, a fine metallic precipitation that beads on leaves, shells, feathers, carapaces, and the rubbery hides of things too large to see all at once.

The vegetation is lush but compressed by altitude and cold. Nothing wastes height unless it must. Forests here are not tall in the usual way; they are dense, folded, armored. The dominant growth lies in layered mats over the stone, sometimes three or four meters thick, with living surfaces above and older, darker growth beneath. There are stiff mosses like green wire, blue-black lichens plated as scales, low shrubs whose leaves are thick as tongues, and transparent reeds that fill with light until they glow like tubes of ice. In hollows protected from the wind, the vegetation erupts upward in tangled thickets: fernlike crowns, waxy fans, bladdered vines, cold-blooming flowers with glass petals that ring softly when struck.

Everything is packed. There is no true bare ground except on the newest breaks of stone, and even there spores have already silvered the cracks. Roots enter the rock before the frost can widen it. Fungus ropes bridge crevices. Curtains of fibrous algae hang from overhangs where condensation gathers, trembling with insects or things like insects. The air itself seems occupied: pollen grains, ice dust, drifting seed-wings, tiny translucent grazers, vapor, shed scales, electrical threads, the suspended debris of too much life.

From a distance the plateau looks industrial.

There are no buildings. No roads, no machines made by hands, no walls, no chimneys assembled from parts. Yet the land behaves as if industry has emerged without builders. Vast biological engines throb under the mats of vegetation. Root systems pump meltwater from pocket to pocket with a steady peristaltic force. Forests exhale in timed pulses, releasing steam from thousands of small vents along the slopes. Mineral terraces click and clatter as frost-driven plates expand in the sun and contract in shade, feeding ore-rich dust into hungry root mouths. Some cliffs are striped with colonies of metallic lichens that separate elements from the stone, laying down coppery, lead-gray, and sulfur-yellow bands in precise, repeated thicknesses.

In the largest basins, the cold gardens run like factories. Not quickly; nothing here is quick. Their production is slow, patient, continental. Acres of bladder plants inflate over months, drawing thin water from fog and melt, concentrating it with dissolved minerals, then passing it through root-valves into lower mats where pale fungal membranes refine it into antifreeze sap. Herds of heavy animals move through these basins along ancient feeding circuits, crushing older growth and pressing it into dark heat. Under them, bacterial furnaces smolder. The ground gives off a faint warmth and a smell of iron, resin, wet wool, and ozone.

The sound is immense.

Because the place is so high and open, sound travels strangely. It does not vanish into distance; it layers. There is the constant low drone of wind dragged across ridges and through reed fields. There is the creak of frost in stone, the crack of new fractures, the slow avalanching hiss of gravel falling into unseen clefts. There are the pumps below the vegetation, a muffled hydraulic beating like the pulse of an enormous submerged animal. There are animal calls everywhere: whistles, booms, rasping bellows, wing-thunder, the dry clicking of mandibles, the choral grinding of shell-plates. At times the whole plateau seems to hum with labor, though no one is working.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Every niche has been filled, and most niches have been subdivided beyond reason. Small white grazers comb frost from moss filaments with comb-like lower jaws. Beetle-bodied things the size of helmets move in tight ranks through lichen fields, scraping mineral films and leaving clean black stone behind them for a day or two before life recolonizes it. Transparent winged swarms gather in sunshafts, each body flashing with internal prisms. Long-necked browsers kneel to feed, their backs furred with secondary ecosystems of moss, mites, and flowering parasites. Dense flocks of highland birds—or animals using feathers for some other purpose—settle over the reed beds until the plants disappear beneath them, then rise all at once in a roaring sheet.

Larger creatures are present but often mistaken for terrain. A gray ridge shifts and reveals a row of breathing slits. A snow-crusted boulder opens a black eye. What looked like a fallen log flexes many paired limbs and moves, almost imperceptibly, to a better patch of light. Some of the largest animals stand for days with their feet buried deep in the vegetation mats, drawing sugars through symbiotic roots while flocks nest in their heat vents. Their calls come hours apart: single notes so deep they loosen frost from the cliff faces.

Predation is not rare, but it is seldom dramatic. A hunter may take a week to close a distance. Pale, jointed things move through the undergrowth with the patience of glaciers, each step placed after long calculation. Nets of sticky filament grow between shrubs and are tended by blind, many-legged animals that feel vibrations through acres of line. Carnivorous plants fold over nesting colonies by fractions of a degree each day. The slow pace does not make the place gentle. It makes danger difficult to notice until it has already included you.

Light dominates. The sky is a hard, thin blue-white, and the sun strikes the plateau with almost mineral force. Snowfields and wet leaves double it. Ice lenses embedded in the soil catch it and scatter it upward, so shadows are never fully dark. Many organisms have adapted by becoming reflective, translucent, or deeply pigmented. Some leaves are mirrored on top and black beneath. Some animals carry matte crests to shade their own eyes. In the brightest hours, the whole landscape fractures into glare: frost, mica, wet bark, glass petals, insect wings, exposed bone, metal-rich lichen.

But the brightness does not mean warmth. Under the light, water freezes in shaded footprints. Breath becomes a white cloud and hangs low before being pulled apart. The wind has teeth. It pours over the high passes, carrying the smell of distant ice and living resin. In exposed places the vegetation hunkers down and forms tight aerodynamic skins, but in cracks and basins it grows extravagantly, taking advantage of any shelter, any seep, any warm exhalation from the earth.

The terrain is young enough to resist softness. Valleys are narrow, often no more than splits in the uplifted crust. Rivers, where they exist, have not yet made peace with gravity. They plunge, vanish, reappear as chains of dark pools under vegetation, then emerge from cliff faces in silver threads that freeze at their edges. Waterfalls hang in partial suspension, half liquid, half ice, their lower portions hidden by ferns and vapor. Talus slopes are angular and unstable, but already stitched by roots and tunneled by burrowing colonies. The land is still deciding where drainage belongs.

In places, recent emergence is obvious. Entire shelves of stone stand raw and uncolonized except for first lichens blooming in circular stains. Volcanic glass lies in twisted ropes. Fresh cones smoke faintly, not with fire but with warm mineral breath. Around them the cold jungle thickens, feeding on new chemistry. The boundary between lifeless and overgrown can be as narrow as a handspan: bare black rock on one side, crowded green machinery on the other.

The weirdness of the place is not ornamental. It is structural. The plateau’s ecology seems to obey rules adjacent to familiar ones but not identical.

Here, light is a nutrient almost as material as water. Many animals bask not for warmth but for charge, their hides patterned with photovoltaic scales that feed internal electrochemical organs. During clear days, herds turn in unison like fields of heliostats, angling their bodies to the sun. At dusk they discharge into the ground through root networks, and the vegetation stores the current in swollen nodules. If one walks near those nodules at night, one hears faint ticking and sees blue sparks crawling between stems.

Here, decomposition is a manufacturing process. Dead matter does not simply rot. It is sorted. Beetles remove mineralized tissue. Fungi strip proteins into translucent ribbons. Root-mills grind bone and shell into powder. Wet hollows bubble as microbial vats separate salts, acids, and oils. The products feed different guilds. The landscape is full of supply chains, but none were designed. They accumulated out of hunger, cold, and time.

Here, migration is vertical as much as horizontal. In bright seasons, swarms climb the air itself, riding thermal columns rising from dark rock. In colder phases, they descend into root caverns and animal fur. Some species spend part of their lives as frost patterns, their bodies reduced to dormant crystalline films on stone, rehydrating when condensation returns. Others exist as colonies distributed across many hosts, thinking—if it can be called thinking—through chemical messages passed by parasites.

Despite the density, change is slow. The plateau does not bustle so much as grind. Days are full of movement, but the true motions are seasonal, geological, almost doctrinal. Vegetation mats migrate downslope by centimeters. Herd circuits widen over generations. A basin becomes wetter over a century as roots dam meltwater under themselves. A ridge is rounded not by rain but by the repeated anchoring and release of frost moss. New stone emerges, is stained, is furred, is occupied, is consumed. Nothing happens once. Everything is process.

Weather forms inside the scale of the place. One can see, across impossible distances, separate climates standing on the plateau like nations: a bright dry ridge under violent sun; a fog-filled basin where condensation rains from the undersides of leaf mats; a storm trapped against a wall of new peaks, its snow falling upward for moments in the wind; a blue shadow valley where hoarfrost grows all day. Clouds do not merely pass over. They are combed, harvested, and altered by the living surface. Forests induce fog. Animal herds darken snow and create warm plumes. Vast reed beds release aerosols that seed tiny local storms. The plateau makes its own weather because it is large enough, high enough, and alive enough to interfere with the sky.

At night the brightness changes character but does not disappear. The sun drops behind the serrated horizon, and for a short while the world becomes silver: frost, fur, mineral glass, the pale undersides of leaves. Then the stored light begins to return. Root nodules glow dull amber. Lichen bands shine green along the cliffs. Animals open lateral organs that pulse with cold blue fire. The industrial impression strengthens in the dark: a continent of slow engines, lit panels, exhaust vents, signal lamps, humming conduits. But still there are no structures, no masters of the system, no ruins, no tools. Only life and stone behaving with the complexity of machinery.

The loudness deepens after dark. Winged swarms fold into the vegetation, but subterranean pumping grows stronger. Long-distance calls pass from basin to basin. Ice cracks. Something huge exhales steam. The reed fields rattle in waves. Beneath it all is a continuous vibration at the edge of hearing, the sound of innumerable organisms moving fluids, grinding minerals, flexing fibers, storing charge, freezing and thawing and feeding.

To cross this place would be less like traveling through wilderness than entering an organism too large to recognize as alive. There are no paths, but there are flows. There are no factories, but there is production. There are no cities, but there is no emptiness. The high cold continent is packed to its edges with presences, every surface claimed, every drop of moisture contested, every beam of light caught and used.

And yet, because everything essential happens slowly, the first impression may be stillness: a brilliant frozen upland, sharp and young, crowded with low green growth under a hard sky. Only after standing there for a while does the stillness separate into motions. The moss tightens. The rocks click. The air fills with wings. The ground pulses underfoot. A distant ridge lifts its head.
