At the top of the structure there is a garden no larger than a village square, though it stands higher than most mountains.

It is not on a mountain. There is no rock underfoot, no soil older than the thing that holds it. The place is built all the way down, if “down” can be said to end anywhere. Beneath the gridded floor and the root-vats and the corroded service ribs, there is a depth of braced metal, ceramic piling, hollow elevator shafts, cable bundles thick as tree trunks, and cooling chimneys descending into cloud. The whole place is a manufactured summit: a small, warm, damp chamber of life perched at an extreme altitude on the crown of an industrial tower.

The garden itself occupies a shallow basin, oval and bounded, perhaps two hundred paces from one end to the other. Its edge is marked by a waist-high rail of green-black bronze, polished bright on the side where hands or paws or maintenance drones have brushed it for generations. Beyond the rail is immediate height: pale air, passing cloud, and the dizzy vertical drop of the tower’s exterior, where steam vents and maintenance ladders vanish into brightness. Sometimes the cloud is below, sometimes level with the railing, sometimes passing through the garden in ragged white sheets that bead on every surface and make the leaves shine.

There is no wilderness here, but there is lushness. It grows from troughs, sleeves, wall-slots, hanging cisterns, gutter-nests, cracked insulation, and mesh panels where rooting fibers have been invited or tolerated. Broad-leaved vines climb the inside of the basin walls and spill over pipes. Ferns grow from the seams of access hatches. Spongy moss mats cover old pressure gauges. Warm condensation gathers under the overhead ducts and falls in fat drops, each drop darkening the metal leaves of engineered shrubs that have adapted, or been altered, to drink from vapor.

The ground is not ground. It is a deck of perforated iron plates, ceramic tiles, grates, rubberized walkways, and old conveyor slats, patched again and again until no original surface remains visible. Some plates have sagged into shallow depressions where rainwater collects. These puddles are small, tea-colored, oily at the edges, and full of life: wriggling larvae, translucent shrimp-like things, thread algae, floating scales of pollen, the tiny dark commas of newly hatched amphibians. The water comes intermittently. There are brief warm rains, sudden as breath on glass, when clouds snag on the tower and empty themselves. There are longer dry intervals, though never truly dry, because every pipe sweats, every coolant line weeps, every surface exhales moisture at dawn and dusk.

The air is warm, not tropical exactly, but close and blood-temperature near the machines. It smells of wet leaves, mineral scale, hot lubricant, old metal, sour pollen, and the faint clean sting of ozone. When the wind changes, another scent arrives from the vents below: charred dust, alkali steam, something like burnt sugar and engine grease. Nothing here is raw. Even decay has been processed. Fallen leaves slide into drainage slots and are carried to digesters; the digesters breathe warmth back through the floor; the warmth feeds roots threaded through manufactured loam; the roots bind the old machinery more tightly in place.

The place is small, but it contains a great emptiness. The basin itself is not crowded. Vegetation flourishes mostly along edges, verticals, and prepared channels, leaving the center strangely open: a bare circular expanse of worn plating with only a few puddles, a rusted inspection pedestal, and the skeletal frame of some obsolete hoist. Space gathers there. The plants remain at the margins like an audience. Above, there is no canopy except the passing vapor and the suspended service arms that arc overhead from the rim—long, slow, jointed booms with nests of cable hanging from them. Below the grates, one can see empty shafts dropping through the structure, stacked darkness relieved by occasional red lamps and the slow pulse of fans.

The sound is enormous.

It does not arrive as a single noise but as a pressure in the bones: turbines laboring in the tower core, intake throats roaring at lower levels, water hammer in vertical pipes, elevator counterweights grinding in their housings, relays clapping like iron insects, cooling fans thudding in vast synchronization. Somewhere beneath the garden, a compressor cycles every few minutes with a boom so deep it seems less heard than experienced as a change in weather. Steam vents shriek along the rim. Loose panels tremble. Railings sing at certain wind angles. In the puddles, the surface quivers constantly.

The deafening machinery makes the abundant animal life seem stranger, because none of it behaves as though quiet ever existed.

Birds nest inside the horns of dead loudspeakers and in the warm mouths of ventilation cowls. They have bright, blunt beaks for prying grubs out of filter cloth. Their calls are visible more than audible: throats flashing, crests lifting, wings snapping open in ritual display. Small lizards with coppery skin bask on heat exchangers. Moths the size of hands cling to condensation screens, their wings patterned like warning labels. Beetles graze on algae growing over painted hazard stripes. Pale frogs live in the drainage channels, their bodies almost translucent, their lungs pulsing under skin veined with green. There are slow, soft-bodied creatures too—snail-like scavengers with magnetite shells that cling to vertical metal and scrape rust, lichen, and mineral bloom from the walls.

Many animals here are marked by the machinery. Some carry metallic dust in their feathers. Some have learned to time their movements to vibration cycles: insects lift from the moss just after the compressor boom; frogs breed during the brief lull before the evening turbine shift; birds court on rails that shudder at a frequency too low for predators to perch comfortably. Their abundance does not fill the space. It flickers at the edges, in seams and warm pockets, as if life here has accepted that volume belongs to machines and only surfaces may be inhabited.

The light is ordinary in the most peculiar way. There is no magical radiance, no perpetual neon gloom. Daylight falls cleanly into the basin when clouds permit it. Mornings are white and diffuse, with beads of water bright on every cable. Midday is golden and hot, sharpening the shadows of leaves on steel. Afternoon brings a gray thickness as vapor rises from below. At night, industrial lamps come on with reluctant buzzing: sodium amber over the central deck, cold blue inspection strips along the rail, small red beacons marking the maintenance arms. But even then the darkness above and beyond remains natural, full of stars when the air clears, astonishingly close because of the height.

The surfaces reveal long establishment. This was not built yesterday, nor recently abandoned, nor recently reclaimed. It has arrived at a settled state. The erosion is not catastrophic. It is a mature weathering, a negotiated wearing-down. Rain has drawn dark vertical stains from bolt heads. Moss has softened the corners of concrete housings. Metal has flaked, sealed, flaked again, and been painted over in colors no longer standard. The rail is pitted but intact. The deck plates flex but do not fail. Every leak has made its own ecology; every patch has become habitat; every obsolete machine has been assigned a second function by roots, animals, condensation, and time.

Nothing in the garden appears wholly natural, and nothing wholly artificial remains obedient.

The trees—if they are trees—grow from cylindrical tanks riveted to the floor. Their trunks are braided, glossy, and ribbed like hoses. Their leaves are broad and dark, with small stamped numbers faintly visible along the veins, as though the pattern of manufacture was inherited into living tissue. Some branches end in valve-like flowers that open when humidity rises, releasing a smell of pepper and wet cloth. Vines follow cable runs with suspicious precision, wrapping junction boxes but never crossing active contacts. Moss forms square patches over old sensor grids, growing in geometric fidelity to the circuitry beneath.

At the north edge of the basin stands the slowest machine: a vertical wheel half-buried in vegetation, perhaps twenty meters tall, turning once every several hours. Its rim passes through a curtain of dripping roots. Scoops along its circumference lift small amounts of water from a lower cistern and spill them into a high trough, from which the garden is fed by gravity. The wheel groans continuously, but compared to the turbines below its motion is meditative. Ferns have colonized its spokes. Birds ride it for minutes at a time, stepping casually from spoke to spoke as it rises.

The garden’s rules are simple, though never written in one place.

Do not block the drains; the floor remembers floods.

Do not cut the red vines; they carry sensor filament as well as sap.

Do not stand on the central plate when the deep horn sounds; pressure releases through seams there, and the air can lift a body.

Do not drink from still puddles with a silver skin.

Do not sleep against the warmest pipes; they sometimes wake.

Do not mistake emptiness for safety. The open center is kept open by invisible needs: access paths for machines, airflow columns, old alignments of crane and hatch. Anything left there too long is nudged, shaken, flooded, dried, or quietly grown over until it migrates to the edge.

The entire place changes slowly. The drama is not in sudden storms, though storms come. It is in accumulations: mineral teeth forming under leaks; roots thickening around a pipe over years until the pipe and root become inseparable; animal trails polishing the same curve of railing; a maintenance arm lowering one degree each season as its bearing wears; a puddle becoming a pondlet, then a reed bed, then being drained by a newly opened crack. The garden seems stable because its changes are patient. It has already survived whatever first purpose built it. It has already absorbed generations of repair, neglect, adaptation, and return.

Sometimes a rain cloud passes directly through.

Then the whole summit vanishes into warm gray. The machines continue their deafening labor unseen. Leaves appear only at arm’s length, black-green and shining. Birds become flashes of motion in vapor. Water runs from ducts in sudden strings. The grates hiss. The great drop beyond the rail disappears, and the basin feels briefly like an enclosed room: a loud, wet, living room at the top of the world, walled by mist and machinery.

When the cloud thins, the height returns all at once.

The rail shines. The abyss opens. Far below, other portions of the constructed world protrude through cloud layers: stacks, platforms, bridges, exhaust crowns, the faint geometry of industrial terraces built down the tower’s sides. They are too distant to seem connected, and yet the vibration underfoot proves they are all one body. The garden is only a small organ on that body, a damp green lung grown in a manufactured socket.

It is empty in the way a bell is empty: hollow so it can resound.

It is lush in the way an old engine can be lush when every leak feeds moss, every heat source breeds insects, every abandoned conduit becomes a burrow, and every surface has had time to learn weather.

It is not natural, but it has become ecological.

It is not quiet, but it has rhythms.

It is not large, but it stands in so much air that the smallness of it feels ceremonial. A bounded grove of metal, vapor, leaves, animals, and roar; a high warm pocket of life maintained by slow machines; a made summit weathered into belonging.
