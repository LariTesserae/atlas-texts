At this height the world has been worn almost smooth.

It is not a mountain range anymore, though it must once have been one. The old peaks have been eaten down into a vast high tableland, a continent of rounded stone backs and shallow basins lifted into warm, thin air. The horizon is far away and low, almost circular, with no sharp summits to interrupt it—only long swells of land, like the backs of buried animals, all softened by ages of rain, root, heat, and wind. The place feels older than weather. Not barren, not dead, but finished: a landscape that has arrived at its final shape and now only breathes.

Almost every surface is plant.

Not forest in the ordinary sense. There are few trunks, few high canopies, few things that stand upright. The vegetation has become the ground itself: mats, skins, cushions, ropes, pads, scales, wet felt, living thatch. The stone is visible only where something has recently torn open—at the lip of a dry wash, under the foot of a landslip, inside a cracked basin where water once stood. Everywhere else the plateau is upholstered in green, bronze, yellow-black, and reddish violet growth.

Some plants grow like old leather stretched over rock. Some are springy as packed moss but tough enough to blunt a blade. There are broad colonies of low succulent leaves arranged in interlocking plates, each plate rimmed with fine hairs that collect condensation at dawn. There are vine-fields no higher than the ankle, braided so tightly they can carry the weight of a person, then suddenly parting over hidden hollows. There are pale root curtains hanging from undercut ledges, not feeding from soil but from the air, sieving fog and insect dust. In the shallow depressions, where rain gathers for a few days after storms, the plants swell and shine, becoming translucent, storing water in bladders and swollen stems. Then the basins dry, and the living floor wrinkles back into a dull, dusty hide.

The air is warm, surprisingly warm for such elevation, but not heavy. It has a highland clarity: sunlight comes down clean and ordinary, neither magical nor dim, yet everything it touches looks exposed. The sky seems too large. Clouds travel fast, casting shadows that move over miles of low vegetation like the passage of invisible herds. In the mornings the ground smokes with condensation. Droplets bead along every hair, every thorn, every woven stem. By noon, the beads are gone, pulled inward by the plants or lifted away by the wind. By afternoon, heat shivers above the mats, and the whole plateau smells of bruised leaves, mineral dust, and animal breath.

Water is never absent, but it is rarely gathered. It appears as a brief event: rain ticking across the living surface, fog snagged in the root curtains, seepage darkening the sides of old stone bowls, trickles vanishing into green seams. There are riverbeds, but most are dry most of the time—broad, shallow, braided scars lined with darker growth. When rain falls in the far reaches of the plateau, water arrives hours later as a low brown sheet sliding over plant-mats, filling hoofprints and burrows, lifting seeds, larvae, skins, dung, and pollen into temporary streams. Then, just as quickly, the flow breaks apart. Puddles remain in the hollows. Animals gather. The plants drink downward and inward. The river becomes memory again.

Despite the enormous openness, the place is not quiet.

It is deafening.

The sound does not come from large bodies or obvious crowds. It rises from the living ground itself and from the air above it: rasping, clicking, whistling, buzzing, shrieking, throat-drumming, wing-beating, seed-popping, scale-rubbing, claw-scraping. Insects live in such abundance that they become a weather system. They boil up from the mats in sheets when disturbed, glittering in sunlight like blown grit, then drop back into the vegetation and vanish. Beetles the color of dark oil chew channels through succulent stems. Long-legged hoppers spring by the thousands across open flats. Tiny winged things cluster over every damp patch until the air trembles. Their noise is continuous, layered, physical. It presses against the ears. Even at night, when the sunlight drains from the plateau and the stones release their stored warmth, the chorus changes rather than ceases: deeper pulses, dry rattles, amphibian clicks from hidden water pockets, far cries from hunting animals.

There are animals everywhere, but the land still feels empty because distance remains huge and vertical structure is scarce. A person can see for miles and see almost no trees, no dense shelter, no walls—only low growth and the moving marks of life. Herds appear first as shimmer, then as dust, then as hundreds of narrow backs grazing across a slope. Small antlered creatures crop the plant-skin in lines, their muzzles stained green and red. Thick-bodied grazers kneel to tear up mats with shovel-like teeth, exposing mineral soil for a day before creepers begin to seal the wound. Bands of long-necked browsers move from one rare standing thicket to another, stripping the leathery leaves of plants that grow around old springs.

Predators hunt in the open because there is nowhere else to hunt. Some lie flat and patterned against the vegetation, almost indistinguishable from the mats until they erupt. Some run in low packs, using the shallow folds of the ancient terrain as cover. Some hunt from the air: wide-winged shapes circling in the ordinary blue light, descending so slowly they seem to be falling asleep, then dropping with sudden violence into the insect haze or onto the backs of young herd animals. Scavengers are never far behind. Neither are parasites. The abundance of fauna is not picturesque; it is relentless. Everything feeds, breeds, sheds, calls, tunnels, stains, molts, and is eaten.

The ecology is built around interruption. Dryness is the default skin of the world, but moisture is the command. When rain comes, life accelerates with almost frightening coordination. Dormant bulbs split the surface overnight. Flat lichens unroll into frilled cups. Buried eggs hatch in the temporary pools. Frogs or frog-like things, unseen for months, begin calling from water no deeper than a footprint. Insect towers rise from the mats, made of chewed plant fiber and saliva, only to sag and collapse when the sun returns. Seeds with corkscrew tails drill themselves into wet felt. Flowers open for a single afternoon, low to the ground and intensely colored, then are stripped by mandibles before evening.

The plants are not passive scenery. They make the rules here.

Nothing stays exposed for long. Bone is furred over. Stone is netted. A dropped tool becomes a trellis. Tracks persist only if the ground was dry and crusted; otherwise they close, slowly and visibly, as tendrils lean into the depressions. The living mat repairs itself after grazing, after fire, after flood. It does not grow upward much, as if height were an extravagance the plateau learned to abandon long ago. Instead it grows across, around, through. The old land has been flattened by erosion, and the life upon it has accepted flatness as a principle. To survive here is to spread, cling, braid, store, and wait.

There are scattered signs of people, or something near enough to people.

They do not dominate the landscape. They barely interrupt it. Low shelters made of stacked stone and woven plant-rib stand on rare knobs of exposed rock, placed where the mats are thin and drainage is good. From a distance they look like darker stones. Their roofs are thatched with cured vine-sheets, weighted by flat slabs against the wind. Some are abandoned and half-swallowed, their walls threaded with roots, their doorways narrowed by creeping growth. Others still show use: scraped paths, drying racks, piles of cracked seedpods, skins stretched between bone pegs, smoke-blackened interiors smelling of ash and bitter herbs.

The technology is hand-made, patient, local. Stone blades, bone awls, fiber nets, digging sticks hardened in fire. Clay vessels are rare because clay is rare; most containers are gourds, shells, woven bladders, or hollowed woody stems sealed with resin. Metal, if known, is precious and small: a copper hook, a hammered needle, a green-stained pendant traded from lower lands. People here build lightly because permanence is difficult. The plants take back anything still. The insects take the rest.

Old paths cross the plateau, though many are less roads than agreements with the ground. They follow raised ribs of stone beneath the plant-skin, detour around deep mats that can swallow a leg, and pass from water-basin to water-basin. Along them stand occasional markers: forked poles, cairns, carved stones so eroded their symbols have become shallow shadows. Some mark safe springs. Some mark places where the mat is hollow. Some are warnings about nesting grounds, predator dens, or fields of plants that close when stepped on.

There are strange features, but the strangeness is modest, almost biological.

In certain basins the plants grow in perfect rings, each ring a different color and texture, as if the water remembers previous shorelines and instructs the vegetation accordingly. In some hollows, a footfall sends a tone traveling through the mat—a deep stretched hum that moves outward for hundreds of paces, causing insects to lift in a synchronized cloud. There are ancient stone pavements, naturally cracked into hexagons, where root-hairs gather only in the seams and glow faintly after rain, not with light exactly but with a pale wet reflectiveness that makes night animals avoid them. There are ridges where no animal will cross at noon, though they cross freely at dawn and dusk. There are flowers that open only to thunder.

The wind has its own behavior. Because the plateau is so high and open, air moves constantly, but the living surface breaks it into layers. At ankle-height it carries warm plant smell and the powder of spores. At chest-height it is cleaner, faster. Above that, it belongs to birds and flying insects. During storms, wind runs low and hard, flattening the vegetation until the whole land streams in one direction. Rain follows in slanted bands. Then the sound becomes almost unbearable: water striking millions of leaves, insects screaming from flooded burrows, animals calling to their young, mats slurping and creaking as they swell.

Afterward, there is a brief shining peace, though not silence. Every surface gleams. The shallow basins hold pieces of sky. Herd animals arrive from beyond the horizon, drawn by scent. Birds land among them to pick parasites from wet hides. Small predators wait near puddles where hatchlings gather. The low plants lift their pores and open their cups. The entire plateau drinks.

At sunset, the openness becomes immense. The light stays normal, golden and then red, but the lack of vertical forms lets it travel without obstruction. Every low ridge is outlined. Every insect swarm becomes visible as smoke. The scattered shelters throw long shadows disproportionate to their size, as if trying briefly to become monuments. Far away, thunder may continue over another basin, another temporary river, another eruption of life.

Night does not cool the land much. The stone holds warmth. The plant-mats exhale dampness. Condensation begins again, quietly, bead by bead. Nocturnal animals step out onto the open flats. Their eyes flash low to the ground. The deafening chorus deepens until it seems not like sound produced by creatures, but like the plateau itself vibrating in its old age.

This is a high, warm, ancient place: worn down, lifted up, almost empty in shape and overwhelmingly alive in substance. A world of immense distances and thin water, where plants have replaced soil, animals have replaced silence, and every built thing is temporary unless it learns to grow.
