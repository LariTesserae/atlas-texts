At the lowest part of the basin, where a river might have wandered if rivers were allowed here, there is a wide constructed marsh.

Nothing underfoot is original. The ground is not ground but a continuous assembly of fired-clay plates, pale shell-lime, woven reed mats sealed with resin, stone blocks dragged from elsewhere, and terraces of compacted brick dust. Even the mud is manufactured: a deliberate mixture of powdered tile, peat, ash, crushed seed husks, and warm water kneaded into dark pads between the walkways. It smells like earth, but too evenly. It has no stones, no old roots, no buried bones unless someone placed them there.

The whole region lies low and bright beneath a white-hot sky. Heat does not shimmer so much as press down. Every surface is lit plainly, without softness: the reed roofs, the wet brick ramps, the standing pools, the limewashed towers that are not tall enough to be towers but still insist on height. Shadows are few and black. The air is heavy with moisture, as if rain has just passed or is always about to, though no storm comes. Water beads on carved lintels and slides down wall faces in slow threads. The rocks weep, but the rocks are blocks set there by hand, pierced with channels so fine that water seeps through them in perfect, patient lines.

The marsh extends farther than a village but not as far as a kingdom. From a raised causeway one can see its order: open flats of shallow water, low platforms planted with thick greenery, empty plazas of pale clay, scattered huts and shrines, pools arranged in chains, causeways crossing them at right angles or in uncertain curves. There is space everywhere. Nothing crowds. A grove stands apart from a field of reeds. A pool lies alone under the sky. A single wall runs for two hundred paces and stops without joining anything. The place feels both planned and unfinished, as if its makers began laying out an entire wetland-city and then accepted the first draft as final.

The vegetation is lush, but it grows from containers, troughs, seams, baskets, clay combs, and floating rafts. Broad-leaved plants rise taller than a person from square basins. Vines climb frames of lashed bamboo and bone-white plaster. Ferns grow in deliberate vertical strips along damp walls, every frond fed by hidden threads of water. Fruit hangs from espaliered trees whose roots are folded into brick cisterns. Reeds, sedges, lilies, thick mosses, sweating pitcher plants, and glossy-leaved shrubs flourish in the heat. Their abundance is undeniable, yet the spacing between them is strange. They do not form a wilderness. They stand like placed thoughts.

There are many animals, though they too seem to obey the quiet.

Small frogs sit in rows along the edges of cisterns, throats still, eyes gold and open. White insects cling to the undersides of leaves in clusters like beads. Long-legged wading birds step through shallow tanks without splashing. Fish move in the narrow canals, but their movement appears decorative, repeating: three silver turns, a pause, three silver turns again. Lizards bask on warm plaster. Snails climb reed poles. Brilliant beetles drift from flower to flower with the unhurried certainty of objects suspended in clear oil.

The place should be loud with heat and life. It is not. There is sound, but little of it: the occasional tick of water falling into water, the hush of a wing, the tiny rasp of a reed against another reed, the damp pop of a bubble rising from manufactured mud. No wind moves across the basin. Leaves do not stir unless touched by an animal, and even then they settle at once. The water does not lap. It holds its surfaces flat and bright, reflecting the sky so intensely that some pools look like holes cut through the world.

The youngness of the place shows everywhere. Edges are sharp. Brick corners have not rounded. Plaster has not flaked. Channels cut in stone are clean and pale inside. The terraces have not slumped. The reed bundles are still yellow at their cut ends. Even the algae appears newly introduced, laid across water in fresh green skins. There are no old ruins here, no layers of repair, no deep stain of generations. Yet the place also feels ancient in another way: not old from weathering, but old because it may never have had a beginning.

The builders used simple means. Their marks remain visible. Fingerprints hardened in clay. Cord impressions on plaster. Peg holes. Wedge scars. Mortar smoothed with palms. Every sluice gate is a plank, every hinge a cord or wooden pin, every lever a branch polished by use. There is no hidden machinery beyond gravity, balance, capillary seepage, and hands. Water is lifted by jars, guided by gutters, stored in cisterns, persuaded through porous stones. The most elaborate devices are woven wheels turned not by current but by people, though no one is seen turning them now.

And still the water circulates.

That is one of the rules of the basin: things continue without cause. A clay spout pours a thread of water into a trough forever, though its feeding jar is dry. Condensation gathers on the underside of a roof in the exact pattern of a vanished morning. A puddle remains after a rain that never falls. A canal has a visible incline, yet water rests in it perfectly level from end to end. In some places, droplets hang halfway down walls, not falling, not evaporating. If touched, they are warm.

The brightness also has rules. It comes from above, but not only from the sun. The plaster glows in response to attention. Pale shells embedded in paths shine as if wet even when dry. Pools reflect objects that are not present: a ladder, a pair of bare feet, a procession of people carrying baskets, a moon at noon. These reflections do not ripple. They wait.

Reality is thin here, but not chaotic. It has broken into a stable pattern.

A walkway may be longer in one direction than in the other. If one counts the clay tiles crossing a pool, there are forty-seven outbound and forty-two returning. A doorway opens into the same courtyard no matter which side one enters from. The horizon bends slightly around certain shrines, making a distant reed bed appear both far away and close enough to touch. Shadows point correctly at first glance, then contradict each other if followed. Some trees bear fruit from seasons that cannot coexist: green buds, yellow flowers, ripe red globes, and dry seed husks all on the same branch, all permanent.

Nothing changes, but everything is in the act of changing.

A heron stands with one foot raised above the water and never lowers it. A dragonfly hovers beside a blue flower with wings too fast to see, yet it remains in the same finger-width of air all afternoon. A vine curls around a pole, its tendril caught halfway through the spiral. The air smells of rain beginning, rain ending, cut grass, hot clay, and opened fruit. These smells do not fade. They occupy fixed territories. Step from one paving stone to the next and the scent of wet ash becomes the scent of crushed mint. Step back and ash returns.

Because the basin is entirely made, ecology here is a craft rather than a history. Each plant has a basin, each insect a niche, each bird a perch, each fungus a damp seam. The food chains are simple but abundant, arranged like woven patterns. Mosquitoes rise from one set of jars; frogs wait beside them; snakes wait under the frog platforms; birds wait on posts above the snakes. Fruit drops into water where fish gather, though the fruit never seems to lessen. White flowers open continuously for moths that never arrive at night, because night does not properly come.

There are signs of inhabitants, or former inhabitants, or makers who have stepped just outside perception. Low houses stand on clay piers above the wet flats. Their walls are woven reed plastered with lime, their roofs layered with palm leaves and sealed with pitch. Doorways are open. Inside are sleeping mats, baskets, grinding stones, shell knives, wooden bowls, cords, drying racks, painted jars. Everything is clean. Everything is ready. Smoke stains rise above hearths, but no embers glow. A pot hangs over a fire that is not burning; inside, water trembles at the edge of boiling without producing steam.

There are plazas too, broad and empty, paved with cream-colored slabs. They are too bright to cross comfortably. In their centers stand arrangements of upright stones, carved not with writing but with repeated marks: waves, eyes, leaves, ladders, open hands. The carvings are fresh. Water collects in them and shines.

At the marsh edge, if it can be called an edge, the constructed ground does not give way to wilderness. It simply stops at a low retaining wall. Beyond the wall is brightness, haze, and a suggestion of more basin, more platforms, more planted water, but looking too long makes the distance rearrange itself. The region extends, but not reliably. It has a town’s intimacy and a valley’s breadth. One can see far across it, yet never reach the farthest visible pool.

The heat is constant. The wetness is constant. The light is constant. Decay is present but paused at its first gesture. Growth is present but held in lush suspension. Every leaf is full, every fruit almost falling, every surface newly damp. The world has not been abandoned so much as set down carefully and forbidden to proceed.

To walk there is to hear your own movement become improper. A footstep on clay sounds too loud, then is swallowed. Your reflection walks beside you in canals, occasionally delayed by a breath. Frogs watch without fleeing. Birds do not call. Somewhere, unseen, a wooden gate knocks once against its frame, though there is no current to move it. After that, quiet returns so completely it seems less like absence than law.

The marsh is artificial down to its mud, primitive in every joint and knot, lush beyond reason, and almost perfectly still. It is not a ruin, not a garden, not a city, not a wetland, but a made lowland where the world’s processes have been imitated by hand and then fixed in place. Water is always just arriving. Plants are always just flourishing. Animals are always just about to speak or leap or feed. The sky is always noon.

Nothing finishes happening here.
