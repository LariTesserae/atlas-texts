At this height the world should be bare, wind-cut, and severe, but it is not. It is warm and dim and green beyond any ordinary meaning of green.

The plateau lies so high that lower clouds scrape its underside and spill up through cracks in the rim like breath through teeth. From below it may look like a mountain range whose summits have been planed flat by an immeasurable hand, a tableland stretching farther than sight or story. But on top, there are no clean horizons. The land is too vast and too overgrown to reveal its shape. Distances are hidden behind layers: leaf behind leaf, reed behind trunk, moss over stone over root over older root. The sky is a low pewter lid, rarely blue, usually veiled by vapor rising from the warm wet ground. Light arrives diluted, sifted through clouds, through canopy, through hanging sheets of lichen and translucent leaves, until noon resembles a long green evening.

Water is everywhere, but seldom as a lake or river with a proper edge. It seeps. It beads. It gathers in the curled cups of leaves large enough to drown in. It slides down bark in slow black threads. It trembles in the pores of stone and in the seams between roots. The ground exhales moisture constantly, as if the plateau rests on a buried sea. There are marshes here without open water, forests that are also sponges, meadows that sway like flooded hair. Walking would be less like crossing land than negotiating a living surface that remembers every pressure placed upon it.

The terrain is ancient in a way that has passed beyond drama. There are no sharp young peaks, no fresh cliffs, no roaring scars of recent catastrophe. Everything abrupt has been softened. Ridges have slumped into long backs. Basins have filled with peat and fibrous humus. Old lava, old reef, old bone-colored stone—whatever the first body of the plateau once was—has been swallowed and reworked until only rounded knuckles show through the plant-mass. These exposed stones weep constantly. Their surfaces are blackened with wet films and pale mineral tears. Some are so eroded they resemble folded skin; others are holed through like exhausted coral, with roots passing through them as if threading beads.

The vegetation is not merely abundant. It is the medium of the place.

There are trees, though “trees” is too narrow a word for many of them. Some rise on buttressed feet from the marshy substrate, their trunks wide and fluted, their bark layered like old paper soaked and dried a thousand times. Others spread horizontally, crawling over the plateau in woody mats, sending up branch-pillars where they find support. In some districts the forest is built from a single organism, or from many organisms grown into such intimacy that distinction becomes ceremonial. Roots fuse with roots. Vines enter trunks and emerge elsewhere with different leaves. Moss climbs into the canopy and descends again as ropes. Ferns grow on fallen branches that are not dead but merely traveling downward into another phase of themselves.

There are plant-arches broad enough for valleys to pass beneath. There are chambers roofed by overlapping leaves, dim and humid as lungs. There are groves where trunks have grown around empty spaces in regular rings, like abandoned meeting halls, except no tool has touched them and no intention can be proved. Some of these rings are old enough that their living columns have petrified at the core while remaining green at the edges. Rain collects in their hollows. Small animals nest in the wet cavities. Fungi the color of bruised ivory glow faintly on the inner walls, not bright enough to illuminate, only enough to suggest that darkness here has depth and texture.

The plateau’s plant life makes architecture without ever becoming artificial. Walls occur where woven stems decide to thicken. Bridges occur where roots seek air over waterlogged ground. Towers occur where dead trunks become scaffolds for new ones, generations climbing the soaked skeletons of their elders. From a distance, in certain vaporous weather, these structures might be mistaken for ruins: ribs of a city, the remains of causeways, terraces, shrine-roofs sagging under leaves. But close at hand they reveal no cut stone, no mortar, no deliberate joining. They are grown, accreted, persuaded by gravity, water, and time.

The few true structures—if they are true structures—are scattered and difficult to classify. On some of the drier rises stand low mounds faced with flat stones, each stone so rounded by age and lichen that its placement may be coincidence. In the reed-marshes are circles of upright poles, but the poles have rooted, flowered, and become trees. In a western basin there is said to be a stair of pale slabs descending into a bog; each step is exactly the height of a human knee, though no human presence otherwise belongs to the plateau. The stair vanishes after twelve steps beneath black water crowded with floating leaves.

These marks feel less like evidence of a lost people than like gestures the landscape once made and then forgot how to repeat.

The warmth is constant but never harsh. It is the warmth of compost, of breath under cloth, of a greenhouse abandoned to its own intelligence. Nights do not become cold; they thicken. Mist rises from the ground in broad unmoving veils. Drops form on the tips of leaves and fall one by one into the undergrowth, producing a patient ticking that can continue for hours after any rain has ended. Sometimes rain falls without sound, a fine vertical condensation, as if the air itself has tired of holding water. Sometimes thunder moves under the plateau rather than above it, a deep muffled turning in the stone.

Wind is rare at ground level. The forest takes it apart before it can become motion. High in the canopy, leaves may rub and flex, but below there is mostly stillness: humid, padded, attentive. Sound does not carry far. A footstep vanishes into moss. A wingbeat becomes a brief tremor among ferns. Water speaks softly from every direction, yet never gathers into a single voice. The loudest sounds are occasional and therefore immense: a branch-column collapsing after centuries of rot; a hidden flock lifting all at once from the canopy; the slow wet sigh of a peatbank giving way into a pool.

Fauna is abundant, but much of it is quiet, concealed, or slow. The plateau belongs to creatures adapted to compression and moisture, to dimness and touch. There are amphibians with leaf-shaped backs that cling to vertical bark and wait through entire afternoons for the passing of gnats. There are pale crabs in the root-pools, far from any sea, feeding on fallen fruit and the soft edges of fungi. There are long-bodied grazers that move through the fernlands on splayed feet, their hides blotched with moss and algae, their horns not for fighting but for parting curtains of vine. They chew endlessly and without urgency, half-visible in steam.

In the canopy live animals rarely seen from below except as weight and shadow. They travel along branch-bridges and root-cables, shaking loose showers of old water. Some sing only during fog, producing low flute-notes that seem to come from the plants themselves. Others communicate by tapping hollow stems. The resulting patterns pass through groves like distant rain, soft, irregular, almost language and almost weather.

Insects are everywhere but not in swarming fury. They work in minute, persistent societies. Some polish the surfaces of giant leaves clean enough to reflect the dim sky. Some farm blue-green films on submerged roots. Some live inside pitcher flowers, emerging at dusk to carry pollen through air so humid their wings must be combed dry after each flight. There are moths with transparent wings that rest on wet stone and disappear; beetles that mimic unopened buds; slow velvet worms that hunt in the moss with threads of glue shining like dew.

Predators exist, but they too are patient. A vine may close over a small animal in the space of an hour. A pool may be lined with carnivorous fronds that respond only to warmth and repeated touch. A bird with a silent, round face waits in the gloom above game trails, so covered in epiphytes during its long stillness that insects colonize its back. Nothing here wastes effort. Hunger is old and damp and methodical.

The ecology is layered into vertical countries.

At the lowest level is the soaked foundation: peat, root-felt, buried wood, black water, mineral seep. Here things are blind or nearly blind. They feel chemical gradients, pressure, heat. They digest the past. Fallen leaves do not simply decay; they enter a long bureaucracy of soft-bodied organisms, fungal threads, microbial skins, root hairs, and water. A fruit that drops into the lower mat may take decades to vanish entirely, feeding hundreds of lives in sequence.

Above that is the knee-high world of ferns, sedges, moss antlers, liverwort fans, and small pools hidden under vegetation. This is where the plateau breathes most visibly. Spores rise in faint clouds when disturbed. Leaves fold and unfold according to moisture. Tiny flowers open during the dimmest part of day, not seeking sun but the attention of specific midges. Trails made by animals close within hours unless continually used. The living surface is elastic, forgetful, and crowded.

The middle level is all columns and curtains: trunks, lianas, aerial roots, hanging mats, shelves of fungus. Here the oldness of the place is most apparent. Many trunks are hollow, not dead, containing interior gardens fed by rain entering through broken crowns. Roots descend from branches but may take centuries to reach soil; some never do, thickening in midair into suspended pillars that host their own moss, their own snails, their own miniature drainage systems. Water falls from leaf to leaf to leaf, losing force each time, until it reaches the ground as scattered drops rather than rain.

The canopy is a continent above the continent. It holds marshes in the sky: accumulated leaf-muck deep enough for shrubs, pools held in branch forks, mats of floating grass rooted in decay. Some animals are born there and never descend. Some trees flower only in the upper fog, where pollinators drift between crowns over distances impossible to perceive from below. The canopy’s surface influences the weather. Warm moisture rises from below, cools against the upper leaves, and returns as private rain. Whole regions of the plateau may be dry beneath while their canopy rains upward and downward at once, cycling water through green tissue before any drop reaches stone.

Because the scale is so great, there are climates within the plateau. The eastern reaches are warmer and more vaporous, with broad marsh-canopies and flowers that smell of pepper and overripe fruit. The central flats are darker, dominated by immense root-lattices and peat domes, their surfaces trembling underfoot. To the north, where the high land tilts toward old glacial scars now erased by vegetation, the forest lowers and spreads into endless moss barrens interrupted by shallow black pools. In the west, eroded stone rises in long whale-backed ridges, each ridge furred with dwarf trees and weeping with mineral springs.

The plateau’s edges are rare and dangerous. Rivers do not rush toward them openly. Water moves hidden through the plant-substrate, gathers in underground seams, then emerges suddenly as curtains from the cliff face far below. The rim itself is often masked by vegetation growing out over emptiness: root-bridges, moss shelves, mats of sedge thick enough to appear like land until they flex under weight. Birds nest in the hanging forests of the cliffs. Plants bloom upside down there, fed by mist and seepage, their petals facing the abyss.

The rules of the place are not the rules of open country.

Nothing remains exposed. A stone left bare will be filmed within days, furred within months, rooted within years. A carcass becomes a garden. A fallen tree becomes a road, then a wall, then a nursery, then a ridge in the ground, then no separate thing at all. Edges are temporary negotiations. The distinction between living and dead matter is not absent, but it is softened, made porous. Death is a change in tenancy.

Dryness is treated as an injury. Wherever sun or wind opens a gap, plants gather around it quickly. Leaves angle to shade wet soil. Moss advances over cracks. Fungal veils spread at night. The place heals toward dampness. Even fire, if it comes from lightning in the canopy, rarely travels far. It smolders in a hollow trunk, steams, is surrounded by wet tissue, and becomes a black mineral pocket that orchids later favor.

Time behaves heavily here. Days are recognizable, seasons less so. There may be flowering cycles that take eighty years, fruiting events that feed whole migrations once in a lifetime, root-bridges that descend toward earth across centuries. A pond fills, becomes a bog, becomes a fern meadow, becomes forest, sinks, and becomes a pond again, but the full cycle is too slow for ordinary memory. The landscape changes as a face changes with age: continuously, intimately, almost imperceptibly until comparison becomes impossible.

There is strangeness, but it is not theatrical. It is embedded in natural processes that have gone on too long in isolation. Some plants remember touch for years, altering their growth around the path of a vanished animal. Some groves flower in response to distant thunder rather than light. There are leaves with veins that pulse visibly, moving water upward even when no wind stirs them. There are root systems that avoid certain stones as if the stones are bitter, leaving circular clearings that remain open for centuries despite the plateau’s hunger to cover all things.

In some hollows, sound is absorbed almost completely. You can stand beneath a dome of interwoven leaves while rain falls hard above and hear only your own blood, the soft creak of plant fibers, the faint click of droplets forming and releasing. In other places, quiet sounds travel strangely. A drip from far away repeats through hollow roots until it seems to come from beneath your feet. The call of a small animal may return minutes later, altered by the green corridors, lower and more mournful, as if answered by something larger that never moves.

The dimness gives everything a submerged quality. Colors are saturated but low: greens tending toward black, yellow moss like old wax, red stems dark as clotted sap, white fungi with the dull gleam of bone under water. Flowers are often pale, not bright, designed for twilight pollinators and mist. When sunlight does break through, it does so in narrow columns that seem almost solid. Leaves steam. Insects rise. Hidden water flashes everywhere at once. Then the clouds close, and the brief brilliance is remembered by the forest only as an increase in growth.

There are open areas, but even openness here is dense. A clearing might be a mat of waist-high sedges stretching for miles over a shallow saturated basin. Mist lies in it like milk. Small deer-like animals move through channels invisible from above, their antlers trailing strands of floating moss. Tall flowering stalks stand at intervals, each one hosting beetles, frogs, nesting birds, and pendulous sacks of seeds. The ground trembles under large steps because beneath the mat is water, peat, and older vegetation compressed into a dark buoyant mass.

In the oldest central region, the plateau has nearly forgotten rock. The land is a living accumulation so deep that roots grow through the remains of ancestral forests without ever touching mineral soil. Here the trees are low but vast, spreading outward in interlocked plates. Their leaves are small and numerous, making a ceiling like scales. Pools appear black not from depth but from dissolved ages. If disturbed, bubbles rise carrying the smell of ancient rain, old pollen, and wood buried before the present canopy existed.

This center is almost silent. Animals pass through carefully. The plants grow slowly, even by the standards of the plateau. Some trunks are wider than houses but only shoulder-high, their surfaces ridged and convoluted, covered in miniature forests. They may be thousands upon thousands of years old, not immortal but unwilling to finish. New shoots emerge from their edges while the centers hollow into wet chambers where pale roots hang like threads. The whole region feels less like a forest than like a single elder organism resting in dim warmth, thinking in moisture.

Yet the place is not oppressive. It has no malice, no urgency. It is simply complete. It has had enough time to answer almost every wound with growth, every fall with absorption, every emptiness with a new form of life. Its strangeness comes from continuity. It does not need to become anything else.

If you remained there long enough, the plateau would begin to measure you as it measures all presences: by warmth, pressure, salt, shade, disturbance. Moss would take the shape of your sleeping place. Small insects would learn the rhythm of your breath. Vines would test the stillness of your belongings, if you had any, and rootlets would enter seams. Your paths would exist only as long as you walked them. Your voice would be taken by leaves and returned as dripping.

Above, the dim sky would continue to press low over the high green world. Below, hidden water would move through roots and stone. Across distances too large to comprehend from within, fog banks would form over warm marsh-canopies and drift into old eroded basins. Flowering would begin somewhere, unnoticed at first, then spread over hundreds of miles in a slow blush of pale blooms. Animals would alter their routes. Pools would sweeten with fallen nectar. Fungi would wait.

And after many seasons, after storms that barely changed the shape of anything, after branches fell and were consumed, after old structures of root and stone sank another finger-width into peat, the landscape would remain what it has always been becoming: a vast high wetness, ancient and alive, dimly breathing under its own weather, built almost entirely from the patience of plants.
