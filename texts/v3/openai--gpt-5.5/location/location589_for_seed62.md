The plateau is not old enough to have learned softness.

It rises in a long, open shelf above the lower cloud-belt, a high country of pale stone ribs and unfinished valleys, where the ground still looks recently broken from whatever pressure lifted it. Cliffs stand with edges too clean for their height. Gullies run straight before they remember how to wander. Landslides have not yet become soil; they lie as raw fans of splintered shale, green glassy basalt, and chalk-colored dust, only partly claimed by mosses. The whole region has the feeling of a place exposed yesterday and immediately overgrown.

Light fills it almost without shadow.

The brightness is not hot. It is a cool, white, high-altitude glare, scattered by thin mist and the pale undersides of leaves. The sky is usually open, blue at the zenith and milked near the horizon, but moisture hangs everywhere in small, stubborn forms: beads on railings, silvering on fern-fronds, condensation threaded along cable bundles, shallow pools caught in hollows of rock. Rain comes briefly and vertically, more like a correction than a storm. It darkens the stone, feeds the roots, gathers in gutters and wheel-ruts, then withdraws into vapor before the land can become truly wet.

The vegetation has accepted this bargain. It is lush but not tropical, abundant but cool-blooded. The slopes are crowded with waxy-leaved shrubs, pale grasses, horsetail stands, dwarf alder, fern towers, and climbing vines with translucent stems. Moss packs itself into every seam of the young rock. Lichens form bright maps on the cliffs: sulfur yellow, porcelain green, bruised violet. In the ravines, where moisture lingers longest, the plants grow layered and nearly vertical, one life taking hold on another until trunks, cables, pipes, and fallen girders all wear the same green felt.

There are trees, but they are oddly shaped by the plateau winds. They do not make a forest so much as a series of dense, interlocking chambers. Their crowns are flattened on one side, combed by cold air. Their roots grip fresh stone like hands around bone. Some grow directly through slag heaps and crushed brick, drawing minerals from industrial waste with visible satisfaction; their leaves are darker there, almost blue.

The land opens outward in stages. From any rise one can see several valleys at once: narrow green clefts, pale quarry bowls, terraces of rock and vegetation, old service roads vanishing into thickets, a distant line of towers on the ridge. It is not wilderness exactly, and not a settled region either. It is a working landscape after work has slowed almost to stillness.

The industrial remains are scattered, but wherever they appear they are heavy.

A concrete pumphouse sits beside a pool no wider than a courtyard, its roof furred with sedge and its windows bright with condensation. A row of skeletal pylons steps across the plateau, carrying no visible wires, though on clear mornings their ceramic insulators glow faintly. Half-buried rails emerge from the moss and disappear again under root mats. There are intake grates in places where no river runs, steel doors set into cliff faces, narrow chimneys rising from the shrubs like blackened tree trunks. On the southern slope stands a processing shed of corrugated metal, long abandoned or perhaps simply between shifts; its vents are sealed by spider plants, and its loading bay is filled with knee-high ferns.

Nothing is ruined in the dramatic sense. There are no collapsed cities, no rusting armies of machines. The structures persist as if built for patience: small, tough, functional, distributed across the high country like instruments in a field station. Their paint is chalking. Their bolts are bright with dew. Their warning signs have faded into blocks of color. Sometimes, beneath vines, one finds clean seams in the concrete, hatches that have not rusted, indicator lenses intact and unbroken.

The machines, where they still operate, do so quietly.

That is the first rule of the plateau: nothing loud survives here.

Sound is absorbed before it travels. Moss takes it. Leaves take it. The cool air seems to press it flat. There may be engines beneath the ground, but they turn with a low, almost embarrassed pulse felt more in the soles than heard. Condensers drip into metal pans with intervals so long one forgets to expect the next drop. Relay boxes click once in an afternoon. A cable, contracting in the evening chill, gives a single faint note. Even rain makes little sound; the leaves catch and pass it downward softly, from broad blade to narrow frond to stone.

Walking here, one hears mainly the small betrayals of one’s own presence: cloth brushing wet leaves, grit under a boot, breath in the cool air. The silence is not empty. It is densely occupied, but by things that have agreed not to announce themselves.

Fauna is rare. A pale moth sometimes clings to the warm side of a metal door. Thin-legged beetles live under the lichens on transformer housings. In the pools, nearly transparent larvae hang motionless just below the surface. Once in a while, high above, a black bird crosses from ridge to ridge without calling. Its shadow slips over vegetation, pipework, stone, and is gone.

There are rumors of larger animals, but they leave signs too ambiguous to trust: a tunnel through reed beds, a row of crushed moss on a service road, a clean bite taken from a vine swollen with condensation. The plateau seems capable of supporting more life than it displays. It may be waiting. Or it may have chosen other forms of abundance.

The strangest forms are botanical and industrial at once.

In some ravines, vines have learned to follow insulated cable, coiling along it in precise spirals, their leaves aligning toward electrical fields rather than sunlight. Where old conduits leak warmth, mats of silver moss grow in rectangular patches matching the buried grid below. The small pools beside pumphouses sometimes show perfect circular ripples though no wind touches them and nothing falls in. Ferns near the compressor stations unfold all at the same hour, not dawn but a little before noon, when buried equipment reaches its daily temperature.

There are flowers, too: white, cup-shaped, glossy as enamel, growing around rusted pressure valves. They open only under the brightest sky. Inside each bloom is a dark central point, almost mechanical in its symmetry. No insect visits them. Still, they set seed.

The second rule of the plateau is that fresh things inherit old purposes.

A young stream follows an access trench because it is easier than cutting stone. Roots enter bolt-holes. Moss seals cracks better than mortar. Shrubs grow in rows where foundation gravel was laid. A ravine, too new to have a name, deepens along the path of a buried pipeline. The land is being formed not only by rain and frost, but by the memory of construction.

Erosion has begun, but it is still experimental. The plateau’s edges remain sharp. Drainage patterns are tentative. Water gathers in pockets, tests a route, abandons it, returns after a month of mist, then vanishes again. Tiny deltas form in wheel ruts. Stone flakes from new cliffs in bright, angular pieces. Frost loosens seams at night; by day, roots enter them. Nothing here has rounded into tradition.

The coolness is constant enough that decay is slow. Fallen leaves do not rot quickly; they laminate into dark, damp pages under moss. Wood hardens before it softens. Metal corrodes in delicate skins rather than flakes. A discarded wrench, found under liverwort, may still hold the warmth of the hand that left it decades ago—or seem to, because the plateau preserves intention almost as well as objects.

There are paths, though few are official. Narrow catwalks bridge gullies, their railings beaded with water and wrapped in creepers. Stone steps cut into slopes lead to locked doors, weather stations, valve cabinets, terraces where nothing now stands. A road of pale aggregate crosses the open shelf, bends around a stand of dwarf trees, and ends abruptly at a cliff whose face is too new for the road to have anticipated. Beyond the drop, a lush valley lies packed with vegetation, its floor hidden under leaves and mist. Something metallic glints there in a straight line for half a kilometer, then disappears.

The air smells of wet leaves, cold mineral dust, ozone, and the faint oily sweetness of old machinery. In bright weather, the smell of crushed fern is strongest. After rain, the stone releases a clean, raw scent, as if newly quarried. Near the small industrial buildings there is sometimes a breath of warmed rubber, transformer wax, or iron-rich water.

No smoke rises. Whatever industry remains has become internalized. It is underground, sealed, low-output, automatic. Perhaps the plateau was once designed as a distributed production field: pumps, condensers, mineral extractors, atmospheric collectors. Perhaps it still manufactures something very slowly—coolant, seed stock, distilled water, charged mineral salts, weather, silence. The evidence is inconclusive. The machines do not explain themselves, and the plants have made the labels unreadable.

Yet there is order.

At certain intervals, vents open on the ridge and release pale vapor that spills downhill through the greenery without haste. Leaves pearl over. The pools tremble. For an hour afterward, every surface shines. Then the vapor thins and the brightness returns, almost severe.

Every seventh or ninth day—no one agrees which—the pylons hum without sound. Not audibly, but visibly: fine hairs on stems lift toward them, and the white flowers turn by a few degrees. In the ravines, beads of water slide uphill along cable sheaths before gathering and falling. This is accepted as weather.

The place changes, but only through patient accumulation. A root widens a crack. A rill claims a service track. Moss closes over a stencil. A cliff sheds one blade of stone. A pool shifts from clear to green to clear again. The industrial skeleton does not vanish; it becomes more deeply embedded, more useful to other systems. The plateau is not reclaiming the built world in a single green surge. It is studying it, piece by piece, and deciding what can be used.

The brightness makes everything visible and still leaves the place difficult to read.

From the highest maintenance tower, if one climbs the slick ladder and stands among the nesting ferns at the top, the whole region appears as a dense, radiant fabric stretched over hard new bones. Green masses crowd the valleys. Pale stone cuts through them. Small roofs shine in the vegetation. The rails, where exposed, draw thin lines between facilities. Pools flash like fragments of sky set into the earth. Nothing moves except vapor, slow leaves, and the almost imperceptible drift of water seeking its next habit.

It is a young highland learning to be ancient.

It is a garden growing over an engine.

It is bright enough to reveal every detail, quiet enough that each detail seems secret.
