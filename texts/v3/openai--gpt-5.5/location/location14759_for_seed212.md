At low elevation the land does not rise so much as swell.

It is a warm basin, wide enough to contain neighborhoods, avenues, districts, and the suggestion of a city, but nothing in it is mineral in the ordinary sense. There are no stones except those recently made by roots squeezing clay into hard knots. There are no roads except the places where growth has agreed, for a few days or minutes, not to occupy the same line. The whole settlement is plant: plant underfoot, plant overhead, plant standing in walls, plant hanging in curtains, plant forming arches and alcoves and municipal-seeming chambers. The ground itself is a living mat, layered and springy, not grass but interwoven stalk, rind, tuber, root-felt, and broad veined membranes that tense and slacken like the skin of a breathing animal — though there are no animals here.

The basin is damp but not wet. Moisture appears in episodes. Rain comes in brief warm sheets, sudden and local, falling over one block of green architecture while the next block steams dry under full light. Condensation gathers under leaves the size of roofs and drips into cup-shaped plazas. Narrow channels run for an hour, then close as roots drink them. Pockets of brown water remain in hollows, in the throats of flowerless pitchers, in translucent cistern-fruits hanging from vines between living towers. The air is humid enough that every surface shines, but the larger land keeps a memory of dryness. It is a place of intermittent saturation, of thirst constantly interrupted.

The light is strong and ordinary in color, which makes the rest stranger. Sunlight arrives cleanly, revealing too much. It catches on waxy leaves, on fibrous walls, on pale green tendrils furred with new growth, on orange sap where a stem has split under its own pressure. Shadows are dense blue-green. Steam moves through them in slow bands. Nothing is hidden by darkness; everything is hidden by abundance.

The city has districts.

There is a low quarter of root-domes pressed together like market stalls, their entrances oval and damp-edged, opening into rooms lined with soft cambium. There are colonnades of braided trunks, each trunk composed of hundreds of plant bodies that have grown around one another into communal pillars. There are plazas floored with tight hexagonal leaf-plates, always replacing themselves from below. There are long corridors roofed by overlapping fronds, green on top, purple underneath, their ribs clicking as they adjust to the heat. There are towers, but young ones: sharp, fresh, not yet weathered, their outer bark still glossy, their vertical ribs ending in needlelike shoots. The whole urban form seems recently emerged, as if the basin only last season decided to become a city and did so in a frenzy.

Edges are everywhere fresh. Broken stems are bright inside. New buttresses push through older walls, distorting them. A passage may have a raw seam where it split open at dawn. A square may still be soft where it was extruded in the night. The erosion here is not the rounding-down of stone over ages, but the immediate wearing and regrowing of living material. Corners are sharp because they are new. Surfaces are scarred because they are already being replaced. Decay exists only as another construction material: black compost steaming in gutters, collapsed leaves feeding pale shoots, old walls sagging into mulch while new walls spear up through them.

It is deafening.

Not with voices, not with birds, not with insects. There is almost no animal life at all. The noise belongs to the plants and to the breaking of the rules around them. Leaves clap and scrape in warm gusts. Pods rupture with cannonlike cracks, scattering seeds that do not travel far before rooting where they land. Vines tighten around support trunks with the groan of wet rope. Hollow stems boom as air passes through them. Sap knocks inside channels. Root systems grind below the surface, a subterranean creaking like ships twisting in harbor. Enormous leaves flex, slap, unfurl, fold, and flex again. Some walls hum. Some plazas pulse with a low percussive thudding from beneath, as if the ground is being struck by buried fists.

Rain, when it comes, is not a softener. It multiplies the sound. Drops strike a million leaf-surfaces in uneven rhythms; water pours from terraces; cistern-fruits overflow; gutters of living bark cough and gurgle. Steam hisses from sun-hot plates. Then the rain stops abruptly and the whole city continues dripping with the volume of an orchestra tuning itself badly.

The turbulence is constant. Nothing holds its shape long enough to become background.

A street opens ahead as a lane of bowed stems leaning aside, their inner surfaces pale and wet. Ten minutes later it is a wall. A courtyard lifts into a mound, splits along five seams, and reveals a spiral stair of root-flesh descending into warmth. A tower sloughs its outer rind in heavy sheets, and the sheets root where they fall, becoming low houses by evening. Bridges made of braided lianas crawl sideways across open air, seeking new anchors. The basin reorganizes itself without tools, plans, or apparent command, but not without order. Its order is botanical, impatient, recursive. It repeats motifs — arch, chamber, avenue, cistern, fan, rib, hollow — then mutates them as soon as they are recognizable.

There are places where the city seems to imitate the idea of human settlement without ever having seen humans. Rows of bulbous chambers resemble dwellings. High green vaults resemble halls. Raised paths resemble causeways. Thin vertical shoots stand in clusters like railings, then flower into blind white cups, then harden into spines. Wide circular courts appear, ringed by tiered leaf-benches, as if gatherings might occur there; but there is no one to gather. The architecture is social without inhabitants. It has thresholds but no owners, windows but no interior privacy, plazas but no crowds. The absence of animals makes the abundance more severe.

And then the reality of the place begins to fail.

Not catastrophically. Not all at once. It fails like a wet seam giving way.

Distances are unreliable. A boulevard of mossy plates may look a hundred paces long and take an hour to cross, not because it lengthens physically, but because the middle of it repeats: the same leaning trunk, the same drop of amber sap, the same torn leaf-edge, each recurrence slightly altered, each one insisting it is the original. A tower seen from across a plaza bends in two directions at once. Its shadow falls west while its crown leans east. Reflections in water-pockets show corridors that have not yet grown, or have already collapsed, or exist only when seen upside down.

Some plants grow from their future selves. You can see a sapling rooted in the rotted stump it will become, both present at once, sharing a single column of air. Leaves unfold with their undersides already scarred by weather they have not encountered. Vines loop around absences, gripping invisible posts; a moment later the posts arrive as pale shoots, embarrassed into existence by being held. Seeds fall upward from burst pods and strike the underside of clouds of pollen, where they germinate into hanging gardens that rain roots.

The basin tolerates contradictions the way a swamp tolerates decay. It accepts them, feeds them, builds with them.

In one district, the ground has forgotten which side is surface. Roots descend from the air in thick curtains and vanish into the sky-colored undersides of leaves below. Walking there means passing between two soils: the brown root-mat underfoot and the suspended dark tangle overhead, both moist, both seeded, both claiming to be ground. Water drips upward in trembling beads, collects on the lower surfaces of high leaves, then falls normally from adjacent leaves as though nothing unusual has happened.

In another district, sound has weight. The booming of hollow trunks gathers in visible bands, translucent and greenish, pressing leaves flat as it passes. A sharp pod-crack can knock dew from surfaces a block away. The continuous roar of growth piles in certain intersections until the air thickens; vines there sway as if underwater, and any falling leaf slows, caught in accumulated noise. The place is loud enough to become structural. Some arches appear to be held up by resonance alone, their stems not quite touching at the keystone, the gap filled by a trembling chord.

There are no machines, no metal, no glass, no carved stone, no woven cloth, no fire pits, no tools. The technologies of the basin are all growth behaviors. Doorways dilate. Floors seal after being crossed. Reservoirs close their lips when the air dries. A bridge chooses tension over collapse by thickening one side and digesting the other. A wall repairs itself by forgetting the wound’s outline. Communication, if it exists, moves through sap pressure, root contact, shade, chemical drift, and coincidence. The city is not built. It is continuously decided.

Warmth presses down. The air smells of crushed green stems, latex, fermenting sweetness, wet bark, peppery pollen, and the mineral odor of new clay exposed by roots. In the lowest hollows the heat becomes almost edible, dense and yellow, trapped between overlapping leaves. Fungal films glisten there, though they too seem partly plant, veined and photosynthetic, spreading like living paint over shaded walls. Pale shoots nose through them. Tendrils write and erase spiral patterns. The whole ecology is less a chain than a crowd of plant intentions occupying every available depth.

At times a wind moves through, though there is no clear source for it. It arrives low, from between root-domes, carrying warm dampness and flakes of shed epidermis. The city reacts violently. Fronds align. Towers creak. All the long leaves turn their pale undersides outward, so that for a few moments the basin flashes silver-green. Pollen lifts in clouds dense enough to obscure whole streets. Seeds rattle in dry pods. Unrooted fragments skitter along paths, find crevices, and begin at once. After the gust passes, the arrangement has changed. A district that faced south now has openings to the east. A plaza has acquired a grove. A corridor has become a throat.

The lack of animals does not mean stillness, and it does not mean silence. It means no eyes peer from branches, no wings cut the steam, no burrowing things disturb the mats, no calls answer from canopy to canopy. Pollination occurs by pressure waves, by rainburst, by collapsing distance, by structures passing briefly through one another. Seeds are carried by architectural rearrangement. A fruit ripens in one district and appears cracked open three streets away, not transported but locally remembered. Nectar pools go undrunk and thicken into amber lenses through which the world appears arranged according to a different season.

The canopy is not continuous, but it is total. There are openings to the sky, and yet every opening is edged, framed, negotiated by vegetation. Massive leaves overlap high above like temporary roofs. Young tree-towers rise through them, tapering into spears. Curtains of vine descend and then retract. Sun shafts reach the basin floor in bright columns, each column crowded with drifting spores and pollen grains. Where light strikes directly, growth accelerates visibly: plates widen, stems twist, tendrils search, buds swell and split. Shade is not emptiness but stored intention.

Because the land is low, water has nowhere dramatic to fall. Instead it lingers, is caught, lifted, hidden, redistributed through bodies. There are no grand waterfalls, only innumerable leaks. Drip-lines score living walls. Root gutters braid along avenues. Shallow basins appear in plazas, lined with glossy red leaves, reflecting towers that are not present from any other angle. Warm fog rises after rain and clings near the ground, making the lower city seem underwater while the upper leaves remain sunlit and loud.

The edges of the basin are difficult to define. The urban plant-mass thins outward into marshy thickets, then thickens again in satellite clusters: small civic knots of chambers, wells, and arches connected by green causeways. Beyond them, perhaps, there are ordinary forests, or dry plains, or nothing coherent. From within, the city feels regional, extending beyond the immediate senses but not infinite. It has neighborhoods of texture and behavior. One hears distant collapses. One sees far towers sway and exchange crowns. The horizon is a jagged, living skyline of leaf, stem, and impossible geometry.

Rules exist, but they are local and temporary.

In the red-veined quarter, nothing grows straight; all stems coil clockwise until noon and counterclockwise after. In the cistern district, water only falls if observed indirectly, so pools fill behind the back and empty when faced. Along the pale arcade, every third arch leads to the same clearing, regardless of direction. In the thornless zone, surfaces are soft and yielding but shadows are sharp enough to cut leaves. The plants adapt to these rules without surprise. A vine meeting a paradox bifurcates. A leaf exposed to contradictory light develops two suns’ worth of veins. A root encountering an impossible void grows around its own absence and continues.

Sometimes an entire block enters a state of accelerated becoming. The sound rises first: a crackle, a drumroll of splitting sheaths, a wet tearing chorus. Walls bulge. Floors ripple. Buds appear by the thousands, swell, open, collapse. New chambers inflate inside old ones. Green pillars twist into stairlike helices, then flatten into fan-walls. Water is squeezed from tissues and runs in hot streams down gutters. The air fills with bitter vapor. Within minutes, what was a narrow residential-looking maze becomes an open cathedral of ribbed leaves, its ceiling trembling with suspended rootlets. Then the cathedral begins to subdivide.

At other times the basin exhales and all motion synchronizes. Leaves still moving at different speeds suddenly align in one visible pulse from one edge of the city to the other. Towers bow. Floors tighten. Water trembles in every cup. The roar dips into a single immense note, too deep to be heard only by ears. The note passes through root, wall, air, and wet soil. During this pulse, the reality fractures become momentarily legible: all streets at once, all seasons overlaid, the city as seed, sprout, ruin, compost, and seed again. Then the pulse breaks, and chaos resumes with greater force.

Nothing here waits to be old. The youngness is aggressive. Even decay is young decay, hot and active, steaming with conversion. The basin has not had time to smooth itself, and perhaps time does not move evenly enough for smoothing to occur. Its fresh angles remain fresh because they are constantly born. Its wounds shine. Its constructions are raw. Its failures become new supports before they can settle into ruin.

Underfoot, the living pavement sometimes becomes translucent. Through it one can see lower layers: roots braided like streets beneath streets, pale storage organs swollen with water, dark channels where sap surges in pulses. Deeper still are unfinished spaces, chambers forming around bubbles of air, avenues being rehearsed in root before they rise. The city has an underground not of burial but of preparation. It is always planning upward.

There is danger, though not predation. The landscape does not hunt. It simply grows where there is room, and sometimes the room is occupied. A doorway may close from thirst. A floor may root over a foot. A vine may mistake stillness for support. Pollen can be thick enough to blind. Sound can press the breath out of lungs. Paths can repeat until exhaustion. Reality can fold a traveler into a district that has not happened yet. None of this is malicious. The place has no need for malice. It is too busy becoming itself.

By late afternoon, if afternoon is a stable concept, the warmth deepens and the basin shines. Condensation beads on every edge. The city’s towers glow through their skins, backlit by internal sap channels. The noise grows richer, less percussive and more oceanic, though there is no sea. A rain cell forms over the central canopy, small and intensely dark, and releases a vertical column of warm water onto a single plaza. The surrounding districts remain sunlit. Steam rises in a white plume. The plaza floor opens into drinking mouths. The mouths fill, close, and become tiles again.

Then the sky visible through the canopy briefly turns the green of new leaves. Not reflected green — actual green, veined faintly, as if the basin has grown a membrane across heaven. Roots descend from it like rain that forgot to be water. The towers answer by lifting their crowns. For a few deafening minutes, up and down negotiate.

When the membrane tears, ordinary blue returns behind it.

The torn pieces flutter down as leaves no plant admits to having made. Where they land, new civic forms begin: a gate, a gutter, a hollow tower, a plaza with no entrance. By morning, if morning comes in the expected order, those forms will have joined the city’s restless grammar.

This is a low warm city of vegetation without citizens, a damp and roaring basin where plant life has replaced terrain, architecture, infrastructure, weather, and perhaps causality itself. It is lush past comfort, organized past wilderness, wild past organization. It does not contain ruins because nothing remains ruined. It does not contain animals because motion has no vacancy left for them. It is an ecology pretending to be an urban plan, or an urban plan remembered by an ecology, or a thought the green world is having too loudly to stop.
