At this height the world has not yet decided what shape it is.

It rises in the dark: a plateau, or the beginning of one, lifted so far above any gentler country that the air has thinned into a warm, mineral breath. There is no mist, no cloud in the familiar sense, no water carrying light from place to place. The darkness is dry and lucid. Distances are not hidden by weather but by scale, by blackness, by the uneven glow of stone still remembering heat.

The ground is new.

Not young in the way of spring, because nothing grows here, and nothing is waiting to grow. Young in the way of poured metal. Young in the way of a wound that has not yet formed a scab. The surface is made of fused black crusts, gray saltlike plates, ocher dust, and ridges of vitreous rock that catch the faint light with a dull internal shine. Some areas look like cooled lava, but there are no old flows, no riverbeds, no gullies, no rounded stones. Erosion has barely begun its grammar. Edges remain sharp. Slopes are raw. Cliffs break cleanly, as if snapped from within.

The land stands at an extreme elevation: a high anvil of mineral country suspended above invisible lower worlds. Around its margins, immense drops open into darkness. The horizon is not a line but a sequence of broken rims, black shelves, tilted spines, and far-off verticals. Beyond them are more high places, scattered like islands in a depthless night. The region is open, broad enough to contain valleys, basins, and ranges, yet everything feels unfinished, as though the entire plateau is being assembled from beneath while one watches.

There is warmth everywhere, but not comfort.

It rises from cracks. It presses through the soles. It pools in low basins where powdered minerals lie in pale drifts. It seeps from glassy vents and seams that glow a muted red or violet far below the surface, never bright enough to illuminate the whole place, only enough to give forms a suggestion of existence. A cliff face appears for a moment as a vertical plane of black basalt veined with ember. Then it is gone again. A field of angular stones lies ahead, each casting no shadow because there is barely any light to cast one from. The sky above is a heavy vault, almost starless, stained in places by slow curtains of brownish electrical radiance.

The turbulence is not in water or wind alone, though wind moves here in sudden, violent sheets.

It is in the ground. It is in the making of the ground.

At irregular intervals the plateau shifts. A tremor passes outward, not like an earthquake in an old landscape but like a thought moving through soft matter. Plains buckle into low ridges. Cracks open with a dry report. Fresh stone extrudes in knuckled ropes, steaming without vapor, shedding flakes as it cools. Entire shelves lift a finger’s width, then a body’s height, then settle at an angle that makes the eye distrust levelness. In one basin, cones of pale mineral push upward from below, hollow and brittle, growing for an hour and then collapsing into their own dust. In another, black glass blisters swell from the ground and burst soundlessly, leaving star-shaped scars.

There is no rain to soften any of this. No runoff to connect heights with lows. No sedimentary patience. Nothing is carried away except by gravity, heat, and the occasional hard gust that drags dust in low, fast veils across the flats. The dust does not smell of soil. It smells of hot ceramics, iron, sulfur, and something alkaline enough to sting the mouth. It gathers in pockets between slabs and then is gone, blown into the dark, leaving the ground exposed as if recently born.

Vegetation is absent, not merely scarce. There are no roots working into cracks, no lichens staining stone, no dry shrubs clinging to impossible slopes. The emptiness vegetation would have occupied is intensely present. Niches exist without inhabitants. Sheltered hollows wait and remain unused. Shelves of stone resemble ledges where nests could be, but there are no birds. Caves open like mouths but breathe only heat. If there is life here, it is below recognition: a slow chemistry along mineral seams, a pattern of crystallization that repeats too precisely, a dark film inside fractures that may be deposit rather than organism. Nothing moves by intention.

Scattered through the region are structures, though they may not have been built by hands.

At first they look like ruins: isolated pillars, low walls, rings of upright stones, half-arches standing alone on high saddles between basins. They appear too deliberate to be dismissed and too primitive to be called architecture. A line of dark blocks crosses a slope, each block taller than a person would be, spaced irregularly, as if marking a path no one has walked. Farther on, a cluster of cuboid masses sits at the edge of a chasm. Their faces are flat, their corners nearly square, but their material is the same black mineral as the surrounding ridges. Some have openings: not doors, exactly, but rectangular absences where the stone has withdrawn or never formed.

They are scattered sparsely, with long distances between them. No settlement gathers. No road connects them. They might be the first experiments of geology imitating shelter. They might be the remains of a civilization that never learned tools, or of one dissolved so completely that only its simplest gestures remain. But there are no devices, no metal fittings, no inscriptions, no carved ornament, no sign of use. Nothing here suggests a hand holding an implement. The forms have the innocence of accident and the unease of intention.

In one place, a wall grows.

It begins as a bright seam in the ground, then thickens, pushing upward along a straight line for several hundred paces. Warm black material extrudes slowly, folding against itself, hardening almost at once. The result is a low barrier no higher than the knee, glossy along its top. After a while it splits down the center and the two halves lean apart, becoming not a wall but twin ridges. Dust collects between them. Later, when the plateau shudders, one section falls outward and breaks into plates. By morning—if morning exists here—it will look ancient.

The darkness makes time uncertain.

There is no sun crossing the sky, or if there is, it is hidden behind a dense upper atmosphere of ash-colored gases. Light arrives indirectly: from the ground’s heat, from faint mineral phosphorescence, from intermittent sheet-glows above the horizon. Sometimes the underside of the sky pulses a dim copper, revealing the far country in a single immense exposure. Then one can see how large the place is: high basins linked by saddles, serrated ridges, isolated towers, fields of pale evaporite-like crust though no sea ever lay here, and beyond all that, a raised rim encircling the region like the lip of an unfinished crater.

Then the light fades, and the world becomes near again.

Sound is sparse but not absent. Most of the time there is a hush so complete that the faint ticking of cooling stone seems loud. Pebbles settle in cracks. A distant slab breaks and falls, the noise arriving late and thin. Wind passes without leaves, without grass, without anything soft to catch it; it makes a low fluting where it crosses holes in the rock, then a dry hiss as it pushes mineral grains over glass. When the ground opens, the sound is abrupt and intimate: a crack like bone, a deep inward knock, a long granular sigh as fragments slide down a new slope.

Occasionally the entire plateau speaks.

A pressure wave moves underfoot. Far away, ridges answer one another with booms too low to be thunder. Dust lifts from basins in simultaneous rings. The air trembles. In the chasms below the high rim, unseen masses grind together, building new elevation or consuming old supports. But even this violence is strangely unaccompanied. No animals cry out. No wings scatter. No insects pause. The turbulence happens in a world without witnesses, and so it seems both catastrophic and private.

The air is thin, warm, and chemically sharp. Breathing it would be difficult, though not because it is cold or damp. It has the dryness of kilns. It desiccates imagined skin. It carries powdered salts and volcanic glass so fine they hang briefly after each gust, making dim halos around the glowing cracks. In low hollows, heavier gases collect invisibly. They do not ripple; there is no moisture to reveal them. Only the occasional darkening of a mineral crust suggests their presence, or the way exposed crystals frost over with a black tarnish.

The ecology of the place is an ecology of forces.

Heat feeds expansion. Expansion opens fractures. Fractures admit gases from below. Gases alter minerals. Minerals swell, split, slump, and re-form. Dust buries, then is stripped away. Glass cools too quickly and shatters. Crystals grow where vapors meet colder stone, then are snapped by the next tremor. Nothing eats, but things consume. Nothing reproduces, but forms recur. Nothing dies, but everything fails.

The rules are simple and severe:

No softness endures.

Nothing flows far.

Edges remain edges until the ground remakes them.

Shelter appears, but does not promise safety.

Height increases without warning.

The dark is not empty; it is merely unlit.

There are places where the surface has the appearance of a dried seabed, polygonal plates stretching across a wide basin in pale gray and white. But the cracks between plates breathe heat, and the plates are not clay. They are mineral skins, precipitated out of exhaled chemistry, curling upward at the edges like scales. Step on one and it would break with a ceramic snap, revealing a red-black cavity below. The basin gives the impression of water remembered by a world that never possessed it.

At the basin’s center stands a solitary tower.

It is narrow at the base, swollen halfway up, then broken into prongs. Its sides are ribbed with vertical bands, alternating dull black and chalky yellow. Around it lies a ring of fallen pieces, each sharp enough to cut shadow. The tower may be a vent chimney. It may be a failed mountain. It may be one of the scattered built things, except no builder could have stood here long enough to finish it. From fissures near its base comes a faint, steady red. Not flame. Not lava exposed. More like the color of heat seen through a closed eyelid.

Beyond the basin, the land rises again.

The ascent is steep and irregular, composed of ledges that have lifted faster than they can collapse. Some are only a stride wide. Others form broad terraces strewn with angular debris. There are no paths, but certain lines of fracture make routes appear: natural stairways, ramps of fused stone, narrow saddles between black teeth. Along these lines the scattered structures recur. A low arch, half melted. A platform of flat slabs arranged in a circle around nothing. Three standing stones leaning toward a central crack. They do not accumulate meaning, but they do accumulate pressure. Each one says: form is trying to happen here.

Near the highest rim, the air grows even thinner and the warmth stranger. Heat comes not from below alone but from the rock itself, as if the stone has absorbed an unseen radiation. The ridge is made of dark, porous material, light as pumice in places and dense as iron in others. It rings when struck by falling fragments. Its crest is sharp and newly raised. On one side lies the plateau with its basins and forming walls. On the other side, an immense descent disappears into blackness.

From this rim, during one of the sky’s dim copper pulses, the larger pattern becomes visible.

The whole region is an uplift blister on a vast dry world. Around it are other blisters, other high formations emerging from flat darkness. Between them lie plains of mineral dust, unmarked by rivers, roads, or migrations. The landscape is not weathering down. It is thrusting up. It is in the first fever of becoming terrain. Mountains are not yet mountains but forces with surfaces. Valleys are not yet valleys but failures between pressures. Ruins precede inhabitants. Architecture precedes tools. The world is building the idea of place before anything arrives to occupy it.

Then the pulse fades.

The plateau returns to fragments: a glowing seam, a black ridge, a pale basin, a distant falling stone. The silence closes over the vastness. Warm dust drifts through the dark. Somewhere below, pressure gathers again. A crack lengthens by a handspan. A wall begins as a line of heat under the ground. A tower leans, holds, and does not fall.

Nothing lives here, but the land is restless.

Nothing grows here, but the world is still making itself.
