At the top of the world there is a crack no wider, from the outside, than a black line in the flank of a mountain-spire.

The mountain itself rises out of a pale salt desert like a shard struck upward by an enormous hammer. Its sides are young and jagged, still bright with fractured mineral faces: blue-black basalt, white seams of halite, greenish copper veins, glassy shelves where ancient heat cooled too quickly. There are no long slopes softened by ages of weather. Everything is abrupt. Edges keep their first sharpness. Scree lies where it fell yesterday, or ten thousand years ago; in this dry cold there is little difference.

The crack is almost hidden behind a curtain of hanging metal plates and wind-polished stone. Step through, and the whole mountain opens inward.

It is not a valley exactly. It is a vertical pocket, a narrow interior canyon enclosed within the spire, descending and rising farther than the eye can judge in the dimness. Above, a ragged slit of sky glows like dull pewter. Below, the pocket drops into industrial haze and shadow. Walls of raw mineral press close on either side, so close in places that bridges touch both faces at once. The sense of elevation never leaves you. The air is thin, cool, and mineral-dry; each breath tastes of salt, metal dust, old electricity, and hot bearings.

No water runs here. No moss beads the stone. No green softens the machinery. Moisture is treated as a contaminant, a dangerous softness. Pipes carry gases, oils, pulverized salts, liquid metals in sealed ceramic veins, but never open water. Floors are dusted with white mineral powder that squeaks underfoot. Workers wrap their mouths not against dampness or rot, but against alkali grit fine enough to polish the lungs.

The city is built inside the crack in layers.

Balconies of riveted iron jut from the stone. Narrow streets loop along cliff walls, stacked five, ten, twenty levels high. Lift-cages climb on chains. Cableways cross the gulf in dense black diagonals. Workshops are wedged into niches, their fronts open to the interior air: stamp-mills, electrolytic vats sealed behind cloudy glass, furnace mouths glowing a tired orange, sorting houses where mineral nodules travel along rattling belts. Chimneys do not rise freely; they bend, merge, and vanish into ducts bored through the mountain.

The place feels crowded without ever becoming loud. That is one of its oddities. Activity is everywhere, but sound is swallowed by altitude, dust, and stone. Engines throb more than roar. Conveyor chains whisper and tick. Far below, a press exhales with the soft finality of a sleeping animal. Boots strike metal grates with muted clicks. Voices are kept low, partly from habit and partly because the canyon carries a careless shout in strange ways, throwing it upward and downward until it returns altered.

The turbulence is mostly visual, mechanical, atmospheric.

Thin winds enter from hidden vents and fracture into sudden spirals. They lift salt dust from ledges and spin it into pale columns, then drop it all at once. Hanging signs twist. Crane arms swing slowly through the dim. Tarpaulins snap once and go still. Powder avalanches slide from upper shelves in silent sheets, pouring over roofs and vanishing through grates into collection bins below. Now and then a whole scaffold section is winched away and another lowered into its place, so the city seems to be revising itself while you watch.

Nothing here appears finished. New metal is bolted onto old stone; old metal is patched with ceramic; ceramic is splinted with wire; wire is braided with bone-white insulation made from ground shell and resin. Fresh cuts in the cliff walls shine where expansion crews have bitten deeper into the spire. The settlement feels young, not because it is small, but because it has not yet learned to weather. It is all wound, brace, fresh scar, temporary solution becoming permanent by repetition.

There is little plant life, almost none recognizable as such. In shaded seams, one finds gray crusts like ash pressed flat against the rock. They grow around heat leaks and electrical fields rather than sunlight, forming scalloped mineral skins on old conduits. Some people scrape them for catalyst. Others claim they are not alive, only chemistry arranging itself conveniently. In certain abandoned ducts hang threadlike mats, brittle and translucent, that tremble when charged air passes through them. They do not smell of rot. Nothing here rots properly.

Yet animals are abundant.

They are everywhere once your eyes adjust.

Small pale cliff-things move through the pipework, not quite rodents, not quite lizards, with split gripping feet and ears folded tight against dust. They lick salts from sweating machinery and gnaw insulation for trace organics. Flocks of black, paper-winged scavengers nest in the warm housings of transformers; they launch silently from one level to another, steering with long tails through the cables. Blind beetles pour in orderly lines along heated rails, feeding on soot and mineral crust. Soft-bodied filter creatures, kept in cages near exhaust vents, strain usable compounds from factory breath. Their keepers call them lungs.

The larger animals belong to the city’s labor. Sure-footed burden beasts with low skulls and plated shoulders climb stairways too steep for wheels, carrying ore baskets and compressed-gas cylinders. They do not drink in public; their bodies conserve internal solvents with almost mechanical efficiency. At night they kneel in heated stalls and chew cakes of fat, salt, and powdered mineral. There are also hook-clawed courier animals trained to cross the open spans where humans hesitate. They wear little brass bells, though in the thin air the bells make only a dry tremor.

The rules of the pocket are precise.

Do not expose water.

Do not sleep below an active chute.

Do not trust a quiet cable unless you have touched it and felt whether it is alive with load.

Do not lean on white stone; salt gives way without warning.

Do not whistle upward into the slit, because the wind may answer downward.

Do not feed the black-winged scavengers near signal lamps, or they will learn to crowd the lights and bring traffic to a halt.

Do not ask what happens in the lowest foundries after the pressure doors close. Everyone knows, more or less: ore is cracked, gases separated, metals persuaded out of stone by heat and current. But the exact processes belong to guilds, and the guilds belong to the mountain.

Dimness governs everything. Daylight rarely enters directly. The sky slit overhead gives only a cold wash, and much of that is caught by suspended dust. Lamps burn in recessed rows: amber along walkways, blue-white near machine halls, red at pressure seals. Farther down, the haze glows from beneath, as though the city were built above a banked subterranean fire. Faces appear and disappear in alternating bands of light. Every railing casts three shadows.

The air changes constantly. A person can stand on a bridge and feel three climates pass in a minute: a cold downward draft from the high exterior; a warm metallic pulse from a smelter vent; a sideways rush of dry powder scented with ozone. Pressure doors open with sighs. Fans reverse. Dust storms form indoors, race along one level, and die against a hanging net. The entire pocket is a weather system engineered badly, or perhaps engineered too well by too many hands with different intentions.

Despite the industry, despite the crowding, there are intimate places. A shrine made from rejected pressure gauges sits in a recess no larger than a cupboard. A tea room without tea—serving hot mineral broth and stimulant vapors—occupies the underside of a stair. Children play a game with polished slag marbles on a board etched directly into the floor of a maintenance platform. Old workers sit with their backs to warm ducts, listening through the metal for faults in machines too distant to see.

From the highest catwalk, the city seems impossibly dense: a hidden organ inside the mountain, all ribs and vessels, quietly laboring. From the lowest visible platform, looking up, it becomes a constellation of lamps and moving silhouettes trapped in stone.

Outside, the salt desert is empty and bright and wide.

Inside, the mountain is full of dry life. It rustles, shifts, manufactures, sheds dust, feeds its animals, extends its scaffolds, replaces its broken teeth. It is a city compressed into a wound in the sky, cool and airless at the edges, warm with engines at the core, forever building itself before erosion has had the chance to begin.
