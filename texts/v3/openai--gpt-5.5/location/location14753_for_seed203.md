It is underground, but not dark.

The roof is so high that it has weather. From the floor, where a person would stand ankle-deep in pale fibrous litter, the ceiling is not a ceiling but a luminous continent: a vault of living tissue, mineral ribs, frozen mist, and white-green leaves pressed so densely together that they form a second sky. Light comes from the plants themselves. Not soft glow in patches, not mushrooms here and there, but a full subterranean day — cold, clear, almost surgical — pouring down from fronds, membranes, hanging root-curtains, and translucent seed bladders as large as houses. The brightness has no sun in it. Shadows are thin and directionless. Everything is visible, yet nothing feels exposed, because the scale defeats sight.

The chamber may be a single cavern, or a chain of hollows joined so broadly that the divisions are meaningless. Its distances are geological. A line of plant-pillars may seem close and take two days to reach. Between one living wall and the next lies an emptiness like a plain, a cold internal steppe under a ceiling of vegetation. The air is clear enough to see far, but perspective fails because there are few familiar objects. The floor rolls gently, not from old erosion but from recent collapse and growth. Edges are sharp. Crevasses split open with fresh black lips. Slabs of young stone protrude through the vegetable substrate like bones not yet weathered down. Some surfaces still have the raw look of something newly torn.

The place is mostly plant, but not lush in the ordinary sense. It is not a jungle crowded with trunks and animals and wet heat. It is an enormous, cold, hollow plant-world. Vegetation is the ground, the architecture, the insulation, the machinery of the air. It grows in mats thick enough to walk on, in suspended bridges, in vertical ribbons that tremble without wind, in vast root-sheets glazed with condensation. There are plains of low white moss that crunch softly underfoot, recovering slowly after pressure. There are forests of hollow reeds as tall as towers, spaced far apart, their skins transparent enough to show blue sap rising in pulses. There are terraces of leaf-stone, where living tissue has mineralized at the edges and forms steps, parapets, basins, and overhangs.

Everything has the pallor of cold growth: jade-white, milk-green, blue-grey, the faint amber of buried resin. Dark colors exist only in fresh breaks, wet hollows, and the mouths of cracks. Even rot is pale. Dead plant matter does not quickly decay here; it dries, freezes, becomes felt, paper, tinder, then is reabsorbed by filamentous roots when the damp cycle returns.

Water is present, but sparingly, intermittently. It is not a flooded cavern. There are no great lakes, except perhaps far below in blacker levels. Here water arrives as episodes: condensation blooms on the ceiling; silver rain falls for six minutes over an area the size of a province; fog rolls low through a root-valley and vanishes into thirsty moss; hidden veins release sudden trickles that run bright over stone and disappear into sponge beds. Pockets of water collect in leaf-cups, in basalt scars, in the throats of tubers. Some are clear as glass and filmed with ice. Others are thick with plant sugars, slow to freeze, giving off a faint smell like cut stems and metal.

The cold is steady. Not killing cold everywhere, but deep cold, cellar-cold, starless-winter cold. Breath smokes in the open hollows. The larger leaves have frost along their ribs. At night — if night can be said to occur, since the light never fully leaves — the air tightens and the living surfaces contract. Bridges shorten. Tendrils curl. Vast curtains of root-fiber draw upward from the floor, leaving bare channels where travelers can pass. Then, when the internal weather shifts, everything relaxes again with almost no sound.

The quiet is deceptive because the place is never still.

Its turbulence is mostly silent.

Growth happens visibly. A wall that was bare in the morning may be veined by evening with pale runners. A moss-field may lift in waves as gas moves beneath it, opening fissures that seal again behind the motion. Seed pods detach from the ceiling and drift downward for hours, rotating in the bright air like lanterns, before striking the ground and unfolding into temporary thickets. Whole districts rearrange through slow botanical convulsions. A root the width of a road may creep across a plain over the course of a week, splitting old mats and burying tool marks, shrines, camps, bones.

There are storms, but they are not loud storms. The ceiling brightens first, as if remembering lightning. Then vapor thickens around the hanging gardens. Condensation gathers all at once on millions of surfaces. Rain begins as a hiss so fine it seems imagined. White sheets of water descend in long moving curtains, not everywhere but in wandering fronts. Where the rain strikes, dormant plants wake explosively. Coiled vines spring from the ground and climb the falling water. Cup-leaves open. Pale flowers appear, release dust, and collapse within minutes. The air fills with luminous spores, each one bright enough to make a tiny shadow.

Afterward the cavern is changed. Paths vanish. Pits are covered. New walls appear where climbing mats braided themselves between reed-pillars. A camp left for half a day may be found sealed inside a mound of breathing green.

Yet because the cavern is so vast and the living things are so thinly distributed across its immense volume, the overwhelming feeling is emptiness. Vegetation fills every surface, but the surfaces are far apart. Between the floor and the lighted roof hang kilometers of bright, cold air. You can stand in a meadow of moss with living cliffs on the horizon and feel as if you are in a bowl scraped out of the world. The plant life is total, but not crowded. It is continental. It has room to behave like geology.

The ground underfoot varies. In some regions it is springy and layered, a peat of white roots and old leaf-skin. In others it becomes hard, slick, and veined, like polished horn. There are fields of upright blade-leaves, sharp-edged and waist-high, that ring faintly when touched. There are young ravines where the floor has split and dropped, exposing raw strata: black stone, pale root, ice, clay, root again, as if the cavern has grown in alternating seasons of rock and plant. The erosion is fresh. Water has not rounded much. The channels are narrow and angular. Falls descend in silver cords over unsoftened ledges. New talus lies at the base of cliffs, each stone still faceted, already threaded by exploratory roots.

Sometimes the plants build forms that resemble ruins, and sometimes ruins are truly there.

The human or human-like presence is scattered, slight, and old only in the way a repeated habit is old. There are small shelters tucked under overhangs: frames of bent reed, woven root, hide-like leaf membrane stretched and cured over bonewood hoops. There are ladders made from knotted vine, driven into soft cliff faces with carved pegs. There are cairns of pale stones at the edges of safe condensation fields. There are windless prayer flags made of dried leaf-skin, hanging limp in the bright air, marked with soot and berry pigment.

Metal is rare or absent. Tools are of stone, shell, glassy mineral, tooth, hardened thorn, and laminated wood. Blades are chipped obsidian from recent fractures. Needles are fishbone-thin splinters of reed bark. Bowls are grown rather than carved: plant cups trained around stones, then cut free and sealed with resin. Rope is everywhere, though one learns not to trust any rope whose ends are not visible, because many vines here imitate cordage perfectly.

Where people have lived longer, the architecture is still modest. A cluster of ten huts on a moss terrace. A smoke-hole cut through a mat of hanging roots, though fires are small and carefully guarded because dry plant felt can smolder invisibly for days. A watch platform high in a reed-tree, used less for enemies than for weather. Store pits lined with cold wax, holding dried tubers, spore cakes, and strips of translucent leaf. Little bridges over black cracks. Little fences around water pockets. Little signs telling travelers which plants will tolerate weight and which will swallow it.

These settlements seem temporary even when they have stood for generations. The world does not preserve straight lines. It accepts them briefly, then bends them, roots through them, lifts them, wraps them, digests them. A village abandoned for a season becomes a bump under moss, then a ring of richer growth, then nothing legible except perhaps a few stone flakes and a post-hole full of blue ice.

Fauna is rare. This is not a place of birdsong or insect drone. Most movement belongs to plants. Still, animals exist in the margins of attention. Pale grazing things move along the edges of moss plains, narrow-bodied and long-legged, with transparent ears held close to their skulls. They are seen at distances, in groups of three or four, and vanish behind root-pillars without haste. Small blind animals live in the warm interiors of seed pods, emerging only after rain to lick minerals from fresh stone. In the upper air drift flat, manta-like creatures that feed on spores; they are almost indistinguishable from fallen leaves until they turn and show a dark mouth.

Predators are rarer still. Perhaps there is one kind: a patient, low creature that moves beneath the plant mat, lifting the ground in a slow ripple. Hunters listen for it by placing an ear to the moss. It makes no growl, no footfall. Only a pressure. Only a change in the way the roots tense.

Sound carries strangely. The air is so open and the surfaces so absorbent that most noises die close to their source. A shout becomes a small local mistake. Footsteps are muffled. Rain hisses and disappears. Far collapses may be seen before they are heard: a distant cliff of vegetation slumping in silence, a plume of bright spores rising, then much later a soft thud like a hand against cloth. The loudest ordinary sound is dripping. The second loudest is the occasional crack of frost splitting a stem.

There are rules here, learned by necessity.

Do not sleep on new green growth; it may be in motion.

Do not cut a glowing vein unless you know whether it carries sap, water, or heat.

Do not cross a white plain immediately after rain; it may be only a skin over a basin.

Do not trust open space beneath hanging seed-clouds.

Do not follow a path more than once without checking whether it has changed its mind.

The brightness also has rules. It increases before condensation, fades before root movement, flickers in the presence of certain animals, and goes blue where stone is about to break. Travelers read light the way surface people read clouds. A yellowing ceiling means sporefall. A pulse moving through the reed-forests means sap surge. Sudden shadow in a place that should have none means something overhead has opened.

There are regions where the living roof descends nearly to the floor, making luminous tunnels through compressed vegetation. There are other regions where the floor falls away entirely and one must traverse suspended root bridges over emptiness. Below those bridges, deeper caverns shine faintly with their own plant skies, stacked one under another like worlds inside a seed. Cold vapor rises from those depths. Occasionally a strand breaks, and a bridge miles long unthreads itself in a graceful, silent catastrophe, raining fibers into the abyss.

In the newest districts, the cavern still seems to be deciding what it is. Stone has ruptured recently there. Fresh faults expose black glass and blue ice. The first colonizing plants grow in simple, aggressive forms: flat lichens bright as bone, needle-grass that drills into cracks, thick ropes of root that steam faintly where they touch mineral warmth. These places are sharper, more dangerous, less absorbent. Sound travels farther. Light glares off wet stone. The plants have not yet softened the geometry. You can see the violence of the opening: the torn strata, the fallen slabs, the way the cavern expanded suddenly and left life rushing to occupy it.

Elsewhere, older areas are calm only by comparison. Immense buttress roots descend from the ceiling and enter the ground with the authority of mountains. Around them lie empty courts of moss where nothing else grows, as if the great plants require silence. Their bark is smooth, cold, and faintly luminous from within. People carve no marks on them. Even primitive tools know better. It is said that cutting one causes rain for a year, or darkness for a month, or simply the slow relocation of all paths within walking distance.

The atmosphere smells of frost, sap, stone dust, and wet fiber. Sometimes there is a sweetness like bruised fruit, but it passes quickly. Sometimes a mineral bitterness announces a new crack opening far away. Smoke from settlements remains low and blue, clinging to the underside of root mats before vanishing through pores in the living ceiling.

Despite the cold, despite the quiet, despite the emptiness, the place feels intensely awake. Not conscious in any single mind, perhaps, but coordinated by pressures, fluids, light, hunger, frost, and the need to fill hollows. It does not sleep. It brightens and tightens, drinks and releases, splits and heals. Its weather is growth. Its erosion is digestion. Its architecture is temporary obedience between plant and stone.

A traveler crossing it would remember most of all the contradiction: a buried world brighter than noon; a vast empty chamber completely claimed by vegetation; a cold place that grows with tropical urgency; a quiet landscape always in upheaval. They would remember looking up into the luminous roof and seeing rain forming there in silver beads, each bead trembling on the tip of a root, and beyond them, impossibly far above, whole forests hanging upside down in the white-green light.
