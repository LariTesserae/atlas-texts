At the top of a young mountain there is a narrow hollow that feels less like a valley than a room bitten out of the sky.

It lies just below the summit ridge, in a place where the rock has not yet learned to soften. Everything is sharp. The walls rise in dark, fresh planes, blue-black and iron-gray, with seams of paler stone showing where the mountain split open too recently for weather to round the wound. The ledges have edges like broken pottery. The floor is tilted, uneven, strewn with angular blocks and splinters of stone that still fit, in the mind, into the cliffs above them. Nothing here has become gravel by patience. Nothing has become soil except in the smallest pockets, where damp dust and old feathers have gathered in cracks.

The hollow is not large. You could cross it in a few minutes if you knew where to place your hands and feet, though the place resists being crossed. It is cathedral-sized rather than landscape-sized: a chamber of open air, walled by cliffs, roofed by cloud, with a slot of sky far above that appears only as a dim whitening. The altitude presses on everything. Breath comes thin. Sound comes strangely close. The cold is not killing, but it is constant, a coolness that sits in stone and seeps into joints.

Light rarely enters directly. The sun, when it passes, touches only the highest rims and leaves the basin below in a deep slate gloom. Most of the day is twilight. At dawn and evening the rock faces glimmer with condensation, not enough to become streams, but enough to bead along cracks and gather at overhangs. Rain comes intermittently, often without warning: brief, needled showers blown sideways over the rim, wetting one wall while leaving another dry. Then the rain stops, and the place resumes dripping.

Water exists here as hesitation.

It collects in shallow basins no wider than a handspan, in the pawprints of fallen stones, in narrow grooves where ice once expanded and snapped the rock apart. There are no true rivers, only threads that appear after rain and vanish into rubble. Some trickles sink between black slabs and reemerge lower down as cold breath. A few pools persist in shadow. Their surfaces are black until disturbed, and beneath them lies a faint greenish sheen, not quite plant, not quite mineral, a skin that trembles when the wind changes.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It clings rather than grows. Pale lichens crust the undersides of boulders where moisture lasts longest. Short, wiry moss forms dark pads in the crevices, each pad dense and old-looking despite the youth of the stone around it. There are a few threadlike grasses, colorless at the tips, bent flat by wind. Tiny alpine herbs bloom in cracks for a few days at a time, their flowers almost translucent, as if made from diluted moonlight. They do not spread into meadows. They appear singly, stubbornly, and then withdraw.

The strangeness of the hollow is not in any single impossible feature, but in the way too much life occupies too little space.

The animals are everywhere.

At first the rock seems bare, and then it moves. Feathers shuffle in the dark. Pale legs withdraw into cracks. Something clicks behind a slab. Something else exhales underfoot. The cliffs are crowded with nests, not built from twigs — there are no twigs — but scooped into dust pockets, pressed into warm hollows, claimed beneath overhangs and between wedged stones. Birds cling to vertical surfaces in masses. They are small, dark, and many-eyed, with wings narrow as knives and calls like flint striking flint. They do not fly far. They burst from one ledge to another in sudden eruptions, then settle again, packed close enough that their bodies seem like a living crust on the stone.

Among them move heavier creatures: horned cliff-mammals no larger than dogs, with soft split hooves and long, blunt faces silvered by condensation. They browse what little moss they can find, but mostly they lick minerals from the wet walls. Their tongues rasp audibly over stone. They move slowly, deliberately, testing each surface before committing weight. In the dark their eyes catch the faint sky and hold it.

There are insects in overwhelming number, though many are unlike lowland insects. Some are wingless, jointed things the color of ash, living in the feather-fall beneath the bird ledges. Some are glass-bodied midges that rise from the pools in cold swarms and hang in the air like dust made conscious. Beetles with soft, translucent shells graze the green films at the water’s edge. Pale worms braid themselves in wet cracks and withdraw when touched by light. The smallest creatures dominate the place. They fill the spaces between stones, under moss, inside droppings, beneath the thin crusts of frost that form before dawn. The hollow is dense not with trees or buildings, but with occupation — every crevice inhabited, every damp surface fed upon, every shadow listening.

The sound is louder than the size of the place should allow.

Because the basin is enclosed, every cry multiplies. Bird calls strike the walls and return sharpened. Pebbles fall often, loosened by hooves, claws, frost, or the slow settling of the young mountain. Each falling stone cracks against another, then another, until the whole hollow answers in brief cascades. Drips tick steadily in the darkness. Wings rattle. Hooves scrape. Insects make a dry electrical hiss near the pools. The wind, unable to enter cleanly, spills over the rim in layers, moaning at one height and whispering at another. Sometimes all these sounds combine into a single rough breathing, as if the mountain itself were asleep but troubled.

Yet the place is slow.

Its movements are patient, cyclical, almost ceremonial. Moisture gathers by night and releases by morning. The animals shift according to shade, not haste. The birds launch when the wind fits their wings, then wait through long stillness. The cliff-mammals follow the wet mineral seams as they darken, licking one face for hours before moving a body-length. The moss grows imperceptibly. The pools shrink, swell, skin over with green, clear again, darken again. Stone breaks, but seldom dramatically; more often a seam widens through frost after frost until, one afternoon, a piece simply lets go.

There are no made structures here. No path, cairn, wall, tool mark, fire ring, or cut surface. Nothing indicates ownership or visitation by anything that builds in the ordinary way. But a primitive intelligence is present in the behavior of the creatures. Some birds use pebbles to crack the shells of cliff-beetles, dropping them again and again from the same ledges until the stone below is nicked with impact marks. The horned animals pry up lichen crusts with sharpened bits of fallen shale held between lip and tooth, then abandon the stones where they fall. Certain insects arrange grains of mineral around pool edges, not in structures, but in repeated little crescents that guide moisture toward their larvae. No artifact survives as artifact. Use dissolves back into geology.

The rules of this world are simple and severe.

Nothing wastes warmth.

Nothing trusts open light.

Water belongs first to stone, then to film, then to mouth.

Height governs everything. A fall is always near. A dry hour can undo a generation of eggs. A ledge wide enough for one body becomes a territory; a crack that holds mist becomes a garden; a shadow that lasts through noon becomes a refuge. Life here does not spread outward. It packs inward, pressing itself into the mountain’s fresh wounds.

In the deepest part of the hollow is a cleft where the rock has split in a clean vertical line. The cleft is too narrow to enter, but cold air issues from it continuously, smelling of wet iron and old snow. Around its mouth the density of life becomes nearly unbearable. Birds nest above it. Moss thickens below it. Pale insects mass along its damp margins. The cliff-mammals come one at a time and stand with their noses in the breath of the crack, eyes half closed, as if listening to something below.

From within comes a low sound. Not loud, exactly, but persistent: a hollow, distant vibration beneath the louder surface noise of wings and water and stonefall. It may be wind passing through inner chambers. It may be meltwater moving far under the summit. It may be the mountain continuing to open.

The animals seem to know its rhythm. Their calls rise and fall around it. The midges lift from the pools when it deepens. The birds turn their heads toward the cleft before storms arrive. Even the dripping changes then, gathering speed, becoming briefly musical before returning to its ordinary count.

At night the place becomes almost entirely black. The sky-slot vanishes. The cliffs become pressure rather than sight. But the hollow does not become empty. It becomes more crowded. Soft bodies move over stone. Wings adjust. Hooves knock delicately against ledges. The pools show faint internal gleams where disturbed organisms answer touch with cold green light. For a moment, if many are stirred at once, the floor appears mapped in dim constellations: water pockets, wet cracks, insect paths, the edges of hidden moss.

Then the light fades back into darkness.

The mountain remains young. The hollow remains small. Around it rise greater ridges, snowfields, impossible drops, cloud-belts, and summits no creature here may ever cross. But within this one chamber of rock and dim wetness, life has crowded itself so intensely that the place feels vast from within. Every ledge is a country. Every crack has weather. Every pool has hunger. Every sound returns with company.
