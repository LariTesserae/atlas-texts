At extreme height, where the air ought to be thin and cold, there is a hot wet upland that steams like a living engine.

It is a broad high valley or tableland set among cliffs, not a single clearing but a whole region lifted above the lower world. From its rim the land drops away in blue-green walls and curtains of vapor; beyond, lower forests appear and disappear under cloud. Up here, the ground is old. Nothing feels newly broken. The ridges have been rounded by long rain, the basins have filled with peat and black silt, the stone has softened under moss, and every channel has found its habitual path. The place has the settled look of a system that has been running for ages.

Water is everywhere, though not always as open ponds. It lies just underfoot, swelling the soil. It beads on leaves and hangs in the air. It leaks from cliff seams in constant threads, darkening the rock face into glossy vertical bands. It collects in shallow marshes between hummocks of root and stone, then slips away through braided runnels, vanishing into mats of fern before reappearing downslope as a warm, clear trickle. After rain, which comes often and abruptly, the whole plateau seems to exhale: boulders weep, bark shines, the air turns white at ankle height, and every depression becomes a lens of sky.

The heat is not a dry heat. It presses close. It gathers under the canopy and in the hollows. The sun, when visible, is ordinary and bright rather than strange — a hard high light softened by haze, broken by cloud shadow, filtered through leaves. But the warmth has a second source beneath the land. In places the marsh bubbles. Thin mineral-smelling steam seeps through cracks. Pools at the bases of cliffs are almost bath-warm, filmed with orange and silver traces, ringed by salt-loving mosses and tough little sedges. The rocks themselves sometimes feel alive with stored heat, as though the mountain keeps a furnace far below and breathes through the wet skin of the plateau.

There are no built things here. No paths cut by tools, no walls, no posts, no ruins hidden under vines. Yet the landscape gives off an industrial impression, as if production is occurring without builders. The water moves through the land in stages: collected, heated, pressurized, released. Vents thump softly under mats of vegetation. Springs pulse in rhythmic surges. Reeds tremble over subterranean cavities where air and water trade places. Certain hollows tick and click with cooling stone. The cliffs vent white plumes in the morning, evenly spaced along faults like exhausts from an unseen plant, though there is no machinery, only geology behaving with mechanical regularity.

The terrain is layered and crowded without being closed. The eye can travel a long way across it — over marsh basins, forested knolls, slick black outcrops, pale steamfields, and hanging gardens along the cliff — but every part is full. Near the ground, roots cross roots. Ferns shoulder into pitcher plants and broad-leaved shrubs. Vines rope between dwarf trees whose trunks are furred in moss and epiphytes. The open water is seldom empty; it holds floating leaves, insect skins, small darting fish, mats of green algae, the round nostril-ripples of amphibians. Even the bare stone is not bare for long. Lichens map it in gray, yellow, and rust. Liverworts fill its cracks. Seedlings cling wherever a spoonful of soil has gathered.

The forest here is lush but shaped by altitude and exposure. Trees do not rise into giant columns; they twist, lean, and interlock, forming low, dense crowns with leathery leaves that shed water in continual drops. In sheltered draws they grow taller, their limbs heavy with orchids, aerial roots, and hanging moss. On the windward escarpments the vegetation becomes tougher: mats, cushions, clumps of grass, succulent rosettes tucked into stone. Along streams it turns extravagant again, with huge leaves and flowering spikes, bright waxy blossoms, and tangles of cane-like stems that knock softly together when the mist moves.

The colors are saturated but not fantastical. Deep greens dominate: blue-green fern, yellow-green moss, black-green poolweed, the glossy dark of wet leaves. The stone is charcoal, brown, and iron-red, with pale mineral crusts around vents. Mud ranges from chocolate to almost black. Flowers appear as sudden points of intensity — orange, violet, white, scarlet — often half-hidden among leaves or reflected in water. The sky is frequently a luminous gray-blue, with cloud banks sliding low across the plateau. When the sun breaks through, everything flashes: each leaf edge, each water bead, each wet rock face.

Sound is present but restrained. There is no roar unless storms arrive or a cliff fall sends echoes across the basin. Most of the time the place works quietly. Water ticks from leaf to leaf. Hidden streams mutter under roots. Insects make fine electrical chirps, then stop all at once. Frogs call from warm pools in short, hollow notes. A bird knocks seeds from a branch, and the splash below is startlingly loud. From the geothermal seams comes an occasional sigh, a low gulp, a soft percussive bump as trapped vapor shifts underground. The whole plateau has the hushed concentration of a factory running in another room.

Animals are abundant, but many are small, concealed, or patient. Bright insects patrol the wet leaves. Beetles climb steaming stones. Dragonflies skim the marshes where the air opens. Lizards bask on black rock, then vanish into green seams. Frogs cling to leaves above pools, their skins shining like polished fruit. Crabs or crablike freshwater creatures occupy the warm rivulets, sorting debris with delicate, methodical claws. Birds move through the canopy in flashes: nectar-feeders, fruit-pickers, quiet hunters with narrow wings. Larger animals leave traces more often than they show themselves — pressed tunnels through fern, scat full of seeds, hooflike prints filling with brown water, a low movement in reeds at dusk.

Predation happens softly. A heron-like bird freezes beside a mineral pool. A snake lies along a warm branch, invisible until it turns its head. Small fish vanish beneath floating roots when a shadow crosses them. Insects are taken from the air, frogs from leaves, eggs from hidden nests. Decay is just as active. Fallen branches soften quickly, colonized by fungus and ants, then by moss, then by seedlings. Nothing stays singular for long. Each surface becomes a host.

The weather is cyclical and close. Morning may begin in a white coolness that is not truly cold, cloud lying directly on the ground. By midday the mist lifts and the heat thickens. Steam rises visibly from dark patches of earth. Thunderheads build against the surrounding peaks, their undersides bruised and heavy. Rain arrives with little ceremony: first a prickling on leaves, then a dense vertical fall that flattens the marsh grasses and turns every channel brown for an hour. Afterward the light returns, and the land glows as if newly varnished. By evening, vapor gathers again in the basins, and the cliffs disappear from the bottom upward.

The rules of the place are rules of saturation and release. Water enters constantly: as rain, mist, seepage, condensation. The mountain takes it in, warms it, strains it through root and mineral, then gives it back in springs, clouds, and runoff. Plants must tolerate drowning, heat, poor mineral soils in one place and mineral excess in another. Animals must know which pools are merely warm and which are scalding below the surface, which mats of moss cover solid ground and which float over black water. The land offers abundance, but little firmness. A careless step can sink thigh-deep into peat or break through a crust of mineral into hot mud.

Because there are no human structures, the sense of industry remains uncanny but mild. It is not an alien machine hidden under paradise. It is more like the recognition that natural systems can be mechanical in their persistence: pumping, sorting, condensing, venting, converting heat into motion and mineral into life. The plateau operates. Its marshes are reservoirs; its roots are filters; its cliffs are ducts; its mosses and microbes are slow chemical workers; its animals are couriers of seed, flesh, and disturbance.

From a distance, seen across the open high basin, the landscape looks almost peaceful: a green wet upland under ordinary daylight, veiled by steam, rimmed by cliffs, alive with birds and quiet water. Up close, it is busier and stranger. Every pool is feeding something. Every rock is sweating. Every leaf is collecting, shedding, breathing. The mountain is old, hot, and lush, and it has arranged itself into a vast unbuilt mechanism whose product is more forest, more mist, more life.
