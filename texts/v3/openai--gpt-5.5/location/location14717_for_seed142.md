At the top of the world there is a city that seems to have been planed down by time.

It stands on a high plateau of black mineral crust, where the air is thin, warm, and completely dry. Not dry in the ordinary sense of thirst or drought, but dry as a sealed furnace, dry as an old battery, dry as a moon of powdered salts. There is no dew, no frost, no memory of rain. The ground has never softened. Nothing here swells, rots, sprouts, or ferments. Surfaces keep their edges until ages of dust blunt them. Metal does not rust in familiar colors; it tarnishes into dull violets and smoked grays. Stone does not carry lichen. Concrete does not stain green. Every material is alone with itself.

The plateau extends farther than the eye can comfortably hold, though there is very little light by which to measure it. Darkness lies over the region like an atmosphere of its own. The sky is not night-blue but nearly black, with a faint brown depth to it, as if filled with suspended soot too fine to fall. No sun is visible. There may be one below the horizon, or behind some permanent ceiling of ash, or extinguished long ago. The city makes its own small lights: pinholes of amber in inspection towers, long dull seams of red heat under foundry doors, a few cold white lamps hung above rail junctions. They do not illuminate so much as mark positions. Between them, whole districts vanish.

The warmth comes upward.

Under the city, beneath the mineral tableland, something vast remains hot and steady. It is not volcanic in the dramatic sense. There are no eruptions, no glowing rivers, no tremors. The heat has settled into a constant gradient, too even to be called alive. It seeps through basalt, brick, slag, ceramic pipe, and iron plates, warming the soles of anyone who walks there. Streets retain a kiln-like mildness. Walls are warm to the touch. The darkness is warm. The shadows are warm. Even the wind, when it moves at all, arrives as a dry breath drawn through old machinery.

The terrain around the city is ancient beyond ruin. Once there may have been mountains here, sharp and high, but erosion has spent them down into broad shoulders and long, low ridges. The plateau is not smooth; it is worn. It has terraces of clinker, pale salt pans hardened into porcelain crust, flats of compacted dust, and fields of mineral scales that crackle under pressure like old glaze. There are canyons, but they are shallow and wide, their walls slumped into calm angles. There are craters softened into bowls. There are stair-stepped escarpments where darker rock appears beneath lighter sediments, each layer exposed like the pages of an unreadable archive.

Nothing cuts quickly here. There is no running water, no root, no thaw, no burrowing abundance. Change occurs by abrasion, thermal expansion, rare collapses, and the slow settling of dust. The landscape has reached something like its final form: not dead, exactly, but finished.

The city shares this condition. It is urban, ordered, and immense enough to contain neighborhoods, factories, transit lines, storage yards, civic towers, and worker barracks, but it feels sparse because so much space lies between its presences. Wide avenues cross empty districts. Rail lines run straight for kilometers between depots. Chimney stacks stand in rows with nothing rising from them. Courtyards are broader than necessary. Plazas are bare except for loading cranes frozen in identical positions, their hooks suspended a few meters above the pavement as if waiting for a command that will never come.

Buildings are constructed of basalt block, black brick, ceramic tile, iron lattice, poured mineral composite, and panels of dull alloy. Their forms are industrial but solemn. Rectangular foundries sit half-buried in the plateau, their roofs low and ribbed. Tall distillation towers cluster like organ pipes against the darkness. Conveyor bridges span dry canals that never held water but once carried powder, ore, ash, or some granular fuel from one district to another. Enclosed tramways run along elevated pylons. There are silos for desiccants, bins for powdered glass, reservoirs for gases, tanks sealed under layers of insulation, and pressure halls whose round doors are bolted shut.

No vegetation breaks the geometry. No weed grows between paving slabs. No moss softens brickwork. No seed has ever found purchase. In the residential quarters, window boxes contain only arranged stones, old ceramic tags, and small idols made from slag. Public squares have no trees. Instead they have shade frames: metal structures with horizontal slats, built for a sun that may no longer exist. They cast blacker rectangles into the general blackness.

The atmosphere is not empty, but it is spare. Dust drifts in vertical sheets where heat rises from vents. Fine particles collect in corners and on ledges, making bevels over hard angles. The dust is pale, almost bone-colored, and in the weak factory light it gives some streets the look of an archaeological trench. Footprints, wheel tracks, and drag marks can last for decades unless crossed by one of the rare warm winds. Many are so old they have become part of the surface, compressed into shallow relief. In some districts, the ground is written over with layered traffic from centuries of work, all flattened into a single gray skin.

The rules of this place are rules of conservation.

Nothing evaporates because there is nothing wet enough to leave. Nothing blooms. Nothing decays quickly. Heat moves, but gently. Machines run only if they have always run; stopped machines remain stopped. A fallen girder may rest where it fell for an age. A closed door may stay closed until its hinges are cut. The city does not weather into wilderness. It can only become more itself: more mineral, more quiet, more evenly distributed between structure and dust.

Sound carries strangely in the thin air. Most of the time there is almost none.

A person standing on an overpass above the rail yards might hear the soft ticking of cooling metal, though nothing appears to have recently cooled. There is a faint electrical tension in some substations, a dry hum like insects imagined from memory. Deep below certain factory floors, piston arrays still compress and release with geological patience, one stroke every few minutes, perhaps every hour. The sound rises through vents as a low, padded thud. Farther away, a chain may shift one link on a conveyor, or a pressure valve may exhale a measured puff of warm gas. Then silence returns, immense and administrative.

There are no birds. No leaves. No water over stone. No animal chorus hidden in the dark.

Fauna exists only in rare, specialized forms, if it can be called fauna. In the furnace quarter, small dark creatures cling to hot ceramic flues. They are flat, many-legged, and jointed like fragments of old instruments. Their bodies have a graphite sheen. They feed on electrostatic films, mineral efflorescence, perhaps leaked chemical residues. They move only when the temperature shifts by a fraction, and then so slowly that one notices them first as errors in a pattern of rivets. Workers once called them cinder mites, though they are much larger than mites and not truly insects.

Higher up, around the communication masts, pale winged things sometimes appear. They are not birds and not moths. Their wings are thin membranes stretched over mineral spars, dusted with reflective powder. They ride the faint thermal currents rising from the city, circling without calls. They may never land in sight. Some believe they are manufactured, old maintenance organisms released to inspect towers and wires. Others say they evolved inside the ventilation systems, eating charged particles and powdered insulation. Either way, they are rare enough that seeing one is treated less like an encounter with wildlife than like a fault in the world’s usual stillness.

The city’s industrial systems are not abandoned, but neither are they busy. They persist.

In the central works, great halls contain machinery too large for any obvious human scale. Flywheels are set into pits. Gear trains disappear behind walls. Furnaces glow with banked heat, their doors sealed by wheel-locks. Pipes cross ceilings in organized bundles, each labeled in worn script: ARGON, DUST RETURN, DRY ACID, PRESSURE ASH, BLACK GAS, CERAMIC SLIP. Some labels refer to substances no longer used or no longer understood. There are gauges whose needles have not moved in generations. There are belts that travel so slowly a mark on them takes a day to cross a room.

Here industry has become climatic. It is less a set of operations than a condition of the landscape. The city breathes through vents. It digests ore stockpiles at an imperceptible rate. It stores heat, equalizes pressure, circulates gases, condenses nothing, wastes little. Its factories may once have produced tools, rail segments, furnace brick, pressure suits, engine casings, black glass, or the city itself. Now they seem dedicated to maintaining the plateau’s equilibrium: keeping the darkness warm, keeping the dryness absolute, keeping all gradients too small to become weather.

The inhabited areas, if they are inhabited, are hard to distinguish from the mechanical ones. Rows of apartment blocks stand along the high avenues, each with recessed doors and narrow windows. Balconies are enclosed in mesh against dust. Stairwells are lit by dim orange bulbs set behind thick glass. Public notices remain bolted to walls: shift diagrams, pressure warnings, maps of transit loops, lists of rationed gases, evacuation routes to sealed shelters. Many are so old that the language has peeled into symbols. Some signs are newer, but not by much.

There are markets, though quiet ones. Stalls under corrugated awnings display machined parts, ceramic filters, lengths of wire, dry protein blocks, polishing salts, memory plates, heat tokens, and folded cloth treated against dust. No fruit, no grain, no flowers, no fish, no skins hanging from hooks. Food, if present, is manufactured from sealed vats or imported in hard bricks from lower settlements no one can see. Smells are mineral: hot iron, alkali, graphite, insulation, old oil, ozone, clean ash.

People here, if met, speak softly. Their voices do not need to compete with weather. They wear dark layered garments, face wraps, goggles, and gloves not against cold but against dryness, dust, and contact with treated surfaces. Their tools are carefully maintained. Their customs seem shaped by the fact that anything misplaced may remain visible for a lifetime. They close doors. They cover lamps. They mark footsteps in forbidden areas. They do not pour liquids, not even ceremonially. Mourning is done with powder: a shallow tray of gray dust smoothed flat, then impressed with the name of the dead and left in a niche until the letters fill themselves.

Above the city, the higher ridges carry older ruins. These are not separate from the urban fabric but earlier layers of it, exposed by the retreat of use rather than by collapse. Terraces of eroded foundations climb toward the rim of the plateau. There are circular plazas whose purpose is obscure, lined with sockets where columns once stood. There are dead rail spurs leading to the edge of dry basins. There are immense retaining walls built from fused stone, their faces polished by centuries of airborne dust until they reflect the faintest light like old mirrors.

From those heights, the city spreads below in broken constellations: warm seams, tower silhouettes, black roof planes, faint grids of avenues. Beyond it lie more industrial districts, or perhaps other cities, reduced by distance to low geometries on the plateau. The openness is not pastoral. It is infrastructural. Space exists for transport, extraction, storage, separation, safety. Empty ground is part of the design. The city breathes because its districts are far apart.

At the horizon, the land drops away into darkness. The elevation is felt not by vistas, because there is little light to reveal them, but by absence: the thinness of sound, the pressure in the ears, the way the distant lower world seems less like a place than a depth. Warm updrafts move over the rim. Dust pours slowly from the plateau edge in dry veils, disappearing before it can be seen landing.

The strangeness of the place is not loud. It does not announce itself through monsters or impossible colors. It accumulates through refusals.

A city without water.

A warm night without stars.

Industry without urgency.

Ruins without collapse.

Desert without sun.

Age without softness.

Everything here has been reduced to essentials and then preserved past the point of explanation. The plateau is high, dry, dark, and stable. The city is built, but almost geological. Its machines are mechanical, but patient as strata. Its creatures are rare enough to feel accidental. Its people, if they remain, are custodians not of growth but of continuance.

Nothing asks to be renewed. Nothing expects rescue. The place has no season, no morning, no storm on the way. It rests in a long equilibrium of heat and mineral silence, extending outward through the dark: streets, kilns, towers, dust fields, rail lines, eroded ridges, sealed halls, and the faint red mouths of furnaces that have forgotten fire but still remember warmth.
