At the top of the world there is a plain so old it has forgotten how to be mountainous.

It lies above the weather, above the last blue stains of forest, above the level where rivers can remember their courses. The ascent to it crosses cliffs and shoulders of stone worn to a dull, powdered softness, not by rain—there is no rain here—but by time, by wind that has long since become too thin to matter, by frost without thaw, by the minute pressure of ages settling downward. The peaks around the plain are not sharp. They are blunted, planed, exhausted. They rise like the backs of buried animals under a hide of pale mineral crust.

The ground is white in some places, gray in others, yellowed with old salts, green-black where minerals have bloomed in seams. It is not sand, though it shifts underfoot with a dry granular sigh. It is crushed shell without shells, ash without fire, flour made from mountains. Here and there are polygonal plates of salt or gypsum or something stranger, fitted together in a cracked skin that extends for miles. The plates have edges as fine as old pottery. When stepped on, they do not break. Nothing here breaks anymore unless it has been waiting to break for ten thousand years.

The air is cool and thin. It has the taste of stone held in the mouth. Breathing feels like drawing cloth through the chest. There is a sun, ordinary in brightness, neither apocalyptic nor dim, but its light has trouble behaving. It falls straight and clean on the plain, yet objects cast several shadows, not all in the same direction. Some shadows are older than the things that cast them. Some remain on the ground after the body has moved away. At noon, certain boulders throw shadows upward into the air, where they hang as transparent blue slabs.

Nothing melts here. Nothing flows. There is no visible water, no ice, no dampness under rocks, no dew forming in the cold mornings. The chemistry of the place has made its peace without liquid. Growth, when it happens, is done by accretion, by dust adhering to dust, by salts flowering directly out of the air, by organisms that seem to eat distance or shadow or the memory of warmth.

The vegetation is sparse and low, if it can be called vegetation. Small black tufts cling to the lee side of stones, brittle as singed hair. They grow in circles, each ring perhaps centuries older than the next, though none of them appear alive in any ordinary sense. A few upright spines emerge from cracks in the mineral flats, translucent and fibrous, like glass reeds. They do not sway. Even when the thin wind passes, they remain in place, but a faint chime can sometimes be imagined around them—so faint it may only be the ear inventing companionship.

There are also crusts: rust-colored, violet, chalk-blue. They spread in maps over the ground and over the bones of old terraces. Some are lichenous, some crystalline, some organized in fractal veins that avoid the shadows of living things. If a hand is laid on them, they feel neither warm nor cold but exact, as though they possess the temperature of measurement itself.

The plateau extends beyond easy comprehension. It is not infinite; the horizon shows rims, saddles, far escarpments, the suggestion of descending basins and other uplands. But the open distance repeats itself with such calm insistence that walking changes little. A row of black stones seen in the morning remains at the same distance all afternoon. A low ridge approached for hours reveals itself to be a line of crouched animals, then a ridge again, then both.

The density of the place is strange because it is open and crowded at once. There is space everywhere, but almost none of it is empty. Every yard contains something: fragments, husks, beads of mineral, toothlike stones, shallow pits, tracks impressed into salt and never filled, sticks bound with sinew, little cairns, shells of unknown dry creatures, slabs etched with hands and stars. The ground is layered with presence. It feels less like a wilderness than like a room emptied of furniture but still saturated with all who have lived in it.

The fauna is overwhelming.

At first this is not obvious, because nothing moves.

They are everywhere: perched, crouched, nested, half-buried, standing in rows along ridges, clustered around vents that emit no vapor, packed into cracks, arranged in spirals on the salt pans, suspended on cliff faces as if gravity has been negotiated differently for each species. Small pale beetle-things cover the underside of stones in dense constellations, their legs folded against their bodies. Tall jointed grazers, ribbed and hollow, stand among the glass reeds with their wedge-shaped heads lowered forever toward the mineral crust. A thousand birds, or bird-equivalents, occupy the air above a dry basin, wings extended, each fixed in a different attitude of flight. They do not flap. They do not fall. Their shadows rotate slowly without them.

In ravines, long animals like ropes of vertebrae coil around pillars of salt. Their skins are matte and gray, patterned with old cracks like dried clay. On high ledges sit blunt-faced watchers with huge eyes, furred in silver dust, each one staring at a different era. Herds of delicate many-legged creatures fill the flats, their bodies no larger than bowls, their antlers interlocked so tightly they form living fences. Some have died, perhaps. Some have never been alive. The distinction is not reliable here.

The silence should be impossible with so much animal presence, yet it holds.

There is no calling, no shrieking, no buzzing cloud, no scrape of claw or shuffle of hoof. The creatures breathe, but too slowly for sound. Their heartbeats, if they have hearts, are spaced across geological intervals. Occasionally there is a dry click from somewhere far off, a single adjustment of shell or bone. Once in a long while, the plain gives a soft, collective creak, like a library settling at night. Then the silence restores itself completely.

The animals are not statues. That would be simpler. They possess attention. Passing among them produces the sensation of entering a council that has been in session since before language and will not interrupt itself for you. Eyes track without moving. Horns remember the shape of your body after you have passed. A beetle on a stone may require an entire day to lift one foot; a flock in the air may be migrating across a century, one wingbeat every generation. The plateau is not lifeless. It is life slowed until it resembles mineral law.

The world’s rules here are old and poorly aligned with ordinary rules. Distance is dense. Time has surfaces. A shout, if attempted, falls from the mouth as a small white pebble and lands soundlessly at the speaker’s feet. Footprints appear ahead of the walker before they are made, then wait to be stepped into. Certain hollows contain yesterday’s light pooled dryly like dust. The carcass of an animal may be found with its future bones arranged beside it in a neat stack.

There are places where perspective fails. A cairn the size of a fist stands beside a cairn large enough to shelter in, but from a few steps away they exchange sizes without moving. Cracks in the ground open not downward but backward, showing moments rather than depths: the same plateau under different skies, the same herd with horns unbroken, the same ridge before it was worn flat, the same human hand placing the same stone again and again. None of these visions flicker. They are stable wounds in reality, old and clean-edged.

The built things are scattered and small. Human, or almost human, work appears rarely but unmistakably.

Low shelters crouch against boulders, made of stacked slabs and ribs from animals too large to imagine. Their entrances face away from the prevailing nothing. Inside, there are sleeping hollows lined with powdered fiber, stone blades, bone awls, bowls carved from porous black rock. The bowls are dry and always have been. Their interiors are polished by use but hold only salt dust and shed scales.

On some ridgelines stand marker posts of dark wood so ancient it has mineralized, bound with strips of hide that have not decayed. The knots are simple, made by hands without metal tools. Beside them hang charms: pierced vertebrae, flat stones scratched with tally marks, bundles of glass reeds, feathers from the motionless aerial flocks. Nothing swings. The cords hang in perfect arcs.

There are terraces too, though it is difficult to know if they were built or inherited from geology and improved by patient hands. They step down the sides of shallow basins where no crop could grow. On their surfaces lie arrangements of seeds, or seedlike stones, each placed in a small depression. The sparse black tufts cluster near these terraces, suggesting cultivation without moisture, agriculture of shadow and mineral dust. Perhaps the old inhabitants farmed silence. Perhaps they fed their herds on altitude.

Some shelters remain occupied.

Figures sit within them, wrapped in hides, knees drawn up, heads bowed. Their tools lie beside them. Their hair has grown into mats of mineral fiber; their skin has taken on the same matte gray as the long coiled animals in the ravines. They may be ancestors, mummies, people asleep in the plateau’s immense pause, or a living culture whose gestures are too slow to register. One figure holds a stone scraper against a hide stretched over a frame. The scraper has nearly crossed the hide. The work may have begun before the last valley below went dry.

No fire burns. There is ash, but it is cold beyond cold, transformed into a pale ceramic powder. Hearths are arranged with care: circles of stone, fragments of char, split bones. The memory of fire seems permitted, but not flame itself. Combustion would be too quick for this place.

The ecology has no hurry. Predation occurs as a relationship of eventuality. A narrow-jawed creature stands with its mouth around the neck of a smaller animal; both appear calm, both covered in equal dust. The bite may close in another thousand years. Parasites grow like beadwork along the flanks of grazers, each one a small shell sealed tight against the air. Carrion birds hang above the dying before death has occurred, attending not an event but a certainty.

Reproduction is likewise patient. Eggs lie in shallow bowls of salt, hard as stones, arranged in vast nurseries that cover entire slopes. Some are translucent, containing folded shapes in suspended darkness. Others are cracked, but the cracks are old and show no sign of widening. Young creatures no larger than seeds cluster around the feet of adults, exact miniatures, already dusted, already solemn. Nothing cries out to be fed. Hunger exists here as a geometry, not an urgency.

Because there is no water, bodies conserve themselves differently. Skins are sealed, waxed, armored. Blood, if spilled, emerges as dark grains or threads of powder. Some creatures carry internal crystals that ring softly when their bodies are touched. Some have no mouths, only porous plates turned toward the sun. Others feed on the thin electrical tension between ground and sky; their horns are arrays, their bones instruments. In storms—if storms ever came—they might glow. But storms do not come.

The sky is broad and usually pale. Clouds are rare and too high to promise anything. They look like scratches in glass. At night the stars are violent in number, crowding the black so thickly that darkness becomes granular. Yet even night does not truly change the plateau. Temperature drops, shadows merge, the animals remain, the old structures persist. Dawn returns without drama. The place does not wake because it has not slept.

There is wind, but lightly, intermittently, like a thought reconsidered. It moves dust from one side of a stone to another over centuries. It carries almost no scent. Sometimes it passes through the open mouths of the bone shelters and produces a low tone too deep to hear, felt only in the teeth. Sometimes it touches the suspended flock and the feathers give off a whisper like pages turned in another room. Then stillness resumes.

The most unsettling thing is not the strangeness but the equilibrium. Nothing appears to be arriving or departing. Decay and growth have reached agreement. Predator and prey, builder and ruin, mountain and plain, life and fossil, past and future—all are present and none dominates. The landscape has settled into a final form without becoming dead. It is not waiting. Waiting implies expectation. This place has passed beyond expectation into continuance.

If one walks far enough, the plateau begins to show its larger pattern. The ridges, basins, salt flats, herds, cairns, shelters, suspended flocks, and terraces are arranged in repeating bands, not perfectly but with ritual consistency. From a high escarpment, the whole region resembles a skin, scarred and stippled, covered in pores and old marks. Or it resembles a woven mat, each thread an animal migration halted mid-weave. Or a language written too slowly to be read by mortal attention.

In one basin stands the densest gathering of animals. They fill the floor from rim to rim: horned grazers, coiled ravine-beasts, pale beetle masses, long-necked watchers, stiff-winged birds standing among those that hover, small armored things piled in layers, eyeless burrowers emerging halfway from mineral vents, all facing inward toward an empty circle of ground.

At the center of that circle is a simple structure: three upright stones supporting a flat capstone, no higher than a person. Beneath it rests a hand-shaped tool made of chipped black glass. Around the tool, the salt has cracked in concentric rings. No body lies there. No inscription explains it.

Every creature faces it.

The silence in that basin is absolute, but not vacant. It has weight, ceremony, an almost unbearable concentration. It seems that at some time outside time, something was made there, or killed there, or understood there, and the entire fauna of the plateau gathered to witness it. Having witnessed it, they did not disperse. The moment became too complete to leave.

Beyond the basin, the land continues: more flats, more worn ridges, more ancient shelters, more animals fixed in their immense quiet lives. The horizon holds steady. The light remains ordinary. The cold remains mild and dry. The world does not end, because ending is a kind of movement, and movement is the one extravagance this place has mostly renounced.

Still, there are changes so small they approach theology. A grain of salt releases from a ledge. A shadow accepts a new angle. One suspended bird advances the width of a feather. In a shelter of stone and bone, the seated figure’s scraper moves another invisible fraction across the hide.

And all across the high, dry, crowded plain, the innumerable animals continue their silent participation in the oldest surviving afternoon.
