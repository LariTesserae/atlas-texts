At first it seems like a forest, because everything is green or green-adjacent: leaf, frond, rind, stem, tendril, bark, filament, spore-felt, waxy membrane, translucent blade. But after a while the word forest becomes too small. There is no soil under it, no river under the moss, no dampness in the seams. The whole low world is made: a continental industrial garden assembled from living plant-matter, scaffolded on alloys and ceramics, grown over itself so many times that construction and ecology have become the same act.

The ground is not earth. It is a woven deck of rootwood plates, pale and hard as bone, laid in hexagonal fields that run to every horizon. Between the plates are seams packed with dry mineral dust: white salts, black powdered basalt, yellow flakes of old nutrient glass. Nothing is wet. If you kneel and press your hand to the surface, it is cool, slightly springy, and faintly alive, but it gives up no moisture. The plants here have forgotten water in the ordinary sense. Their sap is a slow, oily conductor; their cells hold dry gels and charged crystals; their roots drink light, carbon, ammonia, static, trace metals, and the cold exhalations of underground engines.

Everywhere, vegetation performs architecture. Trees stand as chimneys, their trunks ribbed with iron bands, their crowns unfolded into mirror-leaves that track the bright sky. Vines are cables. Reeds are antennae. Bark forms walls, fluted and layered, patched with old inspection plates. Broad leaves roof entire districts, overlapping in translucent scales. Some are matte blue-green, some silver-white, some clear enough to show the capillary machinery inside: branching circuits, valve-knots, tiny shutters opening and closing without sound.

The land is low and very wide. There are no mountains, only shallow swells in the constructed plain, long embankments of grown cellulose, sunken factory-gardens, and basins that look at first like dry lakes. They are not lakes. They are photosynthetic foundries: miles-wide bowls carpeted with black-green mats that refine minerals from dust and air. Their surfaces ripple though no wind touches them. Conveyor roots creep beneath the skin, carrying powdered ore toward central trunks where it is digested into struts, blades, screws, seed-housings, and replacement organs for the landscape itself.

The brightness is immense. Not hot brightness, but cool, white, exacting light, multiplied by aerial mirrors and pale leaves polished to a mineral sheen. The sky is almost empty of clouds. Instead there are high lens-fields, enormous transparent membranes grown on tethered stalks, bending sunlight down into chosen corridors. Shadows are sharp and blue. Under the leaf roofs the air becomes glassy and cold, smelling of resin, chalk, metal filings, dry pollen, and the faint electrical odor before lightning.

There is no rain. Weather here is manufactured or biological, and none of it falls as water. In the mornings, if morning is the right word, a silver dust lifts from the salt seams and moves in slow sheets across the plain. Leaves turn edge-on to let it pass. Cilia along the walls comb the dust, sorting it by charge and grain size. By noon, the dust has become a weather system of its own, streaming through green industrial canyons where root-cranes swing silently overhead. Later, the air cools further, and crystal frost forms on the wax skins of the plants—not ice, but sublimed mineral bloom, delicate as feathers, harvested by fine black tendrils before it can dull the mirrors.

The place is active, but almost quiet. Its industry has been designed not to roar. Sound is swallowed by plant felt, by absorbent moss-panels, by thick leaves hanging like curtains between districts. You hear little things: the dry tick of seed-valves counting pressure; the soft creak of a trunk rotating its crown; a long breath from a vent hidden under lichen; the faint clicking of millions of stomata opening in sequence across a wall. Sometimes, far off, there is a low mechanical pulse, less like an engine than like a thought repeated under the ground.

The factories are not separate from the vegetation. They are groves. Their smokestacks flower. Their furnaces are wrapped in bark and cooled by arrays of pale fins. Inside them, branchwork descends through open shafts into darkness, feeding on mineral belts and cold combustion. Pistons rise within living trunks. Gears are grown from lignin reinforced with ceramic dust. Belts of braided vine turn slowly between wheels. The machines do not seem newly built; they seem cultivated over centuries, pruned into usefulness, repaired by grafting. Their surfaces are weathered but not ruined: polished where dust has moved against them for ages, scarred where old storms abraded the bark, thickened where structural stress taught the plant to grow more ribs.

The entire world has the settled feeling of an ancient system that has learned its own maintenance. Nothing is pristine. Everything bears use. Green catwalks sag and have been regrown from beneath. Old conduits are sealed in amber resin. Dead factory organs remain as trellises for newer growth. A wall may show twenty eras in cross-section: alloy mesh, root-skin, insulating cork, black photosynthetic lacquer, white ceramic scar, fresh tendril lace. Erosion exists here, but not as collapse. It is part of the design. Dry winds sand the surfaces smooth; dust feeds the mats; worn bark becomes powder; powder becomes structure again.

Fauna is rare. When something animal moves, it feels almost improper. A long-legged pollinator may cross a bridge at dusk, delicate as wire, its body sealed in a dry chitin suit, sipping charged pollen with a glassy tongue. Pale maintenance moths sleep in the folds of vents. In the deeper groves there are rumors of larger things: slow herbivores that eat obsolete leaves and excrete usable ceramic pellets; blind foxlike scavengers that nest in abandoned control knots; translucent birds that never land, gliding between mirror fields and feeding on static. But for days you may see no animal at all. The plants and machines have mostly taken over the roles of motion, hunger, repair, and decision.

There are districts where the vegetation is so dense that space becomes layered rather than open. You pass through curtains of vine-cable, under platforms grown from interlocked branches, beside walls of stacked leaf-panels vibrating with captured light. Above one roof is another roof, and above that a moving shade-net, and beyond that a lattice of photosynthetic towers, and beyond that mirrors. The air is crowded with function. Every surface is busy, yet nothing hurries. The world’s pace is industrial and botanical at once: valves cycling by the second, trunks thickening over years, continents of leaf turning over the course of a season.

The rules of the place are not entirely stable.

Distance does not hold still in the bright corridors. A row of identical green chimneys may be ten meters away, then a day’s walk, then close enough to touch without having moved. Roads grown in parallel sometimes cross themselves. A factory seen on the horizon appears also beneath your feet as a diagram in the root plates, and if you follow the diagram long enough, you arrive at the factory from inside. Some leaves cast shadows in directions unrelated to the sun. Some shadows continue after their leaves have folded.

This breakdown is not chaotic. It has been incorporated, engineered around, perhaps even cultivated. The plants use impossible geometry the way desert plants use shade. Branches enter spaces too small for them and emerge as entire groves. Storage pods bigger inside than outside hang from thin stems without strain. A maintenance corridor may loop through five climates of brightness and return to the same door before you have finished walking through it. Seeds are launched into future clearings, where they germinate before the parent flower opens. Workers, if there ever were workers, built warning signs into the bark: not words, mostly, but raised patterns you feel with the palm—do not measure here; do not count the pillars; do not trust the third reflection.

In some regions the reality failure is visibly botanical. Leaves are printed with veins that branch into maps of places not yet reached. Root systems grow toward causes instead of nutrients. Flowers open into brief rooms full of white light and dry air, then close again with the soft click of a cabinet. There are orchards of perspective, where each fruit contains a different horizon. If one falls and cracks, an avenue may appear for an hour, lined with machines that remember being trees.

And still the place is practical. It produces. It refines. It grows replacement parts, stores sunlight, manufactures atmosphere, braids structural fibers, grinds mineral dust, vents waste heat into cold towers, and folds its impossible spaces into usable volume. Its ecology is not wild in the old sense, but neither is it obedient. It has become an industrial organism with the patience of a biome. Built things sprout. Living things obey blueprints. Blueprints mutate.

At the lowest places, where a natural valley might once have held water, there are enormous dry reservoirs of leaf. They descend in shallow terraces, green-black and silver, each terrace packed with thousands of upright blades. The blades hum only when touched by light. They store charge in their skins and release it at night into buried grids. Walking beside them, you feel the hairs on your arms lift. There is no splash, no smell of mud, no insect cloud. Just the cool glare of living panels and the faint, synchronized flex of a continent breathing without lungs.

The absence of water gives everything a strange cleanliness. Decay is powdery, not rotten. Dead stems bleach to ivory and are milled by creeping root-machines. Fallen leaves curl into crisp spirals and are collected by static. Wounds seal with resin that hardens like green glass. There is no softness of damp earth, no mold-dark, no dripping cavern. Even the deep interiors are dry: vaults lined with cork, engine roots pulsing through mineral air, chambers where pollen lies in dunes and tiny harvesters move across it like thoughts.

Night, when it comes, is not dark. The mirrors dim, but the plants keep working. Stored light leaks from veins. Factory trunks glow faintly through their bark, green and amber and surgical blue. The sky shows stars in the gaps between lens-fields, but some of the stars are near, caught in the wrong layer of space, tangled in the high trellises like sparks in cobweb. The quiet becomes deeper. You hear the slow redistribution of charge across miles of leaves: a dry whisper, almost a snowfall, though nothing falls.

This is a desert made of vegetation, a factory made of forest, a low bright continent with no rivers and no raw ground. It has endured long enough to weather itself into beauty. It is too constructed to be natural and too alive to be merely built. Its silence is full of operations. Its greenery is dry as bone. Its horizons are unreliable. And everywhere, patiently, the plant-machines continue to make the world that holds them.
