It is a city under warm black water, though no hand has built it.

From above—if there is an above, if the rain-thick surface still counts as sky—the place would seem low and flattened, a drowned basin spread wide across the old coastal shelf. But from within, it rises in crowded neighborhoods: towers, ribs, colonnades, tunnels, courtyards, domes, all made from shell, lime, bone, sponge, hardened silt, and the slow masonry of animals. Nothing here is mechanical. Nothing has been cut, nailed, fired, or forged. Yet it has streets.

The water is nearly total. It is not merely a sea covering land; it is the condition of the world. Warm, dark, mineral-rich water fills every gap, presses into every hollow, carries taste and history through the city. There is no dry interior, no pocket of air preserved in a temple roof, no forgotten stairwell leading to land. All volumes are liquid volumes. Rain falls above in such density that the surface is less a boundary than a trembling ceiling, hammered continuously, dimming what little light might have descended. At times the rainwater forms a cooler skin overhead, sinking in pale curtains through the warmer deep, and the whole city shivers as invisible weather moves through it.

Light is almost absent. Shapes arrive by nearness. A wall exists when your body is almost against it. A tower becomes real as a darker mass inside darkness. The only illumination comes from living things: weak green pinpoints along the rims of shells, blue smears under the bellies of drifting animals, a soft amber pulse inside the mouths of filter-feeders. These lights are not enough to see by in any human way. They are signals, warnings, invitations, territorial marks. The city is legible to those who read pressure, scent, vibration, salinity, the lean of currents.

It has stood long enough to be weathered into its own law.

The oldest structures are broad foundations of reef-stone, built by generations of tiny architects that never knew the whole they made. Their dead white skeletons have been darkened by manganese, bruised with iron, and furred with soft black films of bacteria. Over them grow newer districts: stacked oyster balconies, coral buttresses, branching worm-castles, bulbous sponge rooms, and lacework vaults of bryozoans. Some towers have collapsed and been colonized horizontally, becoming arcades. Some tunnels have narrowed into gill-slits. Some plazas have filled with drifting silt until only the tips of their spires show, like teeth beneath a tongue.

The place is urban not by intention but by density. Life has learned to use every surface. A column is also a hunting post, a nursery, a current-break, a fungus garden, a map of chemical trails, a sleeping perch, a grave. Walls are layered with occupants no thicker than a fingernail: barnacles, worms, transparent shrimp, blind snails, cup-corals, soft mats that withdraw when touched. Under them, boring clams make hidden rooms. Inside the rooms, eels wait. Within the eels, smaller parasites move with their own hungers. Nothing is empty for long.

Vegetation is scarce. There is little true green here, no waving meadows of kelp, no flooded forest canopy. Plants exist only where accidents allow them: pale threads rooted in roof-silt near breaks in the rain-dark ceiling; leathery red leaves clinging to shallow cornices; mats of algae so dark they are almost purple, drinking the faintest possible light. Most nourishment comes by other means. The city feeds on falling matter, on warm currents, on the endless bloom of microbes in the black water, on bodies becoming other bodies.

The terrain beneath is low, a drowned plain gently tilting toward deeper trenches. There are no cliffs, but there are sudden neighborhoods of height where reef-building life has thickened for centuries. Between them lie avenues of mud, soft and swallowing, marked by the tracks of crawling animals. Some lanes are kept open by current, polished to rippled clay. Others clog with shell fragments and become slow drifts. The ground exhales in places: warm seeps push up through cracks, carrying sulfur and metallic taste, and around these vents the city grows especially dense, like a market around a spring.

Sound is subdued. Water holds sound differently, but here even that is muffled by softness, by silt, by the absorbing flesh of the city. There is the distant hiss of rain striking the surface far above. There is the occasional tick of shell against shell, the rasp of a grazer’s mouth, the brief percussion of a crustacean snapping its claw. Larger sounds travel as pressure rather than noise: a school turning all at once, a predator accelerating through a passage, the slow settling groan of a tower undermined by borers. Most of the time, quiet is not emptiness but attention. The city listens with thousands of skins.

Fauna is abundant, though rarely displayed openly. It layers itself according to size, hunger, and courage.

In the upper streets, near the faint rain-dim, small fish hang in clouds among the spires, each body no larger than a leaf, silver only when they turn. They pulse in and out of courtyards, forming temporary walls that part around predators and close again. Transparent jellies drift like lost lanterns, their tendrils combing the avenues. Long ribbon-creatures wind through windows and arches, tasting the stone with their flanks. Crabs the color of wet basalt occupy intersections, lifting their claws not in threat but in conversation with the current.

Lower down, where the warmth thickens and light is memory, the animals become slower, larger, more deliberate. Blind rays settle over the mud like dropped cloaks, breathing through hidden vents. Eels inhabit vertical shafts, their heads protruding from holes in the masonry, opening and closing their mouths as if reciting old civic law. Soft-bodied scavengers move after every death, turning a fallen fish into a clean architecture of bone within hours. Predators have learned the city’s geometry: they herd prey into dead-end chambers, wait behind sponge curtains, use clouds of stirred silt as doors.

There are districts named only by behavior.

One quarter is always full of larvae, a milky drift of beginnings. Adults of many species arrive there by scent during certain warm phases of the current, release eggs and seed into the dark, then vanish. The water becomes thick with tiny transparent bodies, each one briefly unowned. For several nights, the surrounding predators feed without rest. Still enough young survive to continue the city.

Another district is the shellfall, where old bivalve terraces shed their dead in slow avalanches. The slope rings faintly when disturbed, thousands of hollow halves sliding against one another. Creatures that need armor come here. Hermit crabs queue and contest silently for better houses. Small octopus-like animals sort through the shells with patient arms, testing each chamber, rejecting most.

There is also the breathing court: a broad circular depression ringed by sponge towers. Twice a day, though there are no tides in the old sense, a regional pulse moves through the basin. Water from beyond the city presses inward, cold at first, then warm, full of suspended food. The sponges open. Their pores widen. The entire court seems to inhale. Particles vanish from the water, lights brighten in response, fish gather, hunters gather behind them, and for an hour the quiet becomes crowded with motion. Then the pulse passes. The towers close. Waste drifts out in thin clouds. The court sleeps again.

The rules of the world are not written, but they are strict.

All construction must also eat, breed, shelter, or decay. Nothing remains merely decorative. A beautiful arch is beautiful because the current passes through it efficiently, because fish shelter in its shade, because its surface catches larvae, because its collapse will feed the mud.

No surface belongs to one life for long. Ownership here is temporary, chemical, constantly tested. A patch cleared by grazing mouths is settled by spores before the grazer has gone a body-length away. A dead sponge becomes a nursery. A living coral is tunneled by worms even as it grows. The city is stable because everything is being taken apart at nearly the same rate it is being made.

Darkness is not absence but medium. Vision is a minor language. To move safely, one follows gradients: warmth, salt, rot, mineral seep, alarm-musk, spawning-bloom, predator-skin. Every creature writes itself into the water and reads the others there. A newcomer would feel lost not because there are no signs, but because every sign is dissolving and being renewed at once.

The place is active without being loud. Processes overlap at biological speed. Silt drifts. Reefs accrete. Rain freshens the upper layers. Warm vents stain the lower ones. Shells grow, whiten, blacken, crack. Colonies bloom and retract. Predators patrol and wait. Young emerge in impossible numbers and mostly disappear. Whole neighborhoods migrate by millimeters as living edges extend toward food and abandoned interiors crumble behind them.

Sometimes, a tower falls.

It begins with no drama: a faint leaning, a few released bubbles, a change in the way current moves through adjoining lanes. For days or years, borers have been hollowing its base. Then one warm night, when rain above is especially hard and the pressure pulse arrives from offshore, the tower gives way. In air it might crash. Here it subsides. It folds sideways in a vast, muted motion, shedding fish, shrimp, worms, clouds of powdered lime. The impact rolls through the mud as a low push. Nearby animals freeze, then surge in. Predators take the stunned. Scavengers take the broken. Larvae find new edges. What had been height becomes a plaza, then a slope, then a reef again.

There may once have been a human city below all this, but if so, it is no longer separable from the living one. A straight line occasionally appears beneath growth, too straight for coral. A row of square openings persists in a buried wall. A broad avenue runs farther and more level than current alone would prefer. Yet no tool remains, no metal gleams, no glass holds its shape. Whatever intention made those first geometries has been eaten, softened, mineralized, and made available. The city has accepted the ruins as substrate, not memory.

Warmth lies everywhere, held close by the dark water. It is not tropical brightness but bodily warmth, womb-warm, fever-warm in the vent districts, gentle and enveloping in the open avenues. The water touches everything with the same persistence. It smooths edges, carries spores, hides distances, distributes the dead. It makes the whole place intimate despite its scale.

At the margins, the city thins into drowned flats. The structures lower, spread, become isolated mounds separated by black mud and wandering trails. Fauna still moves there, but with more space between bodies. Sparse red weeds cling to low ridges. Buried shells crunch softly under crawling feet. Beyond that is open basin, rain above, silt below, warm darkness between.

But inside the city proper, everything is near. A mouth is always close to another mouth. A wall is a colony. A street is a current. A shadow may be stone, animal, or both. The place does not sleep, though parts of it close and open according to cycles older than weather. It persists as a submerged settlement without builders, a dense warm architecture of appetite and patience, weathered into balance beneath a sky that has become water.
