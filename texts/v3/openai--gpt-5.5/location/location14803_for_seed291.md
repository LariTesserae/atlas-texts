The city lies low in a cold basin, not quite on land and not quite in a marsh, spread across a broad shallow flat where the ground has only recently decided to exist. It is a wide place, wider than it first appears: districts run outward in belts and loops, under viaducts and through reed fields, around freezing ponds and unfinished quays, until the horizon is crowded with chimneys, gantries, glasshouses, cranes, orchard towers, and the dark backs of distant factories. Nothing here has much height except what has been built. The terrain itself is almost level, a patient gray-green expanse of silt, peat, clay, slag, and new stone, still soft in places, still rising or sinking by inches.

The ground is young. You can feel it in the way streets buckle without having had time to grow old, in the way brick walls lean not from age but from the earth’s indecision beneath them. Some lanes have been laid over woven mats of root and iron. Some neighborhoods stand on piles driven into cold mud. In the newer quarters, the pavement is pale and raw, poured over ground that still exhales moisture through seams. In vacant gaps between buildings, mineral crusts bloom overnight: white salts, black nodules, thin blue films like tarnish on a coin. Foundations are never considered finished. Every cellar has pumps. Every bridge has adjustable joints. Every map is provisional.

Water is present, but not generous. It gathers in pockets, gutters, cistern-yards, tram hollows, low courtyards, and the basins of abandoned machinery. It does not make a great river through the city, only a nervous system of canals, seep-lines, drainage cuts, condensation pipes, and dark channels that appear between warehouses and disappear under wool mills. Rain comes in brief hard veils, then stops. Fog condenses on cables and drips from them for hours after the sky has cleared. Icicles form under catwalks, not long and clean, but stunted, smoky, flecked with soot. In the morning, the whole city seems to sweat cold.

Despite the cold, vegetation presses everywhere.

Not wilderness exactly. Not parks in any ordinary sense. Growth has occupied the city the way smoke occupies a room: finding every edge, every crack, every exposed surface, thickening where no one looks for a week. Moss pads the rails of unused sidings. Ferns grow in the brick throats of furnaces. Black-leafed vines climb the sides of condenser towers, their stems warm where they cross steam pipes and rimed with frost where they leave them. Low orchards have been planted on roofs, their branches trained horizontally beneath nets to keep off rooks and winged rats. Cabbages grow in courtyards over buried slag. Reed beds have colonized the drainage fields, so dense that they hide whole maintenance sheds. There are lichens on the telegraph poles, pale as old bone, and cold-loving creepers over the metal fences, green even in sleet.

The city has no empty spaces. Even its emptiness is occupied.

A square may look open from a distance, but within it are stalls, shrine-boxes, pigeon racks, pumping housings, stacks of cut peat, valves, temporary rails, frost covers, drying nets, vendor tents, municipal beehives, cable drums, and clusters of people standing as if waiting for news. Alleys are crowded with pipes at shoulder height, pipes at ankle height, bundles of insulated wire, hanging laundry gone stiff in the cold, racks of tools, cages of sleeping animals, water barrels, battery carts, and narrow stairways added after the buildings were complete. The roofs are another city: gardens, tanks, wind cowls, greenhouses, repair walks, small foundries, watch huts, and long glass corridors filmed with condensation.

There is industry everywhere, though it is not loud in the expected way.

The factories breathe more than they roar. Their engines turn inside thick walls, deep and muffled, like organs working under flesh. You hear belts whispering, pistons sighing, the click of relays, the soft clank of chain in fog, the occasional distant shudder when a press drops somewhere beyond the next block. The city’s sound is mostly close and minor: water ticking into pans, ice cracking in gutters, birds rustling under eaves, boots on damp boards, steam escaping through pinholes, fabric flapping once and then hanging still. Even the markets are subdued. People bargain in low voices. Bells are wrapped in cloth. Trams glide on wet rails with a restrained metallic hum, as though the whole settlement has learned that loud noises might disturb the ground before it is ready.

Yet the place is turbulent.

Not noisy, but unstable. Things are always changing, being assembled, disassembled, rerouted, shored up, drained, replanted, repaired. A street that was passable yesterday may be fenced off today because the clay beneath it has begun to dome upward in a long whale-back ridge. A warehouse wall may grow warm by noon and be covered in yellow fungus by evening. A canal may reverse its flow during a pressure change in the underground pumps. Scaffolds migrate through the districts like herds. Work crews follow cracks with paint and measuring rods. Steam vents open in courtyards without warning, exhaling mineral fog that beads on windows and eyelashes. The city is constantly under construction because the city and the land are being constructed together, neither one quite preceding the other.

In some places the new geology has been enlisted. There are extrusion yards where pale stone rises slowly from gridded pits, coaxed by heat and pressure into blocks, ribs, columns, and unfinished arches. There are municipal accretion gardens where mineral water drips over frames and leaves behind latticed walls. Some buildings appear grown rather than built: facades of layered calcite, slag-glass windows set into bone-colored deposits, staircases whose steps have thickened year by year around iron spines. Children are warned not to touch the youngest stone. It can take fingerprints. It can continue shaping itself around a hand.

The light is ordinary, or almost ordinary. A cold daylight comes down through a low, active sky, gray-blue in the morning, pewter at noon, briefly silver when the clouds thin. It shows everything clearly without warming anything. Wet brick darkens. Frost on leaves shines. Windows brighten in rows along factory fronts. At certain hours, when the vapor from the boiler districts spreads beneath the clouds, the whole basin glows with a flat luminous pallor, and distances become layered: chimneys, cranes, roof orchards, reed beds, apartment blocks, then farther works at the edge of visibility, each paler than the last.

The built city is dense but not orderly in the old sense. It has plans, many plans, laid over each other. There are imperial avenues interrupted by marsh channels. Worker barracks converted into seed laboratories. Tramlines that pass through the ground floors of markets. Long brick manufactories pierced by public footbridges. Arcades full of repair shops, apothecaries, boiler offices, eel sellers, wool sorters, lens grinders, root merchants, and quiet tea rooms where the windows look out on turbine yards. Elevated pipes carry warm waste water to the agricultural roofs. Cold pipes return condensed vapor to subterranean tanks. Old retaining walls have been cut open and occupied by dwellings. New retaining walls have shops in them already, though the mortar has not finished curing.

Every surface has a second use. Every object is part of a system.

A bench is also a valve cover. A shrine is also a weather station. A greenhouse wall is also a public notice board, crowded with damp paper and etched metal tags. Streetlamps hold nesting boxes. Chimneys carry signal flags. Canal railings support fungal trays. There are no purely decorative things, though many useful things have become beautiful through repetition and repair: brass pump handles polished by generations of gloves, blue ceramic pipe labels, black iron balconies furred with moss, red-painted flood marks, luminous algae jars hung in stairwells, the patterned frost on the inside of tram windows.

Animals thrive in the crowding. Not in great beasts, but in multitudes.

Rooks occupy the cranes. White-throated birds nest in the warm letters of factory signs. Foxes move along the steam conduits at night, their coats thick and dark, their paws leaving prints in soot rather than snow. Amphibians live in the condensation sumps, pale and slow, appearing after rain in basement wells and under market tables. There are eels in the drainage canals, some natural, some rumored not to be. Rats are common, but so are rat-hunting cats with frost-tipped whiskers, municipal owls housed in towers, and small shaggy dogs trained to find sinkholes before carts do. In the reed districts, insects gather in brief swarms whenever a warm vent opens, and birds descend on them in sudden silent spirals.

The fauna has adapted to the city’s rules, or perhaps the city has adapted to theirs. Many creatures know the pump cycles. Birds lift from the low roofs moments before pressure releases. Cats sleep on pipes that will remain warm but avoid those that sing before bursting. Children watch the animals to predict changes in the ground. If the canal eels rise, a frost is coming. If the moss beetles leave a wall, that wall will be demolished or will fall. If the roof geese refuse to land, no tram should cross the western viaduct after dusk.

There are formal rules too, painted everywhere in careful letters.

DO NOT DIG BELOW THE BLUE LINE.

REPORT NEW WARMTH.

KEEP DRAINS CLEAR OF ROOT-MASS.

NO FOUNDATIONS WITHOUT LISTENING LICENSE.

DO NOT FEED THE WHITE FISH.

AFTER RAIN, WAIT FOR THE SECOND BELL.

The rules are not superstitions, though some have become ritualized. The city survives by attending to small changes. A loose cobble may be a loose cobble, or it may be the first sign of a basin forming under the street. A patch of new grass may mean a leaking pipe, a buried corpse, or a seam of fertile clay rising from below. Steam in the wrong alley can kill. Silence in the wrong factory can mean pressure building where no one can see it.

The strangest district is the Unfounded Quarter, though people live there all the same. It stands on ground that did not exist a century ago and may not have fully committed to existing now. Streets are narrow and flexible, surfaced with hinged plates. Houses there have sliding foundations and walls hung from overhead frames rather than trusting the soil. Trees grow in movable tubs linked by roots that cross the street in covered channels. At night, residents claim to hear the ground practicing shapes beneath them: hollow knocks, wet settling, the granular hush of particles arranging themselves into future stone. In the mornings, new ridges appear under rugs. Doorframes shift. A room may become smaller by the width of a hand.

No one calls it haunted. Haunting implies the past. This place is troubled by the future.

Beyond the central districts, the city opens but does not loosen. It extends into production fields, reed lagoons, slag orchards, cold nurseries, brickworks, reservoir yards, and settlements clustered around pump towers. From an overlook on one of the railway embankments, you can see how vast and low it is: a packed basin of roofs and vegetation, gray industry and green persistence, all stitched together by lines of vapor. The horizon is not empty country but continuation. Smaller towns lean against the main city like attached organisms. Their smokestacks answer its smokestacks. Their drainage ditches feed its canals. Their workers arrive by silent electric trains through frost and fog.

The air smells of wet iron, peat smoke, cabbage leaves, coal oil, cold mud, machine grease, and crushed fern. Near the mineral yards it smells sharp, like struck stone. Near the animal markets it smells warm and musky despite the cold. Near the roof orchards in late season, there is the clean scent of hard little apples stored in straw. Sometimes, when the wind comes from the accretion pits, the whole eastern half of the city smells faintly of the sea, though no sea is near.

Weather moves through quickly but leaves residue. A half-hour rain blackens every wall and fills the tram grooves. A brief snow becomes gray slush, then freezes into granular crust along the gutters. Fog climbs from the canals and remains under bridges after the sun has burned it elsewhere. Hoarfrost forms on the inner leaves of vines growing over warm vents, so that a single plant may be green at the root, steaming at the stem, and silver-white at its outer tendrils. The cold is not clean alpine cold. It is low, damp, penetrating cold, the kind that sits in wool and brick and bone.

At dusk, the city does not blaze into brilliance. It lights itself cautiously. Lamps come on behind fogged glass. Factory windows glow in long amber strips. Greenhouses shine soft and dim, full of silhouettes of leaves. Signal lights blink on cranes and pump towers. In the canals, reflections break on thin skins of ice. The quiet deepens, though work continues everywhere. You see movement more than you hear it: carts crossing bridges, figures on gantries, steam folding around scaffold poles, birds adjusting themselves along cables, water trembling in a drain.

There is a sense, walking there, that the place is alive but not as a forest is alive, nor as a city is alive. It is alive as a wound heals, as a shell forms, as a machine regulates itself, as a colony of small creatures builds chambers in the dark. It is full of intention without having one mind. The humans build, the plants occupy, the animals interpret, the engines circulate heat and water, the young ground rises and slumps and mineralizes, and all of it negotiates hour by hour.

Nothing is finished. Nothing is abandoned for long. Even ruin is quickly recruited.

A collapsed warehouse becomes a trellised mushroom hall. A sunken tram becomes a bridge for moss and cats. A failed factory chimney is capped, filled with soil, and planted with winter pears. Old machines are not removed unless they must be; they are built around, inhabited, repurposed, made into supports for stairs, shrines, aqueducts, bird towers, public clocks. The city does not erase its mistakes. It crowds them, greens them, pipes them, braces them, and moves on.

Under all of it, the water gathers and withdraws. Under that, the ground forms itself slowly. Above it, cold light passes over roofs so densely occupied that snow rarely reaches them. The place is damp but thirsty, lush but frozen, urban but embryonic, quiet but never still. It is a city being born too late, after industry, after planning, after overcrowding, after the arrival of machines and rules and municipal departments; a newborn landscape already burdened with factories, markets, animals, gardens, and debts.

And if you stand in one of the low streets after rain, when the engines pause between cycles and the wrapped bells have not yet rung, you can hear the smallest sound in the basin: droplets falling from vines onto metal, then from metal into mud, then from mud into some deeper unfinished chamber beneath the city, where the next street, or river, or fault, or district is beginning to think its way upward.
