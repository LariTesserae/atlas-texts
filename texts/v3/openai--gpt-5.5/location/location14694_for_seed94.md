At that height the world should have been bare.

Instead it is crowded to the edge of suffocation: a high plateau so broad that its far side is not a horizon but a weather system, a green-black country lifted into the cool air, wet everywhere, breathing slowly under a ceiling of ordinary gray daylight. Clouds do not pass over it so much as graze against it. They snag on the ridges, tear open, and refill the land with fine rain. Water stands in every hollow. It gathers in the fur of mosses, beads on the waxy leaves of dwarf trees, trembles in the cups of carnivorous flowers, runs without urgency down stone ribs worn smooth by ages of weather.

The ground is old. Nothing here looks newly broken. The cliffs are rounded at their shoulders, the ravines deep but softened, the boulder fields settled into themselves and half-swallowed by lichen. Even the cracks in the rock have become habitats: fern-fronds sprout from them in dense combs, pale roots descend through them, small cold streams seep out of them with the patience of breath. The plateau has been wearing down for so long that its erosion has become a kind of architecture, though nothing has been built. Basins, shelves, ledges, channels, hanging bogs, seep-walls, root-laced gullies — all are naturally arranged, established, inevitable.

There is no empty surface. Stone is furred. Water is filmed. Mud is threaded. Every depression holds a miniature wetland, every slope a layered skin of vegetation. Low forests cling to the high ground, not tall but intensely packed: twisted resinous trees with black bark and small blue-green needles, broad-leaved shrubs glossy from constant mist, mosses stacked in cushions thick enough to hide an animal, reeds growing in mats over cold pools. Vines, though stunted by the chill, lace themselves through everything, binding tree to stone to peat to fallen trunk. The place has the density of a lung.

The air is cool enough that breath smokes faintly in the morning, but not cold enough to still life. It is a damp, muscular coolness, the kind that preserves scents: iron-rich mud, crushed fern, wet bark, fungal sweetness, mineral water, the faint animal musk of dens hidden under roots. Rain has been here recently; rain is always either arriving, departing, or condensing in silence on the undersides of leaves. Droplets collect until their own weight surprises them. They fall one by one into the undergrowth with soft ticks.

At first the landscape seems quiet. There is no great roar, no wind-wide forest surge, no obvious clamor of birds. Sound is held close by the wetness. The moss absorbs footfall. The peat swallows impact. Even streams, though numerous, run under mats and through stone throats, so that they make only a low internal murmur, as if the plateau is thinking to itself. But listen longer and the quiet becomes full: the minute clicking of insects folding and unfolding shell-like wings; the faint scrape of a hoof on lichen; the drip sequence from a leaning branch; the soft suck of mud releasing a foot; a distant, almost mechanical thump from somewhere below the ground.

That thump is one of the rules of the place.

There are no structures here, no roads, no cuttings, no towers, no remnants of hands. Yet the plateau has an industrial aspect, not because anything has been manufactured, but because its natural processes occur at a scale and rhythm that resemble engines. Beneath the peat and saturated roots, mineral vents pulse. They do not erupt violently. They cycle. Warm, iron-dark water rises through pressure seams, fills underground chambers, and is forced through narrow stone valves by the weight of the marshes above. The result is a slow piston-beat traveling through the ground. In places it lifts whole mats of vegetation by a finger’s breadth and lets them settle again. Pools shiver in perfect circles. Mud bladders inflate, gleam, and exhale vapor rich with metallic scent.

On the lower slopes of the plateau, where ravines cut down into older strata, these vents become more visible. Black stone throats line the streambeds like organ pipes. Some exhale steam. Some knock and clack as pebbles trapped inside them are lifted and dropped by water pressure. Some extrude thin ribbons of mineral deposit, building terraces grain by grain: ocher, silver-white, green, rust-red. They give the ravines the feeling of abandoned machinery, though they are older than any concept of machine. Waterwheels without wheels. Pumps without makers. Foundries made of pressure, gravity, chemistry, and time.

The vegetation has learned these rhythms. Around the vents grow dense colonies of reed-tubes with jointed stems, their inner membranes trembling when the ground pulses. They seem almost to listen. Their roots feed on mineral warmth, and in the coldest months they keep green circles open in the frost. Nearby, mats of coppery moss brighten where the water is richest. Fungi as large as shields grow in stacked shelves on the undersides of fallen trunks, their pores breathing vapor. Some plants open only when the subterranean beat reaches a certain interval; their leaves unclasp all at once, exposing pale interiors where midges gather.

The fauna is abundant but seldom loud. Small amphibians live everywhere: in leaf-cups, in hoofprints, in water gathered between stones. They are dark, slick, and nearly silent, except for the occasional glassy chirp from deep in the moss. Long-bodied fish move through the flooded root channels, their backs mottled like submerged bark. Pale crustaceans inhabit the mineral springs and are briefly visible when the vents flush them out into open pools. Herds of compact, shaggy grazers move across the bog-shelves in narrow files, placing their feet with extreme care. Their horns are flattened and ridged, useful for pushing aside wet shrub and scraping mineral crust from rocks.

Predators exist, but they share the plateau’s restraint. A catlike animal with a heavy tail and water-dark coat hunts from the moss banks, waiting for hours beside reed tunnels. Its ears are small, its paws wide, its whiskers long enough to read air currents under leaves. Higher still, among the stunted forests near the cliff crowns, pale birds nest in cavities eroded into the rock. They rarely call. They drop soundlessly through mist, take insects or small mammals, and vanish again into the gray.

There are larger creatures too, known more by displacement than sight. In the deepest bogs, something moves under the root mats, parting sedge and water with a patient pressure. It leaves channels that slowly close behind it. Sometimes a pool surface domes upward as it passes below, reflecting the sky as a bent, trembling oval. The smaller animals go still then, but do not flee far. This too is part of the plateau’s motion, one of its accepted processes.

Because the land is so high and so vast, weather breeds upon it. Valleys fill with fog while ridges remain clear. Rain may fall upward in the ravines when cold wind slides beneath warmer vapor rising from the vents. At midday, the cloud ceiling thins and normal daylight spreads across the plateau, even and colorless, revealing distance after distance of wet green terrain: endless shelves, bowls, low forests, shining pools, pale rock scars, dark ravine mouths. Nothing flashes brightly for long. Water dulls glare. Leaves hold light in subdued skins. The whole place appears saturated but not tropical, lush but cool, immense but intimate in its textures.

In the afternoon, condensation thickens. The cliffs begin to weep. Long curtains of seep-water appear on stone faces that had seemed dry an hour before. Moss swells visibly, deepening from gray to green. Flowers close. Ferns tilt under the accumulating droplets. Insects retreat into spiral cases or under leaf scales. The mineral vents continue their timed labor below, indifferent to weather. A slow knocking travels from basin to basin, answered by the soft collapse of saturated peat, the trickle of new runnels finding old paths, the occasional sigh of gas escaping through mud.

There are places where the plateau’s age is especially apparent: broad stone pavements polished by water and crossed by root-lines like dark script; ancient sinkholes transformed into circular forests; terraces formed by thousands of years of mineral overflow, each lip crowded with sedge; cliff edges rounded and undercut, their faces furred with hanging plants. Nothing here is raw. Even danger has been incorporated. Quicksilt lies under inviting moss. Cold pools hide sudden depth. Some vents release water hot enough to whiten animal bones, though steam disguises them as harmless mist. Certain flowering mats are not ground at all but floating skin over deep black water.

The world has rules, and most of them are hydrological.

Water must move, but rarely in a hurry. It climbs by vapor, seeps by pressure, falls by accumulation, returns underground through cracks and root-holes. Stone must soften without disappearing. Plants must occupy every pause. Animals must step lightly or learn to swim. Heat belongs below; coolness belongs above; between them the plateau grows its engines.

No fire lasts here. Lightning does strike the high ridges, but flame is brief, hissing out in wet lichen and smoke. Death does not remain exposed. A fallen animal is opened by beetles, softened by rain, entered by fungus, furred with moss, and within a season becomes a raised green place where shrubs grow slightly brighter. Even bones stain with minerals and sink into peat. The land wastes nothing. Its density is not chaotic but total.

From a distance — if distance can be found in a place so large — the plateau resembles a single organism: wet-skinned, cool-blooded, green-backed, its ravines like folds, its vents like valves, its marshes like organs processing cloud and stone. But inside it, among the individual leaves and pools and root tunnels, it is not singular at all. It is innumerable. Every square pace contains competition, shelter, rot, growth, condensation, pressure, hunger, and rest.

Evening does not darken dramatically. The light merely lowers into a bluer gray. Fog gathers first in the hoof channels, then in the basins, then among the trees. The animals become outlines. The vents sound clearer in the muffled air: a distant thud, a hollow gulp, a series of clicks like cooling metal. Water drops steadily from branch to branch. Somewhere a bird shifts in its cliff hollow. Somewhere under the moss, a bladder of warm mineral water fills, tightens, and releases.

The whole high wet country continues its quiet production through the night: not producing goods, not serving any maker, but manufacturing soil from stone, forest from vapor, motion from pressure, life from cold water and time.
