At the top of the world is a city that was not built by hands.

It occupies a long, high basin between black volcanic escarpments, so elevated that the lower clouds lie beneath it like a second, drowned earth. The air is hot and saturated. Breathing feels like drinking from a steaming cloth. Every surface sweats. Stone beads with water. Leaves drip without wind. The cliffs surrounding the basin weep from thousands of pores, and the water does not so much fall as continuously appear, gathering into silver skins that slide down the rock faces and vanish into terraces of moss.

The city rises from the basin floor in tiers: towers, walls, colonnades, plazas, canals, stairs, bridges, all recognizable as urban forms, yet none of them cut or carved. They are grown, accreted, secreted. Their material is a pale mineral like bone and limestone, veined with green roots and translucent amber sap. Some walls are bark. Some arches are folded leaves hardened into architectural permanence. Some streets are braided roots polished smooth by no footfall at all. Domes swell like seedpods. Balconies hang from cliff-sides in clusters like wasp nests, though nothing has ever flown here.

There are no tools, no metal, no wheels, no glass, no fire. The city has the organization of intention without any evidence of makers. Drainage channels run exactly where they should. Causeways lift above marshy ground. Open courts collect mist and shed it into cisterns. Doorways open into chambers, and chambers into halls, and halls into larger civic emptinesses where a population could gather, if population were a thing this place permitted.

But there are no animals.

No birds circle the heights. No insects stitch the damp air. No fish stir in the canals. No worms turn the black mud beneath the fern beds. The only life is vegetable, fungal, lichenous, and even that life seems less to grow than to maintain a decision made long ago. Lushness is everywhere: enormous ferns with lacquered fronds; vines thick as columns; mosses layered like velvet on the steps; hanging gardens spilling down the sides of abandoned-looking towers; orchids rooted in cracks, their flowers the color of bruised moonlight and fresh blood. Yet nothing sprouts, withers, blooms, or rots. The leaves are always at their fullest. The flowers are always just open. Fallen petals hang in the air beneath their stems, suspended a finger’s width from the wet stone, as though the act of falling has been indefinitely postponed.

The heat is immense, but not burning. It is the heat of a sealed greenhouse, of geothermal breath, of a mountain whose heart remains molten beneath a skin of ancient city. Steam issues from grates that are not grates but regular pores in the pavement. Warm water fills the channels and pools. The marshes at the city’s edge simmer gently without bubbling. The smell is mineral, green, and heavy: wet limestone, crushed leaves, salt, fungal sweetness, and something like rain on hot dust, though dust cannot survive here.

Beyond the central terraces, the basin opens into a vast marsh plateau. Black reeds stand taller than houses, their stems packed close together, their upper blades motionless against the white sky. Causeways cross them in straight lines, then curve in impossible arcs, meeting at plazas half-submerged in shallow water. Farther out, the ground breaks into floating-looking districts: neighborhoods of root-pillars and leaf-roofs, pale towers reflected in mirror pools, ceremonial avenues leading nowhere but to cliffs and curtains of vapor.

The elevation reveals itself in glimpses. At the rim of the city, where the marsh ends abruptly, the land drops away into blue emptiness. Lower mountain ranges look like wrinkles. Rivers below are thin threads of light. Storms move under the city, not above it, flashing silently in the depths of cloud. Yet here, on the summit basin, the weather remains constant: hot, wet, bright without glare. The sun is visible but uncertain. Sometimes it appears in two places at once, one disk behind cloud, one reflected in a pool that has no corresponding sky. Shadows do not always belong to the objects that cast them. Some colonnades have shadows of trees; some trees have shadows of towers.

The sound is overwhelming.

Not loud in bursts, not thunder or collapse, but a total, continuous roar that fills the body until thought becomes something done with the bones rather than the ears. Water is the main voice: cliff-sheets, hidden channels, roof-drips, cistern overflows, subterranean rivers under the pavement. Yet the noise is too large for the visible water. It is as if every canal contains the sound of an ocean, every drop repeats the same waterfall, every wet leaf contributes an impossible volume. The city itself hums beneath the roar, a deep standing note held by stone, root, and vapor. In narrow alleys the sound presses close and tactile, vibrating in the teeth. In open plazas it becomes vast, white, almost flat, a deafening silence made of too much continuity.

Nothing changes, but everything implies movement.

The waterfalls are fixed in their falling, yet the sound of impact never ceases. Steam leans from vents in motionless plumes. Ripples pattern the pools, but the ripples neither spread nor fade. A bead of condensation trembles at the tip of a leaf forever, fat with imminent release. A curtain of rain hangs above one district, each drop elongated and bright, stopped between sky and pavement; beneath it, the stones are wet, and the roar insists that rain is falling.

The world has rules here, but they are not the rules of sequence.

Distance is obedient only when watched directly. A stairway of twenty steps may take an hour to climb if counted, or deliver the climber instantly to an upper terrace if crossed without attention. Streets repeat, not as copies but as recurrences, like a word spoken in different rooms. A plaza seen from above is circular; entered from the east, it becomes square; crossed from north to south, it is a bridge over an unseen gorge. Doorways frame interiors that should not fit within their towers: fern cathedrals, root libraries, warm cisterns reflecting constellations not present in the sky.

Time does not pass, yet age is everywhere. The city is weathered into equilibrium. Edges are rounded by rain that never began and never ends. Stone steps are hollowed in the middle, though no feet have worn them. Walls bulge and settle like old masonry. Roots have gripped and split terraces, then stopped at the exact point where ruin becomes structure. Nothing is new, nothing is decaying. It has all already arrived at its final form and remains there, lush, wet, hot, complete.

There are districts that seem residential: stacked chambers with vine-curtains over openings, balconies facing inner courts, water troughs running along thresholds. There are districts that seem ceremonial: long avenues lined with petrified trunks, amphitheaters of mossed stone, circular pools fed by channels from all directions. There are market-like arcades where shelves grow from walls and hold nothing but shallow bowls of rainwater, identical and full. There are tower-clusters joined by bridges so thin and organic they resemble tendons stretched between bones.

But the absence of inhabitants is not emptiness. The place is crowded with presences that are not creatures. Plants lean as if listening. Doorways feel occupied by the idea of passage. The pools reflect figures that vanish if one turns around too quickly, not because anything has moved, but because reflection here is allowed to remember possibilities the world itself rejected. The city has civic density without society, architecture without labor, ecology without animals, sound without event.

At the margins, the marsh thickens into a green-black labyrinth. The reeds there grow in exact grids, forming corridors too straight to be natural and too alive to be artificial. Their bases stand in warm water the color of tea. Mats of floating vegetation form platforms that support pale root-shrines, each one shaped like a small house with no entrance. The air above the marsh is full of suspended droplets, each droplet containing a miniature inverted version of the city. In some, the towers are burning. In some, the basin is dry. In some, animal silhouettes crowd the plazas. None of these versions are true here.

The cliffs enclosing the basin are enormous, streaked with mineral color: red iron, green coppery stains, white calcite, black basalt. Water seeps from them in organized lines, as though the mountain possesses plumbing grown from geology. Caves open behind the veils of water. Inside are chambers cool only by comparison, their ceilings hung with roots and stalactites fused together. The deeper caves contain walls marked by natural patterns that almost become writing, but never quite cross into symbol. Meaning approaches and withdraws. The stone refuses to say whether it remembers language or predates it.

Light remains steady, neither dawn nor noon nor dusk. It is sufficient, warm, diffuse, a pearly brightness filtered through vapor. Colors are saturated but softened: the deep green of wet leaves, the yellow-white of mineral walls, the dark mirror of standing water, the reddish undersides of giant fronds, the blue distance beyond the cliffs. At times the light seems to come from the sky; at times from the pools; at times from the pale architecture itself.

If one were to stand in the central square, the whole place would reveal its balance.

Water would roar from every direction without visibly increasing. Heat would rise without shimmer. Towers would lean but never fall. Vines would bind stones but never strangle them. The marsh would press close to the city and the city would press back into the marsh, neither conquering the other. Above, the fixed clouds would drag their bellies across peaks without moving. Below, the unseen world would continue somewhere far beneath, irrelevant and unreachable.

This is a summit city of wet stone and impossible botany, ancient but not past, loud but still, lush but without animal breath. It is not abandoned. It is simply a place where habitation took a different path: where the forms of settlement grew directly from heat, water, mineral, and leaf; where reality softened under pressure and then set again; where every event has been held forever at the point of becoming.
