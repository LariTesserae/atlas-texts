At the top of the mountain there is a crack no wider than a shoulder, almost invisible unless the light catches the metal filings lodged in its lip. The mountain itself is one of those high, dry teeth of stone that rise above weather: not above air, exactly, but above the ordinary world of dust, rain, birds, and thaw. Its sides are blue-black basalt, old as cooled slag, combed by centuries of wind into vertical fluting. Far below, clouds drag themselves along the lower ridges. Up here the air is thin, cold, and powder-dry.

The crack opens inward.

Inside is a hidden garden.

Not a garden in the generous sense of beds and paths and sky, but a sealed pocket wedged within the summit, an intimate chamber perhaps fifteen paces across, taller than it is wide, pinched at the top like a chimney. It feels discovered rather than entered, as if the mountain had once swallowed a factory greenhouse and then forgotten to digest it. Every surface is occupied. There is no bare wall, no open floor, no easy place for a foot. Growth presses from all sides, layered and matted and interlocked, dense enough to seem woven.

Yet there is no water here.

No drip, no damp scent, no mossy coolness. The stones are dry to the touch. The air has the crisp mineral sting of powdered salt, hot metal gone cold, old electrical insulation. Nothing gleams wetly. Nothing beads. If a tongue were put to the wall, it would taste of alkali and iron, not moisture. The lushness of the place is of another chemistry.

The plants, if they are plants, are built for dryness so absolute it has become a medium of its own. They rise from seams in the stone as translucent blades, black-green and smoke-violet, with edges like mica sheets. Their stems are jointed, fibrous, packed with pale dust instead of sap. Some have leaves like folded foil, ribbed and veined with coppery threads. Others form masses of hair-fine needles, silver at the tips, dark at the roots, packed so tightly together that they resemble fur, though there are no animals here and never have been. Thick ropes of rootwork descend from the ceiling, not searching for water but for charge, clinging to veins of ore and old wiring alike.

The lushest growth is gathered around the machines.

They stand in scattered pieces through the chamber: not enough to make a functioning factory, but too intentional to be ruin. A waist-high flywheel half-buried in mineral fronds. A ceramic switchboard mounted crookedly to the wall, its levers fixed in place, every label worn blank. Three narrow pipes entering through the stone and vanishing beneath a bristling thicket of carbon-black stems. A small engine casing, green with mineral patina rather than rust, set on a plinth of poured concrete that has cracked only once and then remained that way for ages.

Everything industrial here is compact, old, and stubborn. Nothing is elegant. Bolts are oversized. Plates are riveted. Insulators are glazed brown and cream. There are iron brackets, ceramic bushings, glass tubes clouded from within, and bundled cables whose cloth wrappings have become root substrate. The built things have not conquered the chamber; they have become trellises, bones, strata.

The light is dim and sourceless at first. After a while the eye finds it: a low phosphorescence running along the edges of leaves and wire, a greenish-gray shine like the last light inside a dying vacuum tube. It does not flicker. It does not brighten when touched. It lays itself evenly across the packed growth, revealing depth only slowly. Shadows are soft but complete. The back of every object seems to vanish.

The sound is immense.

For such a small chamber, the noise should be impossible. It is not a crash or a roar of motion, but a sustained industrial note, a constant pressure of sound that fills the ribs and teeth. A turbine-hum. A substation drone. A deep metallic vowel held forever in the throat of the mountain. Beneath it are higher tones: the fine keening of tensioned wires, the brittle hiss of dry leaves vibrating against one another, the periodic-seeming but never actually changing clack of something inside the wall. Listen long enough and the pattern refuses to resolve. There is no rhythm, only permanence shaped like rhythm.

The place is loud the way a magnet is strong: not by event, but by field.

Nothing moves visibly. The blades do not sway. The needle-masses do not tremble, though the air vibrates with sound. Dust rests on the upper surfaces of leaves in perfect gray gradients. A fallen washer lies cupped in a calyx of black glassy petals, and it has clearly lain there long enough for the petals to have grown around it—if growth happens here at all. The whole chamber gives the impression of having arrived at its final arrangement long ago. Not dead. Not dormant. Complete.

The ecology is closed and precise.

The root-ropes drink from stone, but not as roots drink in softer worlds. They clasp ore, salts, graphite, the buried tails of cable. The leaf-surfaces gather static from the endless charge moving through the chamber. Fine mineral hairs comb ions from the air. Dust settles, is held, is slowly unmade. There are seed-pods, but they are not meant to travel: faceted kernels locked inside cages of dry fiber, waiting for a break in a wall that may never come. Propagation here is not a season but a contingency. If a stone cracks, a root enters. If a wire is exposed, a sheath of growth embraces it. If nothing changes, the garden remains exactly as it is.

There are no insects, no mites, no nesting things, no bones. No sound of wings, no scratch, no hidden breathing. The absence of fauna is not emptiness but law. The chamber is too sealed, too dry, too electrically constant for animal life. Anything soft-bodied would become a salt outline. Anything that needed blood would fail. The plants have no flowers for pollinators, only conductive vanes, heatless bulbs, sporeless crowns. They do not invite. They endure.

The temperature is cool and unwavering. Not freezing; not warm. The kind of cold found inside thick stone before dawn, but prolonged forever. Touching the metal would numb the fingers. Touching the plants would be worse: not sharp exactly, but dry enough to draw oil from skin, leaving a papery sensation. The air does not circulate in any ordinary way. It seems held under a lid, compressed by altitude, machinery, and mineral metabolism.

Near the chamber’s rear wall, half-hidden behind a curtain of violet blades, is the largest structure: a narrow vertical cylinder bolted between floor and ceiling. It resembles a boiler, though it gives no heat. Its surface is matte black, banded with pale ceramic rings, and from those rings the thickest growth emerges. Leaves overlap it like scales. Copper roots vanish into its seams. On one side is a round glass gauge, intact but opaque. The needle inside points to a mark that has no number.

The cylinder is the heart of the room, or the room has decided to make it so. The loud hum seems to come from it and from everywhere else equally. Put an ear near it and the sound is not stronger, only more intimate, as if the same note has become aware of being heard.

The floor slopes subtly toward the cylinder, though there is nothing here that flows. Instead of soil there is a layered mat of mineral litter: shed leaf-films, salt flakes, powdered ceramic, graphite dust, splinters of insulating glass, fragments of old labels, and the fine black chaff of stems that have dried without decaying. It crunches softly under pressure, then settles back without spring. Every depression remains. The place records touch because touch is rare.

One can see the age of the chamber in its balance. The basalt walls have been polished where the vegetation presses them. Metal has not rusted away; it has mineralized, accepting skins of carbonate and oxide, wearing them like varnish. Edges have softened, not through weather but through contact. Leaves have abraded pipes. Roots have cinched rivets. The machinery has stained the plants, and the plants have insulated the machinery. Nothing is new, but nothing is collapsing.

If there was once a purpose, it has become indistinguishable from habitat. Perhaps the summit housed a relay engine, a pressure station, an atmospheric condenser that never condensed water but harvested charge from dry wind. Perhaps workers once climbed the outer face and entered with lamps and tools, wiping salt from their goggles, shouting over the engine note. Perhaps they built this pocket to maintain something larger in the range below. If so, their traces have been reduced to a few human-scale facts: the height of a handle, the spacing of bolts, a ladder rung almost swallowed by growth, a porcelain cup fused to a shelf by mineral accretion.

But the chamber no longer feels abandoned. It feels inhabited by process.

The rules here are strict:

Nothing evaporates because nothing wets.

Nothing blooms because nothing needs invitation.

Nothing hunts because nothing flees.

Nothing rots because there is no softness for rot to enter.

Nothing changes because all gradients have been spent.

The mountain supplies cold. The old machine supplies tone. The stone supplies minerals. The hidden plants supply form. Together they have made a sealed equilibrium: lush without fertility, industrial without labor, loud without event.

At the entrance crack, the outer world remains almost impossible to believe in. There is sky out there, enormous and thin, with ravens perhaps far below and weather moving somewhere beneath the summit. But inside, the scale collapses to the closeness of a room, a throat, a lung that does not breathe. The hidden garden presses inward from every side, dense as felt, dim as ash, humming with a note older than memory.

And if one leaves, squeezing back through the basalt seam into the cold height, the sound does not fade at once. It stays inside the skull for a long time: a dry green engine, a garden of no water, still running exactly as it has always run.
