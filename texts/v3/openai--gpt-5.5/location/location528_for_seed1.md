The land is low enough that it seems never to have risen from under the sea, only negotiated a temporary exposure. It stretches outward in a wide, dim basin of black stone, gray ice, iron mud, and water lying everywhere in sheets too shallow to be lakes and too persistent to be puddles. There are no trees, no moss, no reeds, no grass along the margins. Nothing green interrupts it. The horizon is open, but not empty: the whole plain is crowded with surfaces, skins, membranes, slick mineral plates, steaming seams, canted shelves of old basalt polished flat by ages of weather. Every inch appears occupied by some process.

The ground is ancient in the way bones are ancient after the animal has been gone so long that the marrow is only a rumor. It has been worn down, compressed, drowned, frozen, thawed, and refrozen until its original violence has become a kind of level patience. Ridges remain, but they are low and rounded, the stumps of former mountains planed into dark backs beneath a wet, colorless sky. Shallow channels braid through them, not cutting so much as remembering the idea of cutting. The water does not flow cleanly. It creeps, beads, gathers under transparent crusts, seeps upward through cracks, and weeps down stone faces that are barely high enough to deserve the name.

Everything is cold. Not the clean cold of snowfields or mountain air, but a soaked, conductive cold that enters through contact: through the soles, through the palms, through breath. Mist condenses constantly, not falling as rain so much as assembling itself on every surface. Rocks sweat. Ice forms in thin black lenses under the water, then fractures with a sound like distant glassworks. Vapor rises from warmer fissures and immediately becomes silver rime, coating nearby stone in delicate, needlelike mineral frost. The air has weight. It presses close to the ground as if the sky is lying down.

There is no built thing here, no wall, no pipe, no road, no tool, no remnant of intention. Yet the place behaves industrially.

From beneath the basin comes a constant mechanical labor. Subterranean engines—if they are engines—turn without operators. They do not sound like machines made by hands, but like machines that preceded hands: huge rotary pressures grinding in the crust, pistons of trapped tidewater shunting through basalt chambers, mineral valves opening and slamming shut under thermal strain. Every few minutes, a low boom rolls underfoot, followed by a shuddering clank from somewhere far away in the fog. Vents cough in sequence. Pools vibrate into concentric rings. Metallic knocking travels through the stone as if through a factory floor buried miles below the world.

The water participates. In some basins it boils without heat, the bubbles rising slow and angular, each one reflecting a different version of the sky. In others, the surface is skin-thick and taut, dented by the feet of animals that should sink but do not. Certain channels run uphill for a few yards before losing conviction and spreading sideways. Some pools are deeper inside than outside; their rims are only ankle-high, but their centers contain vertical darkness and the groan of enormous pressure. When the wind passes over them, they hum in tuned chords, a wet organ of stone throats and hollowed caverns.

The light is dim and reluctant. There is no single sun visible, only a broad pallor behind layered cloud, sometimes doubled, sometimes displaced. Shadows do not agree on direction. At the edges of puddles, a faint blue-white glow gathers and fades, as if the water is remembering lightning. The horizon appears nearer in some places than in others. A ridge half a mile away may loom close enough to touch, while a seal-slick stone at one’s feet may seem to lie across a valley. Perspective tires here. Distance has been overused.

The basin is packed with life, though almost none of it suggests vegetation or growth in any familiar upward sense. The fauna keep low, slick, plated, jointed, armored against cold and pressure. They crowd the shallows and the fissures and the undersides of mineral shelves. Many are pale, nearly translucent, their organs arranged like dim lamps or coiled wire. Others are black and glossy as furnace slag. There are eyeless grazers that rasp at chemical films on wet stone—if grazing can exist where no plant does. They move in herds across the flats, each animal no taller than a hand but long as a child, with dozens of small, synchronized legs that click against the rock like typebars.

Among them hunt larger things.

In the fog, low bodies slide between pools, their hides stippled with frost and oil-sheen. They carry their heads close to the ground, listening through jawbones to vibrations below. When they strike, they do it with terrible mechanical speed: a clamp of cartilage, a backward convulsion, a spray of black water. Their prey scatter in radial patterns, not fleeing away from danger but into alternate angles of the same moment. For a second the plain fills with duplicates—three herds, then one, then none where there should have been many. The predator pauses, confused or satisfied, with half a meal in its mouth and half in another local truth.

There are also tall fauna, though “tall” is relative on this flattened land. They stand on stilts of pale bone or chitin, picking their way through the wetlands with solemn care. Their bodies are small compared to their legs, suspended above the cold seepage like lanterns from tripods. Beneath their bellies hang clusters of breathing sacs that inflate and deflate with wheezing, bellows-like rhythm. They gather around vents where mineral warmth rises, not in nests or colonies but in queues, each animal waiting for a turn at the exhalation. When one steps too near a seam, the ground may open and swallow only its reflection. The animal remains, but its mirrored self thrashes below the water until something unseen consumes it. Afterward the survivor walks oddly, as if missing a weight.

No plants soften the land. Instead, the ecology is built on mineral films, thermal gradients, saline crusts, and perhaps time itself. Slick gray mats spread across certain stones—not fungal, not vegetal, but something simpler and older, a living tarnish that thickens where water carries dissolved metals. Tiny animals plane it away with mouthparts like scrapers. Others filter mist. Some feed on sound. These gather near the loudest vents, unfolding ribs or membranes to absorb the pulses. Their bodies tremble with each subterranean strike, converting vibration into movement, heat, reproduction, thought.

The loudness is never uniform, but it is always present. There is the slap and suck of shallow water under shifting ice. There is the hiss of condensation on warm mineral seams. There are the clicks, rasps, clatters, bellows, shrieks, and wet percussion of innumerable animals. There is the deep labor below: grinding, thumping, cycling, venting. Sometimes the basin releases a long metallic scream, and everything living freezes. The scream rises beyond hearing into pressure. Water lifts in all the pools at once, standing convex above their rims. Mist pulls itself into ropes. The sky wrinkles. Then the sound breaks, and the whole plain resumes, louder than before, as if relieved.

The weather moves in cycles but not seasons. A period of condensed stillness may last hours or centuries; it is difficult to tell. During it, droplets grow on every surface until the world becomes jeweled and heavy, each stone wearing thousands of trembling lenses. Then a pressure change passes through the basin. Fog tears into streamers. The shallow waters begin to crawl in one direction, dragging skins of ice and rafts of mineral scum. Animals emerge from cracks by the thousands, responding to some cue in pressure or magnetism. Predators follow. Vents flare white with cold vapor. The ground ticks and booms.

After the active interval comes the freezing drift. Air temperature falls even further. The water does not vanish, but stiffens into layered transparencies, each layer capturing a different motion. Fishlike things are sometimes frozen mid-turn in one sheet and continue swimming in the next. Footprints fill with dark water from below and seal over. The mineral films go dull, then bright, then dull. Creatures crowd into fissures or beneath overhangs no higher than a wrist. The larger hunters lie flat in brine pools with only their nostril slits exposed, indistinguishable from broken stone except for the slow opening and closing of their flank vents.

Nothing here feels young. Even the animals seem inherited rather than born. They carry the design of long repetition: armored against abrasion, tuned to pressure, adapted to paradox. Eggs are laid in cracks where heat and cold meet, but they do not simply hatch. They accrete. Mineral coats form around them, dissolve, reform. Embryos wait for the correct sound, the correct tremor, the correct impossible alignment of reflected skies. Some may wait a thousand years. Some hatch before they are laid. Some crawl back into their shells after living out their lives, and the shell seals, and the cycle becomes difficult to name.

The rules of the world are local and negotiated.

A stone may be solid until observed in reflection, where it flows like wax. A pool may be shallow to the knee but contain drowned thunderheads beneath its surface. Sounds cast shadows. Heat falls downward like sediment. Certain animals can only be seen when they are behind you, appearing in peripheral water-glints, vanishing when faced. Tracks lead both toward and away from the creature that made them. Dead things do not rot; there is no warmth for rot, no vegetation to take them in. Instead, they are polished by weather and consumed by chemistry, reduced to shining articulated mineral husks that join the packed complexity of the ground.

At times the plain doubles itself. A second basin hovers slightly misaligned with the first, visible through fog or thin ice: the same low ridges, the same crowded pools, but with different animals moving across them, or no animals at all, or immense dark structures that cannot exist because nothing built has ever stood here. They appear only as pressure-ghosts, silhouettes of industry without builders—towers like vertebrae, belts of moving black water, chimneys breathing stars. Then the vision slips. The land remains untouched, unconstructed, yet the subterranean engines continue, and the sound of vanished factories keeps traveling through the stone.

There are places where the ground is so densely marked by processes that it seems impossible to set a foot down without interrupting ten lives and three physical laws. A film is feeding on iron. A cluster of translucent larvae is drinking the film. A crablike hunter waits beneath the larvae. A vent below the hunter exhales mineral vapor that freezes into needles. The needles focus dim light into a pool whose surface reflects not the sky but an older shoreline. In that reflection, a herd of animals crosses. Their passage causes ripples in the present pool. The ripples wake something buried. It knocks once, very deep.

The knock is answered across the basin.

One by one, vents begin to pulse. The rhythm spreads outward: boom, hiss, clank, groan; boom, hiss, clank, groan. Animals raise their bodies. Scrapers lift their mouths from stone. Tripod creatures unfold their bellows sacs. Predators pause with prey half-swallowed. Even the mist seems to listen. The whole wet lowland becomes an instrument, crowded with cold life and ancient mineral labor, dimly lit under a sky that cannot decide how many heavens it has.

Then the water rises a few inches everywhere.

Not from rain. Not from tide. From within.

It wells through seams, pores, old fractures, the microscopic spaces between mineral grains. The plain darkens as if ink is spreading beneath its skin. Creatures climb onto stones that are barely higher than the rest of the world. Some fail. Some adapt instantly, opening fins that were not there a moment before. The thermal vents choke, then resume with deeper voices. Ice breaks into plates and begins rotating in place. The horizon bends inward, making the basin appear like the inside of a vast bowl, though walking toward its rim never brings one higher.

There is no catastrophe in this. It is one of the ordinary moods of the place. The basin floods, unfloods, freezes, steams, splits, heals, remembers, forgets. It is a marsh without plants, a factory without builders, a coast without sea, a tundra without snow, an old wound that has become an ecosystem.

And always the ancient ground remains low and crowded beneath the cold dim air, wearing its water like a second skin, speaking in iron knocks and animal cries, enduring the breakdown of reality not as an event but as weather.
