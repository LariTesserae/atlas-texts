## Preparation Guide for Visiting the High Worn Plateau

An ordinary human traveler can survive here, but should not treat this place as wilderness, ruin, or city in the usual sense. It is a cold high country where geology, weather, memory, and local custom all have practical force. The greatest danger is not a monster or a war. It is misjudging something quiet: distance, damp, frost, a road, a rule, a pool, a wall, a polite instruction from someone who has lived there long enough to know better.

Prepare as if visiting a high-altitude polar plateau, an archaeological city, and a mildly unreliable universe at the same time.

---

## 1. Physical Conditioning and Medical Preparation

### Acclimate for altitude

The air is thin and cold. Before arrival, prepare for:

- Shortness of breath
- Headaches
- Poor sleep
- Nausea
- Impaired judgment
- Slower healing
- Faster dehydration than expected

Spend time in high-altitude environments if possible before traveling. If arriving by portal, do not begin walking immediately. Spend at least two or three days near a settlement or sheltered waystation while your body adjusts.

Bring or arrange:

- Acetazolamide or equivalent altitude medication, if compatible with your biology
- Portable oxygen or emergency pressure hood if available
- A pulse oximeter
- Electrolytes
- Anti-inflammatory medication
- Wound-care supplies
- Treatment for hypothermia and frostbite

Do not assume “damp” means “hydrating.” Cold, thin air can dry you out even while everything around you is wet.

---

## 2. Clothing and Personal Equipment

The plateau is cold, damp, windy, and frost-prone. You need gear that handles moisture as much as cold.

### Wear layered clothing

Recommended:

- Wool or synthetic base layers
- Insulating mid-layer
- Windproof outer shell
- Waterproof but breathable cloak or coat
- Wool socks, several pairs
- Insulated boots with excellent grip
- Waterproof gaiters
- Warm gloves plus thin work gloves
- Face covering or scarf
- Eye protection against snow glare, fog glare, and windblown frost

Avoid cotton. It will hold moisture and make you cold.

### Footwear is critical

You will walk over:

- Frost-polished stone
- Gravel flats
- slick lichen
- moss that may conceal holes
- shallow basins filled with ice
- shifting city streets
- stone stairs of uncertain age

Bring boots with aggressive tread. Carry removable crampons or microspikes. Bring repair materials: waxed thread, awl, boot glue, spare laces, leather patches.

### Keep everything dry, but expect failure

Use:

- Waxed canvas or dry bags
- Oilskin wrapping
- Waterproof inner pouches
- Silica gel or drying salts
- Spare gloves and socks stored separately
- A sleeping system protected from condensation

Condensation forms everywhere at dawn. Anything left exposed will be wet or frozen by morning.

---

## 3. Shelter and Sleeping

Do not sleep unsheltered unless absolutely necessary.

The nights are severe. More importantly, the landscape is less stable at night. Cracks open, frost patterns form, walls adjust, fog thickens, and distances become less trustworthy.

Carry:

- Four-season tent or bivouac shelter
- Ground insulation rated for frozen stone
- Cold-weather sleeping bag
- Emergency heat source
- Reflective blanket
- Small tarp
- Stakes suitable for stone cracks, or weighted anchors
- Rope for tying yourself to stable structures in fog or high wind

If sleeping in ruins, choose carefully.

Avoid:

- Roofless halls filled with heavy moss pillars
- Buildings with no visible doorway
- Rooms reflected differently in standing water
- Streets that appear only during rain
- Any place locals refuse to name
- Structures whose shadows do not match their shapes

Best options:

- Inhabited guest houses
- Maintained cistern shelters
- Low stone rooms with recent smoke marks
- Local waystations
- Dry inner chambers above ground seepage

Always ask locals whether a building is “awake,” “old,” “forgetting,” or “still agreed upon.” Their terminology may vary, but pay attention.

---

## 4. Food and Water

### Water is present but unreliable

Water appears as dew, frost, fog, intermittent streams, cistern storage, stone basins, and condensation nets. It is not always safe.

Bring:

- Water filter
- Chemical purification tablets
- UV purifier if technology works reliably
- Collapsible containers
- Metal boiling vessel
- Cloth for dew collection
- Sponge or absorbent pad
- Electrolytes

Treat all natural water unless a local tells you otherwise.

Avoid drinking from:

- Pools reflecting unfamiliar skies
- Pools reflecting stars at noon
- Basins that show rooms other than the one you occupy
- Cisterns with no sound when stones are dropped
- Water flowing uphill
- Water with orange slime unless locals specifically harvest it
- Any basin always full in an otherwise dry bathhouse

Some water may be physically pure but temporally or spatially contaminated. “Clean” does not always mean “safe.”

### Food

There are no animals. No fish, no birds, no insects, no mammals. Do not plan to hunt, trap, fish, gather eggs, or rely on animal protein.

Bring food.

Good travel foods:

- High-calorie ration bars
- Freeze-dried meals
- Nuts and seeds
- Dried fruit
- Hard cheese if tolerated and protected
- Preserved legumes
- Protein powder
- Salt
- Fat-rich foods
- Tea, broth, or soup packets

Local food may include:

- Root crops
- Moss-derived cakes
- lichens processed to remove toxins
- fungal or plant pastes
- cold-house greens
- terrace grains
- mineral salts
- dew-grown herbs
- fermented plant matter

Do not eat raw local lichens, mosses, slimes, or strange fruits. Many may be chemically defensive, slow-acting, or bound to local reproductive cycles. If offered food by inhabitants, accept small portions first unless you have dietary screening technology.

Also remember: in a world without animals, human travelers may be biologically startling. Do not shed food scraps carelessly. Do not introduce insects, microbes, seeds, or parasites from other worlds.

## 5. Navigation

Navigation is one of the central hazards.

Distance changes with weather. Roads alter destination. Causeways bend when unobserved. Valleys repeat. Stars cannot be trusted. Written measurements may alter.

Use layered navigation, not one system.

Carry:

- Compass, but do not fully trust it
- Paper maps, but expect them to become outdated
- Charcoal and waterproof ink
- Knotted cord records
- Physical trail markers
- Colored flags
- Chalk
- Cairn stones
- Altimeter
- Local moisture charts
- Sun compass, cautiously
- Rope for party travel in fog
- Signal mirror
- Whistle, though sound may not carry far

Most importantly: hire a local guide.

A good guide will navigate by:

- Moss behavior
- Wind taste
- condensation patterns
- frost lines
- shadows at noon
- stone seams
- remembered maintenance routes
- which cisterns fill when they should not
- which roads are currently “honest”

Do not mock this. It is practical knowledge.

### Rules for walking

- Never chase a visible tower across clear open ground without confirming distance locally.
- In fog, stop frequently and check behind you.
- Mark your path physically, but assume some markers may move.
- Avoid walking alone across bare flats.
- If a valley repeats, stop. Do not continue through the repetition without guidance.
- If you pass the same red moss patch three times and each time it is colder, turn back if possible.
- If your shadow detaches, wait in place until it returns or until locals advise you.
- If the road seems shorter when you are tired, distrust it.
- If a causeway brings you to a different gate than expected, do not immediately try to correct the error. Enter only if the gate is inhabited or marked safe.

## 6. Technology and Tools

Complex technology may fail, jam, rust, mismeasure, or behave inconsistently. Fine mechanisms are especially vulnerable.

Bring robust, repairable tools:

- Fixed-blade knife
- Simple multitool
- Hammer
- Wedges
- Cordage
- Needles
- Awl
- Flint or ferro rod
- Waterproof matches
- Hand lens
- Metal cup or pot
- Simple stove, but also local-fuel backup
- Rope
- Carabiners, if metal remains reliable
- Wax tablets or durable notebook
- Charcoal sticks
- Sewing kit
- Small shovel or trowel
- Walking staff

Avoid relying entirely on:

- GPS
- delicate electronics
- battery-only heat
- complex firearms
- drones
- precision watches
- laser rangefinders
- automatic navigation
- mechanical clocks
- fine optics with moving components

Electronics may work in some districts and fail in others. If you bring them, waterproof and shield them, but consider them luxuries rather than survival equipment.

Simple hand tools are respected here. Bring things that can be understood and repaired by sight.

---

## 7. Cultural Preparation

The inhabitants are cautious, maintenance-oriented, and adapted to a world where survival depends on respecting local rules. They may seem conservative, ritualistic, or indirect. In truth, much of what they do is engineering under unstable conditions.

### Behave humbly

Good manners include:

- Ask before entering streets, cisterns, gardens, or abandoned quarters.
- Offer help with maintenance if staying more than a day.
- Do not waste water.
- Do not disturb moss lips at doorways.
- Do not scrape inscriptions unless invited.
- Do not relight or extinguish fires without permission.
- Do not straighten road markers.
- Do not laugh at knots, songs, cairns, or gestures used as practical tools.
- Do not ask “why don’t you build better machines?” They have reasons.
- Do not promise to return by a specific road unless you know the road agrees.

Useful gifts:

- Salt
- Needles
- durable cloth
- waterproof containers
- simple steel tools
- medicinal supplies
- cordage
- glass beads
- accurate lenses
- preserved food
- copper wire
- warm gloves
- waterproof wax
- stories, maps, and names from elsewhere

Avoid giving advanced devices unless you are prepared to explain, repair, and possibly retrieve them. A broken miracle is often less useful than a good knife.

### Language and silence

Some streets reject spoken language. Learn local gesture etiquette and carry knotted cords or writing tablets. If your guide tells you to stop speaking, stop immediately.

Children are taught not to chase echoes. You should learn the same.

If you hear footsteps, voices, or echoes that seem displaced in time or direction, do not answer unless locals do. You may be responding to another year, another street, or someone else’s path.

## 8. Reality Hazards

The plateau’s physical laws are local and worn thin. Most anomalies are quiet, not dramatic.

### Common anomalies

- Distances stretching or compressing
- Repeating valleys
- roads changing endpoint
- pools reflecting other times or places
- uneven gravity seams
- water flowing uphill briefly
- frost forming prophetic or nonlocal patterns
- shadows detaching
- walls lengthening
- trees appearing fully grown overnight
- fires burning cold
- streets rejecting speech
- bridges requiring certain conditions to cross

### General safety rules

1. **Observe before touching.**  
   Watch water, shadows, frost, and plant movement.

2. **Use local precedent.**  
   If people carry damp cloth over a bridge, do the same.

3. **Do not test anomalies for amusement.**  
   Curiosity is dangerous here because the world may accept your experiment as a negotiation.

4. **Keep a personal continuity record.**  
   Each morning write:
   - your name
   - companions’ names
   - date by your reckoning
   - where you slept
   - where you intend to go
   - visible landmarks
   - injuries
   - promises made

5. **Carry an identity token.**  
   Something emotionally significant and physically durable. Touch it when disoriented. This may not be magical, but it helps cognition under stress.

6. **Do not stare too long into strange pools.**  
   Looking may create relation. Relation may create invitation.

7. **If something impossible happens plainly in daylight, do not panic.**  
   The plateau does not announce its strangeness. Treat the impossible as weather: note it, adapt, continue carefully.

## 9. Travel Timing

The best time to visit is not necessarily the warmest season, but the season when routes are most agreed upon.

Ask locals about:

- fog season
- frost season
- road-laying season
- cistern-filling cycles
- terrace repair days
- bridges currently crossable
- districts where language is unsafe
- roads that have recently changed
- abandoned quarters temporarily accessible
- whether the towers seen on the horizon are “near,” “far,” or “lying”

Avoid long wilderness crossings during heavy fog unless guided.

Avoid traveling at night.

Dawn is wet and cold but navigationally useful, because condensation reveals flows, seams, and active stone. Midday is safer for visibility. Late afternoon is dangerous for shadows. Night is for shelter.

---

## 10. Psychological Preparation

The quiet will affect you.

There are no animals. No birdsong, no insects, no distant barking, no small rustles in grass. Many humans rely subconsciously on animal noise to judge safety. Here that layer of the world is absent. The result can feel peaceful at first, then oppressive.

Expect:

- Heightened awareness of small sounds
- Unease in open spaces
- Loneliness even near settlements
- Time distortion
- Distrust of memory
- Dreams involving water, stone, or repeated roads
- Emotional attachment to hearths and inhabited rooms
- Anxiety when crossing silent flats

Bring mental anchors:

- Journal
- music, if devices work
- memorized songs
- prayer or meditation practice
- small familiar object
- regular check-ins with companions
- clear daily routines

Travel with others if possible. A lone traveler may become uncertain of their own continuity.

---

## 11. Environmental Ethics and Contamination

This world’s ecology is slow, old, and vulnerable.

Because there are no animals, introducing even small organisms could be catastrophic. Before arrival:

- Decontaminate boots, clothing, packs, and tools.
- Remove seeds from seams and Velcro.
- Sterilize camping gear.
- Do not bring live plants or animals.
- Do not bring untreated soil.
- Keep food waste sealed.
- Pack out biological waste if required, or use local disposal methods exactly.
- Avoid stepping on lichen rings; they may be centuries old.
- Do not disturb moss colonies unless guided.
- Do not pour foreign chemicals onto stone or into cisterns.

The plateau looks sparse, but much of it is alive slowly.

---

## 12. What to Do Upon Arrival

A sensible arrival plan:

1. **Arrive near an inhabited settlement, not in open plateau.**
2. **Stay still for several hours.** Let altitude symptoms reveal themselves.
3. **Check gear for frost, condensation, rust, or shape changes.**
4. **Meet local authorities, guides, or maintenance households.**
5. **Offer a practical gift.**
6. **Ask for current local rules.**
7. **Learn the nearest safe cistern, shelter, and road.**
8. **Mark your lodging physically and socially.**
9. **Do not enter abandoned quarters on the first day.**
10. **Sleep indoors.**

Questions to ask locals:

- Which roads are dependable this week?
- Which cisterns are safe to drink from?
- Which streets require silence?
- What bridges have conditions?
- What should I carry when crossing open flats?
- Are there pools I must not look into?
- How do I know if fog is ordinary?
- What is the local word for “turn back now”?
- What maintenance can a guest help with?

## 13. Emergency Protocols

### If lost in fog

- Stop moving.
- Tie your party together if not already tied.
- Mark your location.
- Sit or crouch to reduce heat loss.
- Listen for water, bells, human tools, or maintenance songs.
- Do not follow echoes.
- Do not follow lights unless your guide recognizes them.
- Wait for fog thinning if supplied.
- If forced to move, follow drainage downhill only if water behaves normally.

### If distance behaves impossibly

- Stop.
- Record what changed.
- Eat and drink; exhaustion worsens judgment.
- Turn around only if the return path still shows your markers.
- If markers are absent or altered, shelter and wait.
- Do not repeatedly pace the anomaly.

### If a companion is missing

- Do not scatter.
- Mark the last agreed location.
- Call only if sound behaves normally.
- Look for shadow mismatch, footprints ending at water, repeated terrain, or changed road stones.
- Ask locals before entering abandoned areas.
- If the person returns with inconsistent memories, treat them kindly but verify continuity: name, date, shared events, bodily injuries, carried objects.

### If you see another sky in water

- Do not touch the water.
- Do not lean close.
- Cover the surface if necessary with cloth or stone, unless locals forbid it.
- Move away calmly.
- Record location for guides.

### If your shadow detaches

- Stop moving.
- Keep your body warm.
- Avoid crossing thresholds.
- Wait for direct light to change.
- Do not step on the detached shadow.
- Do not let others step on it.
- Ask local help.

---

## 14. Best Reasons to Visit

Despite the hazards, this place is not hostile in the ordinary sense. It is solemn, beautiful, and profoundly old.

You might go to see:

- Cities like reefs on a stone sea
- Dew gathered from roofs into communal basins
- fog nets humming in cold air
- gardens grown in the lee of ancient walls
- terraces built from soil collected grain by grain
- pools holding impossible skies
- moss flowering once in centuries
- causeways older than current distance
- frost maps of cities not yet founded
- a civilization organized around care, repair, and caution

The plateau rewards patience. It is a place for slow walking, listening, and humility.

---

## Final Advice

Do not arrive as a conqueror, scientist-prince, treasure hunter, or tourist seeking spectacle. Arrive as a guest in an old house whose floorboards sometimes remember being trees.

Bring warmth, food, simple tools, humility, and a willingness to be corrected. Trust local craft over imported certainty. Treat water, roads, silence, and moss as serious things. Never assume that because an impossibility is quiet, it is harmless.

Above all: keep company, keep records, keep dry, and do not hurry. The plateau is older than urgency.
