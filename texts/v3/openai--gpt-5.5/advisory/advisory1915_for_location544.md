To visit this place, prepare as if you are entering a cold, high-altitude ocean, an abandoned industrial plant, and a living acoustic instrument all at once. It is not a place for ordinary diving. An ordinary human should go only inside a hard, insulated, pressure-rated vehicle or armored diving system with serious support.

The most important rule: **do not trust the apparent stillness.** The water looks clear and calm, but the hazards are constant, large-scale, and often invisible.

## 1. Do not go in exposed

Even if the plateau is “not abyssal,” the water column is still immense. Kilometers of cold water above you mean crushing pressure, lethal cold, and no practical way to surface unaided. A wetsuit, scuba gear, or even a normal atmospheric diving suit would be grossly insufficient except perhaps near the very top under controlled conditions.

Use one of the following:

- A small deep-rated submersible with manipulator arms.
- A pressure-balanced exploration capsule.
- A powered hard suit rated for extreme cold, pressure, and impact.
- Ideally, a vehicle with an independent return system rather than reliance on cables.

Your craft should have:

- Redundant life support.
- Active heating.
- Vibration isolation.
- Reinforced windows or external camera-only operation.
- Sonar mapping.
- Inertial navigation.
- Emergency ballast release.
- Acoustic beaconing on multiple frequencies.
- Shielding against electromagnetic fields and industrial current leakage.
- External lights, but not so bright or rhythmic that they attract or disturb fauna.

Do not enter the water in anything soft-bodied unless you have already accepted that rescue will be difficult to impossible.

## 2. Prepare for cold that never relents

The cold here is not dramatic; it is worse than dramatic. It is total, stable, patient cold. It will not spike and warn you. It will simply drain heat from everything.

Bring:

- Triple-redundant thermal regulation.
- Heated suit layers inside the craft or suit.
- Battery systems rated for near-freezing or supercooled saline water.
- Anti-ice measures for joints, hatches, thrusters, manipulators, sensors, and vents.
- Emergency chemical heat packs that function under pressure.
- Tools for clearing ice collars from mechanisms.

Assume any exposed hinge, latch, intake, or cable guide may slowly acquire ice. Assume any resting place may become a freezing point if you linger.

## 3. The sound is a primary hazard

The noise is not background. It is part of the environment in the same way pressure is.

It will travel through the hull, your bones, your teeth, your organs. Ordinary ear protection will not be enough because much of the sound is low-frequency vibration conducted through structure and water.

Prepare with:

- Whole-body vibration damping in the vehicle seat or suit frame.
- Bone-conduction dampers or medical-grade acoustic protection.
- Internal white-noise or counter-vibration systems, carefully tuned.
- Communication systems that do not rely on ordinary audio clarity.
- Prearranged visual and tactile signals.
- Exposure limits. Do not remain indefinitely just because you “feel fine.”

Possible effects include:

- Nausea.
- Vertigo.
- Headache.
- Panic.
- Chest pressure.
- Disorientation.
- Auditory hallucination.
- Loss of time sense.
- Difficulty distinguishing new sounds from the permanent industrial chord.

The greatest psychological danger is that the sound never changes. Humans expect noise to mean an event. Here it means existence. After enough hours, your nervous system may start inventing meaning in it.

Have a companion or remote supervisor monitor your speech, reaction time, and navigation decisions.

## 4. Navigation will be harder than it looks

The water is clear, but the distances are enormous. A cliff thirty kilometers away may look present, almost reachable, and remain unreachable for hours. Without vegetation, sediment clouds, or familiar horizon cues, scale becomes unreliable.

Do not navigate by sight alone.

Use:

- Inertial navigation.
- Sonar terrain mapping.
- Preloaded maps if available.
- Acoustic transponders placed along your route.
- Distance alarms.
- Return-vector systems that do not depend on compass reliability.
- Careful fuel and battery margins.

Expect landmarks to be sparse: a pylon, a cable, a basalt ridge, a factory lattice, a field of mineral snow. In between may be vast blue emptiness.

Treat all distances as deceptive. If something seems “near,” ask the instruments.

## 5. Currents behave like invisible walls

The currents are not ordinary wandering flows. They are corridors: stable, sharp-edged, and powerful. You may pass from still water into a fast river with almost no warning. The boundary may be precise enough to feel like striking a soft wall.

Your craft needs:

- Lateral thruster authority.
- Current shear alarms.
- Automatic stabilization.
- Emergency retreat programming.
- No loose external cables unless absolutely necessary.

Avoid crossing unknown current boundaries at an angle. Probe them first with drones or floating markers. A current “river” could carry you kilometers off course, into an intake, across a power cable, or over a chasm before you regain control.

## 6. Stay away from intakes, vents, cables, and moving structures

The machines still work. That is the central practical danger.

Do not think of the structures as ruins. Think of them as active industrial facilities whose safety systems are not designed for you.

Hazards include:

- Pump intakes strong enough to trap or crush a craft.
- Discharge vents producing invisible jets of colder, warmer, denser, or chemically active water.
- High-voltage leakage around transmission spars and cables.
- Rotating machinery.
- Periodic crane motion.
- Conveyor systems.
- Mechanical arms.
- Sudden valve pulses.
- Pressure doors.
- Oil-filled housings.
- Compressed gas cylinders.
- Brine pools.
- Magnetic fields.
- Structural resonance that can damage your vehicle.

A lit structure is active. A dark structure may still be active. A moving structure is not “waking up”; it is continuing a cycle that may have run for centuries.

Before approaching any installation, observe it through at least one full cycle if possible. If cranes move every forty-seven minutes, watch for ninety-four. If a valve opens monthly, do not camp beside it.

Do not enter sealed huts, observation domes, or pressure chambers unless you have a specific reason and remote reconnaissance first. The absence of visible caretakers does not mean absence of systems.

## 7. Respect the animals, especially because they are listening

The fauna are not likely to behave like familiar sea life. Many organisms here orient by vibration, pressure, thermal gradients, and machine cycles. Your craft will be an intruding sound source.

Basic biological protocol:

- Keep engine noise low and steady.
- Avoid sudden pulses, hammering, sonar blasts, or rhythmic lights.
- Do not chase shoals.
- Do not touch animals with bare manipulators.
- Do not collect specimens near industrial outputs without understanding their dependence on those outputs.
- Avoid predator nodes where harmonics intersect.
- Do not enter animal alignments or gatherings casually.

If silent animals turn toward you, assume they are sensing your vibration. If a shoal suddenly flashes or loses formation, reduce disturbance and withdraw. If smaller creatures change alignment in unison, something very large may be passing beyond sight.

The vast animal that resembles infrastructure should be treated as a major hazard. Do not attempt to follow it. Do not broadcast calls toward it. Maintain distance and let it pass.

## 8. The terrain is sharp

The stone beneath the water is young, angular, and barely softened. Basalt horns, volcanic glass fields, feldspar shelves, broken ridges, and intact scree slopes can tear open equipment.

Your vehicle needs:

- Abrasion-resistant exterior surfaces.
- Protected thrusters.
- Skids or standoff legs if landing.
- Forward obstacle sonar.
- Manipulators for pushing off without exposing delicate parts.
- No dangling hoses or lines.

Do not land on scree fields unless you know they are stable. The stones may have rested unchanged for ages, but your arrival may be the first disturbance they have received.

Avoid black volcanic glass blade fields completely. They may not look dramatic until they cut a cable, sensor mast, or suit joint.

## 9. Chemical and density hazards

The water is clean, but not necessarily safe. Around machines and geological tears, expect strange chemistry.

Avoid or sample remotely:

- Dark mercury-like pools.
- Dense brine basins.
- Chemical frost on pipes.
- Warm vents.
- Cold leaks.
- Oil-filled machinery.
- Mineral snow layers.
- Bubble columns.
- Rust blooms around bolts and welds.

Some “pools” within the ocean may be denser liquids sitting in basins. Entering one could affect buoyancy, visibility, corrosion, thermal balance, or sensor readings. Do not descend into reflective dark liquid without a probe.

## 10. Communication and rescue planning

Sound travels far, but the industrial chord may drown or distort signals. Radio will be useless underwater except at very short range. Optical signals will vanish with distance. Acoustic communication may be possible but must compete with the permanent machinery tone.

Use several systems:

- Short-range optical laser communication between craft.
- Hardline tether only in controlled areas, with automatic cutaway.
- Acoustic modems on carefully selected frequencies.
- Emergency pingers on multiple bands.
- Inertial dead-reckoning logs.
- Deployable locator buoys if they can survive the current and pressure.
- A surface or orbital relay if the world permits it.

Have a rescue craft already present. This is not a place where help can be improvised after the emergency.

If operating from the surface, remember: the surface itself is not friendly. It is a shoreless ocean at extreme altitude under thin blue-black air. Surface support vessels must be enclosed, heated, oxygenated, and able to anchor or station-keep without land reference.

## 11. Psychological preparation

This place will feel less like visiting an ocean and more like entering a thought that has been repeating for centuries.

Prepare for:

- Agoraphobia in open water.
- Claustrophobia inside your craft.
- Sensory exhaustion from the noise.
- Time distortion.
- A sense that nothing is happening despite constant motion.
- Irrational fear of the distant structures.
- Irrational trust in the distant structures.
- Compulsion to follow cables, lights, or repeating machine cycles.
- The feeling that the machines are “performing for someone.”

Set strict mission times. Schedule rest. Do not let curiosity extend a dive beyond plan. The environment encourages loops: watching the same crane cycle, following the same current, waiting for a sound to change. It may never change.

Bring recordings of ordinary human sound: voices, rain, birds, wind, music with beginnings and endings. Not for sentimentality, but to remind your nervous system that other acoustic worlds exist.

## 12. What to wear and carry personally

Inside the vehicle or suit:

- Thermal underlayers.
- Medical sensors.
- Anti-nausea medication.
- Emergency sedative under supervision.
- Bone and ear protection.
- Hydration system.
- High-calorie food.
- Wrist slate or physical checklist.
- Personal locator.
- Manual light source.
- Cutting tool.
- Emergency oxygen independent of main system.
- Psychological grounding item: a photo, charm, or other familiar object.

For sampling or exploration:

- Remote micro-drones.
- Water chemistry probes.
- Temperature and density probes.
- Passive hydrophones.
- Noninvasive biological imaging.
- Manipulator-safe collection chambers.
- Sterile sample containers.
- Corrosion-resistant tools.
- Radiation and electromagnetic field meters.
- Structural vibration sensors.

## 13. Suggested conduct during a visit

A cautious first visit should be simple.

1. Arrive with a support vessel or mothership.
2. Deploy passive hydrophones before entering.
3. Map the local sound field.
4. Identify active machinery, currents, cables, and animal concentrations.
5. Descend slowly.
6. Do not approach structures on the first descent.
7. Spend time learning visual scale and acoustic conditions.
8. Test communication at intervals.
9. Approach one landmark only.
10. Leave before fatigue begins.

On later visits, choose one objective: inspect a ridge, observe a shoal, map a pylon, sample a mineral layer, photograph a factory exterior. Do not combine too many tasks. The environment punishes distraction.

## 14. Things not to do

Do not:

- Free-swim.
- Use explosives or loud active sonar casually.
- Cut cables.
- Enter lit structures.
- Follow conveyor tunnels.
- Touch chemical frost.
- Hover near intakes.
- Land on glassy blade fields.
- Approach animal congregations from behind or below.
- Assume darkness means inactivity.
- Assume repetition means safety.
- Sleep in the water column unless your craft is anchored and monitored.
- Let the sound become your only clock.

## 15. What visiting will feel like

Expect beauty, but not comfort.

You will descend through clear, gray-white water that seems too evenly lit. The surface will become a silver ceiling above you. The rock below will look freshly broken, black and pale and rust-red, as if the inside of a planet was exposed and then preserved.

At first you may think the place is empty. Then a shoal will cross the blue distance like a fracture moving through glass. A cable will appear, thick as a road, humming in the water. A tower will emerge from the cold brightness, not ruined, not alive, but functioning. Indicator lights will glow behind thick glass. A crane will move after an interval so long you had begun to believe it was still.

All the while the sound will be inside you.

The most difficult thing to understand is that the world is not waiting. It is not abandoned in the human sense. It is continuing. The machines, animals, currents, ice, and mineral dust all participate in a system that does not need witnesses and does not offer explanations.

Go gently. Stay insulated. Keep your distance from anything that moves on a schedule. And remember: in this ocean, the absence of change is not safety. It is the local form of power.
