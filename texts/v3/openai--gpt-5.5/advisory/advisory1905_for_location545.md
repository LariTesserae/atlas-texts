For an ordinary human traveler, this plateau should be treated less like a wilderness and more like a sacred high-altitude ruin inside a geological time anomaly. The main dangers are not attack, weather, or obvious hostility. The dangers are thin air, total dryness, disorientation, temporal drag, fragile equilibria, and the possibility that your normal human tempo will be interpreted by the place as violence.

Prepare to visit as if you are entering a museum, a cemetery, a desert, a mountaintop, and a sleeping animal all at once.

## 1. Physical preparation

### Acclimatize before arrival

The plateau lies above forest, weather, and ordinary river memory. Its air is thin enough that an unprepared traveler may experience:

- shortness of breath,
- headache,
- nausea,
- confusion,
- poor judgment,
- sleep disturbance,
- fainting,
- altitude edema in severe cases.

Do not arrive directly from sea level unless your transport can provide pressure support. Spend several days at progressively higher elevations if possible, or use a pressurized arrival capsule and remain inside until your body stabilizes.

Bring:

- supplemental oxygen,
- a pulse oximeter,
- altitude sickness medication if compatible with your biology,
- pressure mask or light rebreather,
- emergency descent beacon.

Do not assume that because the air is “cool” rather than freezing it is safe. The air is thin, dry, mineralized, and possibly chemically odd.

### Hydration is critical

There is no liquid water. None. No snowmelt, no dew, no hidden trickle beneath rocks. Plan as if every drop you need must come with you and leave with you.

Bring:

- more water than you think necessary,
- sealed electrolyte solution,
- a closed-cycle hydration suit if available,
- moisture-reclaiming mask,
- high-calorie dry rations that do not require cooking,
- emergency IV fluids if trained to use them.

You will lose water through breathing faster than expected. The air will pull moisture from your lungs, mouth, eyes, and skin. Use lip sealant, eye protection, nasal gel, and full-coverage clothing.

### Protect your skin, lungs, and eyes

The ground is powdered mountain: salts, gypsum-like plates, ash-like mineral flour, possible reactive crystals, fossil dust, and biological crusts. You do not want it in your lungs.

Wear:

- sealed boots with soft soles,
- gaiters,
- gloves,
- full-body windproof layers,
- respirator or fine particulate mask,
- wraparound goggles,
- hood or face covering.

Avoid exposed skin. Some crusts may not be “toxic” in a normal sense, but they may interact with warmth, moisture, oils, or shadow in ways that are not understood.

## 2. Mental preparation

The plateau will feel still, but it is not empty. It is crowded with life operating on a scale of time that will make your own body seem frantic and indecent.

Prepare for:

- extreme silence,
- visual ambiguity,
- persistent sense of being watched,
- time distortion,
- spatial confusion,
- delayed shadows,
- footprints appearing before you make them,
- hallucination-like but stable temporal views,
- emotional pressure in ceremonial places.

Do not rely solely on instinct. Your instincts evolved for moving things, flowing water, weather, animal noise, and ordinary cause-and-effect. Here, most of those cues are absent or misleading.

Bring a calm companion if possible. Solo travel is strongly discouraged. A second observer can help distinguish altitude confusion from actual anomaly.

## 3. Navigation

Distance is unreliable. Objects may remain “the same distance away” for hours. Cairns may exchange apparent size. Ridges may become animals, then ridges again. Footprints may appear ahead of you.

Use redundant navigation methods:

- inertial tracker,
- anchored return beacon,
- physical guide line for short excursions,
- star map calibrated before entry,
- compass only as a secondary tool,
- map made in real time rather than assumed from sight,
- temporal locator if available.

Mark your route with noninvasive, retrievable markers. Do not build cairns. Do not scratch stones. Do not rearrange existing markers. Existing stones, posts, bones, and terraces may be part of a ritual, ecological, or temporal system.

A good rule: if you place something, be able to remove it. If you cannot remove it without disturbing the ground, do not place it.

## 4. Time precautions

This is one of the most important preparations.

Time on the plateau appears layered, surfaced, and locally inconsistent. Some beings move over centuries. Some shadows outlast their casters. Cracks may show earlier moments. Tools may be mid-gesture across epochs. Sound may become matter.

Before entering, establish:

- an external timekeeper outside the plateau,
- scheduled check-ins,
- a maximum stay duration,
- automatic extraction if check-ins fail,
- biological monitoring,
- memory logging,
- a “subjective time” journal.

Carry at least two clocks: one mechanical and one atomic or multiversal standard. If they disagree, leave. If your written notes begin appearing before you write them, leave calmly. If your footprints appear ahead of you and diverge from your intended path, do not follow the diverging trail.

Avoid looking too long into cracks that open “backward.” They may be stable and fascinating, but they are not windows; they are wounds in sequence. Do not reach into them. Do not drop objects into them. Do not try to correct or alter anything seen there.

## 5. Sound discipline

Sound behaves unnaturally. A shout may fall from the mouth as a white pebble. Silence has structural weight here. Noise may be more intrusive than touch.

Speak softly, if at all. Use hand signals. Use text displays. Avoid:

- shouting,
- singing,
- loud machinery,
- impact tools,
- alarms,
- gunfire,
- drones with audible rotors,
- music,
- unnecessary speech near animal gatherings or shelters.

If you accidentally produce “solid sound” — pebbles, dust, flakes, or other matter from speech — do not pick it up with bare hands. Record it, mark it, and leave it unless retrieval is required for safety.

## 6. Fire and energy use

Do not light fires.

Combustion appears incompatible with the plateau’s equilibrium. Hearths exist only as memory. Flame may fail, behave violently, or constitute a profound taboo. Use chemical heaters, battery warmers, or insulated gear instead.

Avoid high-energy equipment unless essential. Lasers, plasma cutters, open sparks, combustion engines, and thermal drills are inappropriate.

Recommended power sources:

- sealed batteries,
- low-emission field cells,
- passive heat retention,
- quiet solar fabric if it does not disturb local light behavior.

Remember: even ordinary light casts multiple shadows here. Do not introduce intense artificial lighting without testing. It may create persistent shadows or disturb organisms that feed on darkness, distance, or thermal memory.

## 7. Ecological conduct

The fauna is overwhelming but slow. Do not mistake stillness for death.

The animals may take hours, years, or centuries to complete a movement. A creature with its mouth around another’s neck may be in the middle of predation. A flock hanging in the air may be migrating. Beetle-things beneath a rock may be alive in a metabolism too slow to notice.

Rules:

- Do not touch animals.
- Do not feed animals.
- Do not move animals.
- Do not “rescue” prey.
- Do not collect eggs, shells, feathers, antlers, scales, or bones.
- Do not turn over stones unless absolutely necessary.
- Do not step on crusts, tufts, nursery slopes, terraces, or patterned salt plates.
- Do not walk between an animal and the object it is facing if avoidable.

Assume every arrangement is intentional, ecological, ceremonial, or all three.

If an animal appears to watch you, slow down. Bowing the head briefly may be safer than staring. Sudden motion is probably the most alien thing you can do.

## 8. Cultural and archaeological respect

Human or almost-human inhabitants may still be present, though slowed beyond ordinary recognition. A seated figure scraping a hide may be alive. A mummy may be a person in a valid state of being. A shelter may be occupied even if no movement occurs during your lifetime.

Treat all shelters as inhabited unless clearly empty and safe to approach.

Do not enter without need. If you must enter:

- announce yourself softly at the threshold,
- wait,
- do not step over tools,
- do not touch bodies,
- do not take artifacts,
- do not sit in sleeping hollows,
- do not disturb hearths,
- do not rearrange bowls, blades, hides, ribs, charms, or cords.

The old marker posts, knots, feathers, and glass reed bundles are not decorations. They may be legal boundaries, prayers, warnings, calendars, or devices for holding the local world in alignment.

The terraces with seedlike stones should be avoided entirely. They may be farms of shadow, mineral dust, or silence. A single misplaced footprint could damage centuries of cultivation.

## 9. The central basin

The basin where all creatures face inward toward the three-stone structure and the black glass hand-tool is the most dangerous place psychologically and ritually.

Not because something will leap at you. Nothing here leaps.

Because the attention there is total.

If you visit it:

- go only after acclimatization,
- approach from the rim first,
- do not cross the empty circle,
- do not touch the black glass tool,
- do not stand beneath the capstone,
- do not speak inside the basin,
- do not use flash photography,
- do not place offerings unless instructed by a qualified local authority,
- do not remain longer than your guide or instruments recommend.

Every creature faces the tool for a reason. That reason may be an event, a death, an invention, a judgment, a first act of making, or a moment so complete that it trapped witnesses into continuance. A human visitor should not try to “complete” it, “wake” it, or “understand” it through contact.

Observe from the edge. Leave unchanged.

## 10. Equipment checklist

Recommended minimum kit:

- pressurized or oxygen-assisted breathing gear,
- particulate respirator,
- goggles,
- full-body dry cold-weather clothing,
- soft-soled boots,
- gloves,
- hydration reservoir with redundancy,
- electrolyte supply,
- high-calorie no-cook food,
- emergency shelter,
- silent communication tools,
- inertial navigation unit,
- external beacon,
- two independent clocks,
- body monitor,
- first-aid and altitude kit,
- dust-safe sample bags only if sampling is permitted,
- noninvasive recording equipment,
- low-light camera without flash,
- written field notebook,
- emergency extraction device.

Avoid bringing:

- firearms unless required for interworld safety, and even then do not discharge them,
- drones unless silent and non-shadow-disruptive,
- open flames,
- loud tools,
- pets or pack animals,
- loose cloth strips or offerings that could be mistaken for local markers,
- anything that sheds moisture, seeds, microbes, or heat uncontrollably.

Use strict decontamination before and after. You do not want to introduce fast-growing organisms, spores, rust, mold, or liquid-dependent microbes into a world that has made peace without water.

## 11. Behavioral principles

The best way to survive and remain respectful is to become slower.

Walk slowly.  
Breathe deliberately.  
Step only where you have chosen.  
Pause often.  
Let your eyes adjust to the density of stillness.  
Do not hurry toward distant objects.  
Do not assume a thing is inert because it is motionless.  
Do not assume a thing is dead because it is dry.  
Do not assume a thing is natural because it is made of stone.  
Do not assume a thing is artificial because it is arranged.

In ordinary environments, a traveler proves competence through action. Here, competence is restraint.

## 12. What it will feel like

Expect the plateau to make you feel embarrassingly temporary.

Your pulse will seem loud. Your breath will seem wet. Your footsteps will seem reckless. You may feel like a spark walking through a library of dry paper, or like a child running into a room where elders have been conducting a ceremony for ten thousand years.

The silence may not feel peaceful. It may feel occupied.

The animals’ attention will likely be the hardest part. They will not threaten you in any familiar way, but their stillness will measure you. You may come to feel that every quick movement, every cough, every adjustment of your pack is a shout.

The landscape will also resist narrative. It is not “dead,” not “frozen,” not “abandoned,” not “waiting.” It is continuing. That is the unsettling thing. It has no need to resolve itself for you.

## 13. When to leave immediately

Depart if:

- you become confused about how long you have been there,
- your clocks disagree significantly,
- your shadow remains behind and does not rejoin you,
- your footprints appear ahead and lead somewhere you did not intend to go,
- animals begin to reorient toward you,
- you hear repeated clicking from multiple directions,
- you feel compelled to touch the black glass tool,
- your companions seem slower or older than they should,
- your speech produces physical matter,
- you find an artifact that appears to belong to you but is ancient,
- you see yourself in a backward-opening crack placing stones or making marks,
- you begin to feel that leaving would be rude, impossible, or unnecessary.

That last feeling is especially dangerous. The plateau’s greatest hazard may be its equilibrium. It may persuade you that motion is vulgar, that urgency is a disease, that remaining still would be a kind of wisdom. Perhaps it would. But you are human. Your body is built for beating hearts, circulating blood, hunger, thirst, sleep, and change.

Leave while you are still able to want departure.

## Final advice

Go lightly. Go briefly. Go with reverence.

Take nothing unless given. Leave nothing unless required. Do not attempt to accelerate, awaken, interpret, harvest, rescue, or repair the plateau. It is not broken. It is simply older and slower than you.

If you are fortunate, you will return with dust on your boots, dryness in your throat, and the memory of innumerable silent animals participating in an afternoon so ancient that ordinary time seems afterward like weather passing over glass.
