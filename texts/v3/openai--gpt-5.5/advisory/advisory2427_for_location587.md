To visit this place safely, prepare less as if you are entering a city and more as if you are entering a living cistern that happens to have streets.

It is cramped, wet, loud, dim, and socially/architecturally delicate. An ordinary human traveler can survive there, but only by moving slowly, carrying the right equipment, and accepting that vision and speech will be unreliable.

## 1. Physical preparation

Expect the visit to be exhausting in strange ways. You may not walk far in absolute distance, but every movement will require care.

You should be ready for:

- **Constant crouching or stooping**
- **Turning sideways through shoulder-width passages**
- **Slippery stone underfoot**
- **Low ceilings and protruding mineral formations**
- **Cool, humid air that chills you over time**
- **Very loud water-noise**
- **Disorientation due to darkness, echoes, and maze-like passages**
- **A sense of claustrophobia**

If you are prone to panic in enclosed spaces, this is a difficult destination. Practice moving in confined environments before arrival if possible. Learn to breathe slowly and keep one hand on a wall without leaning your full weight on it.

Do not rush. This is not a place where speed helps. Haste will injure you.

## 2. Clothing

Wear clothing for a cold, wet cave, not for a normal underground town.

Recommended:

- **Water-resistant outer layer**, but breathable if possible
- **Warm insulating layer underneath**, preferably wool or synthetic, not cotton
- **Close-fitting clothing** that will not snag on hooks, grates, lintels, or mineral teeth
- **Knee and elbow protection**, because you may crawl or brace yourself often
- **Helmet or hard hat**, ideally with a chin strap
- **Thin waterproof gloves** with good grip
- **Waterproof boots with excellent traction**
- **Extra dry socks sealed in a waterproof pouch**

Avoid loose scarves, cloaks, dangling jewelry, long coats, and wide packs. Anything that swings or trails may catch on ledges, sluice handles, ladders, door curtains, or bone/ceramic fittings.

The stone will be slick. The city is full of gutters and runnels. You will step into water whether you intend to or not.

## 3. Light

Bring your own light, but use it respectfully.

The native lighting is low and carefully placed. Bright modern light may be socially rude, visually overwhelming to inhabitants, or damaging to delicate mineral/biological surfaces.

Carry:

- A **headlamp with adjustable brightness**
- A **small hand light**
- **Warm, low-intensity settings**
- Backup batteries or power cells sealed against moisture
- A shield or diffuser to soften the beam

Do not shine bright light directly into cisterns, sleeping alcoves, shrines, or faces. If there are inhabitants adapted to dimness, your light may be painful or threatening.

Also remember: in this city, darkness is part of the infrastructure. Many edges and paths are meant to be felt, not seen. Bright light may flatten shadows and make depth harder to judge.

## 4. Hearing protection and communication

The sound of the water is one of the greatest hazards.

The city is not merely noisy; it is continuously noisy. The main fall, channels, drips, basins, and pipes create a permanent acoustic pressure. After a while it may fill your thoughts and make it difficult to judge direction.

Bring:

- **Earplugs or filtered hearing protection**
- A **bone-conduction or vibration-based communicator**, if available
- A **whistle**, though it may be swallowed by the water noise
- A **small slate/wax tablet/waterproof notebook**
- Prearranged hand signals with companions

Ordinary conversation will be difficult except at very close range or in specially resonant niches. Do not assume someone heard you. Touch the person’s shoulder or signal visually before speaking.

If locals use tactile marks, knots, stones, or struck tones, observe before imitating. Their sound-codes may be civic tools, warnings, or sacred signals.

## 5. Navigation

This city is compact but easy to become lost in. Its streets do not lead to landmarks in the usual way. They lead to functions: settling basins, dry vaults, work ledges, cisterns, sluices, wells, overflow walls.

Prepare for navigation by touch, sound, and repeated reference points.

Bring:

- **A waterproof physical map**, if one exists
- **Chalk or removable clay markers**, but only use them where permitted
- **A guide line/spool** for dangerous or unmapped sections
- **Compass**, though underground geology may make it unreliable
- **Inertial tracker or breadcrumb beacon**, if available
- **A local guide**, strongly preferred

Do not rely solely on memory. Many alleys may look similar: wet stone, low vault, gutter, lamp cup, niche, steps, another gutter. After an hour, the thunder of water may make your sense of direction unreliable.

Pay attention to:

- The sound of the central plunge-well
- Changes in floor texture
- Tactile marks near doorways
- Shell inlays on stair edges
- Air movement near shafts or clefts
- The slope of gutters

If the sound changes suddenly, stop. In this place, altered water-noise may indicate a blockage, gate movement, flood risk, or local emergency.

## 6. Breathing and air quality

The air is cool, saturated, and mineral-heavy. It may feel thick in the throat. There may also be old smoke from lamps, seep gases from deeper cracks, low oxygen pockets, or carbon dioxide accumulation in lower areas.

Carry:

- **Compact air-quality monitor** if possible: oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide
- **Respirator or filter mask** for smoke/mineral particulates
- Emergency rebreather if you plan to explore beyond inhabited or maintained routes

Warning signs:

- Headache
- Dizziness
- Unusual sleepiness
- Nausea
- Breathlessness
- Confusion
- Flame behavior changing strangely
- Animals absent from areas where they are normally present

Because the place is noisy and dim, a companion may not notice you becoming impaired. Travel in pairs or groups and check each other regularly.

## 7. Water safety

Water is everywhere, but do not assume it is safe to drink.

It may contain dissolved minerals, lime, salts, metals, microorganisms, animal waste from unseen channels, lamp residue, or ritual/civic contaminants. Even clear water may be chemically harsh.

Bring:

- Your own drinking water
- A purifier rated for microbes
- Chemical testing strips if possible
- Electrolytes
- Sealed water containers

Do not touch or disturb cistern water casually. In such a city, water is infrastructure, food, timekeeping, sanitation, ritual, and survival. A seemingly minor contamination could be a serious offense.

Never step into a channel unless invited or absolutely necessary. A shallow gutter may feed a drinking cistern, workshop basin, shrine bowl, or clock mechanism.

## 8. Food

There is little ordinary life here. Do not expect fresh produce, grain markets, fruit, livestock, or wood-fired cooking.

If inhabitants exist, their diet may include preserved foods, mineral-grown films, blind fish, shell creatures, rendered fats, clay-sealed stores, or imported goods from elsewhere. Your human digestive system may not tolerate local food.

Bring:

- Compact, dry, high-calorie rations
- Food sealed against damp
- Salt and electrolytes
- A small waste bag for wrappers and scraps

Do not drop crumbs into gutters or basins. In an ecosystem this controlled, even organic contamination may matter.

## 9. Health hazards

Possible hazards include:

- Slips and falls
- Hypothermia from slow chilling
- Head injuries from low ceilings
- Hand injuries from sharp mineral edges
- Skin irritation from mineral water
- Ear fatigue or temporary hearing loss
- Claustrophobic panic
- Mold-like or mineral films of unknown effect
- Infection from cuts exposed to wet stone or stagnant troughs

Carry a medical kit with:

- Waterproof bandages
- Antiseptic
- Blister treatment
- Pain relief
- Anti-inflammatory medication
- Thermal blanket
- Hand sanitizer
- Skin barrier cream
- Spare gloves
- Any personal medication, sealed

If you cut yourself, clean and cover the wound immediately. The city may look clean because it is mineral and water-polished, but wet environments are unforgiving.

## 10. Movement etiquette

Move as if every object has a purpose, because it probably does.

Do not lean on:

- Sluice gates
- Counterweights
- Door pegs
- Drip-catchers
- Notched stalactites
- Clay hoods over lamps
- Grilles
- Hanging plates
- Water-clock bowls
- Message posts

Do not sit on ledges unless you are certain they are not work surfaces, drainage lips, memorial niches, sleeping shelves, or ceremonial thresholds.

Keep your pack small and close to your body. Large packs will scrape walls, knock jars loose, disturb curtains, and block narrow passages.

Use three points of contact on stairs and wet slopes. Test each foothold. Shell-inlaid step edges may help you, but they may also be worn smooth by generations of feet.

## 11. Cultural conduct

Even if the city seems empty, treat it as inhabited.

Its maintenance is too precise to be accidental. The lamps, channels, soot marks, niches, tools, and tactile signs all imply a living or recently living civic order. Do not behave like an explorer in a ruin. Behave like a guest in someone’s home, workshop, waterworks, cemetery, and temple all at once.

Important courtesies:

- Ask before touching water systems.
- Ask before lighting or extinguishing lamps.
- Ask before photographing, scanning, or mapping.
- Do not enter sleeping alcoves.
- Do not disturb burial niches.
- Do not scrape mineral skins or deposits.
- Do not remove shells, beads, bones, tools, or clay seals.
- Keep your voice low when close to others, though the environment is loud.
- Use local paths rather than climbing over walls or drainage works.
- Accept a guide if offered.

The dead are built into the city. High wall niches marked with beads or shells may be graves. Treat them with respect. Do not shine lights into them or use them as handholds.

The central well may be practical rather than sacred, but practical things can be more sacred than idols in a place like this. The survival of the settlement depends on balance. Interfering with the water is the gravest mistake you can make.

## 12. Tools and equipment checklist

A sensible kit would include:

- Helmet with low-output headlamp
- Backup hand light
- Waterproof boots with strong grip
- Warm waterproof clothing
- Gloves
- Ear protection
- Waterproof notebook or slate
- Compact first aid kit
- Drinking water
- Sealed food
- Air-quality monitor
- Small respirator/filter mask
- Navigation line or beacon
- Compact pack
- Dry bags for all electronics
- Spare socks
- Emergency thermal blanket
- Local trade goods, if appropriate

Good trade items may include:

- Salt
- Needles or fine non-rusting pins
- Wax
- waterproof sealants
- Ceramic pigments
- Small mirrors, if culturally acceptable
- Durable cord
- Clean lamp fuel, if compatible
- Medical antiseptic

Avoid bringing wood unless you know its significance. In a place with no timber, wood may be precious, suspicious, ritually charged, or ecologically unwelcome.

## 13. Psychological preparation

The city will likely feel intimate and oppressive at the same time.

There is no horizon, no sky, no growing thing, no breeze in the ordinary sense. The walls are always near. The sound never stops. The water is not scenery; it is law. After a while you may feel as though the city is less a place than a condition: coolness, wetness, dimness, pressure, repetition.

Prepare for:

- Time distortion
- Difficulty thinking over the noise
- A sense that nothing changes
- Unease at the absence of plants and open air
- Heightened awareness of your breathing
- The feeling of being inside a machine or organ

Schedule breaks in the driest available alcoves. Check in with yourself and your companions. If someone becomes quiet, clumsy, irritable, or fixated, they may be overwhelmed.

Do not underestimate how comforting the outside world may feel afterward. Sunlight, wind, and distance may seem almost violent when you leave.

## 14. What not to do

Do not:

- Run.
- Enter alone.
- Drink untreated local water.
- Disturb channels, cups, gates, or cisterns.
- Take artifacts.
- Touch mineral films with bare hands.
- Assume an empty passage is unused.
- Use bright lights indiscriminately.
- Shout unless in emergency.
- Block a narrow alley with your pack or body.
- Ignore changes in water sound.
- Climb structures not meant for climbing.
- Step into black water to “see how deep it is.”
- Treat the place as a ruin.

## 15. Best approach to visiting

The ideal visit should be slow, guided, and humble.

Enter with minimal gear, soft light, protected hearing, and a willingness to learn the city’s sensory grammar. Let your hands read the walls. Let your feet find the shell-marked stair edges. Notice the way each gutter has a note, each basin a function, each ledge a history of use. Speak little. Touch less. Watch where the water goes.

This is not a city of monuments. Its grandeur is maintenance. Its beauty is in exact tolerances: a drip falling into the right cup, a channel singing at the right pitch, a stair worn but not broken, a lamp smoking into an old blackened vault.

Prepare to be made small, slow, and careful. That is the proper way to survive there, and probably the proper way to show respect.
