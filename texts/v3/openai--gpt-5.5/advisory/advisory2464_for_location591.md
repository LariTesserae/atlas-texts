## General assessment

An ordinary human should treat this place as **a high-altitude confined-space electrical site inside a fragile, sealed, non-water ecology**. It is beautiful, but not hospitable. The main dangers are not predators or collapse, but altitude, desiccation, mineral dust, noise, static charge, sharp brittle growth, and the fact that the chamber has likely remained stable because nothing like you has entered it for a very long time.

Go only if you have a clear reason. Do not go alone. Do not treat it as a cave, a greenhouse, or a ruin. It is all three, and none of them.

---

## Physical preparation

You need to be ready for:

- **High altitude**: thin, cold air before you even reach the crack.
- **A narrow entry**: no wider than a shoulder; expect squeezing, scraping, panic response, and difficulty retreating.
- **No open floor**: every step risks crushing growth or slipping on dry mineral litter.
- **Confined-space stress**: loud hum, dim light, no wind, no sense of air renewal.
- **Extreme dryness**: eyes, nose, lips, throat, and skin will dry quickly.
- **Persistent vibration/noise**: the sound may continue in your body after leaving.

Acclimatize before the climb. Do not arrive exhausted and then attempt entry. The garden is not a place to “push through” fatigue.

Anyone with asthma, severe claustrophobia, seizure disorders, cardiac implants, pacemakers, cochlear implants, or unstable heart rhythm should not enter. The electromagnetic environment is too uncertain.

---

## Team and entry protocol

Minimum team:

1. **One entrant**
2. **One belayer/rescue attendant at the crack**
3. **One support person outside**, monitoring weather, time, and communications

Do not send multiple people inside unless absolutely necessary. The chamber is too small and fragile.

Use a **lifeline harness**, but do not drag rope through the vegetation. The line should be managed from the entrance and kept off the walls as much as possible.

Set a strict time limit before entry. For a first visit, **10–15 minutes inside** is plenty. The place will tempt you to stand still and listen. Do not lose track of time.

Have a prearranged abort signal, because speech may be difficult over the hum.

## Clothing

Wear clothing that protects you from abrasion, dust, cold, and electrical uncertainty.

Recommended:

- Smooth, close-fitting outer layer with no dangling straps
- Non-shedding fabric; avoid fleece, wool fluff, or anything that drops fibers
- Full gloves: thin inner gloves plus dry, nonconductive outer gloves
- Eye protection or sealed goggles
- Full-face respirator or at least a tight half-mask respirator with P100/particulate filters
- Helmet without exposed metal edges
- Knee and elbow protection if crawling is required
- Soft-soled boots with clean tread

Avoid:

- Loose scarves, straps, jewelry, chains, dangling tools
- Exposed metal objects
- Wet clothing
- Perfumes, oils, lotions, insect repellent, or anything volatile
- Magnetic fasteners or strong magnets
- Open flames

Do not apply heavy moisturizer immediately before entry. The plants and dust may bind to oils, and the dryness may strip skin unpleasantly anyway. Protect with gloves and barrier clothing instead.

---

## Breathing and atmosphere

The chamber may contain breathable air, but do not assume it is safe. It is sealed, high, mineral-rich, electrically active, and full of fine dust.

Bring:

- Oxygen meter
- CO₂ meter if available
- Full-face particulate respirator
- Emergency oxygen outside the entrance

Best practice would be a **closed-circuit breathing system** or rebreather if you have the training, because it reduces moisture release into the chamber. If you do not have such equipment, keep the visit short and do not linger.

Do not exhale deliberately onto surfaces. Human breath is warm and wet. In this place, that is contamination.

---

## Hearing protection

The sound is not merely loud; it is bodily. It may include low-frequency vibration that ordinary earplugs do not fully block.

Use:

- Foam earplugs plus over-ear defenders if the crack allows them
- Bone-conduction or vibration-aware communication if available
- A written signal system as backup

Expect the hum to remain in your head after leaving. Mild tinnitus, pressure in the teeth, or a sense that the world outside is too quiet may follow. Rest before descending the mountain.

If the tone changes while you are inside, leave immediately.

---

## Light

Use low-heat, low-output lamps.

Bring:

- Headlamp with diffuse red/amber mode
- Backup light
- No hot bulbs
- No sparking switches if avoidable
- No high-powered floodlight unless needed for safety

The garden’s own glow is dim and slow to understand. Let your eyes adjust. Bright light may flatten the space visually and make you step where you should not.

Do not use flash photography casually. The ecology may not care, but the old instruments, dust, and glass surfaces might respond unpredictably to heat pulses or static.

---

## Electrical precautions

Assume the chamber is electrically active, even if nothing appears powered.

Hazards include:

- Static discharge
- Hidden live conductors
- Induced currents in metal tools
- Charge stored in old capacitors, coils, ceramic insulators, or the black cylinder
- Unknown field effects around the plants

Rules:

- Do not touch the machinery.
- Do not bridge two objects with your body.
- Do not place one hand on metal and the other on stone, roots, or the cylinder.
- Keep tools nonconductive where possible: ceramic, wood, polymer.
- Do not bring long metal poles.
- Do not wear crampons inside unless absolutely necessary; change to clean soft footwear if possible.
- Do not approach the rear cylinder closely unless you have instrumentation and a reason.

If your hair rises, your skin prickles sharply, you smell ozone, your lamp flickers, or your electronics behave strangely: retreat.

If you hear snapping, ticking discharges, or a new overtone in the hum: retreat.

---

## Moisture discipline

This is crucial.

The garden is “lush” without water. Water may be disruptive, chemically reactive, or lethal to its equilibrium. Treat liquid water as a contaminant.

Before entry:

- Seal all water bottles in your pack.
- Do not bring hydration bladders that can leak.
- Check that no snow, ice, rain, or condensation is on your clothing.
- Avoid entering immediately after a storm.
- Brush off frost.

Inside:

- Do not pour water.
- Do not spit.
- Do not wipe sweat onto stone or leaves.
- Do not breathe directly onto growth if avoidable.
- Do not introduce damp samples, wet rope, wet boots, or snow.

If you are sweating heavily from the climb, wait outside until you cool and dry. Entering hot and damp is bad for you and bad for the chamber.

---

## Ecological caution

The chamber records touch. Every footprint remains. Every broken blade may remain broken for centuries.

Move as if inside a museum made of living glass.

Guidelines:

- Step only where absolutely necessary.
- Use existing stone or machine surfaces only if you are certain they are stable and not colonized.
- Do not brush against the plants.
- Do not collect leaves, seeds, dust, filings, labels, or machine fragments.
- Do not attempt to “free” machinery from roots.
- Do not clean anything.
- Do not mark the walls.
- Do not leave trail tape, chalk, flags, food crumbs, hair, skin flakes, or packaging.

The seed-pods are not invitations. Do not take them. A seed adapted to cracks, wiring, ore veins, and charge could become a serious contamination risk elsewhere.

## Weather timing

Do not visit during:

- Thunderstorms
- High winds at the summit
- Snowfall
- Freezing fog
- Electrical storms on nearby ridges
- Rapid pressure changes

The chamber may be part of an old atmospheric or electrical harvesting system. Storm conditions could energize it. The crack itself may also become dangerous if wind-driven ice or charge accumulates around the metal filings in its lip.

Choose a calm, dry, stable day after several days without precipitation.

## Psychological preparation

The chamber will feel wrong in a very specific way: alive without animals, green without water, loud without motion.

Expect:

- Disorientation from the constant hum
- A sense that the sound is “listening”
- Difficulty judging distance in dim, sourceless light
- Reluctance to touch anything
- Strange intimacy from the smallness of the space
- A strong urge to identify the purpose of the machinery
- A stronger urge to stay until the pattern of the sound resolves

It probably will not resolve.

Do not interpret the place’s stillness as safety. Do not interpret the machinery’s age as harmlessness. Do not interpret the absence of motion as dormancy.

Before entry, decide exactly what you are there to do: observe, document, verify, then leave.

---

## What not to do

Do not:

- Drink, eat, smoke, or vape inside
- Remove mask or gloves
- Touch the black cylinder
- Pull aside the violet blades near the rear wall
- Operate levers
- Turn knobs
- Try to read gauges by scraping them clean
- Cut roots away from wires
- Follow pipes into thickets
- Bring in water “for the plants”
- Test whether leaves are sharp with bare skin
- Take a seed-pod
- Stay to investigate a new sound
- Assume silence would be better than humming

If the hum stops, leave immediately. A permanent process changing state is not an opportunity; it is an alarm.

---

## Emergency concerns

Possible symptoms requiring immediate exit:

- Dizziness beyond normal altitude effects
- Metallic taste
- Numb fingers after touching surfaces
- Static shocks
- Confusion or time distortion
- Shortness of breath
- Sudden headache
- Pressure in the ears or teeth
- Nausea from vibration
- Eye burning from dust
- Skin cracking or papery irritation after plant contact

If someone collapses inside, rescue is difficult because the entrance is narrow and the floor is fragile. The outside team should have a hauling system ready before entry, not improvised afterward.

Do not send an unprotected rescuer into the chamber in panic. That is how two people become trapped.

---

## Decontamination after leaving

Once outside:

1. Move away from the crack before removing gear.
2. Brush off dust carefully into a containment sheet or bag.
3. Remove boot covers and outer gloves.
4. Seal contaminated items.
5. Check skin for dryness, cuts, splinters, or dark mineral residue.
6. Rinse only after you are well away from the entrance and runoff cannot return to the crack.
7. Hydrate slowly.
8. Rest and monitor hearing.

Do not carry chamber dust into other ecosystems, vehicles, or living quarters. It may contain conductive mineral particles, unknown spores or seed fragments, metal salts, or reactive alkali.

---

## How to behave inside

Enter slowly. Pause just past the crack and let your breathing settle. Do not force your eyes to understand everything at once. The room will appear as a dense mass first, then as layers: blades, wires, roots, ceramic, dust, glass, old handles, pale mineral edges.

Keep your elbows in. Keep your pack small or leave it outside on a tether. Turn your head carefully; a helmet can break what a hand would avoid.

The first instinct may be to step toward the machinery, especially the cylinder. Resist that. Study from the entrance as long as possible. The safest observation point may be barely inside.

When you move, place each foot once. Do not shuffle. The floor is a ledger; do not write more than necessary.

Speak little. The hum will eat your words anyway.

Do not expect the garden to respond. Its danger is not aggression. Its danger is indifference and precision. It has achieved a balance that does not include you.

---

## Final advice

Prepare as though visiting a sacred electrical desert sealed inside a mountain.

Bring less than you think you need. Touch less than you want to. Stay for less time than feels possible. Leave before curiosity becomes negotiation.

And when you come out, do not be surprised if the sky feels unreal for a while. The sound may remain in your skull: a dry engine-note, steady and green-gray, reminding you that some gardens do not grow toward the sun, and some machines do not stop merely because their makers are gone.
