## Preparation Guide for Visiting the Dry City at the Top of the World

This is not a place to “explore” in the ordinary adventurous sense. It is a place to enter briefly, carefully, and with the understanding that the environment does not merely lack water, warmth, life, and stable geometry — it rejects them.

An ordinary human traveler can visit, but should treat the city as a high-altitude, hyper-arid, geologically active, reality-unstable ruin where the main dangers are dehydration, disorientation, spatial/time drift, abrasive storms, altered gravity, and quiet contact with entities or processes that are not hostile so much as incompatible with human assumptions.

You should prepare as if going to Everest, Antarctica, a salt desert, a deep cave, and a subtly sentient architectural paradox at the same time.

---

# 1. Do Not Go Alone

The city’s greatest danger is not that it attacks visitors. It is that it makes ordinary decisions unreliable.

A wall may not remain where it was. A street may not return you to its beginning. Footsteps may arrive before you make them. A person alone can become lost not by taking a wrong turn, but by continuing straight.

Travel with:

- At least two companions.
- One remote support observer outside the basin, if possible.
- A fixed extraction team stationed beyond the city’s edge.
- A prearranged maximum visit duration.
- A rule that any one member can call retreat without debate.

Avoid large groups. The city seems to notice processions, alignments, and repeated patterns. A small group is safer than a column.

Best group size: **three to five people**.

---

# 2. Acclimatize Before Arrival

The city lies at extreme altitude. Even if the air is breathable in composition, it is thin, dry, cold, and chemically abrasive.

Before entering, spend time in a staged acclimatization camp at lower elevations. Do not portal directly into the basin unless your method provides atmospheric support.

Prepare for:

- Hypoxia.
- Rapid fatigue.
- Poor sleep.
- Cognitive slowing.
- Nosebleeds.
- Dry eyes and cracked skin.
- Reduced appetite.
- Increased risk of poor judgment.

Even minor confusion is dangerous here because the environment itself already interferes with orientation.

Bring supplemental oxygen or a sealed breathing system. Do not rely on local air except in emergency.

---

# 3. Hydration Must Be Internal and Protected

The city has a first law: **no water may persist.**

This is not ordinary desert dryness. Water exposed to the environment may become salt, light, distance, crust, or something else. Breath condenses as mineral film. Tears crystallize. Blood spilled on stone becomes script-like mineral residue.

Therefore:

- Do not carry ordinary open canteens.
- Do not expose water to the air.
- Do not attempt to collect, melt, distill, or search for local water.
- Do not trust “wells,” “cisterns,” or chambers that appear to contain liquid.
- Do not drink from any local source, even if one appears.

Use a **sealed hydration system** worn inside an environment suit. The reservoir should be insulated, pressure-stabilized, and preferably warded or technologically shielded against external environmental law. Drink through a sealed tube.

Recommended:

- Full-body suit with sealed joints.
- Internal hydration bladder.
- Electrolyte solution, not plain water.
- Backup hydration capsules or gel stored in hard-sealed containers.
- Moisture-preserving respirator or closed-loop breathing system.
- Eye protection with humidity retention.
- Skin barrier ointment before suiting.

If you begin to feel unusually thirsty despite drinking, retreat immediately. The city may be drawing moisture from you faster than expected.

Warning signs:

- Chalky taste in mouth.
- Cracking lips inside sealed gear.
- Crystalline residue on eyelashes, mask interior, cuffs, or gloves.
- Stiffening of sweat in fabric.
- Sudden salt blooms on skin.
- Dark urine or inability to urinate after fluid intake.
- Feeling “hollow,” papery, or mineralized.

Do not remove gloves to touch stone. Do not lick anything. Do not breathe unfiltered air “just for a moment.”

---

# 4. Use a Proper Environmental Suit

A normal cold-weather outfit is not enough.

You want something between a high-altitude pressure suit, desert survival suit, and light geological hazard suit.

The suit should provide:

- Full skin coverage.
- Positive-pressure breathing or filtered sealed respiration.
- Eye protection.
- Dust exclusion.
- Abrasion resistance.
- Thermal insulation.
- UV and unknown-radiation protection.
- Moisture retention.
- Integrated oxygen.
- Internal hydration.
- Emergency beacon.
- Suit telemetry for heart rate, hydration status, oxygen saturation, and body temperature.

The dust is extremely fine and may be chemically active. It threads rather than billows. It will enter seams, filters, lenses, and equipment housings unless everything is protected.

Avoid exposed mechanical joints. Salt dust will abrade them.

Use:

- Sealed boots with aggressive traction.
- Gaiters integrated into the suit.
- Full-face helmet or mask.
- Redundant filters if not using closed-loop oxygen.
- Anti-static and anti-abrasion outer layer.
- Hard knuckle gloves for climbing and crawling.
- Spare visor films.

Do not rely on flame, open heating elements, or combustion. Fire does not behave properly there.

Use battery, chemical, or metaphysically insulated heat sources.

---

# 5. Bring Cold Protection, But Not Fire

The city is cool by ordinance, not just climate. Heat is tolerated only briefly. Flames flatten and dim. Combustion may fail, smear, or become a red stain in the air.

Do not bring:

- Campfires.
- Open-flame stoves.
- Torches.
- Flare-based navigation.
- Gas burners as primary heat.

Bring:

- Insulated suit layers.
- Battery-powered heat pads.
- Chemical warmers in sealed pouches.
- Non-flame emergency heat packs.
- Redundant power cells protected from cold.
- Low-temperature-rated electronics.

Expect nights to be cold and psychologically colder than the thermometer suggests.

---

# 6. Navigation Requires Multiple Systems — None Fully Reliable

Names do not stay attached. Measurements decay as they are made. Streets acquire or lose arches during counting. Distance is unreliable. Rooms may open into impossible elevations or times.

You need layered navigation:

### Physical Anchors

Use a tether line when entering complex districts, interiors, wells, or alleys. The tether should be:

- High-visibility.
- Abrasion-resistant.
- Marked at intervals.
- Anchored outside the district if possible.
- Monitored by a companion.

However, do not assume a tether is infallible. It may pass through a doorway that no longer has the same relation to the street.

### Beacons

Place inertial or transdimensional beacons at:

- Basin entry point.
- City edge.
- Major plaza crossing.
- Retreat route.
- Camp or staging zone.

Use beacons that do not depend solely on line-of-sight, GPS, magnetic north, or local geometry.

### Personal Trackers

Each traveler should carry:

- Inertial navigation unit.
- Compass, though it may drift.
- Altimeter, though altitude may distort.
- Chronometer, though time may vary.
- Physical map board with erasable route marks.
- Voice recorder.
- Emergency location beacon.

### Human Rules

Most important are behavioral navigation rules:

- Do not split up.
- Do not enter a doorway unless everyone agrees.
- Do not pursue a sound, shadow, animal, map, or apparent shortcut.
- Do not count structures aloud for orientation.
- Do not name plazas or routes as your only method of navigation.
- Use functional descriptions: “the last confirmed beacon,” “the basin-facing arch,” “the tethered route,” not “the third plaza” or “the west street.”
- If the route behind you looks unfamiliar, stop immediately.

A good method is to travel by **confirmed return segments**: move a short distance, verify return route, mark, proceed. Never push deeper just because the next turn looks clear.

---

# 7. Timekeeping Must Be Externalized

Time is uneven. Some streets are newer at one end. Footsteps may precede the walker. Echoes may remain after events.

Each traveler should carry at least two timekeeping systems:

- A local watch.
- A sealed chronometer synchronized to an external reference.
- Optional remote pulse beacon from outside the basin.
- Biological monitoring to detect time-rate mismatch.

Before entering, establish:

- Maximum local time inside.
- Maximum external time before extraction.
- Check-in intervals.
- Abort conditions if intervals disagree.

For example:

> “We remain inside for no more than two external hours or ninety subjective minutes, whichever ends first. If any clock differs by more than six minutes, we retreat.”

Do not camp inside the city unless absolutely necessary. Overnight exposure increases risk from cold, spatial drift, stone activity, and psychological effects.

---

# 8. Avoid Interiors Unless Properly Equipped

There are no normal interiors. Doorways may lead to balconies, skies, corridors that return before entry, chambers from other times, or rooms whose geometry is not compatible with ordinary human navigation.

If you must enter:

- Use a tether.
- Leave a person outside.
- Enter one at a time only if necessary, but maintain visual contact.
- Do not close doors or pass through narrow thresholds that might seal.
- Avoid stairs descending into dry brightness.
- Avoid cellars containing sky.
- Avoid rooms lit by an afternoon that does not match the outside.
- Avoid corridors that appear longer when watched.
- Do not enter any room containing dust cones unless documenting from the threshold.

A doorway that shows a different part of the city is not necessarily an opportunity. It may be a digestive surface of space.

If you see yourself, your group, a future route, an aerial view, or a plaza from above, do not step through.

---

# 9. Stay Away from the Pits

The concentric plazas with central pits are high-risk sites.

The pits do not behave like holes. They reflect aerial views, former versions of the basin, processions of tall shadows, endless salt plains, or nothing. They return no sound. They have no rising air. Leaning over them may expose you to spatial, temporal, or identity displacement.

Rules:

- Do not drop objects into pits unless prepared to lose them absolutely.
- Do not lean over.
- Do not look for more than a few seconds.
- Do not allow your reflection to become the thing you are watching.
- Do not descend.
- Do not anchor ropes into them.
- Do not camp near them.
- Do not perform rituals, measurements, or repeated observations at their edges.

If a pit shows a version of the city containing moving figures, leave the plaza calmly.

If it shows your group from above, retreat immediately.

---

# 10. Treat Gravity Districts as Terrain Hazards

Gravity varies.

In heavy districts, walking may feel like climbing while standing still. In light districts, pebbles may pause midair, dust may collect vertically, and your bones may feel as if they are “listening.”

Preparation:

- Use trekking poles or a staff.
- Wear boots with excellent grip.
- Carry loads close to your body.
- Avoid jumping, running, or climbing unstable structures.
- Use handlines on stairs.
- Cross one person at a time in uncertain gravity zones.
- Do not throw objects to “test” gravity near fragile architecture.

Symptoms of gravity stress:

- Sudden joint pain.
- Nausea.
- Sense of pressure in bones.
- Dizziness.
- Difficulty judging steps.
- Feeling too light or too heavy.
- Dust drifting vertically.
- Loose stones falling at wrong speeds.

Retreat from gravity anomalies before fatigue accumulates. They are especially dangerous on stairs, bridges, terraces, and ravine edges.

---

# 11. Prepare for Abrasive Mineral Storms

Storms bring mica flakes, salt needles, and black shale plates. They do not roar; they whisper. Visibility falls to a few steps. The city’s geometry becomes more mentally vivid but physically less trustworthy.

Before going, learn the storm signs:

- Upper air darkening.
- Sky folding inward.
- Sudden temperature drop.
- Shadows sharpening.
- Distant buildings seeming closer.
- Unnatural clarity of the city’s layout in your mind.
- Fine dust beginning to thread across pavements.

If a storm begins:

1. Stop advancing.
2. Do not try to “beat it” across open plazas.
3. Shelter in the lee of a low, simple wall if possible.
4. Avoid colonnades, archways, and narrow alleys where particles accelerate.
5. Cover visors and filters.
6. Turn backs or shoulders to the wind if exposed.
7. Maintain physical contact with companions via rope or handline.
8. Do not trust apparent nearby landmarks.
9. Wait until visibility and geometry stabilize.
10. After the storm, reassess your location using beacons, not memory.

Storms change the city. Afterward, a staircase may have gained steps, a plaza may have rotated, or a tower may have lost its shadow. Treat the post-storm landscape as a new route, not the one you entered.

---

# 12. Do Not Approach the Tall Ones

The tall ones may be animals, pressures, architecture, time-weather, or mobile errors in stone. They move slowly, like sundials. Their passage produces sudden weathering. Walls near them age in moments. Small clicking creatures avoid their routes for days.

Avoid them absolutely.

Rules:

- Do not follow.
- Do not photograph at close range if doing so requires attention or approach.
- Do not stand in their path.
- Do not attempt communication.
- Do not shine lights at them.
- Do not touch weathered surfaces immediately after they pass.
- Do not cross their path until the surrounding dust and stone appear stable.

If one appears at the end of an avenue:

1. Stop.
2. Lower your profile without crouching into a vulnerable position.
3. Move laterally toward cover if available.
4. Avoid sudden movement.
5. Do not run along the same avenue.
6. Retreat by a route perpendicular to its movement.
7. Mark the area as unsafe.

If nearby walls begin visibly aging, flaking, or losing edges, leave immediately. Your suit, tools, and body may also be subject to accelerated weathering.

---

# 13. The Clicking Creatures Are Less Dangerous, But Still Not Harmless

The small translucent shell-creatures emerge in colder periods and scrape mineral films from shaded stone. They do not drink. They retreat when watched.

They are likely not aggressive, but do not handle them.

Precautions:

- Do not block crevice exits.
- Do not step near clusters.
- Do not collect living specimens without containment.
- Do not allow them into gear.
- Do not sleep near shaded mineral films.
- If they all retreat suddenly, watch for tall ones or environmental change.

Their behavior may serve as an early warning system.

---

# 14. Do Not Trust the Maps Made by Stone

At dusk the stones produce powdery white blooms arranged into delicate maps. These correspond to no known terrain.

Do not use them for navigation.

They may be:

- Mineral stress patterns.
- Salt exudation.
- City self-records.
- Maps of impossible places.
- Lures.
- Predictions.
- Anatomical diagrams of the basin.
- Something left over from processes older than habitation.

Document from a distance if desired. Do not trace them with fingers. Do not copy them obsessively. Do not stand at their centers.

If a map resembles your route, your home, your body, or a place you intend to go next, end the visit.

---

# 15. Avoid Naming Rituals and Repeated Measurement

Names do not remain attached. Counting may alter what is counted. Measurement is not violently resisted; it is simply outlived while it occurs.

For travelers, this means obsessive surveying can become dangerous. The city may not punish measurement, but the act of imposing a grid can entangle you in its own civic geometry.

Safer practices:

- Use temporary operational labels: “Beacon A,” “Shelter Wall,” “Return Arch.”
- Avoid grand names.
- Avoid renaming the same place repeatedly.
- Avoid counting arches aloud while walking through them.
- Avoid making promises tied to locations: “We will return to this exact plaza.”
- Record impressions, but do not depend on them.

If your notes begin changing, becoming illegible, reorganizing into mineral diagrams, or referring to places you have not visited, seal them and retreat.

---

# 16. Protect Your Mind from Silence and Scale

The city’s silence is layered, not empty. It can make your thoughts sound intrusive. The absence of inhabitants is a pressure. The city feels prepared for arrival and abandoned at the same time. This can produce:

- Derealization.
- Anxiety.
- Awe paralysis.
- Compulsion to enter deeper.
- Feeling watched by architecture.
- Hearing significance in stone ticks.
- Belief that the city is “about to speak.”
- Desire to remove helmet or gloves.
- Desire to leave a mark, name, fluid, or offering.

Prepare psychologically.

Before entering, agree on grounding phrases and behavioral checks.

Examples:

- “We are visitors. We are not expected.”
- “No discovery is worth missing extraction.”
- “The route home matters more than the next plaza.”
- “Do not answer silence.”
- “We do not give the city water, blood, names, or promises.”

Use buddy checks every fifteen to twenty minutes:

- State your name.
- State the entry point.
- State the retreat direction.
- State current physical condition.
- Confirm no one is hearing instructions or seeing companions displaced in time.

If someone becomes entranced by a doorway, pit, map, tower, or star pattern, remove them gently but firmly. Do not argue with what they think they see. Use the agreed retreat protocol.

---

# 17. Do Not Leave Biological Material Behind

The city rejects liquidity and converts blood or breath into mineral traces. It may also interpret biological material as inscription, offering, contamination, or seed.

Avoid leaving:

- Blood.
- Hair.
- Skin.
- Saliva.
- Urine.
- Food scraps.
- Cloth fibers.
- Open water.
- Organic offerings.
- Written names in bodily fluid or dust.

Use sealed waste systems. Treat any injury seriously and cover it immediately.

If blood touches stone, do not try to scrape it up with bare tools. Mark the location and leave. The resulting mineral script may not be inert.

---

# 18. Food and Rest

Eat before entering. Eating inside a sealed suit is difficult but possible if using internal rations.

Bring:

- High-calorie gels.
- Electrolyte paste.
- Chewable compact rations.
- Caffeine only in moderation.
- Anti-nausea medication.
- Glucose tablets.

Do not eat local crusts, filaments, mineral films, or clicking creatures.

Rest only in locations that meet all of the following:

- Outside enclosed interiors.
- Away from pits.
- Away from narrow alleys.
- Away from visible gravity anomalies.
- Away from fresh tall-one weathering.
- Shielded from wind.
- With clear retreat route.
- Near a beacon.
- On stable stone not blooming, ticking excessively, or rearranging dust.

Never sleep inside the city unless trapped. If forced, post watches in overlapping shifts and tether all sleepers to a shared anchor.

# 21. Entry Strategy

Approach from the basin edge, not by appearing in the city center.

Before entry:

1. Establish external camp beyond the city’s active zone.
2. Test communications.
3. Synchronize clocks.
4. Hydrate fully.
5. Check oxygen saturation.
6. Inspect suit seals.
7. Confirm retreat protocol.
8. Place first beacon at entry point.
9. Observe the city for at least one full light cycle if possible.
10. Watch for storms, tall-one routes, and gravity anomalies.

Initial visit should be short.

Recommended first incursion:

- No more than 30–60 minutes.
- Stay within sight or instrument range of the edge.
- Do not enter buildings.
- Do not cross major plazas.
- Do not interact with pits, maps, or creatures.
- Withdraw before dusk if unfamiliar.

Only after multiple successful edge visits should you go deeper.

# 22. Behavioral Rules Inside the City

A simple field code:

1. **Stay sealed.**
2. **Stay together.**
3. **Stay tethered when uncertain.**
4. **Do not drink, touch, bleed, burn, or name unnecessarily.**
5. **Do not enter pits.**
6. **Do not follow figures.**
7. **Do not trust shortcuts.**
8. **Do not continue after a storm without reassessment.**
9. **Do not argue with geometry.**
10. **Leave before you feel invited.**

That last rule matters. If the city begins to feel welcoming, legible, or personally meaningful, it may be time to go.

# 23. Signs You Should Retreat Immediately

Leave at once if:

- Any suit seal fails.
- Water reservoir shows crystallization.
- You smell cold ash, old mushrooms, or mineral rot inside the suit.
- You see a tall one within a few streets.
- The sky begins folding inward.
- The same building appears at multiple distances.
- Footsteps are heard before anyone moves.
- A companion’s shadow detaches or arrives late.
- A plaza rotates relative to known landmarks.
- A route gains or loses major features while observed.
- Your clocks disagree beyond agreed tolerance.
- A doorway shows sky, another time, or an overhead view.
- Stone maps resemble your route or body.
- Your notes change.
- You feel compelled to remove protective gear.
- You begin to think the silence is about to speak.
- A pit reflects you from above.
- Clicking creatures vanish all at once.
- Walls near you begin rapidly weathering.
- Stars overhead form a matching map of the city below.
- Anyone says, “Just a little farther,” after an anomaly.

Retreat calmly. Panic wastes oxygen and attention.

# 24. Emergency Procedures

## Suit Breach

1. Cover breach immediately with emergency patch.
2. Increase suit pressure if possible.
3. Move to extraction route.
4. Do not stop to document.
5. Monitor for dehydration and dust inhalation.
6. Evacuate from basin.

## Lost Route

1. Stop.
2. Do not continue “toward where the exit should be.”
3. Gather group physically.
4. Check beacons.
5. Check tether if present.
6. Compare clocks and inertial tracks.
7. Retrace only confirmed segments.
8. If beacons contradict vision, trust the beacon cautiously but move slowly.
9. Signal external team.
10. Avoid doorways and alleys while disoriented.

## Storm Caught in Open

1. Anchor together.
2. Cover visors.
3. Turn away from particulate flow.
4. Keep breathing systems clear.
5. Minimize exposed surface.
6. Wait.
7. Afterward, inspect for suit abrasion.
8. Reconfirm location.
9. Retreat unless route remains unquestionably stable.

## Companion Entranced

1. Do not physically shake unless immediate hazard.
2. Speak agreed grounding phrase.
3. Place a hand on their shoulder or harness.
4. Turn them away from object of fixation.
5. Lead them toward beacon.
6. Do not ask them to explain until outside.
7. If they resist, retreat as a group while maintaining tether.

## Injury with Bleeding

1. Seal wound immediately.
2. Prevent blood contact with stone.
3. Apply pressure dressing inside suit if possible.
4. Use clotting agent.
5. Evacuate.
6. If blood spills, do not remain to examine resulting marks.

# 25. Scientific or Documentary Work

If you are visiting as a researcher, discipline is essential.

Safe-ish observations:

- Long-distance imaging from basin edge.
- Dust sampling in sealed traps.
- Passive acoustic recording.
- Temperature and pressure logging.
- Noninvasive mineral scans.
- Remote photography of architecture.
- Time-lapse from outside active districts.
- Observation of storms from protected positions.

High-risk research:

- Sampling stone blooms.
- Entering towers at dawn.
- Studying pits.
- Tracking tall ones.
- Mapping interiors.
- Measuring gravity anomalies in person.
- Interacting with star-city alignments.
- Naming or classifying districts in detail.
- Leaving instruments overnight inside.

If you deploy instruments, assume some will return altered or not at all. Do not retrieve a device if its location has become anomalous.

---

# 26. Ethics of Visiting

There are no people, but the city is not inert. It has processes, cycles, and perhaps forms of attention.

Conduct yourself as you would in a sacred ruin, a fragile desert ecosystem, and a sleeping organism.

Do not:

- Carve marks.
- Remove large samples.
- Break salt crusts unnecessarily.
- Disturb the clicking creatures.
- Approach tall ones.
- Pour water as a test.
- Light fires as a test.
- Shout to “hear the echo.”
- Camp in ceremonial-looking plazas.
- Treat the absence of inhabitants as permission.

The city may be empty, but it is not vacant.

---

# 27. Departure and Decontamination

Leaving the city does not end exposure.

After exit:

1. Move beyond basin influence.
2. Confirm all group members are present by physical identity, not just voice or suit signal.
3. Check clocks against external time.
4. Inspect hydration systems.
5. Inspect suits for salt crystallization, mica cuts, shale abrasions.
6. Remove dust in controlled decontamination.
7. Do not bring unsealed dust into living quarters.
8. Quarantine samples.
9. Check skin, eyes, lungs, and mucous membranes.
10. Rehydrate slowly with electrolytes.
11. Monitor for delayed altitude sickness.
12. Record memories separately before comparing accounts.

Important: have each person independently write or record what happened before discussing. Shared conversation may overwrite discrepancies that matter.

Watch for delayed symptoms:

- Dreams of the city overhead.
- Persistent chalk or iron taste.
- Hearing stone ticks in quiet rooms.
- Dryness despite hydration.
- Unexplained salt on clothing.
- Notes rearranging themselves.
- Difficulty remembering names of ordinary places.
- Desire to return urgently.
- Seeing distant buildings with excessive clarity.

If symptoms persist, consult someone experienced with spatial/time contamination or metaphysical desert exposure.

---

# 28. The Right Attitude

The safest traveler here is not fearless. Fear is useful. The safest traveler is patient, humble, and willing to turn back before the city becomes interesting.

Do not arrive as a conqueror, pilgrim, scavenger, or prophet.

Arrive as a brief disturbance in a place that has endured longer than your categories.

The city is not dead. It is dry. It is not abandoned in the normal way. It may be waiting, but not for you in particular. Its emptiness is active. Its streets are not invitations simply because they are open.

The best preparation is to accept that most of the city should remain unseen.

Go lightly. Stay sealed. Keep your companions close. Trust your retreat plan more than your curiosity. And if, at the edge of hearing, the stone seems about to speak — leave before it does.
