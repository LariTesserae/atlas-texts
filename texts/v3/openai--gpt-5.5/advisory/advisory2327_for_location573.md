An ordinary human should prepare as if visiting a high-altitude desert, an abandoned planetary-scale machine, and a sacred archaeological site all at once.

This is not a place for casual exploration. Its dangers are quiet, slow, and precise. You are unlikely to be attacked. You are more likely to misjudge distance, dehydrate without noticing, lose your route across a plain that looks featureless for a hundred kilometers, step into an invisible field-gradient, or sleep on ground that has changed its topology by morning.

If you go, prepare for **thin air, extreme dryness, cold, electrostatic dust, anomalous gravity/space effects, unreliable instruments, and profound isolation**.

---

## 1. Do not arrive unacclimatized

The plateau is high enough that altitude is not merely background discomfort; it is one of the defining conditions of the place.

Prepare for:

- Low oxygen
- Low atmospheric pressure
- Rapid dehydration through breathing
- Impaired judgment
- Headache, nausea, insomnia
- Poor wound healing
- Increased UV exposure
- Cold nights even after bright days

You should not step directly from sea-level normality onto the plateau and begin walking. Spend time in a staging environment first.

Recommended preparation:

- Several days of altitude acclimatization if your physiology allows it
- Medical screening for heart, lung, and blood-pressure issues
- Training in recognizing hypoxia in yourself and companions
- Supplemental oxygen or a pressure-assist breathing system
- A pulse oximeter or equivalent biometric monitor that does not rely on local magnetic fields
- Medication for altitude sickness, if compatible with your species/body type
- A strict rule: if you become euphoric, unusually calm, confused, or determined to “just keep going,” stop and check oxygen

The plateau’s silence may make hypoxia feel spiritual. Do not trust that feeling.

---

## 2. Bring all water; expect to find none

There is effectively no usable water.

Do not count on:

- Snow
- Ice
- Dew
- Frost
- Condensation
- Lichen moisture
- Buried aquifers
- Biological water sources
- Ancient structures containing reservoirs

The pale dawn sublimations are not safe to collect. They may be metallic, toxic, reactive, or part of the local field chemistry.

Bring:

- More water than you think you need
- Redundant sealed reservoirs
- Electrostatic-resistant hydration lines
- Emergency injectable fluids or rehydration packs
- Salts/electrolytes
- A personal recycling system if available
- A strict water accounting protocol

Because the air is cold and dry, you may not sweat visibly, but you will still lose water continuously. Your throat, eyes, lips, and lungs will dry out quickly. Use breathing masks with humidity recovery if possible.

---

## 3. Dress for cold desert exposure, not heat

The sun will be bright, but not generous.

You need clothing that handles:

- High UV
- Low humidity
- Abrasive dust
- Electrostatic charge
- Cold windless nights
- Sudden temperature drops in shadow
- Metallic or chemically active frost-like deposits
- Charged particulate storms

Recommended clothing:

- Full-body coverage
- UV-opaque outer layer
- Breathable but sealed dust-resistant suit
- Insulating underlayers
- Hood, neck seal, and face protection
- Goggles or sealed visor
- Respirator or fine particulate filtration mask
- Electrically dissipative outer fabric
- Gloves suitable for cold and abrasive mineral surfaces
- Boots with excellent grip on smooth stone, crust, and powder
- Gaiters or sealed boot cuffs

Avoid loose fabric that can accumulate charge or snag on stone shelves. Avoid exposed skin. The dust is not ordinary sand; it may be chemically reactive, conductive, or field-responsive.

## 4. Protect electronics, but do not depend entirely on them

The plateau interferes with ordinary assumptions.

Expect problems with:

- Compasses
- Magnetometers
- GPS-equivalent systems
- Long-range optical distance estimation
- Radio propagation
- Metal heating/cooling in unexpected conditions
- Static discharge
- Timekeeping drift near active structures
- Sensor ghosting from mirage fields and delayed reflections

Bring redundant navigation systems:

- Inertial navigation unit
- Celestial navigation tools
- Optical sextant or star-fix capability
- Local beacon network deployed as you travel
- Physical route markers, but assume they may move or be buried
- Lidar/radar mapping, but verify with direct observation
- Paper or inert substrate maps generated during travel
- Dead-reckoning logs
- Mechanical chronometer sealed against dust
- Distance line/tether for camp perimeters or structure approaches

Use at least three independent navigation methods at all times.

Most importantly: **do not trust apparent distance**. A tower that looks one hour away may be three days away. A nearby basin may conceal a vast descent. A flat plain may be gently sloping for dozens of kilometers, detectable only in breathing difficulty and fatigue.

Travel by measured intervals, not by visual goal.

## 5. Establish a base camp before exploring

Do not wander from arrival point into the open plateau.

First establish a properly surveyed base camp in a stable area:

Good camp qualities:

- Away from visible structures
- Away from rings, pylons, grooves, and geometrically arranged stones
- Away from basin floors
- Away from escarpment rims
- Away from active dust corridors
- On slightly raised ground, but not on a ridge crest
- On stone that has shown no recent cracking, blistering, tilting, or powder movement
- With clear horizon visibility in several directions
- With anchor points, but not deep-penetrating ones that might disturb buried systems

Base camp should include:

- Pressure-capable shelter or sealed high-altitude tent
- Dust airlock or cleaning vestibule
- Insulated floor barrier
- Independent power
- Faraday shielding
- Emergency beacon
- Oxygen reserve
- Medical supplies
- Water cache
- Marked perimeter
- Seismic/field monitors
- Wind and dust detectors
- Visual warning flags or lights
- Redundant exit route markers

Do not sleep directly on bare stone. At night the subsurface becomes active. Use insulated, nonconductive, sensor-backed bedding and set alarms for ground movement.

## 6. Move slowly and conservatively

The plateau punishes urgency.

Recommended travel pattern:

- Move in teams of at least three
- Keep visual contact whenever possible
- Use short travel legs
- Stop frequently for oxygen and hydration checks
- Record every change in terrain
- Deploy temporary markers at intervals
- Never chase mirages, distant animals, dust formations, or apparent structures
- Turn back earlier than feels necessary
- Avoid travel during dawn/dusk field transitions until you understand local cycles
- Avoid night travel except in emergency

A safe day’s travel may be much shorter than expected. The terrain may look easy because it is open and smooth, but the altitude, distance distortion, dust, cold, and field anomalies will erode stamina quickly.

Use the mountaineer’s rule: reaching the destination is optional; returning is mandatory.

## 7. Learn to read the dust

The dust is the local weather, the local warning system, and possibly the visible edge of buried machinery.

Pay attention to:

- Dust rising without wind
- Vertical sheets
- Cylinders around towers
- Lattices, combs, grids, or suspended planes
- Pebbles lifting or arranging themselves
- Dust moving against slope
- Dust climbing shadows
- Footprints elongating or rotating
- Sudden clearing around a structure
- Basin floors darkening or paling from within

These are not spectacles to walk toward. They are signs that a field is active.

If dust begins to rise around you:

1. Stop.
2. Do not run unless the ground is visibly failing.
3. Crouch or sit to lower your center of mass.
4. Protect eyes and breathing system.
5. Check for static buildup.
6. Avoid touching metal tools.
7. Wait for the pattern to pass.
8. Afterward, verify position and orientation before moving.

If pebbles, dust, or equipment begin arranging themselves in the air, leave the area once safe to do so. Do not interrupt the formation. Do not stand inside geometric arrangements.

## 8. Avoid the ancient structures unless specifically equipped and authorized

The towers, rings, pylons, shelters, grooves, platforms, and cliff installations are not ruins in the normal sense. They may be dormant, but they are not inert.

Rules for structures:

- Do not enter sealed buildings.
- Do not force doors.
- Do not touch pit-pattern controls.
- Do not stand in concentric grooves.
- Do not camp near towers.
- Do not cross embedded rings unless surveyed inactive.
- Do not walk between triangular pylons casually.
- Do not collect material from structures.
- Do not lean equipment against black surfaces.
- Do not assume absence of sound means absence of power.
- Do not stand on rail-less platforms over escarpments unless secured by tether and field-tested.

The technology here expresses itself as altered conditions rather than visible activity. A building may “guide” you away by subtly changing slope. Respect that. If the ground seems to discourage approach, leave.

The black towers are especially suspect. A dust cylinder forming around one may indicate atmospheric reconstruction, memory cycling, defense, calibration, communication, or something else entirely. Observe from a distance.

## 9. Beware of edges, basins, and “flat” land

The plateau contains enormous gentle slopes and sudden lethal drops.

Escarpments may appear without warning because erosion has smoothed their approach. The lower world may be hidden by haze, making the drop visually difficult to judge.

Near rims:

- Stay roped or tethered
- Probe ground ahead
- Avoid overhanging crust
- Beware dry updrafts carrying charged dust
- Do not trust falling-stone tests; objects may vanish silently or fall anomalously
- Do not camp near cliff listening posts or outward platforms

Basin floors are also dangerous. They may be active field nodes. A basin darkening, paling, rippling, or producing nested pressure waves should be treated as an exclusion zone.

Flat expanses are psychologically dangerous. With no nearby features, you can become disoriented, misjudge effort, and walk toward a mirage for hours. Use instruments and pace counts, not intuition.

## 10. Prepare for anomalous physics

The rules are not broken everywhere, but they are unreliable enough to matter.

Known or likely anomalies:

- Dropped objects falling inconsistently
- Lateral displacement during falls
- Dust and footprints stretching northward
- Metals warming in shadow and cooling in sunlight
- Shadows pointing toward buried centers
- Distance behaving honestly only nearby
- Terrain subtly tilting
- Cracks opening without debris
- Trenches appearing overnight
- Surface crust accepting and then erasing impressions
- Objects appearing nearer, taller, doubled, or delayed by optical effects

Practical precautions:

- Secure all tools to your body
- Avoid relying on thrown ropes or tossed equipment
- Test gravity/field behavior before descending slopes
- Use nonmetallic or low-reactivity tools where possible
- Keep critical devices insulated from direct ground contact
- Anchor tents broadly, not with single-point metal stakes
- Do not leave gear scattered; the land may move it, bury it, heat it, or incorporate it into a pattern
- Make camp inventories morning and evening
- Check whether your footprints still lead where you think they do

If an object falls strangely once, assume the area is active. Leave.

## 12. Treat local life as fragile and possibly not purely biological

The lichens, filament colonies, metallic-leaf plants, jointed shrubs, and small under-crust organisms are survivors of extreme selection. They may be rare, slow-growing, and integrated with the field systems.

Do not:

- Step on crusts or mats
- Collect samples casually
- Break shrubs
- Disturb fissures showing olive glow
- Dig into dimpling powder lines
- Touch reflective leaves
- Use chemical waste anywhere near living colonies
- Assume something is dead because it is rigid
- Assume something is plant, animal, or machine in familiar categories

If a shrub locks rigid during dust movement, stay away. It is responding to conditions you may not perceive.

If you smell the cold bitter resin in blue hour, note it but do not approach too closely. It may indicate biological activity, chemical defense, reproductive release, or field-mediated metabolism.

## 13. Psychological preparation matters

The plateau is quiet in a way that can distort the mind.

You may experience:

- Scale disorientation
- Time dilation or monotony
- Uncanny calm
- Fear without obvious cause
- Obsession with distant structures
- Urge to walk toward mirages or dust cities
- Difficulty judging progress
- Heightened attention to tiny objects
- Dreamlike interpretation of machine events
- Loneliness intensified by lack of sound

Prepare with:

- Team communication protocols
- Scheduled check-ins
- Mental state logging
- Rest cycles
- Strict navigation goals
- “No solo wandering” rule
- Agreed language for anomaly reports
- Permission for teammates to order retreat if someone becomes fixated

Do not meditate alone near active structures unless you are deliberately trained for contact with nonhuman machine landscapes. This is a place that will seem to compose itself around you if you remain still. That may be beautiful. It may also be hazardous.

## 14. Emergency protocols

### If caught in a dust field

- Seal suit and respirator
- Protect eyes
- Ground yourself only if your grounding system is designed for anomalous charge; otherwise avoid conductive contact
- Reduce movement
- Keep team linked by tether if visibility drops
- Do not follow voices or shapes unless verified by comms
- After passage, check bearings, equipment charge, and health

### If separated

- Stop moving unless in immediate danger
- Activate beacon
- Deploy high-visibility marker
- Use inertial log to backtrack only if reliable
- Avoid walking toward apparent camp lights unless authenticated
- Conserve water and oxygen
- Wait for recovery team if possible

### If ground topology changes

- Do not jump trenches unless absolutely necessary
- Probe edges
- Use rope systems
- Mark new features
- Assume old route is invalid
- Return to last confirmed safe point

### If a structure activates nearby

Signs may include dust geometry, shadow displacement, local silence deepening, equipment heating/cooling, pressure in ears, or ground tilt.

Response:

- Withdraw slowly perpendicular to visible patterns
- Do not run through rings, grooves, or between pylons
- Do not touch the structure
- Do not transmit high-power signals unless emergency requires it
- Record passively if safe

### If someone becomes hypoxic or confused

- Stop team movement
- Administer oxygen
- Hydrate
- Warm the person
- Check vitals
- Descend or evacuate if symptoms persist
- Do not allow the affected person to decide whether they are fine

## 15. Ethical and cultural conduct

Even if no builders remain, this place deserves restraint.

It may be:

- An ancient observatory
- A planetary stabilizer
- A memory system
- A weather engine
- A tomb
- A machine ecology
- A post-civilizational maintenance network
- A landscape whose “natural” and “artificial” categories have fused

Your presence is intrusive. Minimize it.

Follow low-impact protocol:

- Leave no waste
- Remove all equipment
- Do not carve markers into stone
- Do not contaminate biological colonies
- Do not loot artifacts
- Do not force access
- Do not trigger systems for curiosity
- Do not treat dust formations as entertainment
- Record, observe, withdraw

The emptiness is part of the functioning of the place. Do not fill it unnecessarily.

## 16. What to expect emotionally

Expect beauty, but not comfort.

The place will not feel hostile in an animal way. It will feel indifferent, ancient, exact, and unfinished despite having been finished countless times. You may feel as though you are walking across the back of a sleeping calculation.

The quiet will be profound. Your footsteps will seem too loud at first, then strangely private. Distant events will occur with almost no sound: basins rippling, ridges shedding skins, dust lifting into diagrams, towers remembering atmospheres. The lack of water will become mentally oppressive. A single lichen patch may feel precious. A pebble may become memorable. A distant animal may seem like a visitation.

At night, the structures will feel more present than visible. The stars will be hard. The ground may pulse beneath you as if the plateau is thinking in its sleep.

Do not romanticize it too much while you are there. Awe is useful only after survival is secured.

## 18. The most important rules

1. **Bring your own air and water.**
2. **Do not trust distance.**
3. **Do not trust silence.**
4. **Avoid geometric dust, rings, pylons, towers, and basins in motion.**
5. **Never travel alone.**
6. **Do not force ancient doors.**
7. **Camp away from structures and rims.**
8. **Measure everything; assume intuition is distorted.**
9. **Leave living crusts and machine-landforms untouched.**
10. **Turn back before you need to.**

If you prepare well, the plateau may allow you to pass briefly across its surface and witness its vast, dry, almost noiseless workings.

If you prepare poorly, it may not kill you dramatically. It may simply make your route longer than your water, make your camp unreachable by a subtle change in distance, erase your footprints, dry your lungs, cool your body, and continue its ancient maintenance without noticing that you were ever there.
