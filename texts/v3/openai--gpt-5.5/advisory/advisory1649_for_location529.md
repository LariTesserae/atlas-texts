An ordinary human traveler should prepare as if visiting three places at once: an extreme-altitude industrial city, an active geological worksite, and a region where reality has a bad habit of revising its own paperwork. Do not treat it as a picturesque mountain town. Treat it as a cold, damp, high, unstable civic machine built on a mountain that is still becoming a mountain.

## 1. Acclimatize before entering the basin

The first danger is not the fog, the machinery, or the anomalous alleys. It is the air.

The city sits at an altitude where breathing is labor. The air is thin, cold, and mineral-tasting. Even healthy travelers may experience headache, nausea, confusion, poor sleep, nosebleeds, clumsiness, and irrational optimism or dread. Do not arrive directly from sea level if you can avoid it.

Before entering:

- Spend several days at intermediate altitude.
- Learn the symptoms of altitude sickness, pulmonary edema, and cerebral edema.
- Carry supplemental oxygen, even if you think you will not need it.
- Bring medication appropriate for altitude acclimatization if your physician allows it.
- Avoid alcohol for the first several days.
- Eat even when you have no appetite.
- Walk slowly. Then slower than that.
- Do not trust your judgment on the first day.

The city’s own residents move with an economy that may look calm or even lazy. Imitate them. They are not unhurried because they lack urgency; they are unhurried because the air punishes drama.

## 2. Dress for cold damp, not just cold

The climate is not merely frigid. It is wet in a sly, constant way. Condensation gathers on everything. You may be soaked without ever seeing rain fall.

Bring:

- Layered wool or synthetic clothing.
- A waterproof outer shell that breathes well.
- Insulated gloves thin enough to handle railings, valves, tickets, and tools.
- A warm hat that can fit under a helmet.
- Spare socks sealed in waterproof bags.
- High-traction boots with crampon-compatible soles or removable spikes.
- Gaiters for slush, grit, and mineral seepage.
- Eye protection against wind, steam, and bright snow-reflection.
- A face covering or scarf to warm inhaled air.

Avoid cotton. Avoid delicate fabrics. Avoid anything that will wick mineral water into your skin and keep it there.

Assume every railing is wet, every stair may be iced, every bridge may sweat, and every tunnel mouth may exhale warm fog that freezes ten meters later.

## 3. Bring industrial-grade personal safety gear

This is a city of pipes, cables, gantries, trams, chimneys, vents, fissures, cisterns, and partial geologies. Dress as if you might spend the day touring a factory, a mine, and a construction site.

Recommended equipment:

- Climbing-rated helmet.
- Headlamp and backup light.
- Respirator or mask with filters for dust, sulfurous gases, coal smoke, and unknown particulates.
- Ear protection for foundries, tram sheds, and pressure-valve districts.
- Compact gas detector if available.
- Harness and short safety line if traveling near fissures, high terraces, viaducts, or suspended streets.
- Sturdy gloves with grip.
- Small first-aid kit including blister care, wound disinfectant, and burn treatment.
- Emergency blanket or bivouac sack.
- Whistle, signal mirror, and local flare or lamp code if taught.

Do not touch pipes unless a local tells you they are safe. Color codes have been revised many times, and old paint may lie under new paint. A red pipe may not mean what red meant last year.

Some pipes carry water. Some carry steam. Some carry brine, compressed air, mineral slurry, messages, or civic secrets. Assume pressure. Assume heat. Assume consequence.

## 4. Hire a local guide, preferably two kinds

You want both:

1. A street guide who knows neighborhoods, transit, markets, bad staircases, fog routes, and local customs.  
2. A technical or municipal guide if you intend to enter industrial, geological, archive, high-district, or fissure-adjacent areas.

Do not rely solely on maps. Maps are useful, but here they are more like weather reports: honest, temporary, and incomplete.

A good guide will know:

- Which alleys exist only when wet.
- Which puddles are reflections and which are entrances.
- Which tram terminus should not be waited at casually.
- Which neighborhoods take longer to leave than to enter.
- Which bridges amplify mountain-sound.
- Which animals should be followed, ignored, or avoided.
- Which factory whistles mean ordinary shift change and which mean “go indoors now.”

Pay guides fairly. Do not haggle aggressively over safety.

## 5. Carry the right kind of map

Use the city’s own seasonal maps, not imported navigation systems alone. Digital devices may misread distance, elevation, or time seams. Satellite-equivalent tools may disagree with streets that are technically present but not currently reachable.

Carry:

- A current municipal map printed on flexible paper.
- Pencil, not ink.
- Waterproof notebook.
- Compass, though it may need local calibration.
- Altimeter, likewise locally calibrated.
- Mechanical watch plus a local timepiece if possible.
- Chalk or biodegradable marking tabs for route confirmation, if permitted.
- Written destination cards in the local language or trade script.

Annotate constantly. Note stair counts, smell changes, pipe colors, animal signs, and landmark sounds. In this city, “turn left after the wall of orange lichen where the steam pipe sings” may be better navigation than “second avenue north.”

## 6. Prepare for unreliable distance, gravity, and time

Most of the city behaves normally. That is what makes the exceptions dangerous.

### Distance hazards

A neighborhood visible across a viaduct may not be reachable by the obvious road. Fog can fold routes. Slopes may stretch. A downhill walk can take longer than the ascent. If a local says, “You cannot get there this afternoon,” believe them even if you can see it.

### Gravity hazards

On certain terraces:

- Rain falls diagonally.
- Sparks descend too slowly.
- Dropped tools may not fall toward the floor.
- Birds avoid the air.

Keep loose objects secured. Tie down tools, cameras, notebooks, and water bottles. Do not jump across gaps. Do not lean over railings to test “which way is down.”

### Time hazards

Time seams are civic hazards, not tourist attractions.

Avoid lingering near plaques warning of temporal echo, premature conversation, delayed bells, or unstable schedules. If you hear yourself speaking before you have spoken, stop walking and ask a local official or guide what to do. If a clock rings once when the hour arrives and once when it leaves, do not treat the second bell as decorative.

Never make critical travel plans through the north tram terminus without written purpose and local confirmation.

## 7. Respect empty spaces

The broad raw stretches between districts are not vacant lots. They may be monitored geological emergence zones, future neighborhoods, unstable uplift surfaces, grazing areas, thermal fields, or places the city has deliberately left alone because nobody yet knows what the ground is becoming.

Do not cross fenced stone because it looks empty. Do not camp in open rock fields. Do not collect fresh mineral samples without permission. Do not step into vapor fissures for photographs.

Some ground has not settled on being ground.

## 8. Understand the city’s weather

Weather here is intimate and local. One street may be wet and reflective while the next is dry. Fog may rise from below rather than descend from above. Rain may arrive as slanted threads and stop before your coat is fully wet, leaving the city dripping for hours.

Prepare for:

- Sudden whiteout fog.
- Localized freezing surfaces.
- Condensation inside bags and lenses.
- Mineral-rich rain or seepage.
- Warm vapor near fissures.
- Static or ozone near industrial works.
- Sun glare from snowfields when the clouds part.

Waterproof your documents. Use dry bags. Keep electronics in sealed cases with desiccant. Expect lenses, goggles, and screens to fog repeatedly.

Do not drink untreated water from cisterns, basins, puddles, runnels, or “almost water” sources. Even if locals drink from a particular basin, ask first. Some water is mineral-heavy, some is biologically active, some is temporally questionable, and some has not fully decided where it came from.

## 9. Food and hydration

The altitude suppresses appetite and increases dehydration risk. The damp air may fool you into thinking you are hydrated. You are not.

Carry:

- Insulated water bottle.
- Electrolyte salts.
- High-calorie snacks.
- Iron-safe digestive caution if eating local fruit or fungus.
- Personal medication for nausea or altitude issues.
- A thermos for broth or tea.

Local food likely includes roots, mushrooms, preserved goods, broth, bread from communal ovens, eggs, mountain animal products, and greenhouse herbs. Eat warm meals. Hot broth may be your best friend.

Be cautious with:

- Berries near condenser yards.
- Fungi from stairwells or tunnel mouths.
- Cistern trout.
- Any food advertised as “good for adjusting,” unless recommended by a trustworthy resident.

## 10. Animals: observe, do not interfere

The animals are part of the city’s warning systems, sanitation, mythology, and infrastructure.

You may encounter:

- Alpine crows and swifts in cable towers.
- Bright red birds in fog.
- Near-goats on impossible ledges.
- Marmots under warm factory foundations.
- Large moths around lamps.
- Pale lizards in switch boxes.
- Blind amphibians in waterworks.
- Foxes in slag terraces.
- Scavenger birds at markets.

Do not feed them without permission. Do not chase them for photographs. Do not touch animals inside machinery housings or cistern rooms. Do not disturb nests in cable pylons.

Pay attention to animal behavior. If birds suddenly leave a gantry, marmots whistle, goats descend from high ledges, or amphibian song stops traveling through the pipes, locals may also be listening. Follow their lead.

Some animals predict pressure changes. Some detect gas. Some know which surfaces are about to shift.

## 11. Health precautions beyond altitude

The environment is damp, mineralized, industrial, and biologically unusual. Prepare accordingly.

Before travel:

- Update tetanus vaccination.
- Consider respiratory vulnerability; asthma may be aggravated by cold, smoke, mold, or mineral dust.
- Bring all medications in waterproof packaging.
- Carry prescriptions in written form.
- Bring antifungal foot care if staying long.
- Prepare for joint stiffness and fatigue in cold, wet conditions.
- Consider eye drops for wind and particulate irritation.

During travel:

- Dry your feet every night.
- Inspect minor cuts; industrial grime and mineral water are not kind to wounds.
- Avoid touching moss, fungi, or lichen with bare hands unless cleared by locals.
- Use gloves in markets and transit.
- Sleep with ventilation but not drafts.
- Be alert for carbon monoxide in older lodgings with stoves or boilers.

If you develop confusion, difficulty walking, severe headache, blue lips, chest tightness, or hallucinations not clearly covered by the municipal tourist office, descend or seek medical aid immediately.

## 12. Lodging: choose stability over charm

Do not choose accommodation based only on view. A romantic old inn built into a damp wall above a singing fissure may be memorable in the wrong way.

Look for lodging that offers:

- Oxygen access or altitude medical support.
- Heated rooms with dry storage.
- Local emergency instructions.
- Current evacuation routes.
- Structural inspection marks.
- Staff who can explain district whistles and mountain-note alarms.
- Secured windows and shutters.
- Reliable indoor plumbing, or at least plumbing that has been recently negotiated with.

Ask:

- Has this building changed height recently?
- Are there known time seams nearby?
- What do the whistles mean?
- Where is the nearest stable assembly point?
- Which stairs should visitors avoid in fog?
- Does the room have a level mark? Should I check it?

If the staff laugh warmly and answer in detail, good. If they wave away the questions, choose another place.

## 13. Transportation within the city

Expect trams, elevated rail, cable systems, stairs, viaducts, and footpaths. Some streets are bolted down. Some are suspended. Some move or are periodically winched sideways.

Use public transit when locals recommend it. It may look old and alarming, but it is probably better maintained than the footpath you are considering.

Transit advice:

- Keep tickets and destination cards accessible.
- Board promptly; platforms may be cold and temporally odd.
- Do not lean against tram doors in fog.
- Watch locals when disembarking; platform height may not match the car.
- Avoid standing under cable towers during ice fall, bird panic, or maintenance whistles.
- Never walk along rail trestles, even if they appear inactive.

If a conductor refuses to take you to a district because of pressure, fog, survey correction, or “bad height,” accept it.

## 14. Industrial etiquette and danger

The city’s factories are not background scenery. They are part of its metabolism. Many exist to harvest the mountain’s newness: heat, minerals, soft clays, condensation salts, gases, and materials that may change after exposure.

Do not enter industrial wards casually.

If visiting with permission:

- Wear the required protective gear.
- Stay behind marked lines.
- Do not photograph instruments, gauges, or unnamed pipes without permission.
- Do not distract workers listening to machinery or stone.
- Do not touch clay, ore, condensate, or slurry.
- Do not stand over grates.
- Do not pocket “interesting” nodules.
- Obey stoppage orders immediately.

When machines stop, stop talking. If workers are listening for the mountain’s note, let them listen.

## 15. Social conduct

The citizens live with patient instability. They are practical, observant, and probably tired of visitors treating their home like a paradox museum.

Behave with humility.

Good manners include:

- Ask before crossing private courtyards, roof gardens, or pipe corridors.
- Respect shrines at cracks, drips, stairways, and old fissures.
- Do not joke about the city “falling down.”
- Do not call the place impossible; people have to get groceries here.
- Do not waste water, even though everything is damp.
- Do not mock charms against mismeasurement. You may want one later.
- Step aside for workers carrying instruments, gauges, or pipe tools.
- Learn basic local phrases for greeting, thanks, warning, and apology.
- If someone pauses to listen to the stone, pause too.

Markets are excellent places to learn, but keep your pack close. Not necessarily because of thieves—though thieves may exist—but because large moths, scavenger birds, goats, and children with civic confidence may all investigate unattended straps.

## 16. What to pack

A practical traveler’s pack might include:

- Passport, visas, interdimensional permits, and local entry papers.
- Copies sealed separately.
- Current municipal map and pencil.
- Local phrasebook.
- Oxygen canister or portable concentrator if compatible.
- Altitude medication and personal prescriptions.
- Layered cold-weather clothing.
- Waterproof shell.
- Gloves, hat, scarf, spare socks.
- Helmet.
- Headlamp and spare batteries.
- Respirator or high-quality mask.
- Water purification system rated for metals/minerals if possible.
- Insulated bottle and thermos.
- High-calorie food.
- First-aid kit.
- Emergency blanket.
- Dry bags.
- Desiccant packets.
- Tether cords for tools and devices.
- Boot spikes or traction aids.
- Earplugs.
- Protective eyewear.
- Small local currency for trams, markets, guides, and emergency lodging.
- Notebook for route notes and level/time anomalies.

Optional but wise:

- Walking pole with metal tip.
- Compact barometer.
- Gas detector.
- Mechanical watch.
- Chalk or removable markers.
- Small offering tokens if advised by locals.
- Spare lamp mantles or batteries for barter in outer districts.

## 17. What not to bring or do

Avoid bringing:

- Fragile luggage with small wheels.
- Untreated leather that will warp in damp.
- Cotton-heavy clothing.
- Unsealed electronics.
- Drones, unless explicitly permitted; birds, cables, fog, and gravity anomalies make them dangerous.
- Open-flame camping gear without local approval.
- Magnetic-sensitive instruments without shielding or backup.

Do not:

- Drink from unknown basins.
- Explore tunnels alone.
- Enter fog pockets for curiosity.
- Follow reflected streets.
- Step into puddles that show a different sky.
- Wait at anomalous clocks for fun.
- Touch valves.
- Climb cable pylons.
- Feed goats on ledges.
- Take stones from active fissures.
- Ignore headaches.
- Argue with surveyors about whether a road “should” exist.

## 18. Mental preparation

Perhaps the most important preparation is psychological.

This city does not collapse. It edits itself. That makes it easy to underestimate. There may be no dramatic monster, no single forbidden gate, no obvious disaster. Instead, the city asks you to survive increments: a missed turn, a damp chill, a wrong stair, a delayed echo, a street that is a little longer than it was, a headache you explain away, a pipe you lean against without thinking.

Cultivate slowness.

When in doubt:

- Stop.
- Breathe.
- Check your body.
- Check your guide.
- Check the animals.
- Check the locals.
- Check whether the street is still the same street.

Do not rush to see everything. You cannot. The city itself has not finished becoming everything it is going to be.

## 19. Best way to experience it

Visit modestly. Spend the first day doing almost nothing: sit near a market under dripping tarpaulins, drink broth, watch tram cables vanish into fog, listen to the pumps and birds. Learn the sound of ordinary machinery before you try to recognize extraordinary silence.

On later days, take guided walks: one through residential terraces, one through the industrial ward, one across a viaduct in clear weather, one to a monitored raw-stone field where the mountain’s unfinishedness can be seen without trespass. Visit a roof garden. Watch condensation nets being emptied. Listen to the amphibians through the plumbing if invited. Buy a charm against mismeasurement even if you do not believe in it.

Above all, remember: this is not a ruin, not a dream, and not merely a hazard zone. It is a functioning home for people, animals, plants, machines, minerals, and unfinished laws of nature. Prepare with respect, move carefully, and let the city be stranger than your expectations.
