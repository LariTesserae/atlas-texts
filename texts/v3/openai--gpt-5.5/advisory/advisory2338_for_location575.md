To visit this city, prepare less as if you are going to a “destination” and more as if you are entering a climate, a machine, and a living organism at the same time. It is not a place you pass through casually. It is wet, loud, crowded, mutable, and almost completely without the ecological cues an Earth-born human expects. You will need equipment, local permissions, sensory protection, and a willingness to be inconvenienced by water every few minutes.

## 1. Expect to be wet almost all the time

Do not bring anything that cannot survive humidity, warm rain, immersion, mineral spray, or condensation.

The city is not merely rainy. It sweats, drips, exhales vapor, floods streets deliberately, vents hot condensate from above, and channels water through public space as a normal civic function. Even sheltered streets may rain from their ceilings for hours after the sky has cleared.

Bring:

- A full waterproof outer layer, preferably flexible and breathable.
- Sealed boots with serious grip. Smooth wet ceramic, mineral crust, algae-like chemical films, and self-repairing pavements will all be slippery in different ways.
- Quick-dry underclothing.
- Waterproof gloves.
- Dry bags for all documents, tools, currency, medicine, and electronics.
- A roll-top pack or hard-sealed case.
- A personal towel or absorbent cloths, ideally antimicrobial.
- Spare socks in sealed packets.
- Waterproof labels on all luggage.

Do not bring leather, paper, untreated cloth, wood, or delicate optics unless they are sealed. Dryness is rare and culturally meaningful here. A perfectly dry object may attract attention, envy, suspicion, or a customs fee.

Assume that anything exposed to the air will become damp. Anything left on the ground may be flooded, sealed over by maintenance surfaces, inspected by scavenger animals, or carried away by sluice surge.

## 2. Protect your hearing

The city’s sound is not background noise. It is a physical force.

Pumps, turbines, transit systems, sluices, warning horns, animal calls, ventilation organs, crowd noise, and rainfall combine into a constant roar. In some districts the sound can rattle your chest and make ordinary speech impossible.

Bring:

- High-quality hearing protection.
- Active noise-canceling ear covers if your biology tolerates them.
- Backup passive earplugs.
- A throat microphone, bone-conduction communicator, or gesture-display device.
- A small light-pulse communicator if available.

Learn basic local hand signals before arrival. Many residents communicate by gesture, colored light, touch, or short-range devices in noisy areas. Shouting will mark you as inexperienced and may not work anyway.

If you are prone to migraines, sensory overload, panic responses, or auditory processing issues, arrange for quiet-room access in advance. Silence exists, but it is a paid or protected service.

## 3. Prepare for heat, humidity, and respiratory strain

The air is warm, wet, mineral-heavy, and full of industrial odors. It may smell of hot stone, ozone, broth, animal musk, sewage digesters, metallic salts, steamed protein, and warm rain.

The heat is not necessarily extreme, but the humidity may make it difficult for ordinary humans to cool themselves. You may sweat without drying. Clothes may cling. Breathing may feel thick.

Bring:

- Electrolyte supplements.
- A hydration system that can be sealed against contamination.
- Respiratory filters rated for aerosols, mineral dusts, spores, industrial vapor, and biological particulates.
- Anti-fungal skin treatment.
- Anti-chafing balm.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics only if advised by a travel physician; do not self-administer casually.
- Rehydration salts.
- A lightweight cooling garment if you overheat easily.

Do not drink from canals, gutters, roof channels, condensation curtains, or public runoff unless a local authority certifies it. Water is everywhere, but that does not mean it is potable. Much of it may contain minerals, engineered organisms, animal waste, industrial trace chemicals, or civic maintenance agents.

## 4. Wear clothing appropriate to crowding and machinery

Loose garments may snag on vents, rails, animal tack, drainage teeth, maintenance grates, and moving market frames. Very tight clothing may become miserable in the humidity.

Ideal clothing:

- Close-fitting but flexible.
- Waterproof or quick-drying.
- Resistant to mineral stains.
- Without dangling cords.
- With sealed pockets.
- Easy to clean.
- Visible in rain and fog.

Add reflective strips or local signal colors if allowed. In a place of moving cargo, animal trains, flood crews, and automated infrastructure, being seen is survival.

Avoid:

- Long scarves.
- Open sandals.
- Absorbent robes unless local climate-adapted.
- Delicate jewelry.
- Magnetic accessories unless cleared; some streets contain weight-altering or electromagnetic infrastructure.
- Fragrances. Strong perfumes may confuse animals, irritate residents, or interact badly with air-quality sensors.

## 5. Navigation will not be stable

Do not expect fixed maps to remain accurate. Streets flood, plazas tilt, markets dissolve, buildings molt, bridges change stiffness, doors close for structural reasons, and routes are rewritten dynamically.

Use:

- A live local navigation implant, guide device, or hired human guide.
- Animated civic maps.
- District-specific route permissions.
- Flood-depth alerts.
- Transit status feeds.
- Translation software capable of reading rewriting signs and public light codes.

Carry a backup route plan, but expect it to fail.

If a public wall, floor light, horn sequence, or crowd movement tells you to step up onto raised ribs, do so immediately. Sluices may open with little warning. Residents will not stop to explain basic survival routines to a tourist standing in the water’s path.

Never assume a dry street will remain dry.

Never assume water flowing one way will keep flowing that way.

Never assume a plaza is level just because it was level when you arrived.

## 6. Hire a local guide at least for the first days

This is not a city where a first-time visitor should “wander and discover.” Wandering is possible, but it should begin after you understand flood signals, animal lanes, market permissions, safe food sources, legal dry zones, transit hazards, and district moods.

A good guide will know:

- Which pavements are safe to step on.
- Which walls are undergoing repair.
- Which canals contain dangerous fauna.
- Which markets are lawful for outsiders.
- How to interpret flood sirens.
- Where to find quiet rooms, medical stations, and sanitary facilities.
- Which animal handlers should not be crossed.
- Which neighborhoods are in structural transition.
- How to avoid being rerouted into industrial loops.
- Whether a drainage grate is “negotiating” and should be left alone.

Choose a guide licensed for hydraulic districts, not merely a general translator.

## 7. Respect infrastructure as if it were alive, because some of it nearly is

In this city, walls breathe. Floodgates think. Drainage grates negotiate. Pavements seal themselves. Bridges alter their stiffness based on crowd psychology. Foundations listen to aquifer pressure. Some animals are legally part of the municipal system. Some soft-bodied organisms may be maintenance workers, not pests.

Do not:

- Sit on active vents.
- Block drainage slots.
- Pry open ports.
- Touch shimmering wall patches.
- Scrape mineral films.
- Feed animals without permission.
- Step into condensation curtains marked with civic symbols.
- Remove “interesting” ceramic scales from bridges or walls.
- Throw anything into canals.
- Interfere with repair organisms.
- Photograph restricted infrastructure without checking.

The city is a layered utility system. A harmless tourist action may disrupt water flow, heat exchange, animal routing, or maintenance swarms.

If a grate, wall, bridge, or doorway displays warning lights, changes texture, pulses, clicks, exhales vapor, or closes its pores, back away.

## 8. Learn the flood etiquette

Flooding is not always an emergency. Sometimes it is traffic, sanitation, thermal regulation, market scheduling, animal movement, or civic breathing.

You need to know the difference between:

- Routine sluice release.
- Street-clearing surge.
- Market wash.
- Emergency backflow.
- Structural drainage.
- Contaminated flush.
- Animal migration water.
- Festival water.
- Dangerous pressure event.

Residents will react differently to each. Watch them. If everyone calmly steps onto raised side ribs, follow. If everyone runs upward, run upward. If handlers are moving animals in a hurry, give them more space than you think necessary.

Do not stand at the edge of a canal during sirens. Water can rise, reverse, foam, boil, or carry debris.

Do not enter standing water in the old districts unless locals are doing so and you have protective boots. Ancient canals may hide steps, broken ceramics, living plumbing fauna, suction channels, or automated repair mouths.

## 9. Food will be unfamiliar

Because there are no plants, food culture is based on vats, fungi, animals, canal life, mineral salts, cultured fats, broths, shell creatures, protein cakes, eggs, and engineered organisms.

If you are used to vegetables, fruit, grain, tea, coffee, spices from seeds, or fermented plant products, bring supplements or arrange imports. Local cuisine may be nutritious but psychologically strange.

Expect flavors of:

- Salt.
- Mineral.
- Fat.
- Broth.
- Shellfish.
- Yeast.
- Hot metal.
- Fermented protein.
- Oil.
- Brine.
- Smoke-like ceramic heat.
- Umami-heavy cultured tissue.

Before eating, ask whether the food is safe for ordinary humans. Some foods may contain high mineral loads, local microbes, heavy-metal-tolerant organisms, bioactive maintenance cultures, or proteins your immune system will dislike.

Carry:

- Digestive aids.
- Allergy medications.
- Activated charcoal only if medically appropriate.
- Known-safe ration bars.
- Vitamin C and plant-derived micronutrients if your stay is long.
- A personal utensil set that can be sterilized.

Do not assume “fungal protein” or “cultured fat” is human-safe across universes.

## 10. Medical preparation is essential

The obvious hazards are water, heat, noise, and crowds. The less obvious hazards are skin infections, respiratory exposure, unfamiliar proteins, engineered microorganisms, mineral poisoning, and stress exhaustion.

Before travel, obtain:

- Updated vaccinations relevant to your own world and any known cross-world pathogens.
- Broad medical screening for humidity tolerance.
- Anti-fungal medication.
- Waterproof wound dressings.
- Skin barrier cream.
- Eye wash.
- Respirator filters.
- Ear protection.
- Anti-diarrheal medication.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- A compact medical scanner if available.
- Emergency contact with a local clinic licensed for off-world biology.

Treat small cuts seriously. In a warm, wet, crowded, bioactive city, a scratch can become infected quickly. Clean and seal wounds immediately.

If your feet remain wet for hours, inspect them daily. Trench foot, fungal infections, blisters, and chemical irritation are realistic risks.

If you develop fever, unusual rash, persistent cough, metallic taste, hallucinations, numbness, or skin that shimmers/oils oddly after contact with a surface, seek medical care at once.

## 11. Prepare psychologically for the absence of green

Many humans underestimate how much they rely on plant life for comfort. This city has rain, animals, markets, daylight, and water, but no trees, no grass, no moss, no leaves, no flowers.

The absence may feel wrong. You may experience a subtle homesickness or ecological vertigo. Rain will fall, but nothing green will answer it. Wet walls will not grow moss. Cracks will not sprout weeds. Food markets will not smell of herbs or fruit. Shade will be architectural, not leafy.

Bring small permitted reminders if you need them: an image of forest, a sealed scent vial, plant-based tea in a dry case, or recordings of wind in leaves. Do not bring live plants unless explicitly authorized. In a plantless ecosystem, introducing vegetation, seeds, pollen, soil microbes, or spores may be illegal and ecologically dangerous.

Even a wooden walking stick may be treated as a relic or contamination risk.

## 12. Money, trade, and valuables

Dry objects may be valuable. Plant matter may be rare. Wood may be museum-grade. Paper may be impractical or culturally significant. Clean sealed spices, plant fibers, and preserved botanical goods might be luxury items — but do not assume you can trade them legally.

Use official exchange systems. This city is too complex to improvise black-market trade safely.

Keep currency waterproof. If using digital or biometric payment, protect devices from moisture and electromagnetic interference.

Do not flash rare dry goods in markets unless you want attention.

## 13. Legal and civic precautions

The city’s law is likely entangled with infrastructure. You may be fined or detained not for moral crimes but for disrupting flow, heat, access, pressure, animal routes, or repair cycles.

Likely regulated actions include:

- Blocking drains.
- Contaminating water.
- Damaging maintenance films.
- Interfering with civic animals.
- Entering flood basins during active cycling.
- Crossing flexible bridges during crowd-control states.
- Occupying dry chambers without permission.
- Bringing plant matter, soil, spores, or untreated biological material.
- Recording sensitive machinery.
- Ignoring evacuation or elevation signals.
- Sleeping in transit ducts or animal lanes.
- Touching negotiation grates, active ports, or molting structures.

Carry identification in both physical waterproof form and digital/local-readable form. Register your species, medical requirements, and dietary restrictions with your lodging or district authority.

## 14. Lodging: choose carefully

Do not economize too aggressively on lodging. A cheap bed in this city may mean damp walls, bad air, constant noise, surprise flooding, shared animal access, or no guaranteed dry locker.

Look for lodging that offers:

- A certified dry room or dry cabinet.
- Sound insulation.
- Filtered air.
- Human-compatible sanitation.
- Clear evacuation routes.
- Flood alerts in your language.
- Medical contact.
- Secure storage above flood level.
- Food safe for your biology.
- Staff familiar with off-world visitors.

Ask what happens during routine sluice events. Ask whether the building ever seals itself for molting or load adjustment. Ask whether guests may be temporarily trapped by infrastructure cycles. In this city, “the lower doors are closed today because the building is shedding” may be a normal answer.

## 15. Moving through crowds

The crowds are not merely dense; they are directional, functional, and mixed with animals, cargo, machines, shrines, vendors, children, inspectors, and maintenance crews.

Rules:

- Keep to the right/left/local convention.
- Do not stop in flow lanes.
- Step aside before checking maps.
- Watch floor lights.
- Give animals a wide berth.
- Never pass between a handler and a loaded pack-beast.
- Keep hands and bags close.
- Avoid dangling straps.
- Learn local apology gestures.
- Do not lean against walls unless locals do.
- Follow crowd elevation during sirens.

Children may move along upper rails and routes not safe for outsiders. Do not follow them casually.

Animal trains may be slow but powerful. Amphibious carriers kneel, sway, bellow, excrete, and may shift suddenly under cargo. Treat them like industrial vehicles.

## 16. Animal safety

The animals are adapted to infrastructure. Some are livestock, some vermin, some municipal workers, some escaped engineered species, some legally protected.

Do not touch:

- Pale canal-skimmers.
- Wall eels.
- Sanitation grazers.
- Pipe messengers.
- Membrane-winged scavengers.
- Vent crustaceans.
- Six-legged electromagnetic vermin.
- Anything wearing civic tags or guidance lights.
- Anything emerging from plumbing during repairs.

Do not feed animals. Food can alter routes, cause crowd hazards, or interfere with municipal behavioral programming.

Be cautious near warm vents and outflow lips. Animals congregate there, and some may defend heat sources or mineral deposits.

If bitten, stung, scraped, slimed, or sprayed, seek medical care. Engineered animals may carry maintenance microbes or chemical markers not meant for human tissue.

## 17. Technology compatibility

Your devices may fail from moisture, mineral deposition, electromagnetic fields, or civic signal interference.

Bring:

- Waterproof, ruggedized devices.
- Faraday protection if using sensitive electronics.
- Offline translation backups.
- Local power adapters.
- Chemical batteries or sealed power cells.
- Lens wipes for mineral films.
- Spare seals and gaskets.
- Non-magnetic backup tools.

Do not rely entirely on drones. Airspace may be cluttered with rails, cables, condensation mesh, birds, vents, warning systems, and legal restrictions.

GPS-equivalent systems may be useless unless integrated with local infrastructure. Buildings interpenetrate, routes pass through other buildings, and vertical layers may confuse simple mapping.

## 18. District-specific expectations

### Old low districts

Beautiful, intimate, dangerous. Warm, narrow, half-submerged, echoing, historically dense. Expect low light, standing water, slick polished walls, sudden closures, ancient flood marks, and confusing acoustics.

Visit with a guide. Wear high boots. Do not enter submerged doorways.

### Condenser fields

Not fields in the pastoral sense. Endless collector arrays, dripping mesh, heat vanes, suspended basins, hissing cables. Stunning in daylight but loud, wet, and full of maintenance hazards.

Protect eyes and ears. Avoid touching mesh or droplets unless cleared; condensed water may be chemically active.

### Exchange districts

Fascinating but overwhelming. Buildings overlap legally and physically. A route may pass through a market, court, school, shrine, transit artery, and animal hall in one continuous path.

Have live navigation. Do not wander off-route. Watch signs as they rewrite.

### Flood basins and maintenance belts

Avoid unless invited or professionally escorted. These areas may look abandoned but are often active infrastructure.

## 19. Cultural attitude: do not call it dirty

The city is wet, stained, noisy, animal-filled, and corroded. It is not necessarily neglected. Its weathering is part of its life. Its leaks may be intentional. Its smells may be civic processes. Its animals may be public utilities. Its stains may mark history, safety, or ownership.

Avoid making faces at food, animals, dampness, or lack of plants. Avoid saying “there’s no nature here.” To locals, the city may be nature: an ecosystem of water, mineral, machine, animal, heat, law, and habit.

Better questions:

- “Is this water safe to step in?”
- “May I stand here?”
- “Is that animal working?”
- “Is this surface active?”
- “What does that siren mean?”
- “Is this route stable today?”
- “Where may I dry this?”
- “Is this food compatible with human biology?”

## 21. How to behave when things change suddenly

If a street floods: step up, not sideways.

If sirens sound: watch locals first, then move.

If lights pulse underfoot: follow direction unless your guide says otherwise.

If a wall opens pores or vents vapor: give it space.

If a crowd changes pace all at once: assume the city told them something you did not understand.

If an animal handler shouts or gestures: obey immediately.

If your route disappears: do not force it; find a public map wall or guide node.

If a building seals its lower doors: wait or use marked alternates.

If water reverses direction in a channel: move away from the edge.

If a plaza tilts: do not panic; walk toward the indicated high side.

## 22. Best reason to go

Go if you want to experience a city that has become a watershed, a machine that has become weather, and an urban ecosystem that no longer needs trees to feel alive.

It will not be serene. It will not be clean in the sterile sense. It will not give you quiet avenues, leafy parks, or dry museums. It will give you warm rain glittering under ordinary daylight; markets assembling while water still runs through the street; animals bellowing in steam; ceramic walls healing themselves; bridges breathing with crowds; old inscriptions shining beneath canal water; and a horizon that is not landscape but infrastructure.

The most important preparation is humility. This city is too large to master and too active to ignore. You are entering a body with its own circulation. Learn its signals, keep your feet dry when you can, protect your ears, respect the water, and never assume that what looks like a street is only a street.
