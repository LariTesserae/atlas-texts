An ordinary human traveler should treat this place less like a scenic alpine pool and more like a confined industrial hazard, a sacred site, and an unstable newborn geological event all at once. It is small, but it is not manageable. Its danger comes from compression: too much water, sound, cold, force, uncertainty, and meaning packed into a hollow no wider than a square.

Prepare as though you are entering a mountain throat that is still learning how to breathe.

## 1. Do not go alone

This is not a place for solitary exploration.

Bring at least three people total:

- one person entering the hollow,
- one person supporting nearby,
- one person remaining outside the entrance or at the upper rim.

If something goes wrong inside, the roar will prevent shouting for help. A fall, cramp, loose stone, sudden surge, or disorientation could become fatal in seconds. The outside observer is essential because conditions inside may change without the person within realizing how severe the change has become.

Use a rope system from the entrance onward. Not decorative rope, not hiking cord: proper climbing rope, harnesses, locking carabiners, belay devices, and redundant anchors where possible. Assume every surface is slick and every route may be altered since the last time anyone looked at it.

## 2. Prepare for total acoustic isolation

Inside, speech is nearly useless.

Before entering, establish nonverbal signals. Use:

- hand signals,
- rope tugs,
- light signals,
- written slates or waterproof cards,
- colored flags or cloth strips,
- vibrating alert devices if available.

Do not rely on whistles, shouting, alarms, or ordinary radios unless they have bone-conduction earpieces or visual displays. Even then, the water and stone may interfere.

The psychological effect of the roar should not be underestimated. Continuous deafening sound can cause panic, irritability, distorted time sense, nausea, and fragmented thinking. A traveler may feel stupid inside, not because they are stupid, but because the place breaks thought into pieces.

Before entering, rehearse simple procedures:

- “Stop.”
- “Return.”
- “Rope slack.”
- “Rope tight.”
- “Danger above.”
- “Danger below.”
- “I am injured.”
- “Leave now.”

Keep all decisions simple. This is not a place for long debate.

## 3. Expect cold saturation

You will get wet. Not damp. Not misted. Wet through.

Wear equipment for cold-water exposure, not ordinary mountain rain:

- waterproof outer shell with sealed seams,
- insulating layers that retain warmth when wet, such as wool or synthetic fleece,
- no cotton,
- waterproof gloves with grip,
- spare dry gloves in a sealed bag,
- helmet liner or insulated cap,
- waterproof mountaineering boots with aggressive soles,
- gaiters,
- dry base layer packed in a sealed roll-top bag,
- emergency bivy sack or bothy bag outside the hollow.

A drysuit or canyoning wetsuit may be appropriate if extended exposure is expected. Hypothermia is one of the chief risks. The air may not be bitterly freezing, but constant spray at altitude will steal heat steadily and efficiently.

Hands will numb quickly. Fine tasks like tying knots, handling tools, opening buckles, lighting stoves, or writing notes become difficult. Practice with gloves on.

## 4. Protect your head, eyes, lungs, and teeth

Wear a helmet. The stone is young, sharp, fractured, and unstable. Falling splinters, loosened pebbles, rebounding debris, and sudden rock shifts are plausible.

Wear eye protection. The spray may carry grit, mineral particles, and tiny shards. Ordinary sunglasses may fog; sealed safety goggles or anti-fog mountaineering goggles are better.

Consider a face covering or mask if the spray is heavy. Breathing in a constant blast of cold water mist can become exhausting. In the thickest periods, it may feel as if the air has partly become water.

The roar and vibration may be felt in the teeth and ribs. Use serious hearing protection even if it seems pointless. Earplugs plus over-ear protection under or integrated with a helmet are advisable. Bone conduction through the skull and chest will still transmit the place’s force, but do what you can.

## 5. Move like a climber, not like a hiker

The basin path is not a path in the trustworthy sense. It is a temporary suggestion made of wedged stones, wet ledges, old handholds, swollen ladders, and shifting channels.

Follow these rules:

- Keep three points of contact whenever possible.
- Clip in before traversing exposed sections.
- Test every foothold before weighting it.
- Avoid standing directly below overhangs, fresh cracks, or shell-weighted ropes during surges.
- Do not trust old ladders without backup.
- Do not step on moss mats unless necessary; they may hide slick stone or shallow water films.
- Avoid clay-colored seams; they may be softer and more treacherous than rock.
- Assume shallow water can become deep water without warning.
- Assume dry ledges can become active channels quickly.

Do not enter the central plunge. Do not attempt to swim. Do not lean over it without a rope. The central water may have vertical currents, hidden cavities, undertow, and anomalous returns. A human body entering that system may not come back where expected, or at all.

## 6. Treat the basin as unstable in time

The hollow changes hourly. A map is useful only as a memory aid, not a guarantee.

Mark your route as you go using removable, visible, waterproof markers. Avoid carving new marks or damaging old ones. Use colored cord, reflective tags, or biodegradable chalk if appropriate. But remember that the place may move your markers, coat them in mineral film, or tear them away.

Before entering, observe from the rim or entrance for at least thirty minutes. Watch for patterns:

- thickening rain,
- sideways gusting spray,
- rising hum beneath the roar,
- shell-ropes twisting in one direction,
- visible tremors in stones,
- sudden smoothing of the basin,
- exposed dark mouths in the rock,
- new watercourses forming,
- changes in water taste or smell,
- swelling of the central pool.

If the basin begins to hum, leave. Do not wait to understand it.

If stones begin jittering on ledges, leave.

If the falling water thickens into heavy vertical cords, leave unless you are already secured and sheltering.

If the clear pause begins, do not be seduced into approaching the center. The pause may be a lull before a release, and many places reveal their deepest hazards only when they appear calm.

## 7. Bring light, but do not expect darkness

The place is bright in a disturbing, flat way. Still, bring waterproof headlamps and backup lights. The diffused gray daylight can erase contrast, making wet stone, deep water, and air-filled spray hard to distinguish. Shadows may be weak. Depth perception may suffer.

Use lights less for illumination and more for signaling, marking wet surfaces, and checking cracks or alcoves.

A polarized lens may help you see through the water during clearer intervals, but do not let visibility tempt you toward the plunge.

## 8. Secure every object

Anything dropped may vanish, return altered, or become a hazard.

Tie off:

- tools,
- cameras,
- notebooks,
- knives,
- sample containers,
- lights,
- measuring instruments,
- gloves,
- walking poles.

Use lanyards. Use waterproof bags. Use clips. Do not carry loose items in open pockets.

Avoid bringing anything sentimental unless you are willing to lose it. The hollow seems to have an appetite for objects, and worse, it may give them back changed.

If a dropped stone, tool, or personal item returns from the central water, do not immediately retrieve it. Mark its location and observe. Local custom, if any survives, likely treats returned objects with caution for a reason.

## 9. Do not consume untreated water

The water may look clean, but it is not necessarily safe.

It may contain:

- glacial minerals,
- rock flour,
- heavy metals from fresh stone,
- unusual microorganisms,
- decaying organic material from hidden cavities,
- anomalous mineralizing agents.

Bring all drinking water with you. If forced to use local water, filter, chemically treat, and boil it if possible. Even then, taste changes should be taken seriously. The warning “do not repair a shelter on the day the water tastes sweet” suggests that flavor may indicate a shift in the basin’s chemistry or mood.

Do not drink directly from the central pool.

## 10. Respect the human remnants

The shelters, carvings, ropes, tools, offerings, and marks are not quaint debris. They are evidence of people who learned enough to survive here, at least for a time. Treat the site as culturally significant and possibly sacred.

Do not:

- remove tools,
- take offerings,
- cut ropes,
- sleep in shelters without need,
- overwrite carvings,
- scrape pigment,
- disturb the fishlike bone,
- use old bowls or hooks casually,
- burn shelter material,
- rearrange shell-weights “to see what happens.”

Photograph or sketch if appropriate, but avoid flash if local fauna or ritual constraints are unknown. If you must shelter in one of the structures, leave it better secured than you found it and leave an offering of practical value: cord, a dry fire starter sealed in wax, a blade, a repair peg, or food protected from water.

Do not speak important names into the roar if local rules warn against it. Even if you do not believe names have power, this is not a place to test every taboo.

## 11. Assume the rules are practical until proven otherwise

The local warnings sound ritualistic, but in places like this, ritual often preserves survival knowledge.

Consider possible meanings:

- “Do not sleep below the shell-ropes when they twist eastward” may indicate a dangerous wind or surge pattern.
- “Do not take a stone that has come back from the deep” may warn of unstable mineral deposits, biological contamination, or anomalous return phenomena.
- “Do not fish during the clear pauses” may indicate moments when deep creatures rise or currents reverse.
- “Carry embers in clay” may be the only reliable method for preserving fire in constant spray.
- “Leave by the upper crack if the basin begins to hum” likely means seismic or hydraulic pressure is building.

Follow such rules even if you do not understand them. Especially if you do not understand them.

## 12. Be cautious with biology

There is little life here, which makes any life present more significant.

Do not touch the translucent cupped plants. They may be fragile, toxic, rare, or responsive to vibration and water chemistry. The description of them as “listening devices” may be poetic, but in multiverse travel, poetic impressions are often early warning systems.

Avoid disturbing:

- moss mats,
- lichen crusts,
- larvae,
- mineral-feeding birds,
- pale underwater shapes.

If something larger moves beneath the central water, withdraw from the edge. Do not bait it, photograph it at close range, cast hooks, or throw stones. The creature, if it is a creature, lives in a violent vertical environment and may not behave like ordinary aquatic life.

## 13. Plan your visit around time limits

Do not linger.

A reasonable first visit should be brief:

- 30 minutes observation outside,
- 15–30 minutes entry and route assessment,
- 10 minutes at the shelters or alcove if safe,
- immediate exit at first major change.

Extended study should require a fixed camp outside the hollow, not inside it. Sleeping inside the basin is unwise unless one has local knowledge, proper anchors, and no alternative. The place offers no true rest. The roar, cold, vibration, and damp will erode judgment.

Set a hard turnaround time before entering. Use waterproof watches or timers. The roar may distort your sense of duration.

## 14. Bring the right supplies

Essential equipment:

- climbing helmet,
- harness,
- rope and backup rope,
- locking carabiners,
- slings and protection suitable for wet rock,
- waterproof gloves,
- cold-water clothing,
- hearing protection,
- goggles,
- waterproof headlamp and backup,
- first-aid kit in dry bag,
- hypothermia kit,
- emergency bivy,
- water and high-energy food,
- map, compass, GPS if functional,
- waterproof notebook and pencil,
- knife or rescue cutter,
- repair tape and cord,
- personal locator beacon if compatible with the world,
- portable hand warmers,
- dry socks and base layer outside the hollow.

Useful specialized items:

- canyoning shoes or boots,
- drysuit/wetsuit,
- throw bag,
- compact grappling or hook tool for retrieving lines,
- water chemistry test strips,
- vibration sensor or simple seismometer,
- polarized goggles,
- waterproof camera secured by lanyard,
- chalk or removable route markers,
- compact shelter tarp for the entrance area.

Avoid:

- umbrellas,
- loose cloaks or scarves,
- heavy absorbent clothing,
- unsecured packs,
- delicate electronics,
- anything that cannot tolerate immersion.

## 15. Mind the altitude before the water

The hollow is high on the mountain, above ordinary weather. You must survive the approach before you survive the basin.

Prepare for:

- thin air,
- steep slabs,
- scree,
- sudden exposure,
- cold wind above the rim,
- sun glare,
- rapid fatigue,
- altitude sickness.

Acclimatize if necessary. Hydrate. Move slowly. The last climb through tilted slabs, coarse grass, and stunted shrubs may already leave you tired; entering the roar while exhausted is dangerous.

Do not make the basin your first strenuous act after arriving in this world.

## 16. Psychological preparation

The hollow may feel larger than it is because it denies mastery. You cannot hear yourself. You cannot keep dry. You cannot trust the ground. You cannot fully see the depth. You cannot easily tell whether the place is empty or inhabited.

This can produce a strange combination of awe and helplessness.

Expect:

- sensory overload,
- intrusive fear of the central pool,
- fascination during clear pauses,
- temptation to touch returned objects,
- frustration at failed communication,
- a feeling that the place is “almost alive.”

Before entering, decide what you came to do. Keep the purpose narrow. For example:

- inspect the shelters,
- document the carvings,
- test the route,
- observe one clear pause from a safe point,
- collect water vapor samples from the margin,
- leave an offering and depart.

Do not enter with a vague goal like “explore everything.” The hollow punishes curiosity that has no boundaries.

## 17. Fire and warmth

Do not expect to make a normal fire inside. Any flame will be drowned unless protected under an overhang or within a purpose-built container.

If fire is culturally or practically necessary, use:

- sealed fire kit,
- stormproof matches,
- ferro rod,
- waxed tinder,
- compact stove usable in wet cold conditions,
- windscreen,
- clay or metal ember carrier.

But cook and warm yourself outside if possible. The shelters may smell of smoke memory, but they are not comfortable huts. They are water-deflection devices built by people who accepted discomfort as the price of presence.

## 18. When to leave immediately

Leave at once if any of the following occur:

- the basin begins to hum beneath the roar,
- new cracks appear in the walls,
- water thickens into heavy cords,
- central surface swells upward in rounded humps,
- stones jitter or lift,
- shell-ropes twist sharply in a consistent direction,
- water becomes sweet, metallic, warm, or suddenly clear,
- your hands become clumsy from cold,
- anyone becomes confused or euphoric,
- your rope line is damaged,
- a ledge shifts,
- birds depart suddenly from the rim,
- something large rises near the central pool,
- you feel pressure changes in your ears or chest.

Do not wait for confirmation. In this place, confirmation may arrive as collapse.

## 19. What to do if someone falls into moving water

Prevention is everything. Rescue from the central plunge may be impossible.

If someone slips into a marginal channel:

1. Do not jump in after them.
2. Anchor yourself.
3. Use rope, throw bag, pole, or hook.
4. Pull downstream or sideways, not directly against the full force if avoidable.
5. Get them out of the water fast.
6. Treat for hypothermia immediately.
7. Exit the hollow.

If someone enters the central dark water and vanishes, mark the time, location, and water conditions. Watch from a secured position only if safe. The description suggests objects sometimes return higher than they fell; a person might reappear unpredictably, but planning on that would be foolish. Do not sacrifice additional lives to the plunge.

## 20. How to behave there

Move slowly. Touch little. Listen with your body more than your ears.

A good visitor to this hollow should be humble and methodical:

- enter tied in,
- keep low when crossing exposed sections,
- pause often,
- watch water patterns,
- respect old marks,
- avoid the central pool,
- leave before tiredness,
- take nothing irreplaceable,
- leave no trash,
- do not challenge the place.

If you feel the hollow has “noticed” you, whether through sound, vibration, shifting spray, or the movement of objects, take that feeling seriously enough to withdraw. It does not matter whether the sensation is supernatural, psychological, or environmental. In practical terms, unease is data.

## 21. Best attitude for the traveler

Do not go expecting a waterfall, cave, lake, ruin, shrine, or storm. It is a little of each, but not comfortably any of them.

The right attitude is the one used near glaciers, volcanoes, active construction sites, and holy places: admiration without entitlement.

This is a place still becoming itself. Its stone is not settled, its water is not obedient, its human arrangements are provisional, and its old rules may be the only durable things there. Visit briefly. Learn what you can. Leave before the mountain changes its mind.
