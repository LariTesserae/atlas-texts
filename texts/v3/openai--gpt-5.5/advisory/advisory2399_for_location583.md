An ordinary human should prepare for this place as though visiting a flooded cave, a living bog, a dream with teeth, and a very young ecosystem that has not decided whether humans are too large to admit.

The central fact is simple: **you cannot breathe there, you cannot trust distance there, and you cannot rely on “up.”** Everything else follows from that.

## 1. Do not go alone

This is not a site for solitary exploration. A human visitor should have, at minimum:

- **Two external tenders** remaining outside the entrance.
- **One trained rescue diver** or equivalent aquatic specialist.
- **One multiversal navigator / spatial anomaly handler**, if available.
- **A hard abort protocol** agreed upon before entry.

The tenders should not enter unless absolutely necessary. Their job is to keep track of the tether, monitor time, watch the entrance, and pull the traveler out if signals fail.

The place is small, but that does not make it safe. In fact, its smallness is one of its traps. A person can die in a room-sized flooded hollow as easily as in an ocean trench if they panic, become entangled, or lose orientation.

## 2. Treat it as a confined underwater environment

Even if it seems shallow, even if the entrance is close, even if you can touch both walls at once: prepare as if entering a dangerous submerged cave.

Bring:

- A properly fitted **dry suit or thick wetsuit** for cold still water.
- **Full-face mask** if possible, to reduce panic and allow communication.
- Redundant breathing systems:
  - Primary tank.
  - Backup pony bottle or rebreather redundancy.
  - Emergency spare air that can be reached with either hand.
- **Cutting tools**, at least two:
  - Line cutter.
  - Small blunt-tip dive knife.
- **Low-output lights**, multiple sources:
  - One primary.
  - Two backups.
  - Avoid harsh beams if local organisms react to light.
- **Helmet or head protection**, because the “roof” may become “floor,” and stone/root projections are sharp.
- **Gloves** to prevent cuts from new mudstone, shell edges, and mineral growths.
- **Tether line**, preferably braided, abrasion-resistant, with tactile knots or markers.
- **Non-electronic compass equivalent**, though ordinary compasses may be unreliable.
- **Depth/pressure monitor**, but do not trust it completely.
- **Timekeeping device**, plus an outside timer.

The most important piece of equipment is the tether. Not the light. Not the oxygen. The tether.

A light can fail and you may still follow the line. A gauge can lie and you may still follow the line. Your sense of direction will almost certainly become useless. The line is your memory of the outside world.

## 3. Use a physical tether, not just magical or electronic tracking

This chamber appears to fold scale and direction. It may contain cracks larger on the inside than outside, stones with hidden continuations, reflections that are not reflections, and walls that recede when approached.

A traveler should use a **continuous physical line attached outside the entrance**. The line should be:

- Anchored to something stable outside, not to roots near the slit.
- Managed by a surface tender.
- Marked at regular intervals with knots, beads, or raised tags.
- Never allowed to drift loosely through vegetation.
- Never looped around roots unless intentionally secured.

Do not tie the line to any internal feature unless necessary. Roots may grow, shift, or become “elsewhere.” Stones may have more than one underside. The chamber may interpret attachment as invitation.

If possible, use a **double-line system**:

1. Main tether to the traveler.
2. Separate guide line paid out through the entrance.

This helps if one line becomes snagged or behaves strangely.

## 4. Expect entanglement

The vegetation is dense, filamentous, and nearly everywhere. Rootlets, weeds, fungal threads, and algae veils may catch:

- Buckles.
- Fins.
- Tank valves.
- Mask straps.
- Wrist tools.
- Loose fabric.
- Hair.

Prepare by minimizing anything that dangles.

Before entering:

- Tie back hair securely under a hood.
- Remove jewelry.
- Use streamlined gear.
- Tape loose straps.
- Avoid nets, dangling instruments, loose ropes, charms, or ornamented clothing.
- Keep tools close to the body.

Move slowly. Do not kick hard. Finning will stir silt and tangle weed. Use gentle hand placements only on bare stone or stable root masses, and avoid grabbing living mats if possible.

If entangled:

1. Stop moving.
2. Control breathing.
3. Signal outside by tether.
4. Identify the snag by touch.
5. Cut only if necessary.
6. Do not spin, thrash, or back-kick blindly.

Panic will turn the chamber into a trap.

## 5. Assume the water is biologically and chemically unsafe

The water tastes of bark, clay, stems, old rain, and living rot. Do not drink it. Do not let it contact open wounds.

Potential hazards include:

- Anaerobic bacteria.
- Spores.
- Fungal agents adapted to wet tissue.
- Parasites from larvae, shrimp, eel-like organisms, or unknown eggs.
- Mineral irritants from fresh mudstone fractures.
- Toxins from submerged plants or lichens.
- Memory/metaphysical contaminants, if the fungus truly feeds on “the idea of wood” or similar abstractions.

Wear sealed protection:

- Full-body exposure suit.
- Gloves.
- Hood.
- Full-face mask preferred.
- No exposed skin if avoidable.

After exit:

- Rinse with clean water immediately.
- Disinfect gear.
- Quarantine biological samples.
- Shower with antifungal/antimicrobial wash.
- Monitor for rashes, numbness, fever, cough, hallucinations, dreams of water, persistent smell of clay, or sensations of roots in the lungs.

If any plant matter clings to the suit, do not pull it off barehanded. Bag and seal it.

## 6. Bring your own light, but use it gently

The darkness is not empty. It is inhabited, saturated, and responsive. Some plants fold when shadow passes. Some glimmer weakly. Lichens change when not watched.

Use light with restraint.

Recommended:

- Diffuse green-blue low-intensity lamps.
- Avoid strobing.
- Avoid high-heat lights.
- Avoid intense white beams on translucent bulbs, fungal mats, or reflective vertical pools.
- Keep at least one light pointed toward the exit line, not into side anomalies.

Do not rely on reflected light. Reflections may show other versions of the chamber. Do not follow them.

If you see a vertical black pool between roots showing white reeds, colder water, or a clearer path: **do not enter it.** Mark it mentally and move away.

## 7. Time limits should be short

The place changes slowly, but the human body does not. Cold water, pressure, darkness, and spatial uncertainty will exhaust you.

Set a strict maximum entry time. For an ordinary human, even with equipment:

- First visit: **5–10 minutes inside**, no more.
- Later visits: gradually extend only if the chamber has proven stable.
- Always reserve at least two-thirds of breathing gas for exit and emergency, not exploration.

Do not let curiosity extend the dive. The chamber is designed, or has become, a place where “a little farther” may not mean anything.

Use external timekeeping. Internal time may feel softened, stretched, or sedimentary.

If you lose track of time, exit immediately.

## 8. Navigation rules

Inside this place, ordinary spatial assumptions fail. Therefore:

### Do not trust:
- Upward bubbles.
- Gravity.
- The apparent nearness of walls.
- The direction leaves sink or rise.
- Reflections.
- The location of the roof.
- Your inner ear.
- The apparent size of cracks.
- The apparent harmlessness of small openings.

### Trust, in this order:
1. Physical tether.
2. Prearranged pull signals.
3. Air supply.
4. Touch markers on your own line.
5. The memory of your entry procedure.

Use simple tether signals:

- 1 pull: okay.
- 2 pulls: stop / hold.
- 3 pulls: begin exit.
- 4 or more rapid pulls: emergency extraction.

The outside tender should repeat signals back. If signals become confused, exit.

If the tether appears to lead in two directions, close your eyes and follow the tactile markers toward decreasing count. Do not choose visually.

If the entrance appears to vanish, do not search randomly. Follow the line.

If the line passes through a crack too small for you, stop. Signal for retrieval. Do not attempt to “make yourself fit” unless you are trained in size-altering environments and have external stabilization.

## 9. Avoid touching certain features

Some parts of the chamber should be treated as sacred, hazardous, or both.

Do not disturb:

- Translucent bulbs with curled embryos.
- Dark velvet fungus.
- Lichens forming maplike stains.
- Pale leaves thin as fish skin.
- Shell beds.
- Eggs in the old post.
- The three submerged steps.
- Regular round holes in the clay bank.
- Vertical suspended pools.
- Stones that appear to contain more room inside them.
- Air bubbles holding skies.

The temptation will be to collect samples. Resist it on the first visit. This is a young place, densely balanced, and possibly conscious in a slow distributed way. Removing a stone may be more like removing an organ than taking a specimen.

If samples are necessary for research, take only:

- Loose silt from near the entrance.
- A small water sample.
- Detached plant matter already floating free.

Never cut living roots unless needed for survival.

## 10. Psychological preparation

The chamber will likely feel intimate, smothering, and strangely persuasive. It is not a grand abyss. It is worse in a subtler way: a hidden wet cupboard under the world, packed with growth, where dryness and distance begin to seem fictional.

A visitor may experience:

- Claustrophobia.
- Loss of bodily orientation.
- Fear of drowning.
- Dissociation.
- The sense that the outside world is impossible or childish.
- Fascination with sediment, bubbles, or root motion.
- Desire to remain still and “listen.”
- Compulsion to follow small cracks or lights.
- Scale confusion: feeling enormous, then tiny.
- Time dilation.

Before entering, rehearse mental anchors:

- Your name.
- Your home location.
- Your exit signal.
- The fact that air is finite.
- The fact that the outside is real.
- The fact that you are visiting, not returning.

It may help to carry a tactile object from outside: a dry stone sealed in a pouch, a metal token, a piece of bright cord. Something unambiguously not of the chamber. Do not expose it to the water unless necessary.

If you begin to feel that breathing water would be natural, exit immediately.

If you begin to believe you are inside a seed, lung, footprint, or shell and that this explains everything, exit immediately.

If you feel comforted by the absence of sky, exit immediately.

## 11. Medical concerns

The visit combines hazards from cold-water diving, confined spaces, infection risk, and spatial anomaly exposure.

Pre-visit requirements:

- Be able to swim calmly in darkness.
- No uncontrolled asthma.
- No severe claustrophobia.
- No active ear/sinus infection.
- No open wounds.
- No panic disorder unless exceptionally well managed.
- Up-to-date tetanus and broad travel immunizations, if relevant to your multiverse medical system.
- Training in mask clearing, regulator recovery, and emergency breathing.

Post-visit observation:

- Monitor body temperature.
- Check for hypothermia.
- Check ears and sinuses for pressure injury.
- Inspect skin for cuts or embedded splinters.
- Watch for delayed fungal infection.
- Watch for unusual dreams or sensory persistence.

A strange but important precaution: after leaving, spend time in a dry, well-lit, open place. Stand under sky if available. Let the body reassert normal scale and gravity.

## 12. Environmental ethics

This is not an abandoned room. It is not merely a cave. It is an unfolding wet ecology with traces of making already being reclaimed by growth.

A visitor should behave as a guest in a nursery.

Rules:

- Do not scrape moss from steps.
- Do not pry shells from stone.
- Do not widen the entrance.
- Do not drain water.
- Do not introduce light for long periods.
- Do not leave markers inside except emergency line, and retrieve them afterward.
- Do not introduce foreign organisms.
- Do not use fire, heat, chemicals, soap, dye, or conventional flares.
- Do not seed it with “useful” life from elsewhere.
- Do not attempt to stabilize the geometry unless survival requires it.

The place is “still flooding” with possibility. Human convenience would likely damage it.

## 13. What to wear

A practical outfit:

- Thermal base layer.
- Dry suit with sealed hood and gloves.
- Compact buoyancy control system or equivalent stabilization harness.
- Full-face mask.
- Low-profile breathing rig.
- Soft, short fins or no fins if the chamber is extremely tight.
- Helmet with low-profile lights.
- Chest-mounted line attachment with quick release.
- Wrist slate or tactile recording tablet.
- No backpack except essential life support.
- No loose pockets.

If the entrance is extremely narrow, use sidemount or compact bailout cylinders rather than a large back tank.

## 14. What not to bring

Do not bring:

- Firearms.
- Open flames.
- Incense, smoke, or heat sources.
- Glass containers unless protected.
- Loose sample jars.
- Drones with exposed propellers.
- Loud sonar.
- Bright floodlights.
- Long robes, scarves, or dangling equipment.
- Pets.
- Food.
- Anything made of untreated wood you are not willing to lose.
- Anything emotionally precious that might become a “memory anchor” for the fungus or lichens.

Also avoid bringing objects that depend on stable geometry: rigid measuring poles, folding ladders, survey tripods. They may become misleading or jammed.

## 15. Special precautions around the built fragments

The three steps, the holes in the clay, and the leaning post matter.

They are the remains of intention. The chamber has not preserved them as history exactly; it has digested them into habitat. They may function as weak points, memory nodes, or places where human thought catches.

Approach them respectfully and briefly.

Do not stand on the steps. They descend into nowhere, and “nowhere” may be locally accurate.

Do not put fingers into the round holes. Roots use them now.

Do not straighten the post. Do not tie a line to it unless there is no alternative.

If you hear voices, tools, knocking, children, or the sound of dry footsteps near these structures, leave.

## 16. Signs that the chamber is becoming unsafe

Exit immediately if:

- Silt suddenly clouds the water.
- The water pressure increases noticeably.
- Plants flatten all at once.
- Bubbles begin emerging in large numbers from mud or cracks.
- Your bubbles rise sideways or downward for more than a few seconds.
- The tether becomes slack despite no movement.
- The tether feels as if it is being pulled from inside a stone.
- You see the entrance in more than one place.
- Your light illuminates a room much larger than expected.
- You see white reeds in a reflection.
- The roof appears below you.
- Your breathing rate increases uncontrollably.
- You feel sleepy, peaceful, or invited to stay.
- You cannot remember whether you entered from above or below.

The chamber may not be hostile. It may simply be indifferent in a way that is fatal.

## 17. Best conditions for a visit

The safest time appears to be during a drier period, when:

- The water is clearer and colder.
- Suspended silt has settled.
- Pressure is lower.
- The fauna are less disturbed.
- Shapes can be seen without stirring the chamber.

Avoid visiting:

- During or immediately after heavy rain.
- During seasonal flooding.
- When the entrance is newly shifted.
- When plant glimmers are unusually active.
- When bubbles are emerging continuously.
- When outside roots or soil are moving.
- If the moss curtain at the entrance has changed color, thickened, or sealed over.

Rain does not merely add water here. It changes pressure, mood, and perhaps size.

## 18. If something follows you out

On exit, check carefully for:

- Root filaments wrapped around gear.
- Weed caught in valves.
- Spores or black velvet patches.
- Small shrimp, larvae, eel-like organisms, eggs, shells.
- Air bubbles clinging unnaturally to equipment.
- Mud that reflects a different chamber.
- Water that does not pour downward.

If any organism or material exits with you unintentionally, contain it in water from the chamber and isolate it. Do not release it into local waterways. Do not dry it without study; drying may kill it, or it may teach it something.

If a reflection, second entrance, or patch of black water appears outside after the visit, withdraw and mark the area. The chamber may have unfolded.

## 19. The proper attitude

Go slowly. Go humbly. Do not conquer. Do not map too aggressively. Do not assume the place is “only” a flooded hollow.

It is small in the way a seed is small. It is young in the way a wound is young. It is drowned, but not dead. It is crowded with beginnings.

A human visitor should enter with the discipline of a cave diver, the gentleness of a botanist, the suspicion of a physicist in a broken universe, and the manners of someone stepping into another creature’s unfinished thought.

The best preparation is to decide, before entering, that you are allowed to leave without understanding it. In fact, you should leave before you understand it too well.
