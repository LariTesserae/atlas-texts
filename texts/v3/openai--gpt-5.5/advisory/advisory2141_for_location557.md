For an ordinary human multiverse traveler, this is not a place to prepare for as one prepares for wilderness, mountains, jungle, or a city. It is all of those only in fragments: highland, wetland, settlement, garden, quarry, and hydraulic machine. The main rule is that everything is damp, everything is quiet, and almost every surface is part of a water system.

You should prepare to move slowly, stay dry enough to remain healthy, avoid contaminating the water, and treat the town less like a built environment than like a living arrangement between people, stone, plants, and seepage.

## First expectation: this place is wet in a deep, persistent way

Do not think “rainy weather.” Think “water as the default condition of matter.”

Your clothes will absorb moisture. Your pack will sweat. Paper will soften. Leather will rot. Metal will tarnish. Electronics will fail unless protected. Even when it is not raining, the air, walls, paths, and ground will behave as if rain has recently passed.

Bring:

- A fully waterproof outer layer, but breathable if possible.
- Several changes of quick-drying underclothes.
- Synthetic or treated plant-fiber clothing rather than cotton, wool, or leather.
- Waterproof bags inside your pack, not just a waterproof pack cover.
- Waxed wraps, dry sacks, or sealed cases for documents, tools, medicines, and electronics.
- A compact towel or absorbent cloth that can be wrung out repeatedly.
- Foot powder or antifungal cream.
- Spare socks — more than you think you need.

Avoid cotton if possible. It will become heavy, clammy, and slow to dry. Avoid leather boots, leather belts, leather straps, wool cloaks, fur trim, horn-handled tools, and anything made from animal hide or bone unless you have a very good reason. In this world, animal materials may be unknown, unsettling, ritually inappropriate, or simply associated with impossible foreignness.

If you must bring animal-derived items for survival, keep them covered and private.

## Footwear matters more than almost anything else

The town is made of wet stone, damp clay, plank walks, reed mats, causeways, terraces, channels, and sharp young rock. A bad fall here is easy. The surfaces may look solid and still betray you: slick moss, hidden seepage, saturated soil, loose ledge edges, undermined paths, ankle-deep basins, clay-lined channels.

Bring footwear that is:

- Waterproof or at least fast-draining.
- Excellent on slick stone.
- Protective against sharp edges.
- Secure around the ankle.
- Easy to clean.
- Not made of leather, if possible.

The ideal would be something like modern amphibious hiking boots or water shoes with aggressive grip and reinforced soles. Sandals alone are not enough; the young stone can cut feet badly. Heavy mountaineering boots are also not ideal; they may trap moisture and become miserable.

Also bring a walking staff or collapsible trekking pole. It is not just for balance. You will use it to test suspicious ground, measure water depth, check soft patches, and feel for submerged steps.

## Expect warm damp air, not cold exposure

This high plateau is warm and humid. The danger is not freezing. The danger is staying wet too long, developing skin problems, becoming fatigued by humidity, and underestimating dehydration because the air feels moist.

Prepare for:

- Chafing.
- Foot maceration.
- Fungal infections.
- Heat fatigue.
- Slippery grip.
- Slow drying.
- Mildew in gear.
- Damp bedding.

Bring:

- Light breathable long sleeves.
- A wide-brimmed rain hat or hood.
- Skin barrier ointment for feet, thighs, shoulders, and pack straps.
- Antifungal treatment.
- Electrolyte salts.
- A water bottle you can keep sealed.
- A sleeping layer that still insulates when damp, if you will stay overnight.

The air may make breathing feel easy, but do not confuse humidity with hydration. You will sweat. You may not notice how much.

## Protect your lungs and skin from mold and wet rot

With no animals, there may be fewer familiar zoonotic diseases, bites, stings, ticks, fleas, mosquitoes, or venomous creatures. That is a blessing.

But the absence of animals does not mean the place is sterile. It may be rich in fungi, algae, bacteria, wet-soil organisms, and plant irritants. Rot is slow, damp, fungal, and mineral. Reed beds, storage houses, old walls, wet mats, and shaded seams may contain spores.

Bring:

- A light mask or respirator for dusty, moldy, smoky, or kiln areas.
- Basic antihistamines.
- Antiseptic.
- Waterproof bandages.
- A small medical kit for cuts and abrasions.
- Treatment for fungal skin infections.
- Gloves for touching stone, reeds, wet wood, and unknown plants.

Cuts should be cleaned immediately. Young stone can slice cleanly, and wet environments are unforgiving to wounds.

## Do not assume water is safe to drink

Water is everywhere, carefully managed, and likely central to local life. That does not mean you should drink casually from channels, pools, basins, or roof runnels.

The people may distinguish many categories of water:

- Roof water.
- Drinking cistern water.
- Garden water.
- Floodwater.
- Wastewater.
- Ritual water.
- Settling water.
- Reed-bed water.
- Stone-seep water.

To an outsider, these may look nearly identical.

Bring:

- A reliable water filter.
- Purification tablets or UV purifier.
- A sealed bottle or bladder.
- A collapsible backup container.
- Knowledge of local permission customs before taking water.

Never dip a dirty vessel into a cistern. Never wash your hands, feet, tools, clothes, or cooking gear in an unknown basin. Never assume a pool is public because it is open. In this settlement, water is infrastructure, food security, hygiene, memory, property, and possibly sacred order.

Ask first.

## Navigation will be deceptively difficult

The horizon is open, but moisture softens distance. The town is spacious, low, and spread across basins and slopes. Paths are causeways, plank walks, raised embankments, terraces, and channels. They may curve around water rather than follow obvious lines. Landmarks can vanish into haze. Sound does not carry well. There are no animal noises to orient you.

Bring:

- A compass or equivalent direction-finding tool.
- Waterproof map materials.
- Chalk, cord markers, or biodegradable visual markers only if locally acceptable.
- A small light for low visibility.
- A whistle — though sound may be subdued, it is still better than shouting.
- A local guide if at all possible.

Do not wander off paths. A place like this has safe routes and unsafe routes that may differ by only a few handspans. A flat pale surface may be stable pavement, a slick submerged slab, a roofed channel, a ceremonial basin, or a thin crust over saturated soil.

If locals step somewhere, step there. If they avoid a patch of beautiful moss or clean stone, avoid it too.

## Move quietly and respectfully

This is a nearly silent ecology. There are no birds, insects, frogs, livestock, dogs, carts, bells, or hoofbeats. Human sounds will stand out.

Your normal travel noise may feel intrusive.

Prepare yourself to:

- Speak softly.
- Walk deliberately.
- Avoid clattering metal gear.
- Secure dangling items on your pack.
- Keep electronics muted.
- Avoid loud music or recorded sound.
- Avoid shouting across courtyards.
- Listen before entering spaces.

In a world without animal calls, unnecessary noise may be interpreted as alarming, childish, rude, or dangerous. The quiet here is not emptiness; it is part of how people live.

## Bring non-metal, non-animal trade goods if trade is appropriate

The settlement has little metal and no animal products. That does not mean metal is unwanted, but it may be rare, precious, disruptive, or culturally controlled. A steel knife could be a treasure, a threat, or a contamination of craft traditions. Be careful.

Safer gifts or trade goods might include:

- Plant-fiber cordage.
- Waterproof cloth.
- Ceramic beads.
- Salt, if scarce.
- Charcoal filters.
- Fine woven fabric.
- Seeds only if cleared by local ecological authorities.
- Glass containers.
- Pigments.
- Needles or small tools, if metal exchange is acceptable.
- Waterproof writing tablets or slates.
- Simple lenses or mirrors, if culturally appropriate.

Avoid gifting seeds casually. In a world with no animal seed dispersers and a carefully balanced plant-water system, introducing a vigorous outsider plant could be disastrous.

Avoid gifting food until you know local biology and customs. Also avoid bringing exposed meat, dairy, eggs, honey, leather, bone, feathers, or wool. These may not merely be taboo; they may be incomprehensible and disturbing in a place whose material culture is vegetal, mineral, ceramic, and human.

## Food preparation

Since there are no animals, the local diet is likely plant-based, fungal, algal, mineral-supplemented, fermented, or based on cultivated roots, reeds, leaves, fruits, nuts, and wet-bed crops. Protein may come from legumes, aquatic plants, fungi, seeds, or carefully developed staples unknown to you.

Prepare for a diet that may be:

- Low in familiar fats.
- High in roots and greens.
- Fermented.
- Soft, wet, steamed, or clay-baked.
- Mildly mineral or smoky.
- Unfamiliar in texture.

Bring emergency food that is compact, sealed, and does not advertise animal origin. Good options include:

- Grain bars.
- Nut butters.
- Dried fruit.
- Plant protein rations.
- Electrolyte packets.
- Salt.
- Tea or soluble drink powders.
- Simple starches.

Do not bring strong-smelling meat jerky or fish. In this quiet, damp, animal-less place, the smell could be shocking.

## Respect the hydraulic order

This town is organized around water management. Its channels, basins, cisterns, reed beds, roof gutters, sluices, retaining walls, and courtyards are not background details. They are the civic skeleton.

You should treat waterworks as you would roads, temples, farms, and kitchens combined.

Do not:

- Step into channels.
- Move stones from water edges.
- Block a runnel with your pack.
- Wash soap into a drainage path.
- Pour anything into a pool.
- Sit on sluice gates.
- Cross reed beds unless invited.
- Take shortcuts over low walls.
- Open or close shutters, weirs, plugs, covers, or cistern lids.
- Assume ankle-deep water in a plaza is accidental.

In such a place, a small careless act could redirect water, contaminate drinking supplies, flood a drying platform, drown roots, weaken a wall, or offend the people responsible for maintaining balance.

If you are invited to help, ask exactly how. Water here “remembers every slope”; locals will know consequences you cannot see.

## Understand the architecture before entering

Buildings are low-doored, narrow-windowed, damp-walled, and hand-built from local stone, clay, reeds, bark, wood, and soft slabs. Roofs are heavy and steep because rain is continuous or frequent. Raised pads and stone piers matter. Floors may be uneven. Interior light may be dim.

Prepare to:

- Duck often.
- Remove footwear if required, but only where surfaces are safe.
- Keep wet gear outside or in designated areas.
- Avoid leaning on reed walls or woven screens.
- Avoid touching clay seals or plastered joints.
- Not sit on storage platforms unless invited.
- Keep fire use extremely cautious.

Smoke may be rare and practical, not atmospheric. There may be kiln shelters and drying platforms, but open flame in a damp reed-and-bark settlement is still significant. Do not light a stove indoors without permission.

## Sleeping there

If you stay overnight, your priorities are dryness, ventilation, and keeping your bedding off the floor.

Bring:

- A hammock if there are suitable supports and if locals allow it.
- Or a waterproof groundsheet plus raised sleeping pad.
- A synthetic blanket or compact sleeping bag suited for warm humid conditions.
- A mosquito net is probably unnecessary if truly no insects exist, but a fine mesh can still keep plant debris and spores off your face.
- A dry bag for sleep clothes.
- A small lantern with a low, warm setting.

Do not sleep directly on stone. It may be damp, hard, and cold relative to the air. Do not sleep on reed mats unless they are clearly offered for that use. Some mats may be for drying crops, filtering water, or ritual purposes.

At night, the absence of animal sound may feel psychologically strange. Many humans rely on background life-noise without realizing it. Here, you may hear only water and your own body. Prepare for that.

## Psychological preparation: the silence may feel unnatural

For a traveler from Earthlike ecologies, the absence of animals will be one of the most profound features. No birds at dawn. No insects at dusk. No dogs. No livestock. No rodents in walls. No flies near food. No frogs in wet places. No moths around lamps.

At first this may seem peaceful. Later it may become uncanny.

You may experience:

- A sense that time is slowed.
- Difficulty judging whether a place is inhabited.
- Heightened awareness of your own noise.
- Unease around flowers that receive no visitors.
- Disorientation at night.
- Loneliness despite being in a town.
- Relief from buzzing, scratching, biting, and animal unpredictability.

Prepare mentally. This is not a dead world. It is a plant-water-human world. Life is present, but its motion is mostly vertical, seasonal, hydraulic, fungal, and deliberate.

## Medical concerns

Before traveling, prepare as you would for a humid subtropical highland wetland, adjusted for unknown biology.

Recommended supplies:

- Broad-spectrum antibiotics, if medically appropriate and legally obtainable.
- Antifungal medication.
- Antihistamines.
- Anti-inflammatory medication.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Wound disinfectant.
- Sterile gauze and waterproof dressings.
- Blister care.
- Tweezers.
- Eye wash.
- Water purification tools.
- Any personal prescriptions in waterproof packaging.

Main likely injuries:

- Slipping on wet stone.
- Cutting hands or feet on sharp young rock.
- Twisting ankles on stepped paths.
- Skin breakdown from constant damp.
- Respiratory irritation from mold or kiln smoke.
- Stomach illness from unfamiliar water or foods.
- Heat exhaustion during labor in humid air.

Less likely, if the description is exact:

- Animal bites.
- Insect-borne disease.
- Parasites requiring animal hosts.
- Venomous stings.
- Large predator encounters.

However, do not assume the lack of animals means no microscopic danger. Your immune system may not recognize local organisms.

## Hygiene and waste

This is extremely important. In a hydraulic town, careless waste is not merely dirty; it is socially and ecologically dangerous.

Do not use soap near channels or pools unless given local soap and instructions. Biodegradable soap may still be inappropriate. Do not urinate into water channels. Do not bury waste in saturated soil unless instructed; it may leach immediately. Use designated latrines, composting areas, reed-bed treatment zones, or whatever system locals maintain.

Bring:

- Sealable waste bags.
- Unscented wipes, used sparingly and packed out unless disposal is provided.
- Hand sanitizer.
- A small personal washing cloth.

Scented products may be overwhelming in a place with so few animal odors and little smoke. Use unscented hygiene products.

## Cultural approach

The people here, if present, are likely to value patience, maintenance, water literacy, and respect for quiet. Their wealth may not be coin, livestock, or metal. Wealth may be reeds, dry storage, clean cisterns, stable terraces, fertile mounds, functioning sluices, good roofwork, ceramic vessels, seed stocks, charcoal, and knowledge of water.

Behaviors likely to be respected:

- Asking before stepping off paths.
- Waiting quietly.
- Watching how others move.
- Offering help with carrying, clearing, or sorting only after instruction.
- Keeping tools clean.
- Not wasting dry space.
- Not wasting roofed space.
- Treating reeds as valuable material.
- Treating water as categorized and managed.
- Speaking softly.
- Repairing any damage you cause.

Behaviors likely to be disliked:

- Splashing.
- Shouting.
- Blocking paths.
- Moving stones.
- Touching sluices.
- Taking plants.
- Contaminating water.
- Laughing at low doors or simple tools.
- Displaying animal products.
- Treating the town as primitive because it lacks metal.
- Walking through gardens or reed beds.
- Assuming open space is unused space.

This town may look sparse to someone from a dense city, but its emptiness is functional. Space is drainage, airflow, maintenance access, flood tolerance, and social breathing room.

## If you are arriving from the lower world

The plateau stands above haze and may have steep young edges. The approach could be dangerous. Expect:

- Wet cliff paths.
- Sudden drops softened by mist.
- Water running down rock faces.
- Terraces without guardrails.
- Newly broken stone.
- Poor visibility near edges.
- Slippery ascents.

Do not approach the plateau edge casually. The land may not “feel exposed,” but the drop exists. Moisture and haze can hide depth. Fine water threads on cliff faces may make stone treacherous.

If climbing up from below, bring proper rope and climbing support unless there is a maintained path. If descending, do not trust informal runoff gullies as routes.

## If you work with locals

Common tasks might include:

- Clearing leaves from channels.
- Carrying reeds.
- Skimming cistern lips.
- Repairing low walls.
- Moving stones.
- Cutting moss from steps.
- Lining beds with clay.
- Turning drying plants.
- Carrying baskets along narrow paths.
- Sorting roots or leaves.

These tasks may look simple but require local knowledge. A misplaced stone can change water flow. Reeds may need to be cut at specific heights. Some moss may be removed from paths but preserved on walls. Some standing water may be useful, not neglected.

Ask: “Where should the water go?” before doing anything physical.

## Fire and cooking

Because the air is damp, fire may be precious and difficult. Fuel may be plant-based: dried reeds, bark, charcoal, or carefully stored wood. Dry fuel will be valuable.

Bring a compact stove only if its fuel is safe and culturally acceptable. Do not assume you can gather fuel. Dead plant matter may be compost, soil-in-making, roof material, or water-filter material.

If using any flame:

- Keep it on stone.
- Keep it away from reed walls and roofs.
- Ventilate smoke.
- Ask permission.
- Use minimal fuel.
- Extinguish completely.

Smoke may carry poorly but linger in damp air.

## Ecological caution

This world’s ecology is profoundly vulnerable in ways that may not be obvious. With no animals:

- Plants may depend heavily on water flow and human cultivation.
- Seeds may not be adapted to dispersal by birds or mammals.
- Flowers may not expect pollinators.
- There may be no grazing pressure.
- Nutrient cycling may be slow and fungal.
- Waste decomposition may differ from what you expect.
- Introduced insects or microbes could be catastrophic.

Before arrival, decontaminate gear.

Clean:

- Boot soles.
- Tent stakes.
- Walking staff tip.
- Pack bottom.
- Clothing cuffs.
- Tools.
- Food containers.

Remove seeds, burrs, soil, eggs, larvae, and organic residue from your own world. Do not be the person who introduces ants, mosquitoes, beetles, rats, mites, mold, crop blight, or invasive weeds into a world that has no ecological defenses against them.

This is one of the most important preparations.

## Best attitude to bring

Bring patience.

This is not a place that rewards force, speed, or conquest. Its intelligence is in adjustment: water slowed here, led there, held elsewhere; buildings separated so damp air can pass; roots raised above standing water; stone laid where feet must go; channels cut before walls.

The right traveler becomes quiet, observant, and careful. You should be willing to wait under a roof while rain makes decisions. You should be willing to walk the long causeway instead of cutting across a tempting flat. You should be willing to ask why a wall is low, why a pool is untouched, why a reed bed is edged in stone, why one roof drips into a basin and another into a covered jar.

If you listen, the town will teach you its logic.

## A good arrival routine

When you first arrive:

1. Stop at the edge of the settlement rather than walking straight in.
2. Clean visible mud from your footwear if a cleaning place exists.
3. Cover or stow strange animal-derived items.
4. Lower your voice.
5. Watch how locals use paths and thresholds.
6. Ask where visitors may walk.
7. Ask which water is safe to drink.
8. Ask where waste should go.
9. Ask before photographing, recording, or mapping, if you use such devices.
10. Accept that some open areas are not empty but functional.

If welcomed, offer a small, clean, non-animal gift. Something practical and dry may be appreciated more than something decorative.

## In short

Prepare for warmth, wetness, quiet, slick stone, strict water etiquette, and a culture shaped by hydraulic balance. Protect your feet, your lungs, your gear, and the local water. Bring waterproofing, antifungal care, non-animal materials, sealed food, purified drinking water, and a patient mind.

Do not hurry. Do not splash. Do not move stones. Do not trust dry-looking ground. Do not contaminate a channel. Do not mistake silence for emptiness.

This is a hand-built marsh city on new mountain bones. Visit it as you would enter a house where the floor is also a river, the walls are also weather, and every path is a promise that people have made with water.
