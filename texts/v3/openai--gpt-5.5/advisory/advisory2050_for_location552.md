## Preparation for Visiting the Dry Forest-City of the High Plateau

An ordinary human should treat this place less like a desert city and more like a cold, high-altitude, electrically active biosphere that happens to be dry at every scale. The greatest danger is not simply thirst. It is that nearly every assumption a water-bodied traveler carries — about decay, scent, weather, plants, roads, shelter, and silence — will be wrong.

You will be a leaking animal in a world that has forgotten liquid.

Prepare accordingly.

---

## 1. Wear a sealed environmental suit

Do not visit in ordinary expedition clothing.

You need a suit designed for:

- **Extreme desiccation**
- **High altitude / low pressure**
- **Cold**
- **Fine mineral dust and pollen**
- **Static discharge**
- **Biological contamination control**
- **Internal water conservation**

The suit should be soft, flexible, and quiet, but fully sealed. It should reclaim sweat, breath moisture, and urine. Your body will constantly try to lose water through skin and lungs; the environment will take it greedily.

A good suit should include:

- Closed-cycle hydration recycling
- Breath humidifier and moisture reclamation
- Fine particulate filtration
- Anti-static outer layer
- Non-sparking fittings
- Thermal regulation
- Eye protection against dust, dim glare, and bio-luminescent flashes
- Emergency pressure hood in case of suit breach
- Smooth exterior surfaces, so hooks, tendrils, and burrs cannot easily catch you

Avoid dangling straps, loose fabric, decorative cords, or exposed metal tools. The city is full of living fibers, hooks, sensing hairs, conductive spines, and moving rootways. Anything loose may be interpreted as food, signal, threat, or trellis.

---

## 2. Do not expose liquid water

This is extremely important.

You may need water to survive, but the city does not. Liquid water may be chemically or biologically disruptive. To local life, it may act like solvent, poison, explosive nutrient, sacrament, or weapon depending on where it falls.

Never:

- Pour water onto the ground
- Open a canteen outdoors
- Spit
- Urinate outside your suit system
- Bleed openly if injured
- Use water-based cleaning sprays
- Bring leaking food packs
- Carry unsealed gels or fluids

If your suit is punctured and fluid leaks, treat it as an environmental incident, not merely a personal emergency. Move off rootways if possible, deploy absorbent containment patches, and notify local guides or civic organisms immediately.

Your own blood, sweat, saliva, and mucus may be more biologically shocking here than fire would be in a wet forest.

---

## 3. Expect altitude sickness and dry-lung stress

The plateau is high, cold, and thin-aired. Even if oxygen exists in breathable proportions, the pressure may not be comfortable for ordinary humans.

Prepare with:

- Acclimatization time before arrival, if possible
- Supplemental oxygen or pressure-assist breathing
- Medication for altitude sickness
- Pulse oximeter or equivalent biomonitor
- Slow movement during the first day
- Strict hydration monitoring inside your suit

Symptoms to watch for:

- Headache
- Confusion
- Euphoria
- Nausea
- Poor coordination
- Breathlessness
- Chest tightness
- Nosebleeds or throat cracking despite suit humidity

Do not mistake awe for acclimatization. The dim light, huge distances, and singing architecture can make you feel dreamlike. That may be wonder, but it may also be hypoxia.

---

## 4. Bring power, not fuel

Combustion is a bad idea. Open flame may be culturally offensive, chemically hazardous, or simply useless in the thin charged air. Many local systems are electrostatic, mineral, and biological; flame is not part of their normal metabolism.

Bring:

- Shielded batteries
- Inductive charging adapters
- Static-safe power cells
- Low-emission devices
- Mechanical backups

Do not bring:

- Open-flame stoves
- Smoking materials
- Spark-based tools
- Volatile solvents
- Aerosol sprays
- Unshielded high-voltage equipment

A careless discharge during a charge bloom could get your voice, your nervous system, or your equipment written into a municipal pollen event.

---

## 5. Protect your electronics from the city

The city is alive with charge, signal, vibration, and conductive plant matter. Ordinary electronics may behave unpredictably.

Risks include:

- Static overload
- Mineral dust infiltration
- Biofilament growth into ports
- Pollen-sized devices entering vents
- Local computation groves interacting with neural or wireless interfaces
- Accidental participation in civic communication systems

Use:

- Faraday-lined cases
- Dustproof seals
- Physical switches instead of touch-only controls
- Offline maps
- Shielded data storage
- Biological firewall protocols if your devices interface with living systems

Disable unnecessary wireless transmission. Do not let your translator, comms unit, or implants automatically answer unknown signal-flowers, pollen networks, or vibration pulses.

Here, a “network request” may arrive as scent, light, static, or touch.

---

## 6. Hire a local guide or interpreter

This is not optional unless you have prior experience with dry electro-botanical civilizations.

You need someone who understands:

- Which rootways are safe to cross
- Which districts are molting, dreaming, breeding, grieving, or measuring
- How to read signal-flowers
- Which scents are public announcements
- When a bridge is tightening for transit versus preparing to shed travelers
- How to behave during charge blooms
- Which organisms are civic infrastructure
- Which organisms are private, sacred, predatory, or dead

A map alone is insufficient because roads are agreements among living roots. They may open, harden, soften, or erase themselves depending on season, traffic, law, charge, or mood.

Ask your guide before touching anything. In this city, a wall may be a legal person, a memory archive, a digestive surface, a weather instrument, or someone’s ancestor.

---

## 7. Learn the basic taboos before arrival

At minimum, memorize these:

### Do not spill solvent on the rootways

This includes water, alcohol, fuel, cleaning chemicals, biological fluids, and unknown offworld liquids.

### Do not speak during a charge bloom

Voices may be recorded, amplified, interpreted as consent, or incorporated into pollination algorithms. If the air begins to hiss, blue light crawls along spines, and towers start to hum, be silent.

### Do not cut white growth

White growth belongs to dead districts. It may be funerary, archival, quarantined, or legally protected. Cutting it may be equivalent to desecrating graves.

### If a tower turns toward you, stand still

It is measuring your shadow, heat, charge, probability profile, or civic relevance. Sudden movement may trigger defensive or diagnostic behavior.

### Never follow an animal that walks against the wind

Local animals understand paths, predators, and invisible atmospheric currents better than you do. One walking against the wind may be sick, luring prey, obeying a nonhuman bargain, or moving through a route unsafe for humans.

---

## 8. Prepare for sound

This city is loud in a dry way.

There will be:

- Wind rasping through plant plates
- Hollow trunks booming
- Root cables tightening beneath streets
- Seed capsules cracking
- Transit vines groaning
- Insect colonies clicking
- Towers humming during charge events
- Full-plateau harmonic singing

Bring hearing protection that still allows communication. Bone-conduction systems may work, but test them carefully; the city itself transmits through structure, and you may receive more than intended.

Some sounds may be announcements. Others may be animal warnings. Others may be stress signals from infrastructure. Do not assume that “background noise” is meaningless.

If you feel bass vibration in your teeth or chest, step off major root cables unless your guide says otherwise. Something large may be moving, tightening, or waking.

---

## 9. Use careful movement

The ground is not dead ground.

Root-bridges respond to weight. Living carpets change color behind you. Streets may harden when traveled and soften when abandoned. Doorways may be stomata. Courtyards may narrow like throats.

Walk:

- Slowly
- With even pressure
- In your guide’s footsteps where possible
- Away from exposed conductive spines
- Around fresh cracks, pale dust vents, or breathing hexagons
- Without dragging equipment

Do not jump on rootways. Do not stamp. Do not test plant surfaces with blades. Do not pry at fibers.

If the surface under you begins tightening rhythmically, changing color rapidly, warming, humming, or exhaling dust, stop and wait for guidance.

---

## 10. Expect storms without rain

Storms here bring charge, pressure shifts, mineral dust, seeds, spores, and sound. They do not bring water.

During a storm:

- Seal all suit ports
- Cover optics and filters when possible
- Avoid tall exposed areas
- Stay away from conductive spines unless instructed
- Do not speak during charge blooms
- Do not use unshielded electronics
- Do not run unless directly ordered
- Shelter in designated cavities or arcades, not random hollows

The “dry snow” of seeds and mineral scales may be beautiful, but do not collect it casually. It may contain engineered motes, reproductive cells, civic messages, dormant computation, or legal instruments.

After a storm, clean your suit thoroughly in a controlled environment. Do not bring adhered pollen into sleeping quarters or offworld craft without quarantine.

---

## 11. Protect against pollen, spores, and engineered motes

Even though the world is dry, it is biologically rich. The air may carry countless particles.

Potential effects on humans include:

- Allergic reaction
- Respiratory inflammation
- Skin irritation
- Static adhesion to suit surfaces
- Contamination of equipment
- Bioelectrical interference
- Unwanted data exchange through smart pollen or engineered motes

Bring:

- High-grade particulate filters
- Spare filter cartridges
- External suit cleaning system
- Antihistamines
- Steroids or emergency anti-allergy treatment
- Biohazard sample containers
- Quarantine bags

Do not smell unknown flowers directly. Many public announcements are scent-based, but that does not mean the scents are safe for human lungs. Use a translator or chemical interpreter rather than your bare nose.

---

## 12. Food and waste discipline

Bring food that is:

- Dry
- Crumb-contained
- Non-aromatic
- Sealed
- Eaten only in approved human-safe spaces

Avoid:

- Juicy fruit
- Soups
- sauces
- Fermenting foods
- Anything that leaks oil
- Anything with strong volatile solvents or alcohols

All waste must be sealed. Human waste may be chemically attractive, dangerous, illegal, or biologically disruptive.

Do not assume organic scraps can be tossed aside “for nature.” Nature here is municipal, ancient, and attentive.

---

## 13. Navigation will be strange

Distance perception is unreliable. The dim sky and open plateau make scale deceptive. A grove may be nearby, or it may be a tower district many kilometers away.

Bring multiple navigation methods:

- Inertial locator
- Local map seed or approved route token
- Beacon compatible with local guides
- Visual landmarks
- Emergency return path
- Compass only if local magnetic conditions are trustworthy, which they may not be

Remember: roads may disappear if unused. A route that existed yesterday may be soft, dreaming, molting, or legally closed today.

Do not wander into low ancient mats without permission. Those living carpets may be kilometers-wide organisms. Your footsteps may be interpreted as message, injury, or trespass.

---

## 14. Understand local “technology” as life

Do not look for machines. The transit system may be a tendril. A computer may be a grove of black needles. A memory archive may be a seed vault. A public notice may be a flower. A sensor may be a tower turning toward you.

This means your normal instincts about technology will fail.

Do not:

- Open panels
- Pull fibers
- Cut “wires”
- Remove seed pods
- Lean on signal spires
- Insert probes into bark ports
- Harvest samples from civic organisms
- Assume inactive things are dead

If you need to interface with local systems, do so through approved translators or local caretakers — the people who prune algorithms, graft logic, and listen to stress songs.

---

## 15. Cultural conduct

The inhabitants appear to live in intimate partnership with the city’s organisms. Their civic, architectural, ecological, and ancestral systems may be inseparable.

Be polite in ways that respect living infrastructure:

- Ask before touching walls, flowers, rootways, or transit vines
- Step where directed
- Remain silent during charge events
- Do not complain loudly about dryness; that is the condition of life here
- Do not call the city a ruin
- Do not call plants “dead” because they are not green
- Do not compare everything to deserts or forests from wet worlds unless invited
- Treat civic organisms as public institutions, not scenery

If public announcements are made through scent, vibration, or light, wait for your interpreter. Do not ignore them because you cannot understand them. A pale signal-flower blooming across an avenue may mean “festival,” “bridge open,” “predator transit,” or “district shedding hazardous memory pollen.”

---

## 16. Animal safety

Fauna may be concealed within the architecture. Predators may use elevated rootways. Grazers may scrape mineral films from walls. Arthropods may maintain transit joints.

Guidelines:

- Do not feed animals
- Do not follow animals
- Do not approach cloak-sized moths on signal towers
- Do not step into designated shelter cavities during storms unless your guide says they are safe for humans
- Avoid animals moving unusually still or against wind
- Watch wall color changes; they may reveal predator movement
- Keep limbs close when transit vines flex
- Never place hands into joints, hollows, or breathing cracks

Predation may leave little blood or sound. A resin smell, powder burst, or sudden electrical tang may be the local equivalent of gore.

## 17. Medical preparations

Bring a medical kit suited for:

- Dehydration
- Altitude sickness
- Hypothermia
- Dust inhalation
- Allergic reaction
- Static burns
- Electrical shock
- Skin cracking
- Eye abrasion
- Suit-pressure injury
- Panic response to acoustic/vibrational overload

Important additions:

- Emergency rehydration system compatible with sealed suit use
- Blood/fluid containment patches
- Moisture-preserving wound dressings
- Non-liquid antiseptic options
- Anti-static burn gel sealed for suit-internal use
- Respiratory anti-inflammatory medication
- Sedatives only if safe at altitude

Any injury that leaks fluid into the environment should be treated as both medical and ecological.

---

## 18. Psychological preparation

This place may feel uncanny because it contradicts human expectations.

Plants without water. A forest without green. Weather without rain. A city that is not built but grown. Roads that respond. Towers that measure you. Public speech made of scent and light. Night brighter than day. A landscape loud enough to feel inside your bones.

You may experience:

- Scale disorientation
- Thirst anxiety
- Claustrophobia inside sealed suits
- Unease at responsive architecture
- Fear during plateau-wide singing
- Grief or awe in dead districts
- Difficulty sleeping due to vibration and sound

Prepare mentally. Practice sealed-suit living before arrival. Learn to remain still when startled. Learn not to touch beautiful things.

Most of all, do not expect barrenness. The city is not empty. It is paying attention.

---

## 19. Emergency behavior

If separated from your guide:

1. Stop moving.
2. Do not leave the established rootway.
3. Activate a low-power emergency beacon approved for local use.
4. Remain silent if a charge bloom begins.
5. Avoid white growth, breathing cracks, and animal trails.
6. Do not enter unknown hollows.
7. Conserve internal water and suit power.
8. Wait for retrieval.

If caught in a charge bloom:

1. Stand still or crouch low if instructed by local signage.
2. Do not speak.
3. Disable unnecessary transmissions.
4. Keep hands away from conductive spines and walls.
5. Let the event pass.
6. Afterward, check suit memory, comm logs, and biometrics for unusual recordings or interference.

If the tower turns toward you:

1. Stop.
2. Face no sudden direction unless your guide instructs.
3. Let it finish.
4. Do not wave, photograph with flash, transmit scans, or step into its shadow-line.
5. Wait for local interpretation.

## Final advice

Arrive humbly.

This is not a dead plateau that life has colonized. It is a vast, old, dry organism that has learned cityhood. Its streets are tissues. Its towers are memories. Its storms are reproductive and civic events. Its laws may be older than your species.

You can survive there if you remain sealed, slow, quiet, and observant.

Carry your water like a secret.

Do not wound the rootways.

When the plateau sings, listen — but do not answer unless invited.
