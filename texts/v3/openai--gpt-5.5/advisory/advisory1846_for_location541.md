To visit this city safely, prepare as if you are going to a high-altitude archaeological sanctuary, an alpine monastery, and a place under a subtle law of preservation all at once.

The danger is not obvious violence. The danger is alteration — yours upon the city, and the city’s upon you.

## 1. Physical preparation

### Altitude

The city stands high enough that clouds move through the streets. Assume significant elevation.

Before arrival, prepare for:

- shortness of breath
- headaches
- dizziness
- poor sleep
- slowed thinking
- nausea
- cold-induced fatigue

If you can choose your entry point, do not arrive directly in the central plaza or upper terraces. Arrive at a lower approach, spend time acclimatizing, and climb gradually. An ordinary human should avoid strenuous ascent on the first day.

Bring:

- altitude medication if you tolerate it
- electrolyte salts
- high-calorie food
- warm layers
- a pulse oximeter if available
- a compact emergency oxygen source if your travel method permits

Move slowly. The city itself seems to favor slow movement. Do not fight that.

### Cold and damp

The air is cool, thin, and persistently moist. Nothing is soaked, but everything holds condensation. This is the kind of place where you may not notice you are getting chilled until your hands stop working properly.

Wear:

- wool or synthetic base layers
- an insulating mid-layer
- a wind-resistant outer shell
- waterproof but breathable boots
- gloves with grip
- a warm hat
- spare socks sealed in a dry bag

Avoid cotton. It will become clammy and stay that way.

The stone steps, bridges, and mossed pavements may be slick even when they look dry. Bring a walking staff or trekking poles with rubber tips. Metal spike tips may damage stone and should be avoided unless absolutely necessary.

### Terrain

Expect:

- steep stairways
- exposed ledges
- narrow bridges
- ravines with abrupt drops
- polished basalt
- loose pale gravel
- moss-covered steps
- old timber crossings that may or may not be maintained by visible hands

Do not trust visual stillness as structural safety. A bridge may have been preserved in exactly the state between soundness and failure.

Test every old wooden surface before committing your weight. Cross one person at a time. Avoid jumping, running, or leaning on ancient railings.

Bring:

- a compact climbing harness and safety line if you plan to cross ravines or explore upper districts
- a headlamp, though deep darkness may be rare
- chalk or removable fiber markers for navigation, not paint or carvings
- soft-soled footwear with excellent traction

## 2. Supplies to bring

Even if the city appears to offer food and water, do not rely on it.

Bring your own:

- drinking water
- compact water filter
- purification tablets
- high-calorie trail food
- thermos with warm drink
- small stove only if flame use is permitted and safe
- medical kit
- emergency bivy sack
- repair cord
- knife or multitool
- noninvasive notebook or recording device
- offerings, if culturally appropriate: clean water, salt, uncarved wood, thread, or small smooth stones

Do not bring loud machines if avoidable. Do not bring drones unless absolutely necessary. The city’s order seems built around restraint, quiet, and proportion. A buzzing machine over the plaza would be more than rude; it may be interpreted by the place itself as disturbance.

If you bring modern technology, expect unreliable behavior around the central pool or in districts caught most deeply in the “pause.” Clocks may drift. Batteries may discharge strangely or remain at one percentage too long. Audio recordings may capture too much silence.

## 3. Behavioral rules

The most important preparation is not equipment. It is discipline.

This city tolerates presence, but perhaps not disruption.

Follow these rules:

1. **Take nothing.**
2. **Do not move household objects.**
3. **Do not eat food laid out in homes or courtyards.**
4. **Do not drink from the central pool.**
5. **Do not wash in cisterns or channels.**
6. **Do not prune, pick, harvest, or collect plants.**
7. **Do not feed animals.**
8. **Do not shout unless in danger.**
9. **Do not start fires in communal ovens unless invited.**
10. **Do not complete unfinished work.**

That last rule matters. Do not finish the half-shaped bowl. Do not mend the net. Do not move the needle. Do not knock the suspended drop from the spout. Do not disturb the held curtain. Do not “help” the city continue.

It is not waiting for you in that way.

## 4. Etiquette toward possible inhabitants

The people may still be there, but not in a form or rhythm you can easily perceive. They may be indoors, slowed, withdrawn, hidden, or preserved in the same suspended agreement that holds the rain, moss, smoke, and food.

Behave as if every house is occupied.

Before entering any building:

- stop at the threshold
- announce yourself quietly
- wait
- listen longer than feels necessary
- do not step over sandals or tools
- do not enter sleeping spaces
- do not open storage jars
- do not sit at a laid meal

If you must shelter indoors, choose a plainly public place: a market stall, open gallery, empty watch platform, or roofed passage. Leave no trace.

A suitable greeting might be simple:

> “I am a traveler. I come quietly. I will not take what is kept.”

Say it aloud once. Then do not keep repeating yourself. This is not a city that rewards insistence.

## 5. The central pool

Treat the central pool as sacred, dangerous, or both.

Everything in the city appears arranged around water held in perfect measure. The pool may be the regulator of the city’s equilibrium. It may be a physical reservoir, a ritual axis, a time-anchor, a covenant object, or the visible surface of something much deeper.

Approach only after you have spent time in the outer districts. Do not make the pool your first destination.

At the plaza:

- walk slowly
- avoid stepping in the channels
- do not throw anything into the water
- do not touch the surface
- do not take samples unless you are prepared for consequences
- do not speak loudly into it
- do not lean so far that your breath disturbs it

If the pool reflects something other than the sky, do not immediately interpret it as an invitation.

If the surface ripples when nothing else in the city moves, leave the plaza calmly.

If your own reflection lags, holds still after you move, or appears older, younger, absent, or accompanied, do not continue staring. Back away and return by the same route.

## 6. Time and memory precautions

The city is a held moment. That means time may not behave normally.

Prepare for these possibilities:

- watches and clocks disagreeing
- hunger arriving late or not at all
- fatigue accumulating suddenly
- conversations feeling improper or hard to sustain
- memories of your visit becoming unusually still, vivid, or incomplete
- a temptation to remain “just a little longer”
- difficulty deciding to leave

Before entering, establish an exit protocol.

For example:

- Set an external alarm with someone outside the city.
- Arrange a retrieval window.
- Carry a written note in your own hand:  
  **“I intended to leave after one day. If I feel no urgency, I should still leave.”**
- Mark your route with removable markers.
- Record your entry time in multiple ways.
- Travel with a companion if possible, but agree not to let each other wander silently away.

Do not nap inside the city unless necessary. Sleep may align you too deeply with the local pause.

If you must rest, do so outside the central districts, preferably near the gate-without-walls or an open terrace, not in a dwelling.

## 7. Psychological preparation

The city will feel calm, but not comforting in the usual way. It is quiet enough that your own body becomes loud: breath, pulse, fabric, boot-grit, swallowing.

Some travelers may experience:

- reluctance to speak
- guilt about movement
- intense awareness of small sounds
- the sense of being measured
- reverence without an obvious object
- grief, as if seeing a life interrupted
- envy of the city’s sufficiency
- fear of disturbing perfection

Prepare yourself to be quiet without becoming passive.

This place may seduce you not with pleasure, but with balance. It may make ordinary human restlessness feel vulgar. Remember: you are not part of its agreement. You are a guest.

A good mental practice before entering:

- breathe slowly
- commit to observing without correcting
- commit to leaving without needing answers
- accept that some mysteries should remain untested

## 8. Interaction with animals

The animals are quiet and abundant. They may be ordinary creatures adapted to the city, or they may be part of its regulating pattern.

Do not approach them closely. Do not imitate calls. Do not chase birds from lintels or steps. Do not touch the deer, marmots, lizards, moths, or ground birds even if they seem tame or motionless.

A still animal is not necessarily unafraid. It may simply be obeying the city’s rule: life without disturbance.

If an animal breaks pattern — for example, if a bird suddenly calls loudly, a deer blocks your path, or a fox follows you — treat it as meaningful. Not necessarily hostile, but meaningful.

Leave the area quietly.

## 9. Water discipline

Water is everywhere and nowhere: drops, channels, cisterns, runnels, fog nets, beads, stains. The city’s hydrology seems exact.

Do not interfere with it.

Avoid:

- stepping into channels
- redirecting runnels
- filling bottles from household basins
- wringing cloth into drains
- washing mud into carved gutters
- blocking spouts
- touching fog nets
- breaking ice or condensation formations

If you need water, use your own first. If absolutely necessary, take a very small amount from an outer, flowing source — not the central pool, not a household cistern, not a shrine basin. Leave an offering nearby, preferably clean water poured onto bare stone beside the source, not into it.

## 10. Fire and smoke

The city contains communal ovens and hearths, but smoke here may not disperse normally. It may hang in place for too long, becoming part of the preserved scene.

Avoid making fire unless survival requires it.

If you must use a stove:

- use a contained modern stove
- cook in an open, already disturbed area
- produce minimal smoke
- do not use local wood
- do not light old hearths for curiosity or symbolism

Never relight a hearth whose smoke is already suspended beneath the eaves.

That fire may not be finished.

## 11. Navigation

The city is spread across terraces separated by ravines, bridges, slopes, and open air. It is not dense, but it is complex.

Landmarks to use:

- central sunken plaza
- gate without walls
- shrine district with cairns and junipers
- granaries on stone piers
- quarry of fresh black rock
- bridge lines over ravines
- fog-net fields
- highest terraces
- cliff overlooks

Because sound is absorbed and visibility may be softened by mist, do not rely on calling out to companions.

Use:

- line-of-sight travel
- agreed meeting points
- chalk marks on loose stones, not on walls
- fiber tags tied to your own temporary stakes, not city structures
- compass and map, though magnetic reliability is uncertain near basaltic stone

Avoid exploring ravines alone. They are likely colder, slicker, and more temporally “held” than exposed terraces.

## 12. Clothing and appearance

If you wish to minimize offense, dress plainly.

Avoid:

- bright synthetic colors
- reflective clothing
- jingling gear
- strong perfumes
- dangling metal ornaments
- loud Velcro or plastic crackle

Choose muted colors: gray, brown, moss green, slate blue, off-white. Move like someone entering a working monastery, not a ruin.

Carry your pack compactly. Do not let straps brush against walls, tools, curtains, or hanging fibers.

## 13. What not to document

It is reasonable to take notes or sketches. Photography may be acceptable if done respectfully, but avoid turning the city into a specimen.

Do not photograph:

- interiors of dwellings without pausing at the threshold
- laid meals at close range
- possible inhabitants if seen
- sacred objects in cliff shrines unless permission is unmistakably given
- reflections in the central pool if they are anomalous

Sketching may be safer than photography. The city seems compatible with hand, memory, and repetition. A camera may feel too extractive.

## 14. If you encounter inhabitants

If you see a person:

- do not rush toward them
- do not shout
- do not touch them
- do not assume distress
- do not offer modern medicine unless they clearly request help
- do not attempt to “wake” anyone who appears paused

Stand still. Lower your hands. Speak softly.

Say:

> “I am a traveler. I will leave what is yours undisturbed.”

If they answer slowly, let the silence remain. Do not fill it.

If they gesture for you to follow, consider carefully. Following may mean entering a social obligation, ritual boundary, or slower layer of the city. If you go, leave a marker for your companion or your future self.

If they offer food, you face a serious decision. Eating local food may make you a guest in a binding sense. It may also align your body with the city’s equilibrium. If you need to remain free to leave, politely decline or touch the bowl without eating.

A respectful refusal:

> “Your keeping honors me. I am still on the road.”

## 15. Signs you should leave immediately

Depart if any of the following occur:

- your footprints remain wet and sharp long after you pass
- your breath hangs in the air and does not disperse
- animals begin arranging themselves around you
- a drop falls only when you look away
- a doorway you passed becomes unfamiliar on return
- your companion becomes unusually quiet and unwilling to leave
- your hunger, thirst, or need to sleep disappears completely
- your reflection in the central pool does not match you
- you find an object that appears to have been prepared specifically for your hand
- you hear ordinary city noise — voices, markets, tools — but cannot locate it
- you begin to feel that staying would be morally better than leaving

The last sign is especially important. The city may not trap by force. It may convince you that departure is a kind of damage.

Leave anyway.

## 16. Emergency strategy

If injured:

- move to an open terrace or market square
- avoid entering private houses
- keep warm immediately
- signal visually, not with prolonged shouting
- use your own medical supplies
- do not rely on local food, water, or bedding unless necessary

If lost:

- descend carefully toward larger terraces
- follow major channels outward, not inward, unless you are trying to reach the central plaza
- look for the gate without walls or the cliffside granary district
- avoid chasing sounds

If the city becomes too still around you:

- make small, deliberate movements
- speak your name aloud
- recite where you came from
- eat something you brought with you
- drink your own water
- touch an object from outside the city
- begin walking toward the exit without stopping to interpret signs

Carrying a personal object from home is wise: a coin, ring, cloth strip, photograph, or written note. It can remind you that other kinds of time exist.

## 17. Best way to visit

The best visit is short, quiet, and reversible.

A suggested itinerary:

1. Arrive at the outer terraces.
2. Spend time acclimatizing.
3. Observe water channels and public spaces without touching them.
4. Visit one open market or communal court.
5. Walk to a shrine exterior, leaving a small nonintrusive offering if appropriate.
6. Approach the central plaza only after you understand the city’s silence.
7. Do not linger at the pool.
8. Climb to the gate without walls.
9. Look over the clouded valley.
10. Leave before you feel ready.

That last point matters. You should leave while the city still feels unfinished to you. If it begins to feel complete with you inside it, you have stayed too long.

## 18. What to bring as an offering

If you choose to bring an offering, select something humble and non-disruptive.

Good offerings:

- a small smooth stone from a river below
- a length of undyed thread
- a pinch of clean salt in a clay cup
- a little water poured onto bare stone outside a shrine
- a hand-carved wooden token
- a feather found naturally, not plucked
- a quiet song sung once at the threshold, if song feels permitted

Bad offerings:

- coins of bright metal
- plastic objects
- food that may rot
- burning incense with strong smoke
- anything mechanical
- anything that demands attention
- anything you expect to retrieve later

The offering should not improve the city. It should simply acknowledge it.

## 19. Overall attitude

Do not go as a conqueror, researcher, tourist, rescuer, pilgrim, or thief.

Go as a temporary imbalance that knows it is temporary.

The city is not dead, not abandoned, and not waiting to be solved. Its great law appears to be sufficiency: enough water, enough growth, enough life, enough silence, enough time.

Your preparation should therefore center on one promise:

**I will not require the city to become more alive for my sake.**

Walk softly. Touch little. Listen more than you speak. Do not complete unfinished things. Do not test the pool. Do not mistake stillness for permission.

And when the time comes to leave, leave cleanly. Do not look back too long from the gate. The city is very good at holding moments, and you do not want to become one of them.
