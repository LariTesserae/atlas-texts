An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare as if visiting three places at once: a high-altitude garden, a wet industrial plant, and a very young ecosystem that has not yet decided what rules it will keep.

It is beautiful, but not benign. Its dangers are mostly quiet ones: slick footing, warm pipes, sudden mist, unmarked machinery, fragile plant systems, mineralized water, and the false sense that because the place is green it is “natural” and therefore forgiving. It is not. It is a living installation built on top of a hot machine.

## Physical preparation

Expect **warmth, wetness, and height**.

The summit room is roofless, cloud-pierced, and humid. You may be standing in sunlight one minute, inside a passing cloud the next, then under a scheduled artificial rain. The floor will likely be uneven, slick, and interrupted by grates, capillary channels, pipes, basins, plant roots, and raised seams. Do not assume any surface is dry, stable, or meant for walking unless it is clearly a path.

You should be reasonably comfortable with:

- Walking on wet stone-like surfaces.
- Climbing short industrial stairs or ladders.
- Being near drop-offs, parapets, grates, and exposed machinery.
- Breathing warm damp air.
- Having your visibility reduced by mist.
- Hearing mechanical noises without knowing their source.

If you have severe vertigo, asthma triggered by humidity or spores, heat intolerance, mold sensitivity, or poor balance, visit only with a guide and keep the visit short.

## Clothing

Wear clothing for a **humid greenhouse built out of metal**.

Recommended:

- **Water-resistant outer layer**, light enough for warmth but able to shed mist and spray.
- **Breathable inner layers**, because you may sweat under the humidity.
- **Long sleeves and long trousers**, to protect against hot surfaces, rough ceramic edges, mineral residues, insects, and plant irritants.
- **Non-slip boots** with good tread. This is important. Smooth soles are dangerous here.
- **Thin work gloves** or grip gloves. Railings may be wet, hot, slick with algae, or slightly corroded.
- **A brimmed cap or hood** to keep dripping condensation out of your eyes.
- **Eye protection** if you will be near maintenance zones, pressure vents, or overhead gutters.

Avoid:

- Sandals.
- Long loose scarves.
- Dangling jewelry.
- Wide sleeves.
- Anything that could catch on vines, valve wheels, exposed bolts, guide wires, or grates.

Do not wear delicate fabrics. The place smells wonderful, but it will stain you with mineral water, algae, rust, and plant sap.

## Footwear is especially important

The floor looks like basalt, but it is manufactured slag and ceramic, crossed by channels and shallow basins. Moss may conceal edges, screws, drainage throats, or gaps. Some grates breathe warm vapor upward and may become slick with condensation.

Choose boots with:

- Soft, grippy soles.
- Ankle support.
- Waterproofing.
- No deep mud-collecting cleats if biosecurity rules are strict.

Before stepping, look. Before leaning, test. Before crossing a grate, check whether vapor is rising through it and whether the metal looks corroded.

## What to bring

Bring only what you can keep close to your body. This is not a good place for dangling packs or loose cases.

Useful items:

- **Waterproof field notebook** or sealed note device.
- **Small headlamp** or hand light, even in daytime; mist and foliage can darken corners.
- **Respirator or mask**, preferably rated for spores, damp particulates, and industrial aerosols.
- **Protective gloves**.
- **A small towel or absorbent cloth**.
- **Water bottle**, sealed. Do not drink from the pools, condensate, pipes, or “clean-looking” runnels.
- **Electronics in waterproof cases**.
- **Portable gas/air-quality sensor**, if available.
- **Basic first-aid kit**, including burn gel, antiseptic wipes, blister care, and allergy medication.
- **Tether or wrist strap for important objects** if you will be near parapets or grates.

Optional but wise:

- Lightweight rain poncho.
- Binoculars for viewing the surrounding machinery without approaching unsafe edges.
- A walking staff with a rubber foot, though check whether staffs are allowed; they can damage moss mats or catch in grates.

Do not bring open food unless explicitly permitted. Crumbs, sugars, and seeds may interfere with a young controlled ecology.

## Health precautions

The air may contain:

- Spores.
- Mineral mist.
- Industrial vapor.
- Fermentation byproducts.
- Ozone from relay boxes.
- Warm condensate aerosols.
- Mold or fungal fragments.
- Unknown plant volatiles.

Most healthy visitors may experience only damp skin, a metallic taste, and a sense of pressure in the lungs. Sensitive visitors should wear a mask and limit exposure.

Before visiting, consider:

- Tetanus vaccination status.
- Allergies to mold, orchids, ferns, mosses, or industrial chemicals.
- Sensitivity to warm humid environments.
- Any respiratory condition.

After visiting:

- Wash hands and exposed skin.
- Clean boots.
- Change clothes if you were heavily misted.
- Inspect for minor cuts, rashes, or heat contact marks.
- Do not carry plant fragments away unless authorized.

## Biosecurity

This place is young and managed. Its ecology is still “under instruction.” A traveler should avoid introducing anything.

Before entry:

- Clean mud, seeds, spores, and organic debris from footwear.
- Brush down clothing.
- Do not bring outside plants, soil, untreated wood, feathers, insects, or food waste.
- If there is an air shower, boot bath, ultraviolet arch, or chemical mat, use it without complaint.

Inside:

- Do not pick leaves.
- Do not touch fungal fans.
- Do not move tagged snails.
- Do not remove lacquer markers, moisture flags, plaques, plant tags, or loose-looking rods.
- Do not pour anything into pools or channels.
- Do not “help” by clearing blocked channels unless instructed.
- Do not rescue vines from pipes or pipes from vines.

A root in the wrong place may be a maintenance crisis. A moss patch may be performing drainage. A tray of decay may be allowed decay; decay outside a tray may be a problem. Let the caretakers decide.

## Industrial hazards

The machinery is not scenery. It is active.

Likely hazards include:

- Hot pipes.
- Sudden steam release.
- Wet electrical fixtures.
- Slippery grates.
- Sharp ceramic edges.
- Moving pumps or hidden valves.
- Pressure changes before artificial rain.
- Corroded railings.
- Condensate dripping from overhead structures.
- Unexpected relay snaps or mechanical pulses.

Do not touch valves, wheels, switches, gauges, or colored bands. Especially do not touch the red wheel marked **EMERGENCY DRAIN** unless you have been explicitly told to do so in an emergency. It may drain basins, flood lower cisterns, expose hot surfaces, strand organisms, or trigger alarms.

If you hear a change in mechanical rhythm — a pump accelerating, a hiss, a pressure knock, a repeated relay snap — move calmly toward the marked path or exit and wait for instruction.

If artificial rain begins:

1. Stop moving if footing is uncertain.
2. Step away from grates, open panels, and electrical boxes.
3. Do not shelter under vines attached to pipes or catwalks.
4. Let the first pressure shift pass.
5. Resume only when visibility and footing are safe.

## Environmental conditions

The experience will be intensely sensory.

You should expect:

- Warm damp air pressing against your face.
- Mist beading on eyelashes, hair, and clothing.
- Smells of wet metal, hot ceramic, fern, mineral broth, flowers, ozone, and fermentation.
- A faint sweetness from vents below.
- Water sounds everywhere: tapping, ticking, dripping, soft rain, pool plinks.
- Low vibrations through the floor.
- Sudden small mechanical noises: clicks, snaps, pulses.
- Clouds moving slowly through the room, close enough to touch.
- Sunlight broken into square-edged patches by scaffolds and rails.
- Visibility reduced by layers of leaves, vapor, pipes, and hanging roots.

Prepare mentally for the fact that the place may feel larger than it is. The density of foliage and mist can disorient you. Keep track of landmarks: west grate, east parapet, hot pipe-bank, hanging cisterns, round pool, irregular pool, condensation wall.

## Movement and conduct inside

Move slowly.

The correct pace is not hiking pace; it is maintenance-worker pace. Deliberate, observant, unhurried.

Good habits:

- Keep one hand free.
- Watch both your feet and your head.
- Stay on marked or obvious walking surfaces.
- Assume moss hides an edge.
- Assume pipes may be hot.
- Assume shallow water has machinery beneath it.
- Let mist clear before crossing narrow spots.
- Do not back up without looking.
- Do not lean on parapets, rails, vine guides, cistern supports, or pipework.
- Give hanging roots and vines space; they may be part of water routing.
- Step over channels, not into them.

If the path has narrowed because plants have grown into it, do not force your way through. Wait, ask, or turn back. The garden is actively renegotiating its boundaries; a visitor should not settle the argument by breaking stems.

## Altitude and exposure

Although the garden is warm, it is still at the top of a manufactured mountain. Weather can shift quickly. The open sky means:

- Strong sun through mist.
- Rapid cooling if machinery vents change.
- Wind gusts through parapet gaps.
- Sudden cloud immersion.
- Reduced visibility.
- Lightning risk if storms occur.

Ask whether the summit is safe in electrical weather. Metal pylons, wet railings, pipes, and open height are a bad combination during storms.

Use sun protection even if it feels cloudy. Mist and pale industrial surfaces can scatter light.

## Social and institutional etiquette

Someone built this place. Someone maintains it. Someone probably loves it, worries over it, and keeps it alive by constant adjustment.

Treat the site with the respect you would give to:

- A laboratory.
- A greenhouse.
- A shrine.
- A power station.
- A premature landscape.

Ask before photographing gauges, plaques, control systems, or lower industrial views. Some installations may be proprietary, sacred, militarized, or politically sensitive. The fact that a brass plaque is corroding does not mean the place is abandoned.

If accompanied by a caretaker, listen carefully. Their rules may sound peculiar because the place’s rules are peculiar:

- Nothing may dry completely.
- No root may enter an unlined conduit.
- Decay is permitted only in trays.
- Mist is harvested before rain is requested.
- Heat must never be vented all at once.

These are not poetic sayings. They are operating principles.

## Emotional preparation

The summit room may produce an unusual feeling: not awe in the wilderness sense, but tenderness mixed with unease.

It is not untouched nature. It is also not merely artificial. It is a draft of nature, a prototype ecology, a wet green negotiation between machine authority and living opportunism.

You may feel:

- That the place is too young to be trusted.
- That it is already older than its builders intended.
- That the plants are quietly winning.
- That the machinery is patient, not hostile.
- That the whole room is an organ at the top of a giant body.
- That every drop of water is part of a plan, except the ones that have escaped planning.

Do not expect the clean sublimity of a mountain summit. Expect the intimacy of a greenhouse, the unease of a factory, and the strangeness of watching geology begin before your eyes.

## Specific things not to do

Do not:

- Drink the water.
- Swim, wade, or put hands into pools.
- Touch tagged animals.
- Pick flowers or moss.
- Scrape mineral crusts.
- Collect samples without permission.
- Turn valves.
- Pull vines away from pipes.
- Step into planted seams.
- Open access panels.
- Block channels “temporarily.”
- Sit on grates, cistern mounts, or warm pipes.
- Feed animals.
- Release insects.
- Leave offerings unless there is an established custom and approved place.
- Assume a harmless-looking shallow basin is harmless.

The pools are no deeper than a hand, but they have valves at their throats. The floor looks like rock, but it contains channels. The cliff looks natural, but it is a condensation wall. The whole place is full of things pretending to be simpler than they are.

## Best time to visit

If you can choose, visit in the morning or shortly after a scheduled rain.

Morning gives:

- White-gold mist.
- Cooler surfaces.
- Better visibility before full vapor buildup.
- Active cloud capture on leaves.

After scheduled rain gives:

- Strongest scent.
- Visible steaming surfaces.
- Droplets on every structure.
- A clear sense of how the garden works.

Avoid visiting during maintenance cycles, major venting, storm conditions, emergency drainage tests, or periods of ecological adjustment such as replanting, fungal treatment, pest release, or conduit repair.

## Emergency planning

Before entering, know:

- The nearest exit.
- The evacuation signal.
- Whether alarms are audible through mist and machinery.
- Where safe standing areas are during rain or venting.
- Who controls the emergency drain.
- Whether the parapet side is accessible or restricted.
- How to summon help if visibility drops.

If separated from your group:

1. Stop.
2. Move only to the nearest clear path if you are in an unsafe spot.
3. Avoid following animal tracks, water channels, or apparent shortcuts.
4. Listen for the pump or guide signals.
5. Wait near a visible landmark without touching controls.

If burned by a pipe, cool the burn with clean carried water if available, not pool water. If you inhale irritating vapor, move toward fresher air and lower exertion. If you slip into a basin or channel, get out carefully and report it; you may have contaminated a system or exposed yourself to mineral or biological agents.

## How to behave as a good visitor

Be light.

Not just physically, though that helps. Be light in intention. This is a place where small interferences matter. A footprint in moss, a seed in a crack, a turned valve, a broken vine, a dropped wrapper, a sample taken from a mineral shelf — each may alter a system still learning its own habits.

Walk as if the room is listening.

Notice the human decisions: the cast stone, the capillary veins, the brass plaque, the clipped vines, the lacquered snails. Notice also the first rebellions: moss beyond its panel, mineral crust where no engineer drew it, roots testing accidental gaps. The reward of visiting is not merely the view from the manufactured mountain. It is seeing the moment where construction begins to become landscape.

Prepare to leave damp, warm, slightly stained, and perhaps reluctant to call anything there either natural or artificial.
