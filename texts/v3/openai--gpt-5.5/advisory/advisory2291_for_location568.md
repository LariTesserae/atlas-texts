To visit this underground hamlet safely and respectfully, prepare as if you are entering a living household, a workshop, an animal stable, a cistern, and a sacred machine all at once. It is not a ruin and not a cave. Nothing there is passive. Every surface is being used by someone or something.

## 1. Expect closeness, heat, noise, and layered smells

The place is underground but not cold. Do not dress for a deep cavern. Dress for a warm cellar beneath a hot city: humid in some pockets, dry and dusty in others, with animal heat, lamp smoke, damp plaster, old water, grain, dung, herbs, and human labor all sharing the same air.

You should be ready for:

- Low ceilings and narrow passages.
- Constant background noise.
- Animals moving above, below, and beside you.
- Wet patches where you expect dry floor, and dry dust where you expect damp.
- Sudden changes in smell after rain or water release.
- People working very close to you.
- Little privacy.

If you are sensitive to sensory overload, this place may be difficult. The sound is not just loud; it is crowded. There are always echoes, wingbeats, dripping water, voices, scratching claws, cage noises, and small tools striking brick or pottery.

Bring ear protection, but use it discreetly. You still need to hear warnings, animals, and people calling out.

## 2. Clothing

Wear simple, durable clothing that can get damp, smoky, dusty, and brushed by animals.

Recommended:

- Lightweight long sleeves.
- Long trousers or a long skirt that does not drag.
- Breathable natural fibers if possible.
- A scarf or cloth for dust, smoke, and smell.
- A close-fitting cap or head covering to protect your hair from soot, drips, insects, and bird droppings.
- Soft-soled shoes or sandals with good grip.

Avoid:

- Heavy boots with hard soles. They may damage old brick, startle animals, and mark you as careless.
- Dangling jewelry, loose straps, trailing sleeves, or long scarves. These can catch on cages, hooks, shutters, gutter edges, and animal pens.
- Strong perfume. It will offend people, confuse animals, and interfere with the local scent-based cues.
- Bright, delicate, or ceremonial clothing unless you have been specifically invited for a ritual occasion.

The floor may be slick in one step and powder-dry the next. Your footwear should handle wet clay, glazed brick, grit, and shallow water channels.

## 3. Health precautions

This is a dense human-animal-insect environment with old water systems. Prepare accordingly.

Before visiting, make sure you are protected against common contamination and animal-borne illness appropriate to your home universe: tetanus, gastrointestinal disease, parasites, respiratory irritants, and bites. If you have asthma, mold sensitivity, severe allergies, immune suppression, or a phobia of insects, reconsider or visit only briefly with a guide.

Bring:

- Clean drinking water in a sealed container.
- Rehydration salts.
- A small personal medical kit.
- Antiseptic wipes or wash.
- Bandages for scrapes.
- Any inhaler or allergy medication you need.
- A mild mask or cloth filter for dusty corridors and lamp-smoke pockets.
- Digestive medication if you are likely to be offered food.

Do not drink local water unless it is explicitly offered to you by someone responsible for water distribution. Even then, ask what visitors usually drink. The cistern water is precious and managed, but that does not mean your body will tolerate it.

Do not touch your mouth or eyes after handling walls, animals, ropes, cages, cistern lids, or plant troughs.

## 4. Bring very little

Space is limited. A large pack will make you a nuisance.

Carry a small shoulder satchel or belt pouch. Keep your hands free.

Useful items:

- A small sealed water flask.
- A cloth for your face and hands.
- A compact lamp or light, used only with permission.
- A notebook or memory device, if recording is allowed.
- A small gift: salt, clean needles, good cord, beeswax, oil, a useful pigment, a sharp knife, medicinal herbs, or a water-purifying charm/device if culturally appropriate.
- A soft pouch for shoes if you are asked to remove them in some areas.
- A few coins or trade goods, but do not assume money is the main etiquette.

Avoid bringing:

- Open food.
- Seeds, spores, soil, or live plants unless invited. Their ecology is carefully balanced.
- Pets.
- Weapons, unless required for multiverse transit and kept sealed.
- Loud machinery.
- Strong lights.
- Anything that sheds glitter, fibers, powder, fragrance, or chemical residue.

This place survives by controlling tiny flows: water, insects, heat, droppings, seed, ash, rot. Do not introduce unknown contaminants.

## 5. Secure a local guide

Do not wander alone.

A visitor cannot tell which crack must remain open, which drain is sacred by necessity, which shelf belongs to birds, which puddle is clean runoff, which one is animal waste, and which wall is load-bearing despite looking like a patchwork of repairs.

A guide will know:

- Where you may step.
- Which ledges are animal paths.
- Which channels carry usable water.
- Which rooms are private.
- Which plants may not be touched.
- When to duck, wait, or move quickly.
- Which sounds mean “normal work” and which mean “something is wrong.”
- How to behave near the cistern.

Ideally, your guide should be connected to the cistern keepers, repair workers, or an elder household. Those are the people who understand the place as a system.

## 6. Etiquette: treat water as sacred labor

The most important rule is simple: do not waste clean water.

Do not pour water onto a floor, rinse your hands over an unmarked drain, wash an object without asking, or empty a bottle “just to lighten your load.” Water belongs to the whole system before it belongs to any individual.

Follow these rules carefully:

- Never spill clean water onto an unmarked floor.
- Do not touch cistern covers without permission.
- Do not sit on cistern lids unless invited.
- Do not block gutters, channels, hanging bowls, or condensation pots.
- Do not move a bowl placed under a drip.
- Do not assume a trickle is dirty or clean based on appearance.
- If you knock over a water vessel, announce it immediately and offer restitution.

During rain, the whole settlement may reorganize around water collection. Stay out of the way unless given a task. Rain is not background weather here; it is an event.

## 7. Etiquette: animals are part of the infrastructure

The animals are not decoration. They are food, pest control, fertilizer production, alarm systems, ritual companions, and household wealth.

Do not:

- Feed animals without permission.
- Touch cages.
- Open pens.
- Startle birds.
- Chase cats or catlike hunters.
- Kill insects casually.
- Swat spiders above grain shelves.
- Move thorn bundles, nesting markers, or hanging sprigs.
- Step into lower animal rooms unless guided.

If a catlike creature steals from you, do not strike it. Call for help. Someone will know whether it is a valued hunter, a tolerated thief, or a serious problem.

If a bird lands near you, stay still. Birds likely know the space better than you do.

If you see a lizard in a wall crack, leave it alone. The residents may rely on it to control moths.

## 8. Respect markers and small signs

This place communicates through practical symbols. A sprig over a doorway, a thorn bundle by stairs, a covered pot, a shifted shutter, a tied cord, a bit of limewash, a bowl beneath a tile—these are not clutter. They are instructions.

Assume every object has been placed deliberately.

Especially avoid disturbing:

- Condensation bowls.
- Reed curtains.
- Doorway herbs.
- Thorn bundles.
- Clay disks over the cistern.
- Hanging baskets.
- Bird cages.
- Drying seeds.
- Ropes and pulleys.
- Plaster brackets used for nests.
- Any patched section of wall or pipe.

If something seems in the way, ask. It may be keeping a leak from spreading, collecting water, signaling that a room is clean, protecting a nest, or warning people away from a claimed shadow.

## 9. Movement and posture

Move slowly. Keep your elbows close. Look both down and up.

The hazards are not dramatic but constant:

- Low beams.
- Sudden steps.
- Shallow floor channels.
- Slick moss tiles.
- Hanging cages.
- Hot lamps.
- Animal droppings.
- Loose grit.
- Narrow ledges used by cats.
- Fragile patched brick.
- Wet clay.
- Sharp bone pegs or broken pottery edges.

Do not lean on walls unless told they are safe. Many surfaces are old plaster over older repairs. They may look solid while being important, delicate, or damp.

When passing someone in a narrow corridor, turn sideways and wait. Let workers with water, animals, hot oil, grain, or tools pass first.

Do not block vents. Do not sit in low stairways. Do not stand at the central basin gawking during busy hours.

## 10. Light and fire

The light here is precious and managed. Daylight enters through shafts with adjusted reflective plates. Oil lamps fill the gaps.

Do not adjust shutters, plates, lamps, or reflectors. A small change may heat a niche, dry a plant trough, disturb birds, or blind someone working.

If you carry your own light:

- Keep it dim.
- Shield it around animals.
- Ask before using it near the cistern or storage pools.
- Do not use open flame without permission.
- Do not shine lights up shafts unless invited.

Smoke gathers in tongues on the ceiling. Extra fire is extra burden.

## 11. Food and hospitality

You may be offered food or drink. Accepting politely is wise, but be honest about what your body can tolerate.

Food may include:

- Flatbread or grain mash.
- Bitter herbs.
- small preserved fruits.
- eggs.
- goat or pig products.
- fermented items.
- fungi.
- fish from dark pools.
- insects or insect-fed birds, depending on custom.

Do not insult the scarcity of vegetables or the smell of animal rooms. Food production here is an achievement.

If you bring a gift, practical gifts are best. Wax, clean cordage, salt, lamp oil, medicinal salve, good needles, and repair metal will be more welcome than ornaments.

## 12. Conversation

Speak clearly and close to the person you are addressing. In central rooms, normal conversation may be swallowed by the layered sound. Shouting may alarm animals or irritate residents. Let your guide model the proper volume.

Do not make jokes about being buried, trapped, filthy, primitive, or vermin-infested. The place is not a failed city. It is a finely maintained survival ecology.

Good topics:

- Water management.
- Repair work.
- Plants and herbs.
- Animal care.
- Local crafts.
- Ventilation.
- How light is guided.
- Which sounds mean rain is coming.
- Stories of old repairs.

Avoid prying too quickly into:

- Cistern disputes.
- Sealed sluices.
- The western pipe.
- Birth, death, and inheritance customs.
- Ritual uses of plants or animals.
- “Odd” architectural behaviors unless invited.

## 13. The unexplained features

The place is not overtly magical, but it has habits. Treat them with the same respect the residents do.

Important:

- Do not whistle into the west pipe.
- Do not mock the rule about the west pipe.
- Do not try to provoke the fish-mouth drain into clicking.
- Do not place objects beside humming jars without permission.
- Do not investigate warm walls alone.
- Do not attempt to “debunk” local explanations in public.
- Do not perform experimental magic, acoustic tests, scans, or dimensional readings without consent.

Whether these things are supernatural, architectural, biological, or social does not matter during your visit. They are part of the working order.

A careless visitor who unsettles animals, disrupts airflow, or causes a panic may do real harm.

## 14. During rain

Rain is one of the most important times in the settlement. If you are present when rain begins, expect sudden coordinated activity.

You may notice:

- Dust smell rising from dry channels.
- People uncovering cisterns.
- Children carrying bowls.
- Drains being opened or blocked.
- Arguments over timing.
- Animals becoming restless.
- Insects emerging.
- Birds growing frantic.
- The whole place seeming to turn toward the sound of water.

Stand where told. Do not help unless assigned a simple task. Never assume a flowing channel should be cleared or dammed. The first water may be bitter or dirty; later water may be valuable. Timing matters.

If you are given a bowl to hold, hold it. If told not to spill, do not spill. This is not symbolic.

## 15. Overnight stay

If you sleep there, ask where to lie and what not to lie beneath.

Remember the rule: do not sleep below a sweating tile unless a bowl has been set beneath it.

Bring or request:

- A thin sleeping mat.
- A light wrap.
- Earplugs or a cloth for your ears.
- A head covering.
- A way to secure small belongings from rodents and cats.
- A lidded container for personal waste if facilities are not obvious.

At night, the human work quiets but the place remains active. Crickets, cats, water, birds, and hidden animals continue moving. Do not wander after dark without a resident. You may step into channels, disturb sleeping birds, or enter private or dangerous areas.

## 16. Personal conduct: be useful or be small

The best visitor is either helpful or unobtrusive.

Offer help only after watching. Useful beginner tasks might include:

- Holding a lamp.
- Passing tools.
- Carrying sealed jars.
- Sorting dry reeds.
- Sitting with a guide’s children while they work.
- Turning a hand fan.
- Sweeping dust where directed.
- Keeping animals from a threshold.

Do not attempt repairs unless you are specifically trained in local methods. A badly patched crack may ruin a water path or seal in a needed insect colony.

Do not tidy. This is important. What looks like clutter may be a functioning system.

## 17. Psychological preparation

This place can feel intimate, crowded, and alive in a way that unsettles outsiders. You may feel watched, not only by people but by birds, cats, lizards, insects, and the architecture itself. You may feel that the walls are too close or that every sound is entering your body.

Prepare yourself to slow down.

This is not a destination for grand gestures. It rewards attention. Notice the worn steps, the repaired cistern lip, the hand-polished doorframes, the bowls under drips, the way a wall is shelf, gutter, perch, garden, and message board all at once.

If you become overwhelmed, ask your guide for a quieter dry passage or a place near a vent. Do not flee blindly toward light shafts or service tunnels.

## 18. Departure

Before leaving:

- Thank your host or guide.
- Offer payment or a practical gift.
- Return any borrowed cloth, bowl, lamp, or footwear.
- Check that you have not carried away seeds, insects, eggs, or sacred markers.
- Clean your boots or sandals before entering another ecosystem.
- Do not remove “souvenirs” such as pottery chips, herbs, feathers, clay disks, old rope, or strange humming jars.

Even a small object may have a job.

## In short

Prepare to visit a living machine made of brick, water, animal breath, habit, and maintenance. Go lightly. Carry little. Waste nothing. Do not disturb water, nests, pipes, lamps, or marked thresholds. Respect the residents’ rules even when they seem superstitious, because here superstition and engineering may be the same thing remembered differently.

Most of all, understand that this place survives through attention. If you bring attention rather than appetite, curiosity rather than interference, and humility rather than spectacle, you may be welcomed into one of the warmest and most intricate underground communities a traveler could hope to see.
