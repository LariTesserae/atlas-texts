An ordinary human traveler should prepare for this basin as if visiting a place that is beautiful, sterile, high, sharp, and quietly hostile. It is not a “camping spot” so much as a small exposed mechanism of stone, frost, gravity, and light. The main dangers are not predators or storms, but altitude, cold, ultraviolet light, unstable ground, dehydration, rockfall, and the deceptive stillness of the place.

## 1. Physical preparation

### Acclimatize before arrival
This basin sits above the usual weather layer, likely at very high altitude. Do not arrive directly from sea level unless your multiverse transit system can compensate for pressure and oxygen.

Prepare for:

- Shortness of breath
- Dizziness
- Headache
- Nausea
- Poor judgment from hypoxia
- Slow movement and clumsiness
- Increased dehydration

Spend several days at intermediate altitude if possible. If portal travel deposits you directly at the basin, bring supplemental oxygen and be ready to leave quickly if symptoms begin.

### Train for uneven talus travel
The basin floor is packed with slabs, needles, frost-sorted gravel, shale stacks, ice pockets, and hidden hollows. You will not be walking so much as picking your way through a field of unstable stone.

Before visiting, practice:

- Scrambling over loose rock
- Moving with a loaded pack
- Three-point contact climbing
- Self-arrest or controlled falls if the approach includes snow or ice
- Recognizing unstable talus and frost-heaved ground

Do not plan to move quickly. Even crossing the basin may take longer than expected if you are being careful.

## 2. Clothing and personal gear

The basin’s light and cold are both severe. You need protection from freeze, glare, and abrasion.

Bring:

- Layered cold-weather clothing
- Windproof outer shell
- Insulated jacket
- Thermal base layers
- Waterproof or water-resistant pants
- Warm hat or balaclava
- Neck gaiter
- Insulated gloves plus a thinner liner pair
- Mountaineering boots with stiff soles
- Microspikes or crampons depending on ice conditions
- Gaiters to keep grit, frost, and snow out
- High-UV glacier sunglasses or goggles
- Sunscreen suitable for high altitude
- Lip balm with SPF
- Helmet, especially near basin walls or beneath overhangs

The light is not merely bright; it is reflective from every wet seam, frost grain, and pale rock face. Snow blindness or UV eye damage is a serious risk even without snow.

## 3. Breathing, hydration, and food

### Bring your own water
Do not rely on the basin’s water. It appears in small, precise windows: dawn condensation, noon melt, late-afternoon cloud-wetness, night frost. Some cups may be mineral-heavy or contaminated by unknown chemistry.

Carry enough water for your entire stay, plus emergency reserve. Use insulated bottles so they do not freeze.

If you must use local water:

- Prefer clear meltwater cups that disappear by noon
- Avoid cloudy, bitter, stagnant, or stained pools
- Filter and chemically treat if possible
- Melt ice only if you can do so without damaging formations or wasting fuel
- Assume dissolved minerals may be unpleasant or unsafe

### Bring high-calorie food
The cold and altitude will burn energy quickly. Bring compact, ready-to-eat foods that require little preparation:

- Energy bars
- Nuts
- Dried fruit
- Hard cheese or equivalent preserved protein
- Freeze-dried meals if you have a stove
- Electrolytes

Do not expect to forage. There is nothing to forage. Nothing green grows there, and no animals pass through.

## 4. Shelter and heat

### Do not depend on fire
There is nothing to burn. Fire does not belong here. Bring a stove if you need heat for water or food, and bring all fuel with you.

A good kit would include:

- Four-season tent or bivy shelter
- Sleeping bag rated below expected nighttime temperatures
- Insulated sleeping pad
- Stove that works in cold and low-pressure conditions
- Wind shield for stove use
- Emergency bivy
- Repair tape and cord
- Snow stakes or rock anchors if ordinary stakes cannot be used

The old stone shelter beneath the overhang should not be assumed safe or available. It may be archaeologically significant, structurally unstable, or both. Do not sleep inside it unless survival requires it.

## 5. Timing your visit

The basin changes character through the day.

### Dawn
Condensation forms and frost begins to loosen. Stones may look dry while shaded sides remain ice-skinned. Movement is risky because surfaces are slick and the eastern wall may begin shedding small stones as sunlight reaches it.

### Midmorning
Meltwater moves through hidden cracks. Listen for ticking and popping from sun-warmed walls. If the eastern wall is active, avoid crossing beneath it.

### Noon
Exposed surfaces dry rapidly. This may be the safest time for moving across open stone, though glare is at its worst.

### Late afternoon
Cloud may spill over the rim. Everything becomes wet without rain. Rock surfaces darken, traction worsens, visibility may drop, and temperature can fall quickly.

### Night
The basin freezes shut. Water skins over, stones glaze, and cold intensifies. Navigation becomes dangerous unless you have excellent lighting and know the terrain.

Best plan: arrive after full daylight, survey from the rim, enter only during stable conditions, and leave before afternoon cloud or evening freeze if you are not prepared to bivouac.

## 6. Movement rules

Move as though the basin is listening for weight.

Follow these principles:

- Test stones before committing weight.
- Avoid patterned gravel where upright pebbles suggest frost hollows beneath.
- Do not step into shallow cups or icy hollows.
- Stay away from the base of steep walls after sunrise.
- Do not crawl under overhangs unless necessary.
- Keep your helmet on.
- Avoid leaning on standing slabs or stone needles.
- Place weight where other weight already rests.
- Do not pry, rearrange, or “improve” the terrain.
- If a route feels too delicate, retreat.

The place is constantly being cracked, sorted, sealed, and reopened. A stone that held yesterday may fail today because ice lifted it in the night.

## 7. Navigation and communication

The basin is small, but the approach may be difficult, and the interior can become disorienting under glare, cloud, or darkness.

Bring:

- Map or scanned topographic reference
- GPS or multiverse-compatible locator
- Compass, if magnetism is reliable in that world
- Altimeter
- Headlamp plus backup light
- Spare batteries kept warm
- Emergency beacon
- Radio or portal recall device
- Physical route markers that can be removed afterward

Do not rely on footprints. The basin erases them with freeze-thaw. Breath, prints, and light traces vanish quickly.

If using a portal, anchor the exit point somewhere stable and away from rockfall zones, not in the center of patterned gravel or beneath a wall.

## 8. Medical and emergency supplies

Bring a compact but serious alpine emergency kit:

- First aid kit
- Blister care
- Splinting material
- Pain relief
- Medication for altitude sickness if appropriate and prescribed
- Oxygen canister or concentrator if feasible
- Emergency blanket or bivy
- Chemical warmers
- Trauma bandage
- Satellite/multiversal distress beacon
- Knife or multitool
- Repair kit for boots, gloves, pack, and shelter

Common injuries here would include ankle twists, hand cuts, falls onto sharp rock, eye damage from glare, hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, and altitude sickness.

## 9. Archaeological and ethical precautions

People came here before. Rarely, carefully, and not recently.

The upright stone ring, the worked volcanic glass, the slab shelter, the carved boulder, and the mineral-stiff cord fragments should be treated as cultural remains.

Do not:

- Move stones from the ring
- Pick up the obsidian flakes
- Enter or alter the shelter casually
- Touch or chalk the carvings
- Take rubbings unless specifically permitted
- Remove artifacts
- Build new cairns
- Leave offerings
- Camp inside archaeological features
- “Restore” anything

Photograph, map, and observe from a respectful distance. The basin preserves only what cooperates with frost and gravity. Human traces are fragile here, even if they look like stone.

## 10. Psychological preparation

This is a place without biological welcome.

There are no birds, insects, grass, lichens, animal tracks, or soft signs of habitation. A traveler may experience a strange pressure from that absence. The place is not empty, but it is empty of the kinds of life that reassure humans.

Expect:

- Extreme silence
- Strong glare and visual fatigue
- A sense of enclosure despite open sky
- Height-induced unease
- Time distortion from the lack of ordinary movement
- The feeling that every sound matters
- A heightened awareness of your own breathing and body heat

Do not mistake silence for safety. The basin is active, just slowly and precisely.

## 11. What to bring back — and what not to

Bring back:

- Photographs
- Notes
- Environmental readings
- Sketches
- Memories
- Respect

Do not bring back:

- Stones
- Water samples unless permitted and scientifically necessary
- Obsidian flakes
- Cord fragments
- Carved fragments
- “Souvenirs” of any kind

In a place so stripped down, even a small removal matters.

## 12. Best visiting strategy

For an ordinary human traveler, the safest and most respectful visit would be short.

A good plan:

1. Acclimatize below the ridge.
2. Approach in stable morning conditions, but wait until the first sun-warmed rockfall period has passed.
3. Enter with helmet, goggles, gloves, and full cold-weather gear.
4. Move slowly along stable slabs and existing natural weight paths.
5. Avoid the steep eastern wall after sunrise.
6. Observe the stone ring, shelter, and carved boulder without touching.
7. Take water only from your own supply.
8. Leave before late-afternoon cloud spill or evening freeze.
9. Pack out everything, including dust-contaminated trash and food crumbs.
10. Record conditions for future travelers.

## Final advice

Prepare as if the basin is not trying to kill you, but also will not make the slightest adjustment to keep you alive.

It has no malice. It has rules.

Cold rules. Light rules. Stone rules. Water appears briefly and vanishes. Ice builds at night and breaks things by morning. Human traces survive only when they are humble enough to become part of the slope.

Visit lightly. Move slowly. Listen after sunrise. Trust nothing merely because it looks still. Leave no new claim behind.
