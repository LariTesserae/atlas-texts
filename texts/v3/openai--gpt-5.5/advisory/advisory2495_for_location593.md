An ordinary human traveler should prepare as if visiting a high-altitude industrial port, a wet cave system, a working factory, and a wildlife migration route all at once. The city is not hostile in the sense of being malicious, but it is profoundly indifferent. It has its own rhythms: water, heat, animals, machines, fog, and slow human routines adapted over generations. Your safety depends on respecting those rhythms.

## 1. Acclimate before arrival

The city sits high enough that the air is thin, but it is also warm and humid. That combination is deceptive. You may sweat, feel overheated, and still be under-oxygenated.

Prepare for:

- Shortness of breath on stairs and elevated walkways.
- Headaches, poor sleep, irritability, nausea.
- Faster fatigue than expected.
- Higher dehydration risk despite constant wetness.
- Slower recovery from exertion.

Arrive gradually if possible. Spend several days at intermediate altitude before entering the plateau city proper. Do not plan strenuous travel on the first two days. If you have asthma, heart disease, anemia, blood pressure problems, or panic attacks triggered by breathlessness, consult a competent physician before going.

Carry altitude medication if appropriate, but do not rely on it as a substitute for pacing yourself.

## 2. Dress for wet metal, not wilderness

This is not a green wetland. It is a wet industrial animal city. Your clothing should protect you from condensation, oily runoff, abrasive masonry, droppings, rust, and animal contact.

Recommended clothing:

- Waterproof but breathable outer shell.
- Quick-drying insulating layers; avoid cotton.
- Waterproof trousers or overtrousers.
- High-grip boots with puncture-resistant soles.
- Gaiters or sealed cuffs to keep small animals, water, and grit out.
- Cut-resistant gloves.
- A hood or brimmed cap for dripping overhead structures.
- Spare socks in sealed bags.
- A washable outer layer you do not mind ruining.

Do not wear dangling straps, loose scarves, decorative chains, open mesh, or anything that can snag on pipes, rails, nests, machinery, or animal limbs.

The ground will often look solid but behave like a soaked sponge over stone. Paving slabs may flex. Gutters may be deeper than they appear. Mineral crusts may conceal warm runoff or animal burrows. Good boots are not optional.

## 3. Protect your breathing

The air may contain fog, steam, industrial aerosols, animal dander, ammonia, sulfur compounds, moldlike bacterial films, powdered mineral salts, and smoke from old engines. Even if the locals tolerate it, you may not.

Carry:

- A half-face respirator or high-quality mask with replaceable filters.
- Spare filters sealed against damp.
- Eye protection or sealed goggles.
- Saline rinse for eyes and nose.
- Inhaler if you have any respiratory sensitivity.
- A small oxygen canister or emergency oxygen system if advised.

Ceramic masks and oilcloth gear worn by locals are not fashion. They are practical barriers against aerosols, droplets, and animal grease. If someone tells you to mask before entering a district, do it immediately.

Avoid breathing directly over canals, cisterns, warm drains, stock markets, rendering areas, and lower tunnels unless masked.

## 4. Assume every wet surface is biologically active

The city’s ecology begins with microbial films, mineral mats, sulfur seams, pipe growths, and bacterial skins. These may be invisible. Do not touch them casually.

Rules:

- Do not put bare hands on walls, railings, sluices, pipes, canal edges, or damp stone.
- Do not sit directly on pavement.
- Do not drink from condensation unless it has been collected, filtered, and certified by locals.
- Do not walk barefoot anywhere, even indoors.
- Clean and cover every scratch immediately.
- Disinfect boots and outer clothing after travel through canals, basins, markets, or animal corridors.

Bring a serious first-aid kit: disinfectant, sterile dressings, waterproof bandages, blister care, tweezers, antihistamines, broad-spectrum antibiotics if prescribed, anti-diarrheal medication, rehydration salts, and any personal medications in waterproof packaging.

Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current. Depending on interworld medical advice, consider rabies-equivalent prophylaxis, leptospirosis precautions, antifungal/antimicrobial guidance, and quarantine procedures after departure.

## 5. Take animal risk seriously

The animals are not background scenery. They are the city’s majority population. Many are not individually aggressive, but density makes them dangerous.

Common hazards include:

- Bites from hookbirds or canal scavengers.
- Puncture wounds from crabs, shells, spines, beaks, and needle-legged predators.
- Allergic reactions to feathers, dander, shed skins, and secretions.
- Being knocked over by herd movement.
- Slipping on droppings or shed carapaces.
- Disturbing nests built into vents, cables, windows, and machinery.
- Becoming trapped between moving animal streams and hard infrastructure.

Do not feed animals unless instructed by a local handler. Do not imitate calls. Do not photograph nests at close range. Do not reach into gutters, vents, tanks, signal housings, elevator shafts, or dry-looking cavities. In this city, any enclosed space may be occupied.

If animals begin moving in one direction, do not stand in their path to watch. Get behind a barrier, into a recessed doorway, onto a raised walkway, or beside a local who knows what is happening.

If all animals suddenly go quiet, pause. They may have detected a train, pressure release, predator, machine cycle, or weather shift before you have.

## 6. Hire a local guide

A guide is not a luxury here. It is comparable to hiring a river pilot, mine escort, or avalanche guide.

You need someone who knows:

- Which routes are currently passable.
- Which machines are due to wake.
- Which canals are animal corridors that day.
- Which bridges are roosting sites.
- Which neighborhoods are safe for outsiders.
- Where clean water can be obtained.
- Where sealed sleeping quarters are available.
- How to interpret sirens, foghorns, rail vibrations, and animal pauses.
- What local taboos apply to nests, towers, cisterns, and shrines.

Maps will not be enough. The city changes slowly, but access changes constantly. A dry passage at noon may be contested by animals at dusk. A quiet factory may begin a six-hour cycle. A fog bank may erase a boulevard. A bridge may become impassable when winged animals wake.

Pay your guide well. Follow them without arguing when they tell you to wait, turn back, mask up, or move quickly.

## 7. Learn the route hierarchy

The city has layers: elevated rails, raised pedestrian ways, processional avenues, older half-submerged streets, service tunnels, canals, roofs, terraces, and factory interiors. Not all layers are equally safe.

For an ordinary traveler, safest routes are usually:

1. Marked raised walkways.
2. Maintained public squares.
3. Residential terraces with sealed access.
4. Official market routes.
5. Guided industrial corridors.

Riskier routes:

- Half-submerged older streets.
- Underpasses during cloud or rain.
- Canal edges.
- Rail yards.
- Roofs.
- Bridge undersides.
- Service tunnels.
- Warm basins at the city margins.
- Any route that smells strongly of ammonia, sulfur, blood, or hot oil.

Never enter tunnels alone. The tunnels are warm, loud, wet, and confusing. Waterfalls through grates can obscure holes and tracks. Maintenance engines may move silently until close. Animals may use tunnels as shelter or migration channels.

## 8. Prepare for noise

The city is never truly quiet. It has engine noise, pumps, water, animal calls, rail shriek, wing surges, sirens, foghorns, factory cycles, dripping metal, and sudden mass movement.

Bring:

- Earplugs.
- Over-ear hearing protection if entering factories or rail districts.
- A whistle or signal device.
- A light that can strobe in fog.
- A written or visual communication system for use when speech is impossible.

Do not assume shouting will be heard. In some districts, a passing train or waking stamping hall can erase all other sound.

If you are sensitive to constant noise, prepare psychologically. Sleep may be difficult. Choose lodging with insulated sealed rooms, not cheap quarters near canals, pipes, or bridge roosts.

## 9. Prepare for poor visibility

Clouds move through the city. Fog can reduce visibility to a few meters. Steam, warm rain, and vapor from reservoirs can make navigation disorienting.

Carry:

- Waterproof headlamp.
- Backup light.
- Reflective markings on clothing.
- Physical map in waterproof cover.
- Compass or equivalent navigation tool.
- Local beacon receiver if available.
- Chalk, tags, or authorized route markers.
- Emergency contact signal compatible with local systems.

Do not cross open squares or industrial yards in dense fog unless following a marked route. You may walk into a canal, rail line, animal mass, hot vent, or machine path before seeing it.

## 10. Respect water, but do not trust it

Water is everywhere and yet clean water is precious. Most visible water should be considered contaminated by minerals, industrial discharge, microbial films, animal waste, oils, or heat-loving organisms.

Bring:

- Personal water supply for at least a full day.
- Robust filtration system.
- Chemical purification.
- Metal bottle that can handle boiling water.
- Electrolytes.
- Waterproof storage.

But understand: ordinary camping filters may not remove dissolved metals, industrial contaminants, or alien microbial toxins. Use local clean-condensation systems, certified roof catchments, or traded water whenever possible.

Avoid stepping into warm pools. Warmth gathers life. A pool under machinery may contain grazers, bacteria, larvae, chemical runoff, or unstable flooring.

## 11. Sleep only behind sealed doors

The local saying that a sealed door is “a promise, not a certainty” should be taken seriously.

Your lodging should have:

- Raised sleeping platform.
- Sealed door with intact gasket or equivalent.
- Window grates.
- Drainage channels outside, not through the room.
- Animal-proof food storage.
- Dry storage hooks or racks.
- Ventilation screens.
- Emergency exit not opening directly into a canal or animal corridor.

Inspect bedding for insects, small crabs, shed shells, dampness, and fungal films. Keep boots upside down or sealed. Shake out clothing before wearing it. Store food in hard containers.

Do not sleep in abandoned rooms, vehicles, warehouses, stations, elevator cabins, or dry-looking alcoves. Anything left still long enough becomes habitat, including you if you are careless.

## 12. Understand local etiquette

The city’s human inhabitants survive by managing access, not by dominating the environment. They may seem cautious, ritualistic, or slow. Their habits are practical.

Good manners:

- Ask before entering terraces, cistern areas, kitchens, markets, or maintenance routes.
- Do not mock masks, drainage customs, animal taboos, or sealing rituals.
- Step aside during work parties.
- Never block a raised walkway.
- Do not touch laundry, water catchments, filters, animal traps, tags on shrines, or shell piles.
- Accept that some areas are closed because of animal cycles, not politics.
- Pay for clean water.
- Pay for guide time.
- Do not waste dry space.

Dry surfaces are valuable. If someone offers you a dry chair, sleeping ledge, or storage shelf, treat it as hospitality.

## 13. Food precautions

Local food may include shell meat, rendered fat, microbial vat products, mineral-fed grazers, canal animals, distilled broths, and preserved proteins. Some may be safe; some may not suit an unadapted human gut.

Eat cautiously.

Bring:

- Familiar emergency rations.
- Digestive medication.
- Rehydration salts.
- Allergy medication.
- Eating utensils you can clean yourself.
- Sealed food bags.

Try local foods only from reputable kitchens or guides. Avoid raw canal animals, unboiled broth, unfamiliar fats, and anything fermented unless you know it is safe for your biology.

Do not leave food scraps exposed. They will attract animals quickly.

## 14. Industrial hazards are as dangerous as wildlife

This is still a working city. Machines wake on long cycles. Some may operate only every few days or once a year, which makes them more dangerous because they appear abandoned.

Watch for:

- Stamping halls.
- Active rail lines.
- Pump intakes.
- Pressure vents.
- Steam releases.
- Hot pipes.
- Unmarked electrical systems.
- Collapsing mineral crust.
- Conveyor towers that lean but still move.
- Distillation towers venting unexpectedly.
- Maintenance engines in tunnels.

Do not climb industrial structures. Do not cross rails unless locals do. Do not shelter under pipes or vents during pressure changes. Do not assume silence means inactivity.

When trains pass, locals step into recessed doorways and wait. Copy them.

## 15. Know the daily rhythm

A safe day in this city is planned around heat, fog, machines, and animal movement.

General pattern:

- Dawn: warm, humid, active pumps, trapped heat rising from factory canyons.
- Morning: eastern foundry districts create thermal columns; cloud formation begins.
- Midday: brighter, flatter light; some routes easier to see.
- Afternoon: localized rain, animal movement toward warm outflows.
- Evening: fog from western reservoirs, roosting changes, nocturnal canal activity.
- Night: winged animals move, covered ways preferred, pumps and tunnels louder.

Plan travel for stable visibility and lower animal activity. Avoid dusk near canals and basins unless guided. Avoid early fog in reservoir districts. Avoid factories known to cycle that day.

## 16. Bring the right tools

A practical kit should include:

- Waterproof backpack or dry bags.
- Respirator and spare filters.
- Goggles.
- Headlamp and backup light.
- Ear protection.
- High-grip waterproof boots.
- Cut-resistant gloves.
- Compact first-aid kit.
- Water purification system plus local water access plan.
- Electrolytes.
- Emergency food.
- Walking pole or probe for suspect paving.
- Local map and guide contact.
- Signaling whistle/light.
- Multitool, if permitted.
- Waterproof notebook.
- Spare socks and underlayers.
- Disinfectant wipes.
- Biocontainment bags for dirty clothes.
- Copies of permits and identification.
- Trade tokens or local currency.
- Emergency shelter sheet, preferably non-crinkling and animal-resistant.

Avoid bringing scented cosmetics, exposed food, leather goods treated with animal fats, or anything that smells like bait.

## 17. Mental preparation

The city may feel overwhelming. It is crowded with nonhuman life in a way many people find unnerving. The animals are everywhere: above, below, in walls, in gutters, in tanks, under bridges, inside old machines. The city is loud, damp, and never sterile. Even the architecture seems softened by bodies and weather.

Prepare yourself for:

- Constant animal proximity.
- Lack of greenery.
- Wetness without freshness.
- Industrial beauty mixed with decay.
- Smells of oil, mineral water, feathers, blood, lime, ammonia, steam, and shell.
- The sense that humans are not in control of the place, only negotiating with it.
- Slow time: waiting for fog, waiting for animals, waiting for machines, waiting for trains.

Do not go if you require clean lines, quiet nights, predictable routes, or total personal space.

But if you can adapt, the city will be astonishing. It is not ruined. It is inhabited in every possible sense. Its life is not pastoral or lush; it is mineral, wet, crowded, mechanical, and ancient. You are visiting a place where industry became geology and geology became ecology.

## 18. Essential rules to remember

If you remember nothing else, remember these:

- Stay with a local guide.
- Mask before entering foggy, animal-dense, or industrial areas.
- Do not touch wet surfaces barehanded.
- Do not drink untreated water.
- Do not approach canals casually.
- Do not disturb nests.
- Do not enter tunnels alone.
- Do not assume abandoned machinery is dead.
- Move aside when animals or trains move.
- Sleep only behind sealed doors.
- Treat dry space and clean water as precious.
- When locals wait, you wait.

The city rewards patience. Walk slowly, watch the animals, listen for changes in sound, and let the inhabitants teach you where a human body can safely fit.
