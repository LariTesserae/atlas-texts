To visit this drowned room safely, prepare as if you are entering a small, warm, active cistern during rainfall: beautiful, bright, enclosed, slippery, and more dangerous than it first appears.

## Main expectations

This is not a ruin or a cave. It is a recently built water chamber beneath a courtyard, still sharp-edged and functional. It will feel clean, warm, and strangely luminous, with daylight falling through shafts and breaking across clear water. The room is small, but the flooded geometry will distort distance and depth. You may think the bottom is close, then misjudge a step or ledge. Sound will be intense: rain above, water pouring from spouts, ceramic pipes coughing, jars humming, droplets ticking on stone.

Expect beauty, but do not treat it like a pool. It is a working hydraulic space.

## Primary hazards

### Drowning and entrapment
The water fills nearly the whole chamber and reaches close to the ceiling beams. There may be very little airspace away from the trapdoor. If the level rises during heavy rain, the dry steps may disappear quickly. Overflow tunnels, spillways, pipes, and narrow throats can create suction or strong localized currents.

Do not enter alone. Do not dive casually. Do not swim under beams, ledges, or barred overflow openings unless you have a clear exit and a line back.

### Slippery submerged surfaces
The first submerged step is specifically untrustworthy. New algae has begun there, and smooth stone under warm water will be slick. The stairway, platforms, ledges, and mosaic floor may all be treacherous. Because the water is clear and reflective, you may misread where your foot will land.

Move slowly. Use three points of contact. Assume every submerged surface is slick.

### Rising water
Rain is active above, and the chamber responds quickly. Water may rise by finger-widths in minutes during a hard storm. The bell-and-float system suggests the cistern can overfill. If you are inside when the overflow engages or a sluice is opened, currents may change suddenly.

Before entering, observe the measuring rod and the inflow rate. If the water is climbing steadily, postpone entry.

### Confined-space conditions
Although the chamber is bright and ventilated through shafts, it is still a below-ground wet room. Air quality may vary, especially if the trapdoor has been closed. Warm wet air can become hard to breathe, and voices/sounds may be disorienting.

Open the trapdoor fully before descending. Wait a few minutes. If you carry a simple flame, oxygen meter, or multiverse equivalent, test the air first.

### Water quality
The water is new, lime-touched, mineral, and relatively lifeless, but that does not make it safe to drink untreated. It may contain plaster dust, roof runoff, silt, animal droppings washed from above, or contaminants from the courtyard.

Do not drink directly unless local caretakers confirm it is potable and settled. Filter, boil, or chemically treat it.

## Recommended clothing

Wear:

- Lightweight clothing that can get fully wet.
- Close-fitting garments rather than loose robes or dangling straps.
- Grippy water shoes or soft-soled boots with traction.
- Gloves suitable for wet stone, rope, and wood.
- A helmet or bump cap if you will move beneath beams or through the hatch.
- A compact flotation aid if you are not a strong swimmer.

Avoid:

- Heavy boots that fill with water.
- Long coats, skirts, capes, scarves, or cords that can snag.
- Oiled leather or greasy waterproofing; oil is explicitly unwelcome here.
- Perfumes, soaps, insect repellents, or chemicals that may foul the cistern.

## Equipment to bring

At minimum:

- Waterproof lamp or headlamp, even if visiting by day.
- Backup light.
- Rope, ideally fixed outside the trapdoor and held by a companion.
- Walking staff, probe pole, or boat hook for testing submerged steps.
- Waterproof bag for tools and records.
- First-aid kit.
- Clean drinking water from outside.
- Towel and dry clothes for after.
- Chalk, wax tablet, or waterproof notebook if documenting levels and features.

If entering the water:

- Short safety line attached to a harness, not just held in the hand.
- Eye protection or swim mask.
- Simple respirator/snorkel only if trained; do not use it to push into confined flooded spaces.
- Knife or line cutter in case of entanglement.
- Thermal layer if staying long, despite the warmth; warm water still drains heat over time.

If you are a multiverse traveler with access to advanced gear, use:

- Compact depth and current sensor.
- Water-quality tester.
- Non-contaminating illumination.
- Low-profile buoyancy vest.
- Emergency beacon linked to your exit point.
- Portable air monitor.

Do not bring drones, pumps, dyes, sampling chemicals, or invasive survey tools without permission. This is a living water system, even if life has barely begun.

## Behavioral rules

Respect the chamber as infrastructure. Someone tends this place.

Follow these principles:

1. Do not block grates, pipes, spouts, or overflow channels.
2. Do not muddy the settling troughs.
3. Do not move jars, floats, rods, cords, bells, plugs, or sluice handles unless invited.
4. Do not introduce oil, soap, food scraps, bodily waste, or animals.
5. Do not scrape algae, moss, plaster, or mineral films casually.
6. Do not untie floating wood or rope.
7. Do not open the lower plug without reading the measuring rod and understanding where the water goes.
8. Do not close a sluice just to hear the room change its voice.
9. Leave the trapdoor secured as you found it, unless local safety requires otherwise.

The whole place works by simple means, but “simple” does not mean harmless. A single misplaced board or clogged slat could flood, drain, contaminate, or damage something important.

## Timing your visit

The most beautiful time is during daylight rain, when the shafts blaze and the whole chamber becomes a net of moving gold. That is also a more hazardous time because inflow is active and the water level may rise.

Safest visit: after rain has eased, in daylight, while the chamber is still bright but no longer swelling quickly.

Avoid:

- Night visits unless necessary.
- Heavy storm peaks.
- Times when the bell is ringing or the float is high.
- Entering immediately after a major downpour if you intend to draw or taste water; let silt settle first.

## How to move inside

Open the trapdoor and pause. Listen. If the roar is overwhelming, wait. Look for the measuring rod, the float, and the visible current patterns. Note which spouts are active and where the overflow lies.

Descend only to the dry steps at first. Test the first submerged step with a pole. Do not trust your eyes; refraction will lie. Keep one hand on the wall or ladder. If you must enter deeper, move along known edges rather than crossing the center blindly.

Watch for:

- Bubbles pulled sideways, indicating current.
- Algae flattened in one direction.
- Suspended grains moving faster near spillways.
- Cords leading upward or sideways.
- Niches that may hide tools, holes, or sudden depth changes.
- Pale plaster that looks solid but may be soft or newly set.

Do not swim into the overflow tunnel. Do not put fingers through barred slats. Do not place your body between the inflow and a narrow outlet.

## Sensory preparation

This place may overwhelm in subtle ways.

The brightness will flicker constantly. Moving water and reflected sunlight can make walls seem to breathe. Sound will be magnified until it feels like being inside a drum or under a roof made of rain. The air will smell of lime, wet wood, clay, and warm stone. The water may feel unexpectedly pleasant, almost bathlike, which can lower your caution.

Remind yourself: comfort is not safety.

You may feel the place is empty, but it is not inert. It is adjusting every moment: climbing, cooling, clearing, pulling, settling. Treat it less like a room and more like a small indoor weather system.

## Health precautions afterward

After leaving:

- Rinse off with clean water.
- Dry feet carefully; lime-rich water can irritate skin.
- Clean any cuts immediately.
- Wash clothing separately if it picked up silt or plaster dust.
- Monitor for rash, stomach upset, or respiratory irritation if you swallowed water or breathed mist for a long time.

If you have asthma or sensitivity to mold, lime dust, or humid enclosed spaces, bring medication and limit exposure.

## Cultural and ethical considerations

Because the chamber is hidden under a plain trapdoor, it may not be meant for casual visitors. It could be municipal infrastructure, household water storage, ritual architecture, or a private engineering work. Ask permission before opening the hatch. If no caretaker is present, assume the place is important and leave no trace.

This cistern is in its first season of becoming itself. Its surfaces still remember tools and hands. Its tiny algae and moss are early colonists. Its water may sustain people nearby. The right attitude is quiet usefulness: observe, do not interfere; touch only what you must; leave the water no worse than you found it.

## Best visitor mindset

Prepare to be patient.

The chamber rewards stillness: watching the deltas of silt gather behind mortar ridges, the floating cup turn in its eddy, the jars hum when currents pass their mouths, the daylight shatter and reassemble under the beams. It is not a place to conquer or explore aggressively. It is a place to descend into carefully, listen to, and understand by increments.

Go with dry hands, clean gear, a tied rope, a companion above, and enough humility to turn back if the water is rising.
