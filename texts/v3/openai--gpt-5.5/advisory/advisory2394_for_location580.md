To visit this place, prepare as if entering a drowned continent-sized ruin, not as if diving a reef or crossing a lake. The danger is not predators, weather, or violence. The danger is cold, darkness, scale, disorientation, pressure, entanglement, collapse, and the slow confidence that nothing is happening until you are already in trouble.

You should not go there alone, and you should not go there with ordinary recreational diving equipment unless you intend only the shallowest, briefest survey near a secured entry point.

## 1. Treat it as an underwater expedition, not a “visit”

There is no dry margin from within the place. No beach, no island, no ceiling pocket of breathable air you can count on. Every room, street, orchard, canal, roof, stairwell, cistern, and courtyard is submerged. That means every minute spent there depends on artificial life support.

You need:

- A support vessel, portal platform, or stable base outside the flooded environment.
- A team that remains outside and tracks you continuously.
- Redundant breathing systems.
- Redundant navigation systems.
- Thermal protection rated for long immersion in cold water.
- A hard limit on distance traveled from the entry point.
- A planned route back, not merely confidence that you can “find the way.”

This place is too broad to orient by shoreline. Once inside, distance becomes abstract. One wall looks like another. One courtyard opens into another. Streets vanish into black water. Silt can erase your trail. If you lose your direction, the ruin will not help you.

## 2. Use the right body protection

The cold is one of the main hazards. It is not dramatic cold. It will not bite immediately like mountain wind. It will enter slowly and stay.

Wear:

- A drysuit or fully sealed environmental suit.
- Electrically heated underlayers if available.
- Dry gloves or sealed gauntlets.
- Full hood or helmet.
- Thermal socks/boots.
- Backup heat packs or suit heating redundancy.
- A core temperature monitor.

Do not rely on “toughness.” Immersion cold bypasses pride. Your hands will lose dexterity, your thinking will slow, your breathing will become less efficient, and eventually you will make stupid decisions with great calm.

If the technology is available, a small one-person atmospheric diving suit or pressure-resistant exploration pod is preferable to open-water diving. The environment is not hostile in an active way, but it is enormous and indifferent. A rigid shell gives you time.

## 3. Breathing: bring more than enough, then bring another way out

Because there are no reliable air pockets, your breathing system is your life.

Minimum for direct diving:

- Primary breathing apparatus rated for cold water.
- Redundant independent breathing supply.
- Rebreather only if you are trained and have bailout gas.
- Full-face mask or helmet, not a simple mouth regulator if possible.
- CO₂ monitoring if using rebreathers.
- Anti-freeze regulator design for cold conditions.
- A strict turn-back rule based on gas reserves.

Better:

- Tethered surface-supplied breathing with emergency bailout.
- Small submersible.
- Pressurized exosuit.
- Remotely operated drones for initial mapping.

Do not trust the stillness. A quiet place can kill you by letting you spend too long admiring it.

## 4. Navigation is the central problem

This is a made continent: walls, causeways, terraces, channels, cisterns, rooms, basins. The geometry may feel meaningful, but much of it will repeat. In dim water, architecture becomes a maze even when it was once orderly.

Use several navigation systems at once:

- Physical guideline from entry point, reel-managed and secured carefully.
- Inertial navigation unit.
- Sonar mapping.
- Acoustic beacon network.
- Compass, if local physics permits.
- Depth gauge and pressure tracking.
- Route markers with lights.
- A live 3D map built as you move.
- Tether to support craft if possible.

Never enter an enclosed chamber, cistern, roofed hall, or corridor without a guideline. Never swim through a doorway “just to look” unless you can see the exit, have a line, and have permission from your remaining air supply.

The most dangerous sentence here is: “It’s only a little farther.”

## 5. Lighting: bring wide, soft, redundant light

Light barely reaches this world. The ambient grayness will not be enough. You need your own illumination, but use it thoughtfully.

Bring:

- Primary high-output dive lights.
- Backup lights on body and helmet.
- Low-angle side lights for reading relief, stairs, inscriptions, edges.
- Wide floodlights for orientation.
- Narrow beams for depth checks.
- Chemical or non-electrical emergency markers.
- Lights mounted both forward and downward.

Be careful with extremely bright light in heavy silt or mineral drift. It may blind you by reflecting off suspended particles. In those conditions, lower side lighting can be more useful than a powerful beam straight ahead.

Expect visibility to change. A clear avenue can become a gray wall if your fins disturb sediment, if a current passes, or if mineral-rich water drifts in.

## 6. Move slowly and avoid touching the floor

The surfaces are made, old, and softened. They may look like pavement, but they are not modern pavement. Mortar may be weak. Tiles may lift. Timber may collapse under a hand. Weed mats may conceal holes, cistern mouths, stairwells, or channels.

Use neutral buoyancy. Do not walk unless absolutely necessary.

Avoid:

- Kicking silt.
- Grabbing doorframes.
- Pulling on weeds.
- Standing on tile beds.
- Brushing carved surfaces.
- Entering collapsed dwellings.
- Putting weight on wooden beams.
- Passing beneath unstable lintels.
- Swimming directly over open cisterns without checking depth and current.

This place is full of vertical traps hidden in horizontal geometry: wells, cisterns, stairwells, basins, sunken courtyards, terrace drops, canal cuts. The land is flat in the large sense, but locally it is full of openings.

A roof may now be a floor. A floor may have a room beneath it. A courtyard may be a silt lid over a deeper void.

## 7. Beware entanglement

There are no animals, but there are plants, old ropes, root curtains, hanging filaments, broken timber, and narrow masonry.

Carry:

- Two cutting tools, one on each side of your body.
- Line cutters rather than only large knives.
- Entanglement training.
- A buddy close enough to help.
- Smooth equipment configuration with no dangling hoses or straps.

The vegetation is slow and soft, but that softness is deceptive. Long fronds can wrap valves, lights, fins, and tethers. Fine threads can cloud vision and snag clips. Old buried rope can still hold its twist. A curtain of weed in a doorway can become a net if you panic.

Move like you are passing through a museum made of wet lace.

## 8. Expect silence to affect your mind

The absence of animals will be unnerving. Human divers often rely on motion for emotional orientation: fish, bubbles, waves, sand, light, current, life. Here, movement belongs mostly to water and plants. Nothing startles. Nothing flees. Nothing calls. The silence is not peaceful in a familiar way; it is final.

Prepare psychologically for:

- Claustrophobia, even in broad streets.
- Agoraphobia, because the place is too vast.
- Time distortion.
- Loss of direction.
- A feeling of trespass.
- A sense that every doorway is watching despite there being no watcher.
- Fatigue from darkness.
- Grief or melancholy from the obvious labor of vanished makers.

Travelers should be screened for panic responses, depression under isolation, and poor decision-making in low-stimulus environments. Maintain voice contact if possible. Speak regularly. Report what you see. Let your team answer. Human voices will matter.

Do not underestimate the emotional force of a hand-built world with no hands left in it.

## 9. Take no fire-based equipment for field use

Nothing burns there. Do not plan around flame, heat lamps, chemical fire, combustion tools, ordinary camp stoves, or emergency flares unless specifically designed for underwater operation.

Use:

- Battery systems.
- Pressure-safe power packs.
- Waterproof heaters.
- Sealed tools.
- Cold-water compatible electronics.
- Non-burning markers.

If you need warmth, produce it inside your suit or vehicle. If you need light, use sealed lamps. If you need cutting, use mechanical or specialized underwater tools.

## 10. Respect the archaeological nature of the place

Every surface was once touched by a maker. This is not wilderness in the ordinary sense. It is an artifact so large it has become geography.

Do not loot.

Do not pry tiles from the floor, remove jars, cut beams, harvest plants, or scrape inscriptions unless you are part of a formal research mission with preservation protocols. The fact that everyone who built it is gone does not make it ownerless in the moral sense.

Bring:

- Non-contact scanners.
- Photogrammetry equipment.
- Water-safe sample containers, if sampling is authorized.
- Soft measuring tools.
- Sediment disturbance protocols.
- Conservation specialists.

If you must touch something, assume you are the first hand to touch it since the flood, and possibly the last before it breaks.

## 11. Plan for poor visibility and “underwater weather”

The place has slow currents and drifting opacity. Mineral-rich milky water may move through and erase visibility for days. You cannot outargue this.

Before entering, obtain or deploy:

- Current meters.
- Water chemistry sensors.
- Turbidity sensors.
- Temperature-depth profiles.
- Long-term observation drones.
- Weather/seasonal pattern data if any exists.
- Return beacons that remain findable in zero visibility.

If a milky drift begins, leave if you can do so safely. If visibility drops suddenly, stop moving, secure yourself without damaging the environment, switch to line-and-instrument navigation, and return along your guideline.

Never continue deeper into the ruin because “the route is probably clear.” In this place, probability is a poor substitute for a line tied to home.

## 12. Vehicles are strongly recommended

For an ordinary human traveler, the best way to see this place is from inside a small submersible or pressure-safe observation craft.

Ideal vehicle features:

- Low-thrust maneuvering to avoid damaging plants and silt layers.
- Sonar and lidar/structured-light mapping.
- Manipulator arms with delicate force feedback.
- External floodlights and low-angle archaeology lights.
- Emergency ascent or return capability.
- Acoustic communication.
- Thermal control.
- Long endurance.
- Tether option.
- Soft bumpers or standoff frames.
- Sediment-sparing propulsion.

Avoid large propellers near fragile courtyards, weed curtains, and silted rooms. A careless vehicle could do in minutes what centuries of cold water have not done.

## 13. Entry strategy

Do not begin by entering a building. Begin with open, simple geometry.

A sensible first expedition:

1. Establish base and beacon grid above or beside a known entry zone.
2. Send unmanned drones first.
3. Map a broad avenue, causeway, or terrace line.
4. Identify hazards: cisterns, holes, unstable roofs, dense vegetation, silt pockets.
5. Mark a short loop route.
6. Send human observers only after the loop is proven.
7. Limit first human visit to a short duration.
8. Exit with ample reserves.
9. Debrief immediately while memory is fresh.

The temptation will be to follow doorways. Resist it. Doorways are promises made by the dead. Some promises lead only to black rooms, silt, and trouble.

## 14. Health and contamination precautions

There are no animals, but that does not mean the water is sterile. Cold, old, low-light environments can still contain microbes, mineral hazards, toxins from ancient materials, and anaerobic sediments.

Precautions:

- Full skin coverage.
- No drinking untreated water.
- Decontamination after exit.
- Water testing for heavy metals, pathogens, acidity/alkalinity, and unusual chemistry.
- Avoid stirring black sediments in enclosed spaces.
- Do not open sealed jars or cistern deposits casually.
- Treat cuts immediately.
- Rinse and inspect gear for corrosive residues.

Ancient human-made environments may contain lead glazes, copper compounds, arsenic pigments, decayed organics, and other hazards even without modern industry.

## 15. Emergency planning

Your emergency plan should assume that rescue is difficult and slow.

Prepare for:

- Lost line.
- Failed light.
- Failed heat.
- Regulator malfunction.
- Suit leak.
- Entanglement.
- Silt-out.
- Disorientation.
- Entrapment in architecture.
- Collapse of old timber or masonry.
- Separation from buddy.
- Communication loss.
- Vehicle immobilization.

Carry or maintain access to:

- Personal locator beacon compatible with the environment.
- Acoustic pinger.
- Emergency strobes.
- Backup mask/helmet components if applicable.
- Spare cutting tools.
- Bailout breathing system.
- Thermal emergency reserve.
- Written tactile emergency procedure slate.
- Prearranged lost-contact timings.

Most importantly: have a strict abort culture. Any one serious failure ends the visit. This is not a place to improvise heroically.

## 16. What to bring for observation

If you are going as a traveler rather than a scientist, bring less than you think. You need your attention free.

Useful items:

- Helmet camera.
- Still camera with low-light capability.
- Wide-angle lens.
- Scale markers, if permitted.
- Waterproof notebook or voice recorder.
- Small non-contact scanner.
- Personal light with adjustable intensity.
- Tethered guide reel.
- Minimal, streamlined personal kit.

Do not bring loose charms, dangling straps, unnecessary bags, long scarves, jewelry, exposed cords, or anything that can catch on weed or masonry.

## 17. How to behave while there

Move slowly. Look before moving. Hover rather than land. Keep your fins high. Watch your exhaled bubbles under ceilings; they can dislodge sediment or collect under fragile roofs. Do not chase every interesting shadow.

When entering a courtyard, pause at the threshold and let your light sweep the floor, walls, upper edges, and possible exits. Check for wells. Check for hanging weed. Check for silt depth. Check whether the “floor” is actually a roof.

When passing through vegetation, part it gently with a tool or gloved hand only if necessary. Better: go around.

When you find objects—jars in rows, carved lintels, rope remnants, tile patterns—record them where they are. Their placement is part of their meaning.

## 18. The right expectations

Do not expect grandeur in the usual way. There may be no single palace, tower, statue, or central monument that explains the place. Its grandeur is cumulative: mile-long walls, endless hand-laid floors, basins repeated past sight, terraces descending by pressure rather than view, doorways made equal by water.

The emotional experience will likely come in layers:

- First, the cold and darkness.
- Then, the difficulty of seeing.
- Then, the realization that everything under you was made.
- Then, the absence of animals.
- Then, the scale.
- Then, the patience of the plants.
- Then, perhaps, the grief.

You may feel as if you are inside the memory of labor. Not a city exactly, not a seabed exactly, but a world that hands assembled and water inherited.

## 19. Who should not go

Do not visit directly if you:

- Are not trained for cold-water diving or equivalent suit/vehicle operation.
- Panic in darkness or enclosed spaces.
- Have poor spatial orientation.
- Are prone to impulsive exploration.
- Cannot follow abort rules.
- Have cardiovascular or respiratory vulnerability.
- Cannot tolerate long silence and low sensory stimulation.
- Are unwilling to leave artifacts untouched.

Such people can still experience the place through drone feed, submersible windows, remote mapping, or guided short routes near base.

## 20. The simplest safe advice

If you are an ordinary human multiverse traveler, the safest preparation is:

Go in a small submersible first.  
Use drones before that.  
Do not swim far.  
Do not enter buildings casually.  
Stay warm.  
Stay tethered.  
Keep redundant light and air.  
Touch almost nothing.  
Leave before you are tired.  

And remember: the place is not empty. It is full of water, age, plants, and the shape of vanished work. It does not need to attack you. It only needs you to forget, for a few minutes, that you are a warm-blooded air-breathing creature in a cold drowned world.
