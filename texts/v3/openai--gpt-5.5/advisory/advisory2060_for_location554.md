To visit this city safely, prepare as if you are entering a sacred dry laboratory, a wind instrument large enough to live in, and a place where your own body is considered dangerously wet.

The most important fact is this: **an ordinary human is a walking reservoir of forbidden chemistry.** Sweat, tears, blood, urine, spilled canteen water, damp cloth, condensation from breath — all of these matter here. They are not merely impolite. They can damage walls, crops, roots, stored powders, and foundations. Your preparation should revolve around preventing moisture, dust injury, glare injury, hearing damage, and cultural offense.

## 1. Do not arrive unannounced

Do not try to “discover” the seam and climb in alone.

The city is hidden for a reason, and the entrance is likely watched even if you see no one. From above, the salt plain looks empty, but sound, scent, vibration, and dust movement will betray you. Arrange contact through a guide, trader, interpreter, or local intermediary before approaching.

You should have, at minimum:

- A local guide or sponsor.
- Permission to enter.
- A declared visit length.
- An agreement about how your water, bodily fluids, food, waste, and equipment will be handled.
- Knowledge of the current wind/pollen season.

If you cannot secure permission, do not enter. The place is fragile, and a careless visitor can do real damage.

## 2. Hydrate before arrival — but do not carry loose water inside

This is the central difficulty.

A human needs water, especially in heat, glare, salt dust, and constant wind. But the city forbids liquid water in the hollow. Therefore:

- **Hydrate thoroughly before crossing the salt plain.**
- Eat salted food carefully; do not over-salt yourself before arrival.
- Avoid alcohol, diuretics, stimulants, or anything that increases sweating or urination.
- Plan a short first visit: a few hours, not a full day.
- If you must carry water for survival, it should be in **sealed, inspected, double-contained vessels** and left at the entrance or in an approved outer cache unless the city explicitly permits otherwise.
- Never open a flask, hydration bladder, thermos, medical saline pouch, or wet ration inside the hollow.

Ask whether the city has an approved “wet chamber,” “outer dryness room,” or traveler’s vestibule where humans may drink under supervision. If not, assume you cannot drink until you leave.

## 3. Control your own moisture

Your body will sweat. Your eyes may water from glare and dust. Your nose may run. You may bleed if scraped by salt or ceramic edges. All of this must be planned for.

Wear:

- A dry, absorbent inner layer that captures sweat.
- A loose outer robe or travel garment that shades you and slows evaporation.
- Desiccant or ash-cloth pads at armpits, back, feet, and groin.
- A face veil or dry respirator that traps moisture from breath without dripping.
- Gloves, to prevent cuts and sweat marks on walls, ladders, roots, and tools.
- Head covering with neck protection.
- Closed boots with non-slip soles suitable for brittle salt crust, ladders, and polished steps.

Carry, in sealed dry packets:

- Sterile dry gauze.
- Powdered lime or an approved local blood-drying powder.
- Ash cloths for tears, sweat, and spills.
- Dry wound dressing.
- No liquid antiseptic unless sealed and specifically permitted.
- No gels, creams, wet wipes, lotions, or sprays.

If you cry, wipe tears immediately and discreetly with ash cloth. If you bleed, stop, step aside, and let your guide handle the protocol. Do not drip on the floor.

## 4. Protect your hearing and bones from the sound

The city is not “loud” in the ordinary sense. It is structurally loud. The wind moves through carved vents, root tunnels, flutes, galleries, cracks, and courts. In some places the noise will press against your chest and teeth.

Bring:

- High-quality earplugs.
- Over-ear hearing protection if compatible with local etiquette.
- A vibration-aware communication method: writing board, chalk slate, gesture cards, or tablet in a sealed dustproof case.
- A way to secure loose objects so they do not rattle, clang, or blow away.
- A chest strap or snug outer layer if you are sensitive to pressure vibration.

Do not assume you can speak. In many places, speech will be useless or rude. Learn basic signs before you enter:

- Welcome.
- Thanks.
- Permission?
- Stop.
- Danger.
- Water/liquid.
- Injury/blood.
- Trade.
- I do not understand.
- I will follow.
- I will not touch.

Remember: **silence is sacred because it is rare.** If the wind suddenly stops, do not speak, laugh, cough loudly, take photographs, or ask what is happening. Stop where you are. Watch your guide. Let the locals listen.

## 5. Protect your eyes from the light

The light is relentless and comes from every direction. The salt roof glows; the walls reflect; shadows are sharp and strange. Ordinary sunglasses may not be enough.

Bring:

- Glacier glasses or wraparound polarized eye protection.
- Side shields.
- A spare pair.
- A broad-brimmed hat or veil.
- Non-liquid eye protection strategy: avoid eye drops unless explicitly permitted and only use outside the hollow.

Do not underestimate glare. You can burn your eyes here without ever looking directly at the sun.

## 6. Protect your lungs from dust, pollen, and mineral powders

The city’s ecology reproduces through dust. Some pollen is food. Some is dye. Some is poison. During release events, terraces can fill with green, gold, black, or violet powders until visibility vanishes.

Bring:

- A dry respirator or excellent dust mask.
- Spare filters sealed against salt dust.
- A face veil acceptable to local custom.
- Eye protection that seals reasonably well.
- A plan for pollen alarms.

Ask before entry:

- Is this a release season?
- What colors of pollen are dangerous?
- Where are the nearest screened shelters?
- What signal means “seal your mouth now”?
- What should outsiders do during a pollen release?

If pollen begins to pour upward from terraces or the air becomes fragrant and opaque, do not stand around observing. Retreat behind screens. Seal your face. Wait.

## 7. Dress for heat without relying on sweat

The environment is warm, bright, and dry. Humans normally cool themselves by sweating, but here sweating is both physically risky and culturally troublesome.

Dress for passive cooling:

- Loose layered clothing.
- Pale outer colors.
- Full skin coverage.
- Ventilated but dust-filtering face covering.
- Insulated soles; salt flats and mineral floors can become painfully hot.
- No exposed metal jewelry that can heat in sun or mark local surfaces.

Move slowly. Rest often. Use shade structures. Do not try to “push through” heat stress. The city’s people know how to live here; you do not.

Signs you should leave immediately:

- Dizziness.
- Chills despite heat.
- Confusion.
- Cessation of sweating combined with overheating.
- Headache with nausea.
- Muscle cramps.
- Dark urine before entry or afterward.
- Disorientation from sound and glare.

## 8. Prepare for the salt plain outside

Before you even reach the seam, the outer landscape is dangerous.

Hazards include:

- Extreme glare.
- Heat shimmer and poor distance judgment.
- Brittle crust that may crack underfoot.
- Hidden seams or collapses.
- Lack of landmarks.
- Dehydration.
- Salt dust abrasion.
- Navigation failure.

Bring:

- A guide or reliable coordinates.
- Sun protection.
- Boots with traction.
- Emergency water kept outside the city’s forbidden zone.
- Navigation instruments protected from glare and static.
- A ground probe if crossing unknown crust.
- Emergency shade cloth.
- A rescue plan.

Do not wander the flats at noon unless locals advise it. Dawn and late afternoon are safer for approach.

## 9. Expect to be searched

This is not an insult. It is sensible.

At entry, expect locals to inspect you for:

- Water skins.
- Flasks.
- Wet food.
- Fruit.
- Gel capsules.
- Medical liquids.
- Blood or sweat leakage.
- Damp clothing.
- Leaking batteries or chemical devices.
- Condensation chambers.
- Unsealed biological samples.
- Soil stuck to boots.
- Seeds or spores from elsewhere.

Submit calmly. Declare everything. Hidden water is a serious offense.

If you must carry medicine, explain it in advance. Dry tablets are better than liquid medications. Inhalers, injectors, gels, and ampoules require negotiation.

## 10. Choose equipment carefully

The city is full of salt dust, static, vibration, heat, and fine powders. Many devices will fail or offend.

Good equipment:

- Ceramic or polymer writing slate.
- Chalk, charcoal, or dry stylus.
- Dustproof camera if photography is allowed.
- Sealed light source.
- Dry compass or robust navigation marker.
- Ear protection.
- Respirator.
- Gloves.
- Cloth bags, not crinkly plastic if noise matters.
- Non-leaking dry rations.
- Small trade goods approved by guide.

Questionable equipment:

- Electronics with fans.
- Anything that vents vapor.
- Anything with liquid coolant.
- Loose metal tools that clang.
- Scented products.
- Wet wipes.
- Spray bottles.
- Fuel canisters.
- Chemical hand warmers/coolers.
- Adhesives that soften or off-gas.
- Batteries that may leak.

Do not bring open flame unless invited. Dry fiber, pollen, dust, and resin may be flammable.

## 11. Bring suitable gifts

Metal is rare and treasured, but do not assume all metal is acceptable. A gaudy steel object may be less welcome than a small useful copper pin.

Possible gifts, with permission:

- Copper wire, pins, hinges, or bells.
- Fine dry pigments.
- Durable ceramic tools.
- Good carving needles.
- Lenses or glare-safe glass.
- Dry archival tablets.
- High-quality fiber cord.
- Polished stone blades.
- Salt-resistant writing tools.
- Stories, maps, or songs rendered visually rather than sung aloud.

Avoid bringing:

- Water.
- Wine.
- Oil.
- Fresh fruit.
- cut flowers.
- Wet clay.
- Living plants from elsewhere.
- Soil.
- Perfume.
- Anything moldy or damp.

A gift should be dry, clean, stable, and declared.

## 12. Learn the laws that matter most

Remember these rules as if they were safety instructions, because they are.

### Do not bring water into the hollow

This includes “just a little,” “only for medicine,” “only in my bag,” and “only to wash my hands.” Ask first. Assume no.

### Do not block a singing vent

Do not lean bags against vents. Do not sit in carved openings. Do not cover holes to reduce noise. Do not stuff cloth into cracks. The vents are infrastructure, weather system, alarm system, and sacred instrument.

### Do not cut, pull, step on, or sample roots

Especially black-roots. They may be structural supports, communication lines, living property, ancestral growth, or parts of gardens older than the buildings around them.

### Do not speak in the white court

Even if no one can hear you. Especially because no one can hear you. The restraint matters.

### Do not touch powders casually

Some are food, some dye, some medicine, some poison, some seed, some ancestor, some building material.

### Do not assume empty means abandoned

The city has many empty courts, silent towers, and unused-looking chambers. They may be wind corridors, light basins, pollen paths, cooling structures, shrines, nurseries, or ancestral niches.

## 13. Move like a guest inside a musical instrument

Your body changes air currents. Your bag can block a tone. Your cloak can snap in the wind. Your footsteps vibrate through floors where people communicate by drum and foot-sense.

Inside:

- Walk where your guide walks.
- Keep close, but do not crowd.
- Step lightly.
- Do not lean on walls unless invited.
- Do not sit in vents or root arches.
- Secure veils, straps, and papers.
- Keep your hands visible and gloved.
- Ask before entering gardens, roof ladders, root chambers, courts, shrines, workshops, or towers.
- Do not hum, whistle, clap, or test echoes.
- Do not shout for fun.

The city already has a voice. You are not there to add yours.

## 14. Understand the gardens

The “plants” here are not desert cacti in the ordinary sense. They are mineral-fed, dry, articulate growths that eat salt, carbon, heat, and air. Some release static sparks if cut. Some have leaves like mica, brass, ceramic, or tubes. Some roots are used later as rooms after they lace the salt.

Do not water them. Do not touch them to see if they are “real.” Do not cut samples. Do not scrape mineral bloom unless invited. Do not breathe deeply over pollen beds. Do not compare them aloud to dead plants or fake plants; they are the city’s agriculture, architecture, ancestry, and wealth.

If offered a dry fruit, dust-cake, resin bead, or mineral-fed food, ask your guide if it is safe for humans. Your digestive system may not tolerate local salts, alkaloids, or powders.

## 15. Food and waste

Bring dry, low-odor, non-crumbly food only if permitted.

Good traveler foods:

- Dry flatbread.
- Hard biscuits.
- Roasted grains.
- Nuts if allergens are not an issue.
- Dry protein cakes.
- Salt-balanced rations that do not make you thirstier.

Avoid:

- Soup.
- Fruit.
- Cheese that sweats oil.
- sauces.
- wet meat.
- anything sticky.
- anything that drips.
- anything with strong smell.
- anything that produces crumbs mistaken for pollen or seed.

Waste is a major issue. You cannot casually urinate inside. Arrange this before entry.

You may need:

- A sealed personal waste system.
- Absorbent dry packs.
- A scheduled exit to an approved outer latrine.
- Local supervision for disposal.

Do not improvise.

## 16. Respect the dead in circulation

The dead are placed in wall niches behind root chambers. Their minerals return slowly through plants, resins, fruits, fans, and fibers. This is not metaphorical to the locals; it is civic continuity.

Therefore:

- Do not touch wall niches.
- Do not joke about eating ancestors.
- Do not ask whether a specific fruit “is someone’s grandmother.”
- Do not photograph funerary niches without permission.
- If offered festival food with ancestral significance, receive it respectfully even if you cannot eat it. Ask your guide how to decline safely.

A good phrase to learn in sign or writing: **“May the city keep them in circulation.”**

## 17. Navigation inside

The city is small but layered. You can walk across it in minutes and still become lost because verticality, ladders, terraces, hollowed roots, wind channels, and light shafts distort your sense of direction.

Use:

- A guide.
- Visual landmarks.
- Chalk marks only if permitted.
- A tether in high-noise or pollen conditions.
- Prearranged meeting points.
- A written slate with your host’s name and district.

Do not enter old root-tunnels alone. Some are structural. Some are too narrow. Some carry air pressure changes. Some may lead into chemically unstable salt.

## 18. Photography, recording, and sampling

Ask first.

This city hides under a salt plain. Its secrecy may be part of its survival. Recording the entrance, mapping the seam, or publishing coordinates could be a grave betrayal.

Likely restrictions:

- No images of entrance seams.
- No recordings of sacred wind courts.
- No samples of roots, powders, salt tablets, dead-wall material, or pollen.
- No mapping ventilation routes.
- No sound capture during silence omens.
- No bright flash photography.

If permitted to record, protect your equipment from salt dust. The city’s air will abrade lenses and infiltrate mechanisms.

## 19. Health risks after leaving

Once you leave, do not assume you are fine.

After exit:

- Drink slowly; do not overcorrect with huge volumes of water at once.
- Rinse salt dust from skin and hair outside the city’s boundary.
- Irrigate eyes only after leaving the forbidden dry zone.
- Check for cuts; salt may hide abrasions.
- Monitor breathing for mineral dust irritation.
- Clean respirator filters.
- Inspect electronics and clothing for pollen.
- Quarantine any powders until identified.
- Watch for delayed allergic reactions.
- Rest your hearing; the roar may leave after-sounds or tinnitus.

You may dream in tones for a night or two. That is not necessarily mystical. It may simply be your nervous system continuing to map the city by vibration.

## 20. What to expect emotionally

The city may feel contradictory.

It is lush but dry. Urban but sparsely occupied. Ancient but small. Bright but hidden. Deafening but socially quiet. Empty courts may feel abandoned until you realize they are deliberately open for air, light, sound, and plant geometry.

Do not mistake low population for decline. Do not mistake quiet people for unfriendly people. Do not mistake dry agriculture for poverty. Do not mistake the absence of water for absence of life.

The city is alive in ways a wet-world traveler may not immediately recognize.

Do not bring inside without explicit permission:

## Final advice

Enter humbly and briefly the first time.

Let the city teach your senses: the glare through salt, the vibration in the soles, the dry tick of leaves turning, the pressure of wind in the courts, the smell of resin and chalk at night. You are visiting a place where dryness is not absence but law, medium, architecture, agriculture, and faith.

The safest traveler is one who leaves no wet mark, blocks no vent, cuts no root, speaks little, listens with the body, and remembers that in this city even emptiness is being used.
