An ordinary human should prepare as if visiting a high-altitude volcanic construction site in total darkness, during an earthquake, inside a chemical kiln.

This is not a place to “hike.” It is a place to enter briefly, instrumented, sealed, and with a reliable way out.

## 1. Do not rely on the local air

The air is warm, thin, dry, and chemically hostile. It may contain sulfur compounds, alkaline dust, volcanic glass, heavy gases in hollows, and invisible pockets of asphyxiant or corrosive vapor.

Bring:

- A sealed breathing system, not merely a filter mask.
- Full-face protection with positive pressure.
- Spare oxygen or a rebreather rated for toxic atmospheres.
- Gas sensors for oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, chlorine-like compounds, and unknown volatiles if available.
- An emergency hood or backup respirator in case your main suit seal fails.

Do not remove your helmet “just for a moment.” The danger is not only lack of oxygen; the dust and chemistry may injure lungs, eyes, and mucous membranes quickly.

## 2. Wear a sealed environmental suit

The place is dry enough to desiccate exposed skin, dusty enough to abrade seals and joints, and chemically active enough to tarnish minerals. Treat the dust as glassy, caustic, and electrostatically clingy.

Your suit should include:

- Abrasion-resistant outer layer.
- Chemical-resistant seals.
- Thermal insulation in both directions: from hot ground below and possibly cooler exposed air above.
- Reinforced knees, elbows, gloves, and boots.
- Dust skirts or gaiters over boot joints.
- Helmet with anti-scratch visor and redundant lights.
- Cooling system if the heat load is high.
- Radiation monitor, because some warmth seems to come from the rock itself or from unseen energetic processes.

Avoid ordinary mountaineering clothing. Fabric will load with mineral dust, zippers will clog, skin will crack, and lungs will suffer.

## 3. Prepare for altitude, but do not trust acclimatization alone

This plateau is at extreme elevation. Even if the air were clean, a normal human could suffer hypoxia, dizziness, impaired judgment, headache, nausea, and loss of coordination.

Before visiting:

- Train for high-altitude movement.
- Know the symptoms of hypoxia and altitude sickness.
- Use supplemental oxygen from the start.
- Keep exertion low.
- Move slowly and deliberately.
- Assign a companion or automated monitor to watch your behavior; hypoxia often feels like confidence, not distress.

The warmth may deceive you. This is not a cozy highland. It is a thin-air furnace.

## 4. Bring your own light, and more than you think you need

The darkness is not foggy; it is clear, dry, and enormous. Distances will be hard to judge. Shadows may be absent. The ground may glow faintly from cracks, but not enough to safely navigate by.

Bring:

- Helmet lights with wide and narrow beams.
- Redundant handheld lights.
- Area floodlights or deployable beacons.
- Infrared/thermal imaging.
- Lidar or radar mapping if possible.
- Reflective markers or active transponders for route marking.

Do not navigate by distant glows. A red seam may be nearby or kilometers away. A black shelf may be a step, a cliff edge, or the side of a newly lifted ridge.

## 5. Never assume the ground is stable

This is the central danger.

The land is still forming. Crusts may be hollow. Basins may be skins over hot cavities. Walls may rise while you watch. Cracks may open without warning. Shelves may tilt, lift, or collapse. Pale polygonal plates may look like dried mud but behave like ceramic lids over heat and gas.

Carry:

- A long probe or staff for testing surfaces.
- Ground-penetrating scanner if available.
- Seismic sensor.
- Thermal camera for detecting hot voids and thin crust.
- Climbing rope, harness, anchors, and retrieval line.
- Lightweight bridging ladders or portable mats for suspect crusts.
- Boots with heat-resistant soles and aggressive traction.

Movement rules:

- Do not step on pale crusts unless tested.
- Avoid glowing seams.
- Avoid newly extruded glossy material.
- Avoid basins where gases can pool.
- Avoid the bases of towers, chimneys, cliffs, and leaning structures.
- Stay off narrow ledges during tremors.
- Do not enter caves or rectangular openings without remote scouting.
- Treat every “floor” as potentially temporary.

The ground here is not old enough to have earned your trust.

## 6. Expect violent wind without warning

Wind moves in sudden sheets and drags mineral dust low across the surface. Because there is no vegetation, no water, and little softness, gusts may arrive without the usual cues.

Prepare with:

- Eye and visor protection.
- Dust-resistant suit joints.
- Low center of gravity when walking exposed ridges.
- Tethers near chasms.
- Anchored camp equipment.
- Navigation beacons that remain visible in dust veils.

Do not stand near drop-offs during gusts. The margins of this plateau open into immense darkness, and there may be no slope or ledge below to catch you.

## 7. Use drones or probes before entering an area

A human body is the wrong first instrument for this place.

Send ahead:

- Small flying drones, if the air density permits.
- Crawling or hopping probes for unstable ground.
- Thermal scouts.
- Gas samplers.
- Seismic pingers.
- Mapping beacons.

Use probes especially before approaching:

- Pale basins.
- Solitary towers.
- Newly formed walls.
- Hollow cones.
- Black glass blisters.
- Chasm-edge cuboid structures.
- Any apparent doorway or arch.

If a probe is lost, treat that as useful information, not as a challenge.

## 8. Do not trust the “structures”

The pillars, arches, rings, walls, and cuboid masses may look architectural, but they are not necessarily safe or meaningful in a human way. They may be geological accidents, failed formations, pressure vents, crystallization patterns, or the remains of something incomprehensible.

Prepare mentally to resist interpretation.

Practical rules:

- Do not shelter under arches.
- Do not lean against pillars.
- Do not camp inside cuboid openings.
- Do not assume rings or walls mark safe paths.
- Do not touch glowing or warm surfaces bare-handed.
- Do not collect samples from active seams without proper containment.
- Photograph and scan before approaching.

The place imitates ruins before it has inhabitants. That makes it emotionally powerful, but also dangerous. Your pattern-seeking mind will want to call things “doors,” “roads,” “walls,” and “altars.” Let your instruments disagree.

## 9. Bring all water, food, and shelter

There is no useful ecology. No vegetation, no animals, no surface water, no soil, no shade worth trusting. Nothing here will feed you or help you.

Bring:

- More water than normal, because the environment is desiccating.
- Electrolytes.
- High-calorie food that can be eaten inside a suit system or sealed shelter.
- Waste containment.
- A portable shelter with its own atmosphere.
- Ground insulation.
- Anchoring equipment suitable for hard rock and shifting slabs.
- Emergency bivouac module or rigid pod.

Do not expect caves to be safe shelters. They may breathe heat, gas, or collapse.

## 10. Camp only if absolutely necessary

A short survey is far wiser than an overnight stay.

If you must establish a temporary base:

- Choose a broad, elevated, non-glowing rock surface away from cracks, basins, chasm rims, towers, and fresh extrusions.
- Survey the area thermally and seismically first.
- Place seismic alarms around the perimeter.
- Mark evacuation routes in several directions.
- Keep suits ready.
- Sleep in shifts.
- Anchor the shelter, but do not anchor it so rigidly that a ground shift tears it apart.
- Maintain a powered beacon to guide return through darkness.

Avoid low hollows. Heavy gases may collect there invisibly.

## 11. Navigation must assume the landscape changes

Your outward path may not exist on return. A crack may open, a wall may rise, a shelf may tilt, or dust may erase markers.

Use:

- Inertial navigation.
- GPS-equivalent only if the world supports it; do not assume it does.
- Beacon chain back to the portal or landing site.
- Lidar mapping updated continuously.
- Physical markers that can survive heat and wind.
- A strict “turnaround rule”: return before half your oxygen, power, or daylight-equivalent capacity is used.

Map in three dimensions. Height changes here matter, and the plateau may gain or lose traversable routes quickly.

## 12. Protect your lungs and equipment from dust

The dust is likely one of the most persistent hazards. It may be abrasive, alkaline, electrically clingy, and contaminated with fine glass or reactive minerals.

Preparation:

- Use sealed containers.
- Use dust-rated bearings and joints.
- Carry cleaning tools for seals and visors.
- Bring airlock bags for samples.
- Have a decontamination procedure before re-entering your vehicle, portal chamber, or home environment.
- Do not bring dusty gear directly into living quarters.

Assume the dust can damage machinery and biology long after leaving.

## 13. Treat heat as uneven and deceptive

There is warmth everywhere, but not evenly. A safe-looking plate may conceal a red-black cavity. A glossy ridge may still be cooling. A crack may radiate enough heat to damage boots or suit materials.

Bring:

- Thermal camera.
- Heat-resistant boots.
- Temperature probes.
- Gloves rated for hot minerals.
- Suit alarms for localized overheating.

Do not sit on the ground unless you have checked it. Do not place oxygen tanks or batteries near warm seams.

## 14. Prepare for seismic events

The plateau does not merely quake; it rearranges.

During tremors:

- Drop low immediately.
- Move away from cliffs, towers, arches, and upright stones if possible.
- Do not run blindly; new cracks may open.
- Watch for dust rings indicating gas or pressure release.
- After the tremor, reassess all terrain. Do not assume your previous path is intact.
- Check suit seals, oxygen lines, boot soles, and communication equipment.

If the ground begins to glow in a line, withdraw perpendicular to the seam. Do not follow it out of curiosity.

## 15. Psychological preparation matters

This place will feel profoundly empty, but not peaceful. It has no life, no weather in the comforting sense, no familiar sky, no vegetation, no animal sound. Yet it moves. It makes forms that resemble intention. It may feel like walking through the first draft of a world, or through ruins built before history.

Expect:

- Time disorientation due to lack of sun or ordinary day-night rhythm.
- Scale confusion.
- Loneliness amplified by silence.
- Anxiety from low-frequency ground sounds.
- Pareidolia: seeing purpose in geological forms.
- A strange grief or awe at niches with no inhabitants.
- A temptation to stay and watch the land “become.”

Plan check-ins. Use mission timers. Keep conversations with teammates regular. If alone, maintain audio logs to stabilize attention and memory.

## 16. Scientific and ethical caution

If there is life here, it may be subtle: mineral films, chemical patterns, crystallization cycles, subsurface chemistries. Do not assume sterility simply because nothing walks or grows.

Before collecting:

- Photograph in place.
- Record temperature, gas composition, and location.
- Avoid contaminating cracks or films with Earth microbes.
- Use sterile tools.
- Seal samples immediately.
- Limit disturbance.

This may be a world before biology, or one where geology is doing something that resembles biology. Either way, tread lightly.

## 17. Recommended expedition profile

For an ordinary human traveler, the safest visit would be brief and highly supported.

Ideal team:

- At least three people.
- One geologist/volcanologist or equivalent.
- One medic trained in altitude and toxic exposure.
- One engineer/technician for suit and sensor maintenance.
- Remote support if possible.

Ideal duration:

- Initial visit: 30–90 minutes outside the landing/portal zone.
- Longer surveys only after multiple unmanned scans.
- Overnight stay only with a hardened mobile habitat or return portal immediately available.

Ideal transport:

- Hover-capable or legged vehicle if available.
- Avoid wheeled vehicles unless terrain is mapped; sharp glass, cracks, and crusts will punish tires.
- Maintain a direct evacuation route.

## 18. Emergency priorities

If something goes wrong, your order of concern should be:

1. Breathing system integrity.
2. Ground stability.
3. Distance from heat/gas source.
4. Navigation back to exit.
5. Suit contamination or damage.
6. Injury treatment.

Carry emergency items on your body, not only in a pack:

- Backup oxygen.
- Beacon.
- Cutting tool.
- Light.
- Emergency sealant patches.
- Thermal blanket/suit liner.
- Pain control and basic trauma kit.
- Tether reel.
- Compact water supply.

If you fall through a crust and survive, do not struggle upward blindly; the edges may cut and collapse. Signal, brace, check heat, and use rope retrieval.

## 19. What to avoid above all

Avoid:

- Pale cracked basins.
- Fresh glossy black extrusions.
- Glowing seams.
- Low hollows.
- Chasm rims during wind or tremors.
- Apparent caves or doorways.
- Standing under arches or near towers.
- Touching mineral films.
- Removing helmet or gloves.
- Traveling without a beacon trail.
- Staying longer because the landscape is beautiful.

The beauty is real, but it is not hospitable.

## 20. The right attitude

Go as a visitor to a world that is not dead, but not alive in any humanly comforting way. It is active without being animate. It builds without planning. It makes walls that are not shelters, towers that are not monuments, basins that remember water they never held.

Your preparation should be humble, sealed, redundant, and temporary.

Do not ask, “Can I survive here as in a desert?”

Ask instead: “Can I briefly pass through the workshop of a planet while it is still hot, unfinished, and indifferent to witnesses?”

If the answer is yes, go carefully, leave markers, take readings, touch little, and leave before the ground changes its mind.
