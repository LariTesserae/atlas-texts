To visit this city, prepare as if you are going to a desert, a mine, a kiln, a cave, and a foreign household all at once. The danger is not one dramatic monster or obvious catastrophe. The danger is accumulation: heat, thirst, bad air, dust, disorientation, irritant resins, narrow passages, and the social damage caused by misunderstanding what is precious there.

This is not an oasis. Do not treat it like one.

## 1. Bring your own water — and hide it politely

The city has no water. Not “little water,” not “scarce wells,” not “expensive cisterns.” None.

An ordinary human visitor must bring enough water for the entire visit, the approach, the return, and emergency delay. The inhabitants may have customs for moisture-rich outsiders, but you should not assume they can help you. To them, your body is a walking reservoir. Sweat, tears, spilled drink, wet cloth, and open canteens may be fascinating, disgusting, sacred, dangerous, or socially obscene depending on local custom.

Bring:

- A sealed hydration bladder worn under clothing.
- Backup hard canteens that cannot be crushed.
- Electrolytes, salt balance tablets, and oral rehydration salts.
- A compact emergency still or condensation recycler if your technology allows it.
- Moisture-retaining clothing.
- No open cups, no dangling wet skins, no leaking bottle caps.

Drink privately. Do not splash water on your face in public. Do not pour it onto the ground. Do not offer water casually as a gift unless you fully understand the consequences. In a place where water is mythical, offering it may be generous, insulting, religiously disruptive, or politically catastrophic.

If you must give water to someone, do it discreetly and through a trusted local intermediary.

## 2. Expect severe heat, but not open-desert heat

The surface is bright, exposed, and lethal in the ordinary desert way. The city below is different. It is a stored, close, architectural heat. The walls radiate. The floor breathes. Kilns, vents, bodies, lamps, and mineral gardens all contribute. You may feel as if you have entered the inside of a clay oven that has learned to inhale.

Prepare for:

- Heat exhaustion.
- Heat stroke.
- Salt imbalance.
- Dry airway irritation.
- Skin cracking.
- Eye inflammation.
- Burns from walls, tools, vents, or kiln surfaces.
- Fatigue from moving through hot, tight spaces.

Wear loose, layered, breathable heat gear. Avoid synthetic fabrics that melt or trap sweat unless they are designed for extreme heat. Linen, treated desert cloth, ceramic-fiber travel gear, or equivalent multiverse equipment is appropriate.

You want clothing that:

- Covers all skin.
- Shields from dust and vapor.
- Allows slow evaporation without soaking the outer layer.
- Does not snag easily on roots, ladders, and narrow passages.
- Can be shaken clean of mineral powder.

A broad surface-desert hat may be useful above, but below it may be too wide. Bring a collapsible head covering or tight hood instead.

Gloves are essential. So are protective sleeves. Many surfaces will look harmless and be hot, caustic, resin-coated, or sharp with salt bloom.

## 3. Bring serious respiratory protection

The city is alive with powders, resins, vapors, ash, alkali gas, sulfurous breath, and whatever compounds rise from the lower vents. The locals know which smells mean danger. You will not.

At minimum, bring:

- A full-face respirator with replaceable filters.
- Filters rated for particulates, acid gases, organic vapors, and alkaline dust.
- Backup masks.
- Eye protection that seals.
- A scarf or veil for social blending, but do not rely on cloth alone for safety.
- Portable air-quality sensors if available.

The lower vents are especially dangerous. A gas pulse may be invisible. A sweet, resinous, peppery smell may be ordinary plant exhalation — or the beginning of respiratory distress. Alkali gases can damage lungs before panic begins. Carbon dioxide or other heavy gases may pool in lower rooms. Hot, oxygen-poor air may make you confused before you feel afraid.

If the wind organs sing, stop talking, stop exploring, and follow the locals.

Do not enter lower rooms without a guide. Do not lean over vents. Do not speak into shafts. The local rule is not superstition: gases can carry, displace, and stun.

## 4. Learn the vent warnings before anything else

Before you admire the architecture, taste the food, or ask questions about the mineral gardens, learn the vent sounds.

You should know, at minimum:

- Which tone means seal the lower rooms.
- Which tone means the gas composition is changing.
- Which tone means climb immediately.
- Which shutters close during a vent event.
- Which corridors become chimneys.
- Which courtyards are safe gathering points.
- Which masks locals use and when.

Ask a resident to explain the wind organs. Pay them well. Repeat the warning sounds back until they are satisfied.

If a deep continuous roar begins from below, do not wait to collect belongings. Climb.

## 5. Hire a local guide

This is not optional.

The city is narrow, vertical, layered, and confusing. Some streets are former roofs. Some stairways end in sealed walls. Some walls are alive. Some alcoves may be shrines, seed vaults, family chambers, abandoned dwellings, or hazardous vents. You cannot reliably tell the difference.

A guide will prevent you from:

- Stepping into a heat shaft.
- Touching a taboo wall-root.
- Entering a sealed vent chamber.
- Bringing the wrong salt into a seed vault.
- Standing where pods may burst.
- Blocking a shade lane.
- Sitting in a family alcove.
- Walking under a resin harvest.
- Getting trapped during a pressure change.

Choose a guide who is recognized by locals, not merely someone willing to take your payment. In such a small and old place, legitimacy matters.

## 6. Respect shade as a shared resource

One of the central rules is: do not waste shade.

In wetter worlds, shade is merely comfort. Here, shade is infrastructure. It preserves bodies, food, seeds, resins, walls, tools, and sleep. A shaded ledge or passage may be as important as a public fountain elsewhere.

Do not:

- Stand idly in a narrow shaded passage if people need to pass.
- Move shade cloths.
- Open screens “for a better look.”
- Hold doors open.
- Sit in the coolest alcove without invitation.
- Set hot gear against a shaded storage wall.
- Block airflow baffles.

Your instinct may be to seek shade constantly. That is reasonable, but do it under guidance. There may be public shade and private shade, working shade and sacred shade, emergency shade and drying shade. They are not interchangeable.

## 7. Do not touch the gardens casually

The mineral gardens are not decorations. They are food, insulation, ventilation regulators, fuel, medicine, warning system, building material, and possibly kin or shrine depending on local belief.

They also may be chemically hazardous.

The “plants” do not contain watery sap. They contain oils, resins, volatile compounds, crystalline gels, irritant dusts, and mineral accumulations. Cutting one open may release vapor. Ripe pods may burst. Root-filaments may migrate toward new plaster or mineral salts. Some growths can damage buildings if wounded incorrectly.

Do not:

- Pick leaves.
- Snap stems.
- Brush against root curtains.
- Collect pods without permission.
- Bring foreign fertilizer.
- Offer water to plants.
- Touch glossy green-black wall sheen.
- Lean on living insulation mats.
- Scrape mineral lichens for souvenirs.

If invited to help tend a plant, watch first. Their version of “watering” may involve ash, salts, heat, venting, or reflective foil-leaf. Do not improvise.

## 8. Be careful with salts and powders

The surface salt-flat may look inert, but the city’s rules specifically warn against bringing surface salt into seed vaults. Some salts “wake the wrong growth.”

That phrase should make you cautious.

Before descending, clean your boots, cuffs, packs, and tools. Use a sealed outer gaiter over footwear if possible. Do not track surface crust into storage areas. Do not open sample bags in the city. Do not scatter powders, even accidentally.

Carry your own materials in sealed containers:

- Food.
- Medicines.
- Electrolytes.
- Soil or biological samples from other worlds.
- Cleaning agents.
- Metal powders.
- Chalk.
- Spices.
- Tobacco or incense.
- Fire-starting compounds.

Many ordinary travel substances could have unpredictable effects in a city built around mineral-feeding organisms.

## 9. Move slowly, especially at noon

The locals move slowly during the brightest hours and more quickly in morning and evening. Copy them.

Do not mistake the dimness below for coolness. The fissure is a heat trap. A visitor who charges around exploring will dehydrate, overheat, and begin making bad decisions.

Pace yourself:

- Rest often.
- Drink before you feel thirsty.
- Monitor urine color if possible.
- Eat salty food.
- Avoid alcohol or diuretics.
- Do not climb rapidly from lower levels to upper slit in peak heat.
- Schedule exploration at dawn and evening.
- Use noon for stillness, conversation, or sleep.

If you stop sweating, become confused, feel chills despite heat, develop a headache, or lose coordination, treat it as an emergency.

## 10. Prepare for tight, vertical urban space

There is no wheel traffic. Lanes are narrow. Stairs are steep and worn. Ladders may be polished by generations of bare feet. Platforms may have low ceilings. Rooms are bitten into walls. Courtyards may be no wider than a blanket.

Pack accordingly.

Bring:

- A soft-sided pack, not a rigid frame.
- No dangling tools.
- Knee and elbow protection.
- Good grip footwear that tolerates hot stone.
- A compact headlamp with red/amber settings.
- A handline or short climbing tether if allowed.
- A small first-aid kit accessible without unpacking.
- Gloves thin enough for climbing but resistant to heat and alkali.

Avoid:

- Large backpacks.
- Long weapons.
- Wheeled cases.
- Wide hats.
- Open flames.
- Loose scarves that can catch on roots or kiln hardware.
- Shiny intrusive lights that blind people adapted to dimness.

This is a city of shoulders turned sideways.

## 11. Bring appropriate light, but use it modestly

Much of the city lives in dim amber, red kiln glow, oil-lamp flicker, and mineral fluorescence. Your bright white lamp may be painful, rude, or dangerous in narrow spaces.

Use:

- Low amber light.
- Red light for preserving night vision.
- Shielded beams.
- A lamp with adjustable brightness.
- Backup batteries protected from heat.

Do not shine light into homes, seed vaults, shrines, lower shafts, or directly into people’s faces. Do not use high heat lamps near dry vegetation or resin.

Also remember: some bulbs and glassy plant ribs absorb lamplight. Bright light may affect local growth cycles. Ask before illuminating gardens.

## 12. Food: bring your own, but accept carefully

Local food is likely edible only after proper processing. Resin pods produce oily meal and mineral starch, but ripe pods can burst with irritant dust. The meal is ground, leached through ash, pressed into cakes, and baked. It may taste smoky, metallic, faintly sweet.

A human traveler may tolerate it — or may not.

Bring enough food for yourself, preferably:

- Dry, compact, high-calorie rations.
- Foods that do not smell strongly.
- Foods that do not crumble into biological dust.
- Foods that require no cooking water.
- Electrolyte-rich snacks.
- Heat-stable packaging.

If offered local food, accept a small amount if culturally appropriate. Ask how to eat it. Some resin pastes may be licked from shallow dishes. Some powders may need to be mixed with oil or ash. Some foods may be intoxicating, medicinal, or reserved for certain times of day.

Avoid eating raw pods, unprocessed meal, unknown crystalline gels, or plant exudates. Do not assume “plant” means safe.

## 13. Fire, heat, and kilns are civic systems

Kilns are not just craft facilities. They are part of the city’s heat economy, food system, air movement, and possibly ritual calendar. Cooking pits, chimney-throats, communal kilns, and vent baffles all help the settlement breathe.

Do not interfere with:

- Bellows.
- Shutters.
- Chimney covers.
- Kiln doors.
- Ash banks.
- Drying racks.
- Fuel stores.
- Heat screens.
- Clay seals.

Never “improve ventilation” on your own. Opening or closing the wrong thing may redirect gas through sleeping chambers or kill a garden wall.

If you carry advanced fire-making tools, keep them discreet. This is a dry, resin-rich, ash-filled city. Accidental ignition could be disastrous.

## 14. Medical preparation

Before visiting, be in good health. This is not an ideal destination for someone with uncontrolled asthma, COPD, severe heat intolerance, heart disease, fainting disorders, or panic in confined spaces.

Carry:

- Oral rehydration salts.
- Electrolytes.
- Burn treatment.
- Eye wash in sealed single-use vials.
- Saline nasal gel or protective airway balm.
- Broad-spectrum antihistamine.
- Inhaler if you have any respiratory vulnerability.
- Pain relief.
- Blister treatment.
- Skin crack salve.
- Bandages that adhere despite dust and sweat.
- Antacid/anti-nausea medication.
- Emergency cooling packs if they function in the environment.
- A compact pulse oximeter if available.
- Gas exposure protocol card.

Be especially alert for:

- Dizziness.
- Confusion.
- Metallic taste with weakness.
- Sudden headache.
- Chest tightness.
- Eye burning.
- Unusual sleepiness.
- Loss of balance.
- Skin tingling after touching walls or plants.

If locals put on masks, close shutters, climb, or fall silent, do the same immediately.

## 15. Social conduct

This city has the population of a whisper and the architecture of density. Privacy may not be based on distance. Sound carries everywhere. A quiet comment may return from a wall or drop through a vent into someone’s workroom.

Behave gently.

Good practices:

- Speak softly.
- Ask before entering any alcove.
- Do not photograph or scan interiors without permission.
- Keep your body compact in lanes.
- Step aside for load carriers.
- Do not touch lintels, roots, shutters, or seed cones.
- Learn local greetings for morning, noon heat, and evening.
- Offer trade goods useful in a dry world.
- Pay for guidance, shade, and instruction.
- Accept that some areas are closed.

Bad practices:

- Joking about the lack of water.
- Calling the place barren.
- Comparing it unfavorably to an oasis.
- Asking “how do you survive here?” as if survival is surprising.
- Treating the mineral gardens as curiosities.
- Assuming empty rooms are abandoned.
- Bringing animals.
- Whistling into vents.
- Wasting lamp oil, shade, ash, or processed salt.

The people have adapted to an impossible place with immense sophistication. Approach them as engineers, gardeners, historians, and hosts — not as desert primitives.

## 16. Gifts and trade goods

Good gifts are practical, dry, compact, and non-disruptive.

Possible offerings:

- Fine ceramic tools.
- Heat-resistant cordage.
- Copper wire or sheet.
- Needles.
- Obsidian or high-quality cutting stone.
- Respirator cloth or filter charcoal if culturally acceptable.
- Durable gloves.
- Mirror-polished metal foil.
- Mineral analysis services.
- Heat-stable medicines.
- Lens glass.
- Soft brushes for garden work.
- Sealed pigments.
- Stories, maps, or astronomical knowledge.

Be cautious with:

- Water.
- Seeds from elsewhere.
- Soil.
- Fertilizer.
- Wet foods.
- Alcohol.
- Perfumes.
- Incense.
- Strong acids or bases.
- Living animals.
- Microbial cultures.
- Anything that sheds spores.

In this city, contamination is not a metaphor.

## 17. Navigation and emergency planning

Before descent, establish:

- Your surface marker.
- Your return route.
- A rendezvous time.
- A guide contact.
- A rescue protocol.
- Maximum visit duration.
- Heat exposure limits.
- Gas alarm meanings.
- Safe high ground.
- Safe shaded rest points.

The entrance is only a fault-line shadow from above. It may be easy to lose if wind, glare, or heat shimmer confuse you. Mark it without damaging local concealment. The city may depend on being invisible from the surface.

Carry a surface locator beacon if appropriate, but ask whether transmissions, vibrations, or lights are acceptable. Some worlds’ equipment may interfere with local instruments or attract unwanted attention.

Inside, use a physical thread-map or local chalk substitute only if permitted. Do not mark walls casually; wall surfaces may be living, sealed, sacred, or chemically active.

## 18. Psychological preparation

The city may feel empty even when inhabited. You may walk through lanes where shutters clack, pods rattle, vents boom, roots creep, and no person appears. The absence of animals will be striking. No birds. No insects. No rodents. No scratching life in the walls. Every sound belongs to heat, stone, plant, or human work.

This can unsettle a traveler.

Prepare for:

- Claustrophobia.
- Heat irritability.
- Sensory monotony broken by sudden loud vent sounds.
- Discomfort with dryness.
- Unease at active plant movement.
- Feeling watched by architecture.
- Loneliness in inhabited spaces.
- Fear of being underground without water.

Do not panic if the city seems to breathe. It does.

Its ventilation is deliberate. Its heat is managed. Its gardens fold, click, split, and crawl. Its walls have been patched for generations. What feels alien to you is ordinary maintenance to the people who live there.

## 19. What not to bring

Avoid bringing:

- Pets or pack animals.
- Wet soil.
- Potted plants.
- Unsealed biological samples.
- Open water vessels.
- Large metal armor that overheats.
- Heavy wheeled equipment.
- Explosives.
- Strong perfumes.
- Aerosols.
- Loose powdered chemicals.
- Incendiary devices.
- Drone swarms unless approved.
- Loud machines.
- Anything that drips.

Especially do not bring a casual tourist attitude. This place is delicate in ways that may not be visible.

## 20. Best way to visit

The safest visit would be short, guided, and timed around the city’s rhythms.

A sensible itinerary:

1. Cross the salt-flat before dawn.
2. Rest near the entrance while the guide checks conditions.
3. Descend in early morning.
4. Learn vent warnings immediately.
5. Visit public lanes, kiln yards, and upper terraces only.
6. Rest during noon heat in an approved shaded space.
7. Observe garden tending from a distance.
8. Eat only a small amount of local food unless medically confident.
9. Avoid lower vents entirely.
10. Ascend or settle into approved guest quarters before evening pressure changes.
11. Leave at dawn or after local confirmation that surface conditions are safe.

Do not attempt a deep architectural survey, botanical collection, or lower-fissure exploration on a first visit.

## Final advice

The central discipline here is restraint.

Do not waste shade.  
Do not waste breath.  
Do not waste water.  
Do not touch what is growing.  
Do not open what is sealed.  
Do not assume emptiness means abandonment.  
Do not assume dryness means death.

This city is a living compromise between stone, heat, gas, craft, and memory. Visit it as you would visit the inside of a lung, a temple, and a furnace: quietly, prepared, grateful for every safe breath, and always ready to climb when the deep pipes begin to sing.
