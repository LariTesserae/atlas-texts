An ordinary human traveler should prepare as if visiting a city, a cave, a marsh, a greenhouse, and a living animal all at once.

This is not a wilderness ruin. It is inhabited, maintained, legally and spiritually organized, and much more fragile than it first appears. Your greatest dangers will not be monsters but ignorance: stepping on a nursery mat, blocking a water-channel, wasting heat, shining a lamp upward, cutting what looks like an inconvenient root, or assuming that a dark, wet, low place is empty.

Prepare to arrive small, quiet, cold, damp, and watched.

## Physical preparation

You will be crawling, crouching, balancing, kneeling, squeezing sideways, and walking on surfaces that are not reliably solid. Train or at least condition yourself for:

- Long periods of stooping without complaint.
- Moving slowly in low light.
- Keeping balance on springy reed platforms and slick root bridges.
- Tolerating wet feet and cold hands.
- Remaining calm when insects, amphibians, or small animals touch you.
- Sleeping in damp conditions without thrashing or panicking.
- Breathing through cloth while lying down.

If you are claustrophobic, easily startled by insects, or prone to shouting when surprised, do not go without a local guide and a very clear plan for retreat.

You should also assume that you will be cold the entire time. Not freezing in the dramatic mountaineering sense, but steadily chilled in the wrists, knees, jaw, and lungs. The cold will be wet, intimate, and patient. It will make you clumsy. It will make you impatient. Prepare for that.

## Clothing

Wear nothing bright, stiff, jangling, perfumed, or easily snagged.

Good clothing would include:

- Soft, close-fitting wool or equivalent insulating base layers.
- A warm mid-layer that still allows crouching.
- A waxed or oiled outer layer resistant to water and mud.
- Knee and elbow protection, preferably quiet and flexible.
- Thin gloves for touch and thicker mitts for resting.
- A close hood or cap that keeps condensation from running down your neck.
- A face cloth or sleep veil to cover your mouth at night.
- Soft-soled waterproof boots with excellent grip, or local marsh shoes if provided.
- Spare socks sealed in a waterproof pouch.

Avoid capes, long scarves, loose straps, dangling tools, open pockets, and anything that can brush against fungi lamps, root-hair, insect nurseries, or hanging larvae. In this place the walls are close and inhabited. Your clothing should not behave like a broom.

Do not wear strong insect repellent unless approved by locals. Many insects here are livestock, pollinators, cleaners, oil sources, food sources, or warning systems. Poisoning them may be treated like poisoning someone’s goats.

## Light

Bring light, but do not depend on it and do not use it casually.

This city has its own darkness. Sudden light is dangerous, rude, and possibly catastrophic if it wakes the winged life in the upper vault. Bright beams may also damage glow-lichen, disturb fish, panic frogs, or expose spaces that are customarily kept unlit.

Carry:

- A very dim, shielded lamp with a red or green filter if possible.
- A hooded lantern or covered glow source that can be narrowed to your feet.
- Spare power or fuel sealed against damp.
- No open flame unless specifically permitted.

Practice navigating by touch before entering. Learn to keep one hand on a wall, rope, guide-line, or root rib. Learn to let your eyes adjust. The local illumination reveals nearness, not distance. You will not “see the room.” You will see the next bridge, the lip of a pool, a hand-sign, the fungus cup beside a doorway. Accept this.

Never shine light upward into the vault.

Never shine light into sleeping chambers.

Never shine light into deep water unless your guide tells you to.

## Waterproofing and storage

Everything will get damp unless sealed. Everything.

Use waxed cloth, oilskin, resin-coated containers, dry bags, ceramic jars, or equivalent. Pack as if crossing a swamp in rain and then sleeping inside a cellar.

Keep separate waterproof packets for:

- Fire-starting tools or heat source.
- Medical supplies.
- Spare socks and sleep cloth.
- Food.
- Trade goods.
- Notes, maps, or documents.
- Electronics or multiversal devices, if any.

Assume condensation will form inside poorly sealed cases. Add desiccant if you have it, but do not expect miracles. In this city, dryness is wealth.

## Health risks

The environment is biologically dense. Do not treat any water, moss, insect, fungus, or slime as harmless simply because it is small.

Prepare for:

- Hypothermia from prolonged damp cold.
- Trench foot or immersion foot.
- Fungal skin infections.
- Leech attachment.
- Insect bites.
- Allergic reactions to spores or molds.
- Respiratory irritation from damp smoke and fungal growth.
- Gastrointestinal illness from local water or unfamiliar foods.
- Slips, cuts, and punctures from young sharp stone hidden under mud.
- Eye irritation from condensation, spores, or mud splash.

Carry:

- Water purification method.
- Antifungal cream or powder.
- Broad-spectrum wound disinfectant.
- Sterile dressings and waterproof bandages.
- Tweezers or leech-removal tools.
- Antihistamines.
- Any personal asthma/allergy medication.
- A compact thermal blanket.
- Foot care supplies.
- A mild respirator or cloth mask suitable for damp air.
- Electrolytes and high-calorie food.

Do not enter with open wounds if you can avoid it. If you are cut, clean and cover the wound immediately. The mud may be clean in the aesthetic sense and dangerous in the microbial sense.

## Food and water

Do not assume hospitality includes abundant food. Heat, dryness, protein, and clean storage are costly here.

Bring compact food that does not shed crumbs everywhere. Crumbs feed the wrong creatures. Good options include dense cakes, sealed dried fruit, hard cheese if culturally acceptable, nut pastes, smoked food sealed well, or ration bars.

Do not bring food with strong odors unless you want to attract every quiet citizen of the walls.

Do not wash grease or salt into channels. Every drip belongs somewhere, and contaminants may travel to nurseries, cisterns, fish pools, or sacred basins.

Drink only what is offered as potable or what you purify yourself in a way that does not insult your hosts. The black clean water may be chemically pure, ritually restricted, full of larvae, or reserved for eels.

## Gifts and trade goods

Good gifts are practical, compact, quiet, and respectful of damp conditions.

Consider bringing:

- Fine needles.
- Awls.
- fishhooks.
- Thin copper wire.
- Non-rusting small blades.
- Waterproof thread.
- Beeswax or plant wax.
- Salt in sealed packets.
- Resin.
- Dry tinder in a waterproof container.
- Small ceramic or glass jars with tight lids.
- Soft cloth.
- Antifungal salve.
- Safe pigments for marking bark.
- Magnifying lenses if culturally appropriate.
- Heat-retaining stones or small sealed hand-warmers, if they can be explained and controlled.

Do not bring:

- Perfume.
- Loud mechanical devices.
- Flashing lights.
- Insecticide.
- Herbicide.
- Uncontained seeds.
- Aggressive disinfectants.
- Fireworks.
- Anything that sheds glitter, powder, fibers, or spores.
- Large animals.
- Dry wood unless you know local fuel customs.
- Religious objects meant to “purify” rot, darkness, frogs, insects, or roots.

Remember: rot is not filth here. Insects are not merely pests. Darkness is not neglect. Water is not just water. Do not gift them things that imply their whole way of living is wrong.

## Social etiquette

Arrive as a guest, not an explorer.

Your first words should be quiet. Your first posture should be low. Your first assumption should be that you are standing inside someone’s home, farm, temple, plumbing system, and body of law simultaneously.

Basic etiquette:

- Ask before touching walls, roots, lamps, mats, hanging jars, or tools.
- Ask where to step.
- Ask where not to breathe smoke, spit, wash, urinate, or discard food.
- Do not point with a bright lamp.
- Do not block a passage while staring.
- Do not stand upright if others are crouching.
- Do not laugh at the size of things. The city is small because the root-vault is small, not because its people are childish.
- Do not call the place a burrow unless locals do.
- Do not call the animals vermin.
- Do not compare the inhabitants to insects, moles, frogs, or cave-dwellers.
- Do not touch children, sleeping mats, stored food, or frog basins.
- Do not ask the name of the thing in the central pool unless someone offers it.
- Do not speak loudly near water channels, especially after freeze-thaw.
- Do not compliment a root by suggesting it would make good timber.

Learn the local prohibitions before you enter:

- Do not waste heat.
- Do not cut living roots without permission.
- Do not step on pale mats.
- Do not wake the upper wings.
- Do not block drips.
- Do not sleep with your mouth uncovered during moth season.
- Do not kill white frogs.

Treat these as law, not superstition.

## Movement inside the city

Move slowly enough that hidden life has time to move away from you.

Before each step, look and feel. A surface may be:

- A path.
- A roof.
- A nursery.
- A drain cover.
- A sleeping platform.
- A soft mat over water.
- A living root under tension.
- Someone’s stored crop.
- A seasonal bridge not currently safe.

Use a walking probe if offered, but do not poke randomly into mats or pools. Local people may carry short staffs, hooked sticks, or feeler rods; imitate them only after instruction.

If your foot goes into water, do not jerk violently. You may tear a woven path, kick a frog basin, or startle eels. Stop, say quietly that you are caught or sunk, and let someone tell you how to extract yourself.

If insects are crossing a rope or bridge, wait. If a frog sits in a drainage cup, do not move it. If a root tendril touches your sleeve, do not slap it away unless it tightens.

## Navigation

Do not rely on ordinary maps. The city changes.

Some paths are seasonal. Some doors appear only after rain. Some benches become fruiting bodies. Some winter streets close when vines wake. Water levels alter lanes. Roots thicken. Mats soften. The “same” passage may not function the same way two days later.

Use:

- Local guides.
- Tactile markers.
- Scent cues.
- Water-sound cues.
- Bark signs.
- Knotted cords or permissible trail markers.
- Memory of gradients, temperature, and airflow.

Ask whether marking a route is allowed. Tying a cord to the wrong root may damage it or signal ownership, danger, mourning, or a flood path.

If lost, do not wander. Crouch near a stable wall or raised path, cover your light, and make the agreed-upon soft signal. Loud calling may not carry where you expect, or may carry exactly where it should not.

## Fire, heat, and smoke

Fire is politically and ecologically sensitive.

Bring personal warmth that does not require flame if possible: chemical hand warmers, insulated clothing, heated stones, or safe compact heat devices. But introduce any advanced heat source cautiously. A device that warms without smoke may be valuable, suspicious, sacred, or dangerous depending on local interpretation.

Do not light a flame without permission.

Do not feed a hearth.

Do not blow on coals unless invited.

Do not complain that the hearths are smoky. Smoke is being negotiated through living architecture. The roof breathes, but not infinitely.

If given a place near warmth, understand it as generosity. Do not overstay if others are colder than you.

## Sleeping

Sleeping will be difficult for a visitor.

You will hear tiny movements everywhere. Things may crawl near you. Drips will fall. The plant mass may creak. The central pulse may become noticeable once you stop moving. You may feel that the whole city is breathing around you. This is normal.

Before sleeping:

- Shake out bedding as locals instruct.
- Cover your mouth, especially during moth season.
- Seal food.
- Remove boots only where directed.
- Check for leeches.
- Keep a dim light covered and within reach.
- Know where to urinate.
- Know what sounds require waking your host.
- Do not sleep against a wall unless told it is safe; walls may be inhabited, damp, or slowly moving.

If something crawls over you, do not thrash. Brush it away gently unless it bites or attaches. Thrashing in a low alcove can break lamps, tear mats, or injure animals that belong there.

## The animals

Prepare emotionally for constant proximity to small life.

The animals are not background. They are infrastructure, food, omen, pest-control, spiritual presence, hazard, and neighbor. You may be observed by frogs, cleaned up after by insects, warned by fish movement, and judged by how you treat a beetle procession.

Specific cautions:

- Shake sleeping mats and clothing.
- Check boots before putting them on.
- Keep food sealed.
- Do not kill spiders unless in immediate danger.
- Do not disturb eels in cisterns.
- Do not touch white frogs.
- Do not scrape glow-lichen; snails may already be farming it in their own way.
- Avoid putting bare hands into dark pools or reed hollows.
- If bitten, stung, or attached to, ask locals before applying chemicals. Your medicine may poison a valued species.

Do not assume silence means absence. It usually means presence behaving correctly.

## The plants

The plants are architecture and authority.

Do not cut, peel, prune, lean heavily on, burn, decorate, tie knots around, or harvest from any living plant unless invited. Even dead-looking roots may be load-bearing, sacred, dormant, or part of a water system.

A root withdrawing from repeated touch is not a curiosity to test. Stop touching it.

If a doorway seems to appear after rain, do not enter it without a local. Such doors may be useful, taboo, predatory, unstable, or simply not yours.

If the central root knot pulses, do not joke about it. The inhabitants may count time by it, build by it, bless children by it, or fear changes in its rhythm.

## Cultural mindset

The main adjustment is this: stop separating natural from artificial.

In this city, a root bridge is both grown and built. A drain is both plant and civic infrastructure. A fungus lamp is both organism and streetlight. A compost chamber is both rot and heating system. A frog may be both animal and protected civic worker. A drip may be both water and property.

If you keep asking, “Is this alive or made?” you will misunderstand the place.

Ask instead:

- Who tends this?
- What does it feed?
- What must not disturb it?
- When does it change?
- Who has permission here?
- What season is this passage in?
- Where does this water go?

## Psychological preparation

The place’s strangeness accumulates. At first it may seem only cold, cramped, wet, and dark. Then you will notice that every surface is occupied, every sound is meaningful, and every act has consequences.

You may feel:

- Watched.
- Crowded despite the quiet.
- Afraid to touch anything.
- Irritated by slow movement.
- Disoriented by lack of distance vision.
- Unclean because nothing dries.
- Sleep-deprived from small sounds.
- Uneasy around the central pool.
- Overwhelmed by the intimacy of smells.

Prepare by lowering your expectations of comfort and control. This is not a place where you dominate space. It is a place where you negotiate permission to occupy a few inches at a time.

## What to do on arrival

A good arrival sequence:

1. Stop outside the entrance and listen.
2. Remove or cover bright objects.
3. Dim your lamp before entering.
4. Secure all loose straps.
5. Put on gloves.
6. Cover any exposed wounds.
7. Announce yourself softly if expected.
8. Wait for a guide or sign.
9. Enter slowly, hands first, without forcing the foliage aside more than necessary.
10. Once inside, remain crouched and still until told where to step.
11. Offer gifts only after greeting, not as a bribe at the threshold.
12. Ask for the current rules: flood state, moth season, freeze-thaw condition, forbidden paths, and sleeping protocols.

## Emergency behavior

If there is sudden wing movement above: extinguish or cover light, crouch, cover face, and stay still.

If water rises: follow locals immediately. Do not try to save your pack before understanding where the higher hooks or platforms are.

If stone shifts or cracks: become quiet. Sound may matter. Move only when guided.

If you fall through a mat: spread your weight, do not kick, call softly, and wait for instructions.

If a root tightens around gear: do not hack at it. Unbuckle, withdraw, and let locals decide whether to free the item.

If you see something large move in the central pool: step back from the edge, lower your gaze, and say nothing unless your host speaks first.

If you are separated from your guide: stop, reduce light, avoid water, and use the agreed signal.

## Best things to bring in your attitude

Bring humility. Bring patience. Bring a willingness to be cold. Bring respect for smallness. Bring the understanding that a city can whisper and still be a city.

Do not arrive hungry for spectacle. The place will not reveal itself like a palace. It will reveal itself as a drip routed into a cistern, a beetle cage warmed by compost, a polished mud lane, a child counting by the pulse of a root, a law scratched into bark, a bridge that grows tighter around its own repairs.

If you visit well, you will learn to move with less force. You will learn that darkness can be organized, that wetness can be civic, that rot can be managed, that silence can be crowded, and that architecture can be alive without being tame.

The correct traveler does not conquer this place, map it, brighten it, dry it, or clean it.

The correct traveler enters quietly, keeps warm without stealing warmth, steps only where permitted, covers their mouth at night, does not kill the frogs, and remembers that every drip belongs somewhere.
