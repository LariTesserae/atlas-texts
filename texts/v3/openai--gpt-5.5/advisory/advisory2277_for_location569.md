To visit this town, an ordinary human traveler should prepare as if entering a cold high-mountain monastery, a living coral reef, and a submerged village all at once. The place is beautiful, habitable, and socially ordered, but it is not built for air-breathing bodies, dry possessions, haste, or hard machinery.

## 1. Breathing is the first problem

The town is underwater. Not damp, not misty, not partly flooded: fully submerged.

You will need a reliable way to breathe for the entire visit. Do not assume there will be air pockets, dry chambers, or local hospitality sufficient to keep you alive. The inhabitants may have reed-tube vocal systems, surface-adjacent workspaces, or biological adaptations, but an ordinary human cannot depend on any of these.

Bring one of the following:

- A compact rebreather or long-duration diving system.
- A magical or technological water-breathing aid, if available.
- A pressure-tolerant breathing mask with redundant oxygen supply.
- A guide-provided breathing bladder or local equivalent only if you have tested it first.

Avoid noisy open-circuit scuba if possible. Constant streams of bubbles may disturb animals, damage delicate roofs, or be considered rude in quiet court spaces. A low-bubble or closed system is better.

Carry backup breathing. In this town, getting tangled in a root-lane or delayed by local ceremony could become dangerous if your air supply is exact to the minute.

## 2. Prepare for cold water

The basin is fed by snowmelt and mountain springs. Even if the water seems gentle and green, it will likely leach heat from your body quickly.

Wear:

- A full cold-water wetsuit or drysuit.
- Thermal underlayers if using a drysuit.
- Gloves thin enough to feel textures but warm enough to prevent numbness.
- A hood or head covering.
- Soft-soled weighted boots or sandals if you will walk along lower mats.

Do not rely on “I can tolerate cold.” You may be moving slowly, observing, waiting through ritual, or hovering in place while guides negotiate passage. Cold becomes serious during stillness.

If using a drysuit, make sure it is flexible and not bulky. Many passages are soft, narrow, and full of living handholds. Heavy gear could tear walls or trap you.

## 3. Control your buoyancy carefully

This is not open diving among rocks. This is a town of living walls, fish passages, root-bridges, algae curtains, gardens, and public lanes. Bad buoyancy will make you dangerous.

Before entering, practice:

- Hovering without kicking downward.
- Moving by gentle hand-pulls.
- Turning in confined spaces.
- Avoiding fin strikes.
- Remaining still in current.

Use short, soft fins or no fins if guided through the town by root-rails. Long fins may shred bladder-leaves, disturb silt, frighten bellfish, or damage young grafts.

Carry minimal dangling equipment. Anything that trails behind you may snag on living screens, seed cords, eel gutters, or nursery vines.

## 4. Expect high-altitude issues before and after immersion

The town sits high on a mountain. The basin itself may not contain ordinary air, but your route there and any surface staging area will involve cold, thin mountain conditions.

Prepare for:

- Altitude sickness.
- Cold wind and wet exposure before entry.
- Difficult descent or ascent along cliffs.
- Reduced stamina.
- Headache, nausea, confusion, and shortness of breath.

Acclimatize if possible. Do not hike up and immediately dive unless your equipment and physiology are designed for it. Altitude plus immersion plus cold can overwhelm an ordinary body.

If using compressed-gas diving equipment, consult someone competent about altitude diving. Pressure behavior in a high mountain lake differs from sea-level diving assumptions.

## 5. Bring very little, and make it waterproof

Almost everything here is wet, living, and slow. Dry luggage is absurd inside the town unless sealed and buoyancy-controlled.

Bring:

- Waterproof writing slate or gesture board.
- Sealed identification tokens or offerings.
- Small cutting tool for emergencies only, preferably blunt-tipped.
- Soft tether line.
- Cold-water light with a shielded beam.
- Non-toxic marker cord.
- Small first-aid kit sealed for underwater use.
- Emergency thermal wrap for use after exit.

Do not bring:

- Loose paper.
- Powders.
- Soap, perfume, sunscreen, insect repellent, fuel, or oils that can leach into the water.
- Corroding metal trinkets unless ritually appropriate.
- Glass containers likely to break.
- Anything that sheds plastic fibers, glitter, paint flakes, or toxins.
- Fire-making tools as a primary technology; flame is useless and inappropriate here.

If bringing electronics, ensure they are pressure-rated, waterproof, and silent. Avoid bright strobes unless needed for safety.

## 6. Learn underwater communication before arrival

Sound travels strangely here. Ordinary speech will be useless through a mask and may be socially inappropriate. The locals use gesture, touch, reed-tube song, and short carrying calls adapted to water.

Prepare a basic communication set:

- “I am a guest.”
- “I need air.”
- “I am cold.”
- “I am lost.”
- “May I touch this?”
- “Where may I go?”
- “Thank you.”
- “I am sorry.”
- “Danger.”
- “I do not understand.”

Learn local hand signs if possible before entering. Also prepare a tactile method: many inhabitants may guide by wrist, shoulder, or hand because visibility and distance can be unreliable.

Do not assume pointing is polite. In plant-built towns, pointing at walls, roofs, roots, or animals may imply intent to cut, harvest, claim, or inspect. Let your guide teach you.

## 7. Hire or accept a local guide

Do not wander alone.

The settlement is not a museum, ruin, or natural attraction. It is a living town with private dwellings, public channels, animal nurseries, sacred cisterns, food gardens, and structural plants that may be damaged by ignorance.

A guide will know:

- Which root-bridges can bear your weight.
- Which walls are young and must not be touched.
- Which currents are public lanes.
- Which areas are for animals, mourning, birth, seed storage, or repair.
- When bellfish activity signals time, ceremony, or disturbance.
- How to avoid eel hollows, turtle paths, and compost baskets.
- When to remain still.

The rule “No one owns a current” does not mean “anyone may go anywhere.” Currents are shared obligations, not tourist corridors.

## 8. Treat every structure as alive

This is the central etiquette.

The houses are plants. The doors are plants. The bridges are plants. The railings are plants. Some rooms may be old enough to have known generations of residents. A wall may be both architecture and organism.

Do not:

- Cut anything.
- Pick leaves.
- Peel bark.
- Scrape moss.
- Kick roofs.
- Tie equipment to living stems without permission.
- Force open a plant-screen door.
- Block fish passages.
- Sit heavily on young root mats.
- Collect seeds, shells, fungi, eggs, or “souvenirs.”

Even dead-looking material may be part of compost, mourning, repair, or nursery systems.

Ask before touching. If told to stroke a door in a certain direction, do exactly that. A trained living screen may furl if touched correctly and tighten if mishandled.

## 9. Bring offerings that do not poison the lake

A good guest gift should be useful, inert, and non-contaminating.

Possible gifts:

- Obsidian flakes or well-shaped stone blades.
- Shell, bone, or ceramic tools.
- Non-rusting needles made from appropriate material.
- Clean cordage of natural fiber, if it will not decay harmfully.
- Seeds only if approved beforehand; never introduce species casually.
- Polished stone weights.
- Stories, songs, maps, or news from other mountain waters.
- Labor: careful silt-moving, pruning under instruction, carrying cargo.

Avoid metal unless specifically requested. Avoid copper, treated wood, dyed cloth, modern alloys, plastics, batteries, medicines, and preserved foods that might leach into the water.

The safest gift is often service performed under guidance.

## 10. Understand the local laws before entering

The town’s rules are ecological, practical, and moral at the same time. Breaking them may not merely offend people; it may harm a home, food source, public lane, nursery, or ancestral place.

Important principles to remember:

- Do not cut living walls without permission and proper feeding of roots.
- Do not block openings used by fish, light, or seed.
- Do not disturb silt deposits; silt has assigned uses.
- Do not enter fallen structures until locals have cleared them.
- Do not harvest upper leaves out of season.
- Do not claim, redirect, dam, or pollute a current.
- Do not treat ruins as abandoned; decay here is part of civic order.
- Do not remove bones, fungi, eggs, snails, or votive objects from shrine areas.

If you make a mistake, stop moving. Signal apology. Let your guide correct the damage if possible. Thrashing in panic will make things worse.

## 11. Expect sound to affect you physically

The town is loud in a submerged way. Waterfalls, channels, frogs, bellfish, work songs, and rain on the lake surface all transmit through water and body.

You may feel sound in your chest, teeth, joints, and skull. The bellfish in particular may produce resonant, temple-like clonks strong enough to startle an unprepared visitor.

Prepare with:

- Ear protection suitable for diving, if needed.
- Practice staying calm when sudden underwater sound occurs.
- Awareness that local timekeeping may be acoustic.
- Willingness to pause during bellfish events.

Do not panic when the whole town seems to boom. Morning, midday, and dusk bellfish are normal. Sudden, disorderly bellfish may indicate disturbance, courtship, or danger; follow your guide.

## 12. Be careful with light

The town lives in filtered green-gold daylight and mild biological glow. Bright artificial beams may distress animals, blind residents, or disrupt evening behavior.

Use:

- Low, warm, shielded light.
- Narrow beams pointed downward or toward your hands.
- Bioluminescent or dim lantern equivalents if offered.

Do not sweep powerful dive lights through homes, nurseries, fish schools, or the Lung. Never use flashing lights unless signaling emergency.

At night or in deeper rooms, accept the dimness. The town is designed for touch, memory, and gentle illumination, not human floodlighting.

## 13. Food and drink

You cannot casually eat while submerged unless equipped for it, and local foods may be unfamiliar: larvae, tubers, aquatic greens, fish, snails, fungi, fermented plant matter.

Before visiting, eat well but not heavily. Bring sealed emergency nutrition that will not contaminate the water. If invited to share food, ask how to consume it properly; some meals may take place at the lake skin, in partial air-water spaces, or through specialized vessels.

Do not refuse local food with visible disgust. If you cannot eat something, indicate gratitude and incapacity rather than revulsion.

Drinkable fresh water is all around you, but do not assume it is safe for your body. Mountain mineral springs, organic matter, animal life, and local microbes may upset you badly. Use your own purified supply.

## 14. Health risks

Likely hazards include:

- Hypothermia.
- Equipment failure.
- Entrapment in roots, cords, or plant screens.
- Altitude sickness.
- Panic from underwater confinement.
- Cuts from shell tools, stone thresholds, fish spines, or obsidian.
- Infection from unfamiliar microbes.
- Allergic reaction to plant exudates, moss, fungi, or animal mucus.
- Slips on mossy stone.
- Sudden cold inflows.
- Silt clouds reducing visibility.
- Rockfall near cliffs.
- Strong outflow at the eastern torrent.

Before going, update tetanus protection if relevant in your universe. Carry broad-spectrum wound care, but do not release antiseptics into the lake. Treat wounds outside the water if possible.

If you are prone to claustrophobia, panic diving, vertigo, or cold-induced asthma, reconsider the visit or keep it very short.

## 15. Moving through the town

You will probably move in one of three ways:

1. Swimming gently through open lanes.
2. Pulling yourself by root-rails and vine ladders.
3. Walking with weighted sandals on lower mats when carrying loads.

Do not hurry. The whole town rewards slow movement. Fast swimming creates turbulence, frightens animals, and may damage fragile growth.

Use hands carefully. Some “handholds” are public rails polished by generations of use. Others are structural roots, young grafts, door tendrils, or sacred growth. Your guide should show you which is which.

Keep your body compact. Avoid wide arm sweeps. Think of yourself as moving through a library whose shelves are alive and sensitive.

## 16. Social behavior

The inhabitants likely value patience, ecological attentiveness, competence, and restraint.

Good guest behavior:

- Wait when others wait.
- Observe before acting.
- Keep your tools tucked away.
- Ask before entering enclosed spaces.
- Accept touch-guidance calmly.
- Give right of way to children, elders, cargo carriers, animals, and work crews.
- Do not interrupt work songs.
- Do not joke about drowning, flooding, cutting, or decay.
- Compliment maintenance, not conquest: “This wall is well tended,” “The current is clear,” “The roof admits good light.”

Avoid calling the town “primitive.” Its technology is slow, biological, and precise. A bridge that takes four years to grow may be more sophisticated than one built in a day if it remains alive, self-repairing, and integrated into the watershed.

## 17. Sacred and sensitive places

The Lung, the central court, deserves special respect. It is civic, spiritual, ecological, and architectural at once.

At the Lung:

- Stay close to your guide.
- Do not descend into the cold well without invitation.
- Do not touch bone baskets, fungi, root offerings, tools placed for repair, or hanging chambers.
- Avoid bright lights.
- Be silent during mourning, birth, council, or seed rituals.
- Do not swim upward into leaf-platforms unless invited.

The dead are not hidden away here. Their bones may be cleaned slowly among roots and fungi. Do not stare as if at a curiosity. Treat these places as cemeteries, not biological displays.

## 18. Environmental contamination is a serious offense

You are entering a closed, delicate water-town. Anything you shed becomes part of someone’s drinking medium, wall surface, garden bed, animal habitat, or shrine.

Before entry:

- Rinse all gear in clean water.
- Remove chemical residues.
- Avoid lotions, sunscreens, perfumes, insecticides, hair products, detergents, and lubricants unless certified safe.
- Inspect for invasive seeds, spores, larvae, mud, or algae from other worlds.
- Use gear dedicated to this ecosystem if possible.

Never urinate, bleed freely, dump food, vent chemicals, discard batteries, or release nanotech/magic residue into the lake.

If you come from another universe, biosecurity matters even more. A single foreign fungus, snail egg, or microbial culture could change the color of the walls for generations — or kill them.

## 19. Time works differently there

The town is not hurried. Building, repair, decision, and hospitality may follow seasonal logic. A “short visit” may still include long pauses while a current changes, bellfish settle, animals pass, or elders are consulted.

Plan for delays.

Do not schedule your exit tightly around an air supply, portal window, or mountain storm. Have a safe staging point and a generous margin. Bring enough breathing capacity for the unexpected.

If a local says “not yet,” they may be referring to the behavior of water, animals, or plants, not social reluctance. Respect it.

## 20. Emergency behavior

If you are lost:

- Stop.
- Do not swim upward blindly; roofs, cords, or algae may trap you.
- Signal with approved light or sound.
- Follow current only if instructed; some currents lead to dark root-depths or the eastern torrent.
- Look for public root-rails, shell markers, or red weed boundary markers.

If entangled:

- Do not thrash.
- Signal.
- Use a blunt tool only if your life is in immediate danger.
- Cut dead cord before living root if possible.
- Report all damage afterward.

If cold:

- Signal early.
- Exit before shivering becomes severe.
- Do not tough it out for politeness.

If equipment fails:

- Move toward your guide, not toward an unknown surface.
- The visible “up” may not lead to breathable air.
- Use backup breathing and emergency ascent protocols only where safe.

## 21. What to prepare emotionally

This place may feel uncanny at first. Humans often expect towns to be dry, stable, angular, and dead-built. Here, the streets sway. Walls pulse faintly. Fish pass through civic spaces. Decay is not failure but process. Sound arrives through bone. Privacy may be defined by gesture and plant screens rather than locked doors. The dead remain present for years among roots.

Prepare to feel:

- Slowed down.
- Watched by animals.
- Clumsy.
- Overlarge.
- Dependent on guides.
- Quietly moved.
- Disoriented by the absence of air.

Do not fight the slowness. Let the place teach you its pace. The town is not inert scenery; it is a living agreement.

## 22. Best way to arrive

Ideally, arrive at an appointed outer marker with a local escort waiting. Enter at a public approach, not through gardens or roof leaves. Pause at the boundary cords and carved stones. Let your guide announce you.

A respectful first visit might proceed like this:

1. Acclimatize in the mountain staging area.
2. Clean and inspect all gear.
3. Review hand signs and emergency signals.
4. Enter with guide through an accepted lane.
5. Visit a public court before any private house.
6. Offer a non-contaminating gift or service.
7. Spend time simply observing the current, bellfish, and root-streets.
8. Avoid sacred sites until invited.
9. Leave before fatigue or cold affects judgment.
10. Thank the guide and ask whether any damage or disturbance must be repaid.

## 23. Final advice

Go lightly. Bring less than you think you need, but make every survival system redundant. Be warm, quiet, slow, and clean. Touch nothing casually. Cut nothing except to save a life. Assume every current, root, fish passage, silt bed, and shadowed hollow has a purpose.

The town is not fragile in the sense of being weak; it has endured snowmelt, storms, decay, and centuries of use. But it is vulnerable to the kind of visitor who mistakes living patience for simplicity.

If you prepare well, the visit will not feel like touring a drowned ruin. It will feel like being admitted, briefly, into a cold green breathing world where architecture grows, water is weather and atmosphere, and a town survives by never pretending it is separate from the lake that holds it.
