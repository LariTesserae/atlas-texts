An ordinary human traveler should treat this city less like a destination and more like a living cold-weather machine whose laws are partly ecological, partly architectural, and partly ceremonial. Do not arrive as a tourist with a bright lamp and a backpack. Arrive as a patient guest entering an organism.

## 1. Do not go alone

Bring or hire a local interpreter, guide, or sanctioned transit escort if any are available. This is not optional if you value survival.

The city’s hazards are not merely “dangerous animals” or “bad weather.” The streets, drainage, doors, temperature systems, animal movements, light levels, and acoustic fields all appear to interact. A person who does not know the rhythms of the place could block a migration route, step onto a pressure plate meant for a herd, wake something with a flashlight, waste heat unlawfully, or take shelter in a duct that is about to become a thaw-channel.

If no local guide exists, use remote scouting first: drones, probes, tethered sensor crawlers, or expendable mapping devices. Assume many will fail, vanish, or return with geometrically inconsistent maps.

## 2. Prepare for cold, wet, dark altitude

This is a high, saturated, freezing environment where rain does not fall cleanly from above but hangs in the air and condenses on everything. You will be wet unless you are sealed.

Wear:

- A fully waterproof outer shell, not merely water-resistant clothing.
- Insulated underlayers that retain warmth when damp.
- Sealed boots with aggressive traction for wet stone, ice, ceramic, and metal.
- Crampon-compatible soles or retractable microspikes.
- Waterproof gloves with tactile inner liners.
- A hood or helmet with anti-condensation visor treatment.
- Face protection against freezing mist.
- A respirator or mask capable of filtering mineral aerosols, metallic dust, spores/films, and unknown biological particles.
- Eye protection. The mist, mineral salts, frost shards, and airborne droplets may irritate or damage human eyes.

Expect hypothermia even if the air is not brutally cold. Continuous wetness steals heat quietly. Your clothing should be designed for long exposure to near-freezing saturation.

Also prepare for altitude sickness unless you know your body can tolerate high elevation. Bring oxygen support, acclimatization medication if appropriate, and a pulse oximeter. Do not assume the local atmosphere is human-normal just because animals breathe it.

## 3. Bring your own heat, but do not radiate it carelessly

Heat is socially, legally, and infrastructurally important here. The city captures warmth from bodies, machines, friction, exhalation, vents, and animal passage. Waste heat may be considered theft, pollution, sacrilege, or sabotage.

Your equipment should include:

- A closed-loop thermal suit or expedition garment that conserves body heat.
- Heat exchangers that do not dump warmth randomly into the air.
- Emergency chemical warmers kept sealed until needed.
- Low-emission power systems.
- Thermal signature dampening if local fauna are attracted to heat.

Do not sit on random vents to warm yourself. Do not plug heaters into unknown conduits. Do not open a warm shelter door repeatedly. Do not vent hot exhaust into lanes, gutters, or animal passages. A small careless plume of heat may thaw something that was deliberately frozen, wake a dormant organism, change pressure in a condensation farm, or mark you as edible.

If there is a local exchange custom, bring heat as a gift: charged thermal cells, contained chemical heat packs, or portable energy cartridges designed to be surrendered safely into communal systems.

## 4. Light discipline is essential

This is not a place where you should flood the darkness with a headlamp.

Light is rationed because light “wakes certain things too quickly.” Bright illumination may attract animals, trigger growth in mineral films, disrupt city systems, or violate local law. Use dim, shielded, narrow-beam lights only when necessary.

Recommended lighting:

- Low-output red, amber, or violet lamps with adjustable brightness.
- Hooded lanterns that do not spill light upward or sideways.
- Passive low-light goggles.
- Thermal imaging.
- Lidar or sonar mapping, used cautiously.
- Nonvisual navigation aids.

Avoid:

- White floodlights.
- Strobe lights.
- Laser pointers.
- UV lamps unless cleared by a guide.
- Flash photography.
- Open flame.

When in doubt, stay darker than you would prefer.

## 5. Move slowly

The city appears to discourage rapid movement. Floors may soften, corridors may lengthen, doors may delay, and animals may respond collectively to sudden action. Running could turn a minor mistake into a citywide alarm.

Practice deliberate travel:

- Walk slowly.
- Pause before entering intersections.
- Let herds pass.
- Do not jump over channels or pipes.
- Do not force doors.
- Do not climb railings, cableways, animal routes, or maintenance ribs.
- Avoid sudden gestures near creatures.
- Keep your breathing steady if possible.

If startled, freeze first. Look. Listen. Let the city settle. Then move.

Emergency evacuation planning should account for this. You may not be able to sprint to safety. Your survival plan should be based on sheltering, signaling, and slow withdrawal.

## 6. Sound is not background here

The city is an instrument. Its soundscape carries mechanical, animal, hydrological, and possibly communicative information. Loud noises may be dangerous. So may silence.

Bring:

- High-quality hearing protection that reduces harmful vibration without making you deaf to warnings.
- Bone-conduction communication devices.
- Subsonic and ultrasonic sensors.
- Vibration monitors.
- A personal alarm that can signal visually or through coded low-power pulses rather than screaming sirens.

Avoid shouting unless necessary. Avoid metal tools clanging against railings. Do not play music openly. Do not imitate animal calls. Do not strike resonant road plates for curiosity.

In narrow lanes, sound may become physically oppressive. You may feel it in your teeth, skull, chest, or gut. Travelers prone to migraines, vertigo, panic attacks, tinnitus, or sensory overload should prepare carefully or reconsider visiting.

## 7. Treat the animals as citizens, infrastructure, and weather

The fauna are not decorative wildlife. They operate valves, warm conduits, synchronize pumps, carry materials, feed on leakage, and possibly communicate for the city.

Rules of conduct:

- Do not feed them.
- Do not touch them.
- Do not collect shells, scales, membranes, eggs, shed skin, or “dead” material.
- Do not block herds.
- Do not step on puddle-swarms.
- Do not shine lights into eyes, vents, nests, drains, or roosts.
- Do not stand under cable-roosting flying creatures.
- Do not enter animal passages unless specifically guided.
- Do not assume a slow creature is harmless.
- Do not assume a small creature is insignificant.

Some animals may eat heat, salt, electricity, sound, memory, or electromagnetic leakage. Secure all exposed cables, batteries, neural devices, implants, recording media, and warm packs. Keep medical implants shielded if possible.

If a group of small animals freezes and turns toward you, stop immediately. Lower your light. Reduce noise. Back away slowly only after the response relaxes.

## 8. Navigation will be unreliable

This city contains impossible geometry: rooms larger inside than outside, unsupported spans, channels crossing without junctions, stairways descending into angles that should not exist. Ordinary maps may fail.

Bring multiple independent navigation systems:

- Tether spools or breadcrumb beacons.
- Inertial navigation units.
- Local transponders.
- Hand-drawn route notes.
- Environmental memory: sound, slope, temperature, smell, vibration.
- A return-line protocol: never enter a complex passage without marking a retreat route.

Do not rely solely on GPS, stars, sun, compass, or visual landmarks. There may be no sky reference, no magnetic reliability, and many towers may look similar in dim wet light.

Be cautious with drones. Flying creatures, magnetic dust, gravitational pylons, condensation, and non-Euclidean spaces may destroy or confuse them.

## 9. Assume the water is not for drinking

Water is everywhere, but it belongs to the city’s circulation. It may contain salts, engineered films, metallic compounds, animal residues, machine coolants, memory-bearing media, biological agents, or reactive minerals.

Bring all drinking water in sealed containers or carry a serious purification system rated for:

- Heavy metals.
- Unknown organics.
- Salts and mineral loads.
- Microbial contamination.
- Nanotechnological contamination, if relevant in your multiverse.
- Cryogenic/freeze-thaw damage.

Do not wash gear in public channels. Do not spit into reservoirs. Do not urinate into drains. Do not pour out leftover soup, alcohol, medicine, or cleaning fluid. All fluids may be part of law.

Human waste should be sealed and carried out unless you have explicit permission to interface with local sanitation.

## 10. Biosecurity matters

There is no vegetation. No moss, no grass, no roots. This may be because plants cannot survive, or because the city excludes them, or because introduction would be catastrophic.

Before arrival:

- Sterilize boots, clothing, tent fabric, tools, and packs.
- Remove seeds, pollen, soil, spores, and organic debris.
- Avoid bringing untreated wood, leather, plant fibers, untreated food scraps, potting soil, or companion animals.
- Keep food sealed.
- Carry out all organic waste.

Likewise, protect your homeworld from the city. Do not leave with unexamined frost, mineral film, dust, animal residue, or water in your gear. Quarantine yourself and your equipment after departure.

The “simple films” on surfaces may not be alive in a familiar sense, but they respond to charge. Do not scrape them, step on unusual membranes, or expose them to static discharge.

## 11. Electrical and magnetic caution

The city contains electromagnetic leakage, charged films, buried diodes, magnetic dust, and animals that may feed on circuitry.

Prepare:

- Shielded electronics.
- Waterproof and condensation-proof casings.
- Faraday pouches for critical devices.
- Manual backups for everything.
- Non-electronic fire-starting alternatives, though open flame should not be used casually.
- Mechanical compass only as a last-resort curiosity, not a primary tool.
- Surge protection for implants or powered medical devices.

Do not lay electronics directly on wet stone or metal. Do not string cables across streets. Do not expose battery contacts. Small creatures may be attracted to swallowed or leaking circuitry.

## 12. Respect doors, vents, bridges, and plates

A door that opens for “no visible user” may be opening for pressure, vapor, sound, or an animal you cannot see. A bridge may be part of a condensation system. A road plate may be a musical control surface for herds. A vent may be communal heat.

Before touching infrastructure, ask:

- Is this a path for humans?
- Is this a path for animals?
- Is this carrying water?
- Is this carrying heat?
- Is this a pressure relief surface?
- Is this sleeping, growing, or listening?

Do not pry. Do not wedge doors open. Do not anchor ropes to pipes, cables, rails, or animal roost lines. Do not chip ice unless told to. Ice may be structural, thermal, or informational.

## 13. Bring shelter that can survive wet cold without contaminating the city

A normal tent will be miserable and possibly unsafe. Better options:

- A compact sealed bivy pod.
- A low-heat-loss expedition shelter.
- Condensation-managed sleeping system.
- Insulated ground platform.
- Internal humidity control.
- Quiet air cycling.
- No dripping runoff into local channels unless permitted.

Your shelter should not glow, hum loudly, leak heat, shed fibers, or off-gas strong chemicals.

Never camp in:

- Animal passages.
- Under cable roosts.
- Near steaming vents.
- On resonant road plates.
- In dry-looking basins; they may be reservoirs waiting to fill.
- Under ice fins.
- Beside black water loops.
- Against walls that weep periodically.

The safest shelter may be an authorized sealed room, machine dormitory, human alcove, or guided pressure chamber.

## 14. Food should be sealed, low-odor, and self-contained

There are animals everywhere, and many may have unfamiliar appetites. Human food smells, salts, heat, sugars, or packaging may attract attention.

Bring:

- Sealed ration blocks.
- Odor-controlled containers.
- No crumb-shedding foods.
- Minimal cooking requirements.
- A no-flame heating method.
- Waste bags that seal completely.

Avoid cooking aromatic meals. Avoid spilling broth or oil. Do not share food with fauna or leave offerings unless a local guide instructs you in a specific ritual.

## 15. Prepare psychologically

This place may be beautiful, but it will not be comforting in ordinary human ways.

Expect:

- Almost no sunlight.
- No green living things.
- Constant wetness.
- Continuous sound.
- Crowded nonhuman life.
- Slow systems that ignore your schedule.
- Claustrophobic architecture.
- Vast dark overhead clouds.
- Animals in drains, ledges, tunnels, bridges, and plazas.
- The sense that buildings are organs.
- The sense that you are inside weather rather than under it.

Bring methods for mental steadiness: breathing exercises, a quiet routine, a small familiar object, journaling tools, or guided audio kept private through earpieces. Travelers with severe claustrophobia, sensory sensitivity, fear of insects, fear of darkness, or panic responses to animal crowding should train beforehand or avoid the visit.

## 16. Time moves differently here, practically if not physically

Gates may open over days. Machines harvest ice seasonally. Streets tilt over years. The city dislikes hurry.

Plan for delays. Carry extra supplies. Do not schedule a tight return window unless your transit method can extract you safely from a known point.

A “short crossing” may become impossible because a herd is passing, a corridor has lengthened, a drainage channel is being rewritten, or a gravitational pylon is active. Patience is survival equipment.

## 17. Medical preparation

Bring a robust medical kit including:

- Hypothermia treatment supplies.
- Emergency warming packs.
- Oxygen.
- Altitude sickness medication if suitable.
- Anti-inflammatory medication.
- Broad-spectrum wound cleaning supplies.
- Waterproof bandages.
- Antihistamines.
- Eye wash.
- Electrolytes.
- Antifungal/antimicrobial options, though local life may not respond to standard drugs.
- Treatment for trench foot and skin maceration.
- Ear protection and nausea medication for vibration/vertigo.
- A way to monitor body temperature.

Any cut can be complicated by mineral-rich water and unknown films. Seal wounds quickly. Do not expose open skin to puddles, mist-heavy surfaces, animal residue, or frost membranes.

## 18. If you become lost

Do not run.

Recommended lost protocol:

1. Stop moving.
2. Reduce light.
3. Reduce noise.
4. Step out of animal traffic if possible, but do not enter unknown ducts.
5. Check temperature, oxygen, and injury status.
6. Mark your position.
7. Listen for regular transit sounds or guide signals.
8. Use a low-power beacon.
9. Wait.

If animals begin to gather, stay calm. They may be investigating heat, salt, charge, or vibration. Do not kick or swat. Slowly insulate exposed warmth and secure electronics.

If the city itself seems to be redirecting you—doors opening, floors softening, corridors delaying—do not fight it unless it is clearly leading you into immediate danger. It may be preventing you from entering a hazardous cycle.

## 19. If something goes wrong with the fauna

If a large creature blocks your path: wait. It may move in an hour. The pavement may be adjusting to its weight. Going around may put you into a worse route.

If puddle-swarms approach: lift your feet slowly out of the water and stand on dry stone or an approved walkway. Check electronics.

If blind gutter-creatures brush your boots: remain still, let them taste the runoff, and avoid stepping on them.

If flying creatures drop from cables: crouch slowly, protect your face, dim lights, and avoid sudden heat venting.

If herds are moving: never cross between them. Their passage may be operating the street.

## 20. Cultural posture: humility

Even if no visible human citizens greet you, behave as if you are being observed. The city may communicate through frost, pressure, animal movement, delayed doors, or changes in sound.

Good manners likely include:

- Moving slowly.
- Conserving heat.
- Using little light.
- Keeping water clean and circulating.
- Not touching membranes, animals, vents, or shells.
- Yielding to nonhuman traffic.
- Accepting delays.
- Leaving no biological contamination.
- Offering energy or useful minerals only through proper channels.
- Not taking souvenirs.

A polished railing, a tenderly arranged alcove, or a narrow door may be sacred or domestic. Do not photograph private spaces without permission. Do not enter sealed rooms because you are curious.

At minimum:

- Sealed boots with ice traction.
- Gloves, hood, face shield, respirator.
- Low-light goggles.
- Shielded dim lamp with filters.
- Hearing protection.
- Oxygen and altitude kit.
- Closed-loop thermal support.

- Waste containment.

- Waterproof medical kit.

## 22. Best attitude for survival

Think of the city as a cold, wet, dark commons where nothing is wasted: not heat, not water, not sound, not animal motion, not geological change.

You are warm, bright, noisy, fast, leaky, and biologically messy by local standards. Your preparation should make you less of all those things.

Be dim.  
Be slow.  
Be sealed.  
Be quiet.  
Be clean.  
Yield to water, animals, and the city’s timing.

If you can do that, the place may let you pass through it. If you cannot, it may not need to attack you. It can simply become too cold, too wet, too dark, too loud, too confusing, and too alive for a human body to endure.
