An ordinary human traveler should prepare as if visiting a high mountain, a swamp, a cave system, a flooded ruin, and a very quiet sacred village all at once. The greatest danger is not violence. It is damp, disorientation, slow structural failure, mineral water, respiratory trouble, and misunderstanding the local pace of life.

## 1. First decision: do not arrive alone

This is not a place for independent exploration.

You should arrange, before arrival, for:

- **A local guide or host family**
- **Permission from the settlement elders or tether-keepers**
- **A reliable interpreter**, preferably someone fluent in hand signs, knock-signals, and spoken local phrases
- **A place to sleep above the current flood mark**
- **An agreed emergency signal**
- **A return route that does not depend on clear weather**

Maps are unreliable here. The town moves, water levels change, mineral growth alters passages, and rooms may become unusable between visits. A traveler relying on a drawn map may find that a canal has narrowed, a platform has drifted, or a safe air pocket has been sealed by plant growth.

Think of the city less as a fixed location and more as a living arrangement of ropes, anchors, terraces, currents, and obligations.

## 2. Physical conditioning

You do not need to be an athlete, but you should be comfortable with:

- Swimming in low visibility
- Climbing wet ladders and ropes
- Balancing on floating platforms
- Holding your breath calmly
- Moving slowly while weighted
- Remaining damp for long periods
- Sleeping in humid, noisy, unstable conditions
- Not panicking when the floor, wall, or waterline is not where you expect it to be

If you are afraid of deep water, enclosed flooded spaces, murky water, or unstable footing, this place will be extremely difficult. Even “streets” may require partial immersion. Some doorways open into flooded chambers. Some chambers contain breathable air trapped below structures, while others only appear to.

Practice beforehand in controlled flooded environments. Learn to move without splashing, shouting, or grabbing at things. A clumsy visitor can damage a garden, break a tether, tear root mats, or stir silt badly enough to blind everyone nearby.

## 3. Medical preparation

The environment is warm, wet, mineral-rich, and biologically active. Prepare for skin, lung, gut, and ear problems.

Bring or arrange:

- Waterproof wound coverings
- Antifungal treatment
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics if appropriate and legally/medically available
- Anti-parasitic precautions if advised by your multiverse medical service
- Electrolyte salts
- Safe drinking-water treatment
- Ear-drying solution
- Anti-inflammatory medicine
- Spare prescription medications in sealed dry containers
- A medical note explaining allergies and chronic conditions
- A tetanus-equivalent vaccination, if relevant to your biology and the local microbial ecology

Common visitor problems will likely include:

- Foot rot or skin maceration
- Infected scrapes from basalt, shell, reed, or mineral crust
- Ear infections
- Respiratory irritation from constant vapor and mineral aerosols
- Gastrointestinal illness from untreated water or unfamiliar plants
- Heat exhaustion, despite the high elevation
- Sleep disruption from humidity and the constant sound of rain

Do not assume warm spring water is sterile. Mineral vents may discourage some organisms while feeding others.

If you have asthma, immune suppression, severe eczema, chronic ear disease, or poor mobility, consult a specialist before going.

## 4. Clothing

Bring clothing that accepts being wet rather than trying to remain dry.

Recommended:

- Lightweight, quick-draining garments
- Close-fitting sleeves and leggings to protect from abrasion
- Soft-soled water shoes with excellent grip
- A second pair of shoes, sealed dry if possible
- A warm layer for nighttime damp chill, even if the air is not cold
- A simple waterproof cloak or poncho for upper platforms
- Gloves for ropes, reeds, and rough mineral edges
- A head covering against constant rain and dripping condensation

Avoid:

- Heavy boots that fill with water
- Thick cotton
- Loose dangling fabric that catches on roots and tethers
- Brightly dyed clothing if local customs favor muted utility
- Metal jewelry, especially if it corrodes, catches, or signals wealth

Expect everything to smell of wet leaves, clay, rope, oil, and mineral water within a day.

## 5. Essential equipment

You need waterproofing, flotation, breathing assistance, and redundancy.

Bring:

- Waterproof dry bags, several small rather than one large
- A personal flotation aid
- A compact mask or goggles suitable for murky water
- A cutting tool that can be operated one-handed
- Cordage compatible with local tying methods, or trade cord
- A low, hooded light source
- Spare light sources sealed against moisture
- Fire-making tools in waterproof containers, though you should not use them casually
- A whistle only for emergencies, because loud signals may be culturally offensive
- A slate or waterproof writing surface
- Simple pictorial communication cards
- Skin-safe insect or leech deterrent if applicable
- A small repair kit: needles, cord, patches, resin or sealant
- A personal cup or drinking vessel
- A sleeping sling or hammock if not provided
- Earplugs, though you must still be able to hear alarm signals

Do not bring large rigid luggage. It will be hated immediately. Pack as though boarding a narrow canoe through a floating garden.

## 6. Breathing and water movement

The locals use breathing gourds, trapped air jars, reed lungs, and known air pockets. Do not improvise with these without instruction.

Before entering flooded chambers, learn:

- Which markers indicate breathable air
- Which air pockets are public and which belong to households
- How to recognize stale or unsafe trapped air
- How to signal distress underwater
- How to use weighted belts
- How to remove a weight belt quickly
- How to avoid being lifted into root mats by buoyant currents
- How to cross a vertical warm current safely
- How to move without stirring silt

The water does not always behave like ordinary water. In some areas, density gradients may create layered spaces where plants seem suspended overhead. In others, warm currents rise like invisible columns. You may float unexpectedly, sink unexpectedly, or find that a movement that worked in one lane fails in another.

Never assume that “up” means air.

Never enter a submerged room unless a local has confirmed the route, the exit, and the current state of the air.

## 7. Navigation

You will navigate by relationships, not by streets.

Learn the basic local markers:

- Spirals: warm vents
- Crossed lines: safe tethering
- Notched circles: public trapped air wells
- Warning reeds: unstable or forbidden formation
- Carved post patterns: depth, current, family, age of wood, hazards, obligations

Carry a tactile route memory: knots, textures, post shapes, rope directions. Visibility can collapse quickly if silt rises or rain thickens the surface. You may need to return by touch.

Ask your guide to teach you:

- The route from sleeping place to public platform
- The route to the nearest safe air pocket
- The route to the nearest high dry stone
- The emergency assembly platform
- The sound of a tether alarm bell
- The local signal for “do not enter”
- The local signal for “current changed”
- The local signal for “vent opened”

Journeys outside the settlement should be taken only with experienced locals. Distances are measured in breaths, currents, knots, and safe rests. Your instinct for distance will be unreliable.

## 8. Food and water

Eat what your hosts provide unless you have a medical reason not to. The local food system is subtle and tied to water chemistry, seasonal rain, and vent warmth.

Likely foods include:

- Taro-like roots
- Waterferns
- softened tubers
- floating gourds
- waxy fruits
- edible leaves
- mineral-warmed basket-cooked foods
- fermented plant matter
- occasional eel, shell-creatures, or other aquatic protein if culturally permitted

Do not harvest casually. A plant may be someone’s garden, someone’s medicine, a current marker, a root binding a platform, or part of a shrine.

Do not drink directly from canals, pools, or vents. Use water identified by locals as safe, then treat it if your biology requires.

Be cautious with mineral-rich water. Some may contain dissolved compounds that are harmless to locals but hard on visitors’ kidneys, gut, or nervous system.

## 9. Fire etiquette

Fire is rare, precious, and dangerous.

Do not light a flame without permission.

Do not smoke.

Do not use an open-flame stove on reed platforms, moss roofs, or near drying pith.

If you carry high-energy technology, batteries, chemical heaters, or energy weapons, treat them as fire hazards and cultural disruptions. A dropped hot device could burn through fiberwork, ignite precious dry fuel, or boil a small air pocket.

Use hooded, low lights. Bright lights at night may attract insects, offend local norms, or draw unwanted social attention. Lamps are carefully shaded here for a reason.

## 10. Cultural behavior

The people live by maintenance, patience, and attention. They will judge you less by your bravery than by whether you notice small changes and avoid damaging shared systems.

Important manners:

- Speak softly.
- Do not shout unless someone is in immediate danger.
- Learn basic hand signs.
- Wait before stepping onto platforms.
- Ask before tying anything to anything.
- Ask before cutting any rope, reed, root, or vine.
- Do not lean on walls unless invited; walls may be woven, swollen, or load-bearing in non-obvious ways.
- Do not enter flooded rooms casually.
- Do not touch carved posts without understanding whether they are markers, records, or sacred objects.
- Do not mock slow decision-making.
- Do not call the place “unfinished” as an insult.
- Do not describe the water as “wrong” or “unnatural” in front of locals.

Ownership here is maintenance. If someone keeps a thing from drifting, rotting, drowning, or overgrowing, it is theirs in part. That may include a garden raft, a mineral shelf, a roof-root system, a warm vent basket, a wall, a platform, or a route.

If you break something, the proper response is not merely apology or payment. You may be expected to help repair, tend, re-tie, scrape, watch, or compensate with labor.

## 11. Gifts and trade

Good gifts are practical, durable, and not socially destabilizing.

Consider bringing:

- High-quality cordage
- Non-rusting needles or awls, if acceptable
- Waterproof containers
- Fine fishhooks or small cutting edges
- Medical salves
- Salt, if scarce
- Resin, wax, or waterproofing compounds
- Simple lenses or goggles
- Durable pigments
- Soft cloth
- Knowledge of safe water testing
- Quiet lighting that can be repaired locally

Avoid bringing:

- Weapons
- Loud machines
- Cheap trinkets
- Anything that requires fuel or parts they cannot replace
- Strong alcohol unless invited
- Flashy jewelry
- Seeds, spores, or live organisms without strict ecological clearance
- Iron tools if they corrode quickly or imply unwanted dependency

The best trade item may be labor: scraping mineral from tools, braiding cord, carrying baskets, repairing rafts, keeping watch during rain, or helping tend a roof garden.

## 12. The Unlaid District

Do not enter the Unlaid District without explicit permission and expert guidance.

It is not simply a ruin or quarry. It is an active mineral-forming zone where straight pale stone walls rise in unexplained patterns. The locals harvest its edges cautiously, but they mark it with warning reeds for a reason.

Risks may include:

- Soft mineral crust that collapses underfoot
- Sudden density changes
- Hot seepage
- Entrapment between growing ridges
- Disorientation in right-angled submerged passages
- Social or spiritual taboo
- Unknown geological agency

If a new wall has recently risen, keep away. Fresh mineral may look stable and be soft as chalk. It may harden over months, not minutes.

Never chip stone there as a souvenir.

## 13. Environmental hazards

The major hazards are slow, quiet, and easy to underestimate.

Watch for:

- Fraying tethers
- Shifting rafts
- Mineral shelves blocking drainage
- Root masses closing doorways
- Sudden warm patches under floors
- Vents opening or changing flow
- Silt clouds
- Slippery algae on stairs
- Sharp young basalt
- Collapsing mineral chimneys
- Stale air pockets
- Rain so dense it erases horizon and sound
- Floating vegetation mats that appear solid
- Shell-creatures on handholds
- Infections from tiny cuts
- Getting separated in low visibility

The local saying, if they have one, would probably mean: disaster arrives by becoming normal first.

Make a habit of checking where you place your feet and hands. Pause often. Notice whether a rope has changed tension, whether water feels warmer, whether a platform sits lower, whether a current now pulls sideways, whether plants are bending differently.

## 14. Psychological preparation

This place may unsettle you.

The ordinary categories do not hold cleanly. Mountain behaves like reef. Water behaves like air. Streets are canals. Houses float and sink. Rooms change purpose across generations. Stone grows. Maps decay. Ownership is responsibility. Silence is social grace. Danger is gradual.

A visitor from a fast, dry, mechanical civilization may feel:

- Claustrophobic
- Watched by the environment
- Frustrated by slow decisions
- Unclean from constant damp
- Disoriented by dim green light
- Anxious about drowning
- Lonely because voices do not carry
- Awed by the patience of local life

Prepare to slow down. This is not a place to tour aggressively. It is a place to sit on a damp platform at noon while rain erases the sky, listening to a rope creak and watching someone repair a basket with the same seriousness another civilization gives to law.

If you cannot respect slowness, you should not go.

## 15. Daily routine for a visitor

A sensible day might look like this:

- Wake in a hammock or raised sleeping space.
- Check your skin, feet, and gear for damp damage.
- Confirm water level and current changes with your host.
- Eat a simple warm meal.
- Review hand signals before leaving.
- Travel by platform or narrow boat, not alone.
- Visit gardens, vents, craft areas, or public plazas.
- Keep notes on waterproof slate, not loose paper.
- Rest often; humidity and balance work are tiring.
- Return before evening darkness thickens.
- Dry your feet and ears as well as possible.
- Store gear above flood marks.
- Sleep lightly enough to respond to bells or cord signals.

Do not wander at night. The town becomes beautiful then, with hooded lamps reflected in black-green water, but it also becomes harder to read. A familiar platform in daylight may be a trap in darkness.

## 16. Emergency conduct

If a tether breaks:

- Do not grab the nearest rope unless told.
- Move to the platform or structure your guide indicates.
- Keep your weight low.
- Stay out of water if loose rafts are moving.
- Listen for clay bells and knock-signals.

If you fall into water:

- Do not thrash.
- Protect your head from undersides of platforms.
- Orient by light, bubbles, and known structures, but remember air may not be directly above.
- Use your flotation aid unless weighted movement is required.
- Signal with agreed gestures or knocks.

If trapped in a submerged room:

- Do not waste breath shouting.
- Find the wall and follow it slowly.
- Look for tactile markers near exits.
- If you have a line, use it.
- If you find an air pocket, test cautiously; do not inhale deeply at once.

If separated:

- Stop moving unless remaining still is dangerous.
- Find a stable marker or tether point.
- Use the agreed low signal.
- Do not choose random canals.
- Wait for locals; they can read the city better than you can.

## 17. Technology caution

High technology may work poorly here.

Problems include:

- Constant condensation
- Mineral fouling
- Biofilm growth
- Corrosion
- Weak signal propagation through dense water, stone, and vapor
- Battery failure from damp heat
- Sensor confusion in layered-density water
- Drones tangling in roots, ropes, or rain curtains
- Sonar disturbing animals or people
- Bright screens ruining night vision and drawing attention

Bring rugged, waterproof, low-profile tools. Keep them secondary. Local knowledge is the primary survival system.

## 18. What to learn before arrival

At minimum, learn:

- Basic swimming and breath control
- How to tread water calmly
- How to use a mask in murky water
- Rope safety
- Basic knots, especially quick-release knots
- How to climb wet ladders
- How to recognize hypoxia and bad air
- How to treat wet-environment wounds
- How to move quietly in someone else’s home
- Local greetings, thanks, apology, and emergency signs

Also learn to sit still. That may be the most important skill.

## 19. Attitude to bring

The right attitude is humble attentiveness.

Do not arrive as an explorer claiming discovery. People already live here. They have made a city inside conditions that would defeat most outsiders. Their technologies are not primitive; they are precisely adapted. A woven wall that swells tight in water, a roof that filters rain and feeds roots, a basket that cooks in a spring without fire, a knot readable in darkness—these are sophisticated responses to a world where metal towers and engines might simply rot, sink, or poison the settlement.

Praise function. Ask before admiring sacred things. Notice maintenance. Offer help. Move slowly.

## 20. Final advice

Bring less than you think, waterproof it better than you think, and trust local instruction more than your instincts.

The drowned highland is beautiful, but it is not scenic in a safe, distant way. It surrounds you. It gets into your clothes, your breath, your tools, your sleep. It changes while you are looking away. Its dangers are not dramatic; they are patient. Its hospitality is real, but it depends on your willingness to become part of a web of tethers, platforms, roots, air pockets, and obligations.

Visit as you would enter a living house during a flood: softly, gratefully, with clean hands, quiet feet, and no assumption that anything is yours to move.
