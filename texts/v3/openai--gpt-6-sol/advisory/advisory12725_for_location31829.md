Prepare for this as a **high, dry plateau with unreliable distance**, not as an easy walk across flat ground. Before going far, check the air pressure and your own response to the altitude; being above the clouds may mean thin air even though the daylight feels ordinary. Bring water, food, warm layers, sun and eye protection, sturdy shoes, and a way to keep mineral dust out of your mouth and equipment. There is no visible source of water, and the grass is not a useful sign that you will find one.

Make the first visit short and go with a companion. Agree on a return time using your own clocks rather than the apparent dusk. Establish and test a return point *before* leaving it behind. Do not navigate by shadows, the horizon, or a distant cairn: none has proved dependable here. Follow the hand-cut routes one short section at a time, noting nearby animals and seams in the pavement as local references. Keep your companion and the next reference in sight. If a route seems to cross another without meeting it, or your return point ceases to behave as expected, stop and retrace only what you can verify; do not walk farther in hopes that the landscape will resolve itself.

Carry a means of close-range communication that does not depend on calling across the basin—agreed hand signals are a good start. Keep ordinary emergency supplies with you rather than at a distant camp. The animals appear calm, but their stillness is no invitation to touch, feed, or walk through them. Give the herds room, use the routes around them, and leave the bone, cairns, and cuts as you found them.

Most of all, allow more time than the terrain seems to require. On this plateau, a nearby animal may be a better landmark than an entire shelf of stone, and a place that looks an hour away may never get closer.
