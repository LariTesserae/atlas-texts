Prepare as though you are visiting a cold, wet ruin whose roads cannot be trusted to lead back where they led yesterday. The city may be quiet and apparently gentle, but its chief danger is separation: from your companions, your supplies, or the way out.

**Bring everything you need to remain independent for several days.** Carry shelter, warm layers, a waterproof outer layer, dry spare clothing, food, a stove, and more fuel than an ordinary walk would require. There are plants, but no evident animals or working food supply; do not assume any leaf is edible. Take containers for water and a means to filter and disinfect it. A full cistern, a clear pool, or water emerging from a gutter may still be unsafe to drink. Wear boots with grip on wet, frost-covered stone.

**Make navigation a procedure, not a memory.** Agree on a departure point and a latest return time before entering. Record each doorway and junction as you pass it, including which *side* of a square you arrived from. Draw the view through an opening before crossing, then check it again on your return. Landmarks, compass bearings, and walking times may all remain useful observations, but none should be treated as a guarantee of distance or connection. Even the apparently continuous plateau can make an afternoon’s walk much shorter or longer than expected.

Travel with a companion if possible, and stay within sight of each other when crossing thresholds. The silence may make calling less effective than you expect, so agree on visible signals. Do not split up to “quickly check” two doors: they may lead to places miles apart. If a route changes, stop and reassess rather than pursuing what you remember around one more corner. Leave enough daylight and supplies to wait for a safer route instead of forcing a return at night.

Choose camps for their **present** shelter and drainage, not their apparent proximity to an exit. Avoid drain mouths, cistern edges, roofless courts that collect water, and old structures whose brick or timber has spent centuries damp. A dry street can receive water from a storm you cannot see. Frost will settle in the empty stone spaces first.

Finally, treat the gutters, gates, roots, and planting beds as parts of one surviving system. Do not block a drain for convenience or cut a vine to mark a turn; a small alteration here may affect a courtyard you cannot reach by any obvious road. The city changes slowly enough to invite confidence. Keep the habits that would let you leave even after it has quietly changed around you.
