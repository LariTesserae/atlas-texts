Treat this as a functioning city whose inhabitants may be its roads, buildings, and water-collecting mechanisms. It looks empty, but it is not still. For a first visit, plan a short reconnaissance from the field, not a walk to the distant towers.

**Prepare to turn back before you arrive.** The towers are much farther away than they appear, and the open stretches offer little shade, shelter, or water. Set a return time, carry more water than a warm day ordinarily calls for, and bring a map, compass, and an independent way to retrace your route. Do not rely on the road markings staying visually constant: they brighten during the pulses. If possible, travel with another person and leave someone outside the city with your route and return deadline.

**Protect your hearing, but pay attention to your whole body.** Bring well-fitting earplugs and over-ear protection. Neither will necessarily protect you from the low vibration that shakes windows and can be felt through the ground. Start well back from the curb and spend time observing a few cycles before going farther. If the sound causes pain, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or difficulty keeping your balance, retreat rather than trying to acclimate. The birds’ sudden silence, trembling frames, and turning leaves may give you warning of a swell; do not count on a fixed schedule.

Wear sturdy boots with good traction, long sleeves, and eye protection. The light is poor despite the warmth, so carry a headlamp for seeing pavement edges and concealed openings—not for assuming the sky tells you how late it is. Ferns hide catch basins, and brief falls of water may make otherwise dry pavement slick. Keep away from tower façades during pulses: windows, roots, and masonry are all under repeated vibration.

Bring your own drinking water. The condensation, gutters, and curb collectors show that water is valuable here, not that it is safe to drink. Likewise, leave the collecting devices alone. Watch them operate from a distance before deciding where it is safe to stand. Avoid testing the newly forming road: its edge advances without visible machinery, and you have no reason to assume the work will stop for a pedestrian.

The place may feel deceptively spacious—an empty six-lane road, groves separated by bare ground, towers that seem to wait at the end of a straight line. Resist the urge to cover distance simply because nothing appears to be coming. First learn where the pulses reach, what they shake loose, and how long it takes to get back to the grass.
