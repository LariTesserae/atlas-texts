Prepare for a **submerged settlement, not a seaside town**. Its houses, streets, and square are all underwater; the roofs keep silt out, not air in. Unless you can breathe water in this universe, arrange a pressure-safe submersible or a properly equipped dive, and confirm where you will sleep in a dry habitat. Do not assume the inhabitants can supply human breathing gas.

Before going, find out the town’s **depth, water chemistry, and route in**. Depth determines whether an ordinary recreational dive is possible at all, what breathing mixture is needed, and how long a safe ascent takes. The warm seams and mineral deposits suggest hydrothermal activity: have a local guide identify water that is safe to enter and rock that is safe to touch. The summit is geologically young, so treat its ledges and steep outer slope as unstable.

Go with a local escort, reliable lights, redundant breathing and navigation equipment, and a plan for returning if visibility fails. Learn which guide lines are public routes before touching or following them. The distinct markers around the bare square may be important to navigation or communal life; ask what they mean rather than moving one. Keep fins and equipment clear of the gardens. Plants grow on thin sediment or directly in cracks, and a careless kick could send an entire plant drifting away.

Expect a visit that feels slow and close despite the open sea overhead. Light fades into green; a little disturbed silt may erase the next house. You may navigate by ropes and shapes felt through a glove more than by sight. Bring a way to communicate underwater, and make arrangements for food, drinking water, and shelter that suit a human visitor. The town’s light, woven buildings are adapted to water and settling rock—not to keeping you dry.
