Treat this as a hot, dark volcanic cavity that happens to be full of animals—not as a sheltered place to rest. The main danger may be the air. Heat rising through the holes could bring harmful gases with it, and a person can become confused or collapse before noticing a smell. The steps, crusts, and fitted stone rings also make it easy to lose footing, while almost every apparent handhold or landing place is occupied.

**Before going in**, arrange a companion to remain outside and agree on a firm turnaround time. Ask a local guide or geologist whether the vents are known to be active. If you cannot assess the air with appropriate gas monitors, do not descend. A dust mask will not protect you from volcanic gases or low oxygen; if a monitor alarms, leave immediately. The stronger evening updraft and glowing fissures are reasons to be out well before dusk.

If conditions have been checked and a short visit is reasonable, bring a helmet, sturdy boots with reliable grip, gloves, eye protection, a headlamp and backup light, drinking water, and a way to communicate with the person outside. Wear clothing that covers your skin without trapping so much heat that you overheat. Test each step rather than trusting a pale crust or a stone worn flush with the floor.

Move slowly and be willing to stop at the entrance. Do not reach into holes, brush animals off a ledge, or assume that a quiet creature will remain still when touched. The crowded passages may transmit a disturbance much farther than you expect. The fitted rings may be old human work; leave them, the mineral crusts, and the animals undisturbed. If there is no clear patch of sound, unoccupied stone for your feet, there is no safe route deeper in. Admire the cleft from outside.
