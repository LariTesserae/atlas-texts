Treat this as a damaged, active service chamber—not a quiet room that happens to have plants in it. Its most important hazards are hard to see: the cracked floor, heat rising from below, water near old electrical equipment, and air whose safety you cannot judge by its smell.

**Before entering,** arrange access with whoever operates the machinery, if anyone does. Go with a partner who stays outside, knows when to call for help, and can keep the exit clear. Bring a strong headlamp and a backup light, sturdy boots with good grip, gloves, and clothing suited to hot, humid conditions. Carry a means of communication that works there. An air monitor capable of checking oxygen and common hazardous gases is more useful than a filter mask; a filter cannot make oxygen-poor air safe. If you cannot assess the air or the stability of the slab, inspect from the doorway rather than going in.

**Inside,** move slowly and keep to sound-looking concrete. Do not step over or test the fresh fracture: its sharp edges and warm draft suggest an ongoing problem beneath the slab, even if nothing visibly moves during a visit. Do not touch the cabinets, cable, wet pipe, or roots near them. The condensate vanishes into the crack, so dry-looking floor does not mean the area is dry or electrically safe. Mark your return route with light rather than assuming the inspection lamp will show you the door from the far end.

Keep the visit short. Heat can build under the ceiling, and the machinery’s slow airflow changes may make the room feel safer or less safe without actually resolving the hazard. Leave immediately if you feel dizzy, unusually breathless, confused, or overheated, if your air monitor alarms, or if you hear the floor or equipment change.

For a multiverse visit, add one more precaution: avoid taking leaves, water, or residue back with you until you know the destination’s contamination rules. This place rewards observation from a safe distance more than exploration by touch.
