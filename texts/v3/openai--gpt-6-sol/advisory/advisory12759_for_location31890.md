Treat this as an expedition onto a moving, high-altitude surface—not a hike across level ground. The apparent ease of walking is one of its hazards: there may be no shelter within a day’s travel, little air to breathe, and no dependable landmark to guide you back.

**Before arriving, arrange a reliable way out.** Travel with a partner and leave your route, check-in times, and extraction plan with someone outside the plateau. Bring a pressure-capable suit if the air pressure is unknown; at minimum, carry breathing equipment rated for extreme altitude, with independent reserves. Add cold-weather insulation, eye and skin protection against strong sun, and sealed protection for your lungs and equipment against fine powder. Carry more water and power than the walk seems to require. Do not plan to live off the sparse growth or wildlife.

**Navigate by measurements, not appearances.** Take inertial navigation and a record of your starting position, and compare them often with any other instruments that work there. A visible ridge may be much farther away than it looks. Footprints may persist for years—or vanish by evening. The black masts are useful things to observe, but poor things to trust as fixed markers: both they and the space between them may change. Do not camp beside one or walk between a pair when the horizon is bending there.

**Watch the ground continuously.** In air this thin, a breaking rib or approaching crack may be nearly silent; by the time you hear it, the event may be over. Have one person watch while the other works, and use visual or tactile alerts rather than shouted warnings alone. Keep clear of long ribs that could split, broad cracks, and powder flats showing sudden movement. If a crack starts toward you, move away from its line at once; do not count on outrunning it. Choose camps for their distance from visible faulting and masts, not merely for shelter from the wind.

Keep excursions short at first. Survey how powder moves, whether known objects change position, and whether your instruments still agree before committing to a distant crossing. Photograph and log changes, but resist investigating a newly shifted rib simply because it is extraordinary. A place that makes fresh stone look ancient in days may also erase the evidence you need to retrace a mistake.

Above all, set a firm turnaround time. The plateau offers almost no audible warning and few signs of how far you have gone. Leave while your air, daylight, and route home are still certain.
