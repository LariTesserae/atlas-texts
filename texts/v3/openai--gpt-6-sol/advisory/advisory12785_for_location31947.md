Prepare for a hot, wet mountain that is still high enough to make breathing difficult. The warmth and lush growth could make it easy to forget the altitude. Before committing to a climb, verify that the air is safe to breathe, acclimatize gradually, and carry supplemental oxygen if your measurements or your own symptoms call for it. If you become dizzy, confused, or unusually breathless, descend; do not mistake heat and humidity for proof that altitude cannot be the cause.

Travel light, but bring equipment for exposed, slippery ground: boots with reliable grip, a helmet, a harness and rope, and layers that work when soaked but can be removed in the heat. Protect your drinking water and treat any water you collect. The cliff seeps may look pristine, but their chemistry and biology are unknown. Keep electronics and emergency supplies waterproof.

Plan short routes with generous time to turn back. A shelf that looks continuous from across the valley may end at a drainage, a root mat, or an open drop. Mist can erase the far side of the valley in minutes, so carry a way to navigate without sight of landmarks and agree on a rendezvous and rescue plan before setting out. Avoid climbing beside running water or trusting vegetation as an anchor. Check rock and soil before putting weight on them, especially where roots hold a shelf together.

Treat the mountain as a living structure, not an abandoned ruin. Stay off the persistently moist patches where possible: the yielding sheets beneath them appear to regulate water for whole stands of trees. Do not cut them, pry at cliff seams, move stones, or collect the clear studs. Even a small alteration to a spring or a root-bound shelf may have consequences that unfold long after your visit. Observe the stair, shelter, and studs without assuming they are safe—or unowned.

Give the animals space, particularly at dusk when grazers approach the mineral seeps. Keep food sealed, avoid brushing against unfamiliar plants, and watch where you place your hands among reeds and rock crevices. If you can choose your travel hours, move during the cooler part of the day and be settled somewhere secure before evening mist rises.

Above all, resist the mountain’s invitation to keep climbing toward the next visible wall. From the valley, each apparent height hides another. Set your limit by your supplies, breathing, weather, and route home—not by the hope of seeing the summit.
