Prepare to visit the **riverbank**, not to enter the pocket. From above it is no wider than cupped hands, underwater, nearly dark, and roofed by freshly broken clay. An ordinary person cannot get inside without destroying it—or being caught beneath a collapsing bank. The distance between the pebble walls is deceptive, but there is no reason to assume that their enlarged interior would admit a human safely.

Go with a companion who stays on firm ground, and choose a time when the river is low and unlikely to rise. Bring warm, waterproof clothing, a headlamp for the bank, and a way to call for help. Do not stand on the new clay shelf or reach beneath it. Work from a stable position farther back: a small waterproof camera on a flexible probe, gentle low-intensity illumination, and a marked scale held **outside** the opening should be enough for a first visit. Keep the probe from touching the roots, leaves, or pebble walls.

Expect your eyes and your sense of distance to disagree. Photograph the walls from several positions, but do not use the apparent gap as a measure of how far anything must travel. A probe or loose object might take much longer to cross it than its visible width suggests, and might come to rest on a surface you cannot see. Do not send in a tether you cannot afford to lose, and do not try to retrieve a snagged instrument by pulling on the bank.

Watch quietly before doing anything else. The little movements are part of the place: a flex passing through stems, a drifting clay grain, an occasional creature felt before it is seen. Note the dull taps and whether they coincide with fresh falling grains or changes in the shelf. If the bank cracks, sheds more clay, or the current rises, leave.

Most importantly, leave the pebble walls as they are. They may be deliberate, and even if they are not, the living growth and impossible-looking space between them are easily disturbed. The best preparation is patience, a safe vantage point, and a willingness to come away having observed rather than entered.
