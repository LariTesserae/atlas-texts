Treat the shelf as an **active construction site at the edge of a cliff**, not as a scenic overlook. The stone is being added beneath your feet, the ground moves every few minutes, and cloud conceals the drop. The fact that beetles cross a closing seam safely does not mean it will accommodate a human boot.

Before going, arrange a local guide or a remote observer who understands the shelf’s growth cycle. Confirm that the air is safe to breathe and that the instruments are safe to approach; their pulses appear to coincide with movement in the stone, but you should not assume you understand or control them. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return. Go in daylight, with a reliable way to navigate back if cloud removes your view of the route.

Wear grippy boots suited to smooth, wet rock, long trousers, and gloves for sharp fragments. Bring rain protection, drinking water, a light, and a means of communication that works there. Do not count on the pools as drinking water: they are warm, mineral-depositing, and full of insects. If you need to work near the edge, use a properly placed anchor and rope rather than trusting a loose stone or an unattended instrument.

On the shelf, move slowly and stay well back from both the clouded edge and the newest, steaming ground. Keep clear of seams, especially when the deep note sounds; stop walking and let the movement finish. Watch for shallow pools that make the dark plates especially slick, and for hot patches where water steams. Avoid touching or stepping beneath the instruments, and do not disturb the sparse grass or the animals sheltering there. Birds may land on fresh stone almost immediately, but they can retreat upward in a way you cannot.

Plan a short visit and an easy retreat. The shelf will not have quite the same shape when you leave as it did when you arrived; mark your return route using distant, stable reference points rather than footprints, puddles, or the apparent line of the edge.
