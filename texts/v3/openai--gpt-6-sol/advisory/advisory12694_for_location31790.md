Treat this as an unstable, flooded stone mechanism, not a plain. Its basin rims may look like a continuous walking surface, but they are thin, icy, and set above moving seams. An ordinary traveler’s safest plan is to observe from a verified ridge and leave before conditions change, rather than attempt a crossing.

Before going, arrange a reliable return route and tell someone when to expect you. Go with a companion if possible. Carry a map or route markers that do not depend on visibility, a light, first-aid supplies, and enough food and dry clothing to wait out a delay. Wear a waterproof cold-water suit or equivalent insulation, a flotation vest, gloves that still permit a firm grip, and boots suited to wet stone and ice. Keep essential gear attached to your body and protected from water. Bring drinking water; the basin water may carry grit, organisms, or water forced up from deep strata.

On the plain, move slowly and test every step. Avoid basin rims, open seams, flooded channels, and places where spray is venting or warmer water is rising. Do not reach into the water: the long-bodied hunters use the seams, and even an empty-looking opening may close or shift around a hand. The larger dark animals are another reason not to enter the channels. A walking pole can help assess footing, but do not put your weight on a rim merely because it held the pole.

**Leave immediately if the turning falters.** The animals retreat at that point, and the subsequent pulse may bring shifting stone, surging water, escaping pressure, or ice breaking underfoot. The odd order of sounds and visible events also means you should not count on a splash, crack, or rumble giving you time to react. Choose your retreat route in advance; do not wait for a warning you recognize.

Expect the cold and wet to become dangerous before the landscape looks dramatic. Mist will erase landmarks, noise will make speech difficult, and the near-identical basins will make distance hard to judge. Set a short turnaround time and honor it. There is little shelter, no dependable dry ground for a camp, and no good reason to be on the stone when fatigue starts making decisions for you.
