Prepare for a cold mountain visit, but pack as though you will spend much of it beside running water. Bring waterproof boots with good grip, spare dry socks and gloves, insulating layers beneath a windproof shell, and a way to keep your bedding and essentials dry. The sun will be fierce even when the air hurts to breathe: carry sunglasses and sunscreen. If you are coming from lower country, allow time to adjust to the altitude and expect to move slowly.

The chief danger is trusting the ground to be where it was. Treat a dry stair as a possible streambed and clear ice as a cover over moving water, not as a floor. Use a walking pole to feel for edges, take short steps on wet shale, and ask before crossing any plank or narrow bridge. A route that worked in the morning may not work on the way back. Leave yourself daylight and an alternate way home.

Arrange a place to stay before arriving. The hollow has little spare space or growing ground, so carry your own food, medicines, and other necessities rather than assuming a household can supply them. Ask which water is safe to drink and where washing or waste disposal is permitted. Do not shift stones, clear ice, or redirect a trickle on your own: a small change upstream may send water through someone’s doorway.

Most of all, follow the residents’ attention. If they stop to watch a new wet line, stop too; if they point rather than shout, look where they point. Keep out of the narrow lanes when people are carrying planks or wedges, and avoid disturbing the birds and animals sheltering among the houses. In this settlement, being a considerate guest means noticing a change early, then letting the people who know the hollow decide what to do about it.
