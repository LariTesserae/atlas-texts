Prepare for this as a **high, exposed plateau**, not a gentle walk across level ground. Its broad swells and warm stone could make it feel hospitable until wind, rain, and cloud take away your bearings.

- **Confirm the basics before crossing over:** breathable air, tolerable gravity and temperature, and whether local water and organisms pose unfamiliar risks. Do not rely on appearance alone.
- **Dress for rapid changes.** Wear close-fitting layers, a windproof and waterproof shell, sturdy boots with grip on wet stone, and gloves. Bring eye protection against blown grit. Keep loose straps secured; an umbrella will be more trouble than help.
- **Carry your own treated water and food.** The little pools may be seasonal, biologically active, or needed by animals and other visitors. If you must use one, filter and disinfect the water rather than drinking directly from a seam or shelter bowl.
- **Navigate independently of what you can see or hear.** Record your arrival point and route, carry a map and compass or a reliable locator, and mark a turnaround time. Low cloud can hide the distant ranges, shallow rises can conceal your return path, and the plateau’s noise may mask a companion’s voice. Travel with someone if possible and agree on hand signals.
- **Plan for exposure.** Check the weather, leave time to retreat before dark, and avoid the plateau during thunderstorms or severe wind. Stop and brace yourself when a gust arrives rather than trying to hurry across slick rock. Keep back from edges, hidden openings, and passages beneath the stone; the loudest tones may indicate moving air through places you cannot see clearly.
- **Treat the green seams as fragile islands.** Walk on durable bare rock where safe. Do not pull at vines, disturb animals sheltering under the leaves, or put weight on soil that has taken centuries to collect.
- **Leave the shelters as you find them.** Their small markers and water-catching bowl suggest deliberate, continuing knowledge of the place, even if nobody is present. Do not move the upright stones, use stored water without necessity, or assume an empty shelter is abandoned.

Expect the sound to be tiring as well as magnificent. Ear protection may help during a long stay, but keep enough awareness to notice your companion and changing weather. The best visit is a measured one: arrive with supplies, learn the route between sheltered places, and turn back while you can still recognize the stone under your feet.
