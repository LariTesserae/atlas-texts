Prepare for this as a **flood-prone cave with an unknown atmosphere**, not as an unusually lively walk underground. An ordinary traveler should go only with experienced cavers who have assessed the air, water routes, and way out. If that assessment is unavailable, do not enter.

Before going in, arrange a surface contact who knows your route and return time. Take a surveyed route or reliable mapping method, and do not rely on a phone signal. Each person needs a helmet, multiple independent lights, warm layers, gloves, and boots with good grip. The smooth, closely spaced ridges will make even level ground tiring: expect twisted ankles, slips into troughs, and slow progress.

The sharp smell from the cracks is a warning, **not a way to test whether the air is safe**. Gas can collect low to the floor, and breathable air cannot be judged by smell. Have the atmosphere checked for oxygen deficiency and hazardous gases; leave if readings are unsafe. Do not use flames. If conditions call for breathing apparatus, that is a job for a trained, properly equipped team—not a reason for an ordinary visitor to press on.

Treat the deep rolling sound and sudden quiet among the animals as a cue to **leave the low basin immediately**, not to watch what happens next. Water may arrive through passages you cannot see and cut off the exit before it looks dangerous where you stand. Check conditions upstream before the trip, set a firm turnaround time, and never enter a water-filled passage without specialist training.

Move gently around the wet seams. The creatures are crowded around a very thin food supply; boots and hands could destroy more of it than is obvious. Keep food and bare skin away from the water and animals, drink only what you brought, and clean equipment before and after the visit to avoid carrying organisms between worlds.

Above all, plan for the way out while the basin is noisy, dark, and disorienting. Its sounds may be extraordinary, but they are poor landmarks.
