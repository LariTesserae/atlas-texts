Treat this as a short stretch of serious alpine terrain, not an easy walk through a gap. The main danger is the combination of an exposed drop and a floor that shifts underfoot. A stone that looks solid may tip when weighted; a patch of gravel may slide; frost or evening ice can make either one worse.

**Before setting out**, confirm that the air is breathable, the weather and daylight window are adequate, and there is a safe route both into and out of the pass. Acclimatize if the altitude is unfamiliar. Tell someone your route and expected return time, and carry a means of calling for help that works in this world. Go with a capable companion if possible. If the route requires a rope to make the exposure acceptable, bring a qualified climbing partner and proper anchoring equipment—not just a rope to hold between travelers.

Wear grippy, stiff-soled boots; warm layers and a windproof shell; gloves; a helmet; and strong sun protection, including sunglasses. Carry water, food, a headlamp, a first-aid kit, and navigation equipment. The dripping meltwater is too little and too unreliable to plan around. The roofless stone crescent may offer a moment out of the wind, but it is not dependable shelter.

Cross in settled weather with ample daylight, preferably before daytime thaw loosens stones and before evening refreezing makes them slick. Keep your pace slow and your hands free. Test each foothold, favor rock that is firmly seated, and avoid the outer edge. Give companions space so that one person’s dislodged stone—or loss of balance—does not endanger another. Watch the wall for falling rock as well as the floor for movement. Do not pull on loose slabs or rely on the old shelter stones for support.

Set a turnaround point before entering. If ice, fresh rockfall, worsening weather, altitude symptoms, or uncertain footing make the crossing feel beyond your ability, retreat while you still can. Leave the hand-built shelter and possible tool marks undisturbed.
