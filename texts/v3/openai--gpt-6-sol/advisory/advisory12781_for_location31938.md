Prepare as if you are visiting a high, flooded worksite with no dependable visibility. The greatest danger is not the darkness alone; it is taking one confident step onto ground that is thinner, steeper, or wetter than it feels.

**Arrange a guide and a way back before you arrive.** Travel with someone who knows the maintained paths, the current river levels, and which service stations are occupied. Agree on a return time and a meeting point at a known station. Do not count on finding a cable cart when you need one: crews appear for repairs, not passenger service. Confirm that your communications and any rescue beacon actually work here, and carry a separate means of signaling if they do not.

**Bring light, but prepare to travel without it.** Carry a waterproof headlamp, a handheld backup, and spare power kept dry. Light may help you inspect a handhold or a patch of paving, but it will not reveal the edge of a distant ravine through rain, leaves, and spray. Learn a short, agreed set of voice and touch signals with your companions. A probing staff is more useful than a free hand for testing wet stems, submerged steps, and apparent roots. Do not use a rail, cable, or pipe as a guide until you know what it is and where it goes.

Wear grippy boots that you can free from mud, warm layers that still insulate when damp, a hard hat, and waterproof protection for essential supplies. Pack food, a first-aid kit, and more dry clothing than you expect to need. The altitude may leave you short of breath; allow time to acclimatize and turn back if breathlessness, headache, or confusion develops. Treat the abundant water before drinking it—flowing water can still carry microbes and runoff from the old machinery.

**Move slowly enough to test each step.** A mat of stems can hide stone; stone can slope into a channel. Keep back from rims, flooded handrails, intakes, and any paving with water rising through it. Never step into moving water to check its depth. Rain heard approaching through the plants is a reason to reach higher, known ground, not to hurry across a low saddle. Ravines can suddenly become too loud for shouted instructions, so agree in advance to stop together if you lose contact.

Listen, but do not navigate by sound alone. Water, echoes, wind in a ravine, and machinery can all mislead you about distance and direction. Avoid full-time ear protection that masks calls or changing water sounds; use it selectively if work near an engine demands it. Keep away from service mouths, buried-cable works, and warm pipes unless a crew has invited you in. A door that thrums may conceal operating equipment, and a quiet station may still flood.

Finally, treat the living ground as living ground. Some plants respond to vibration, and animals use the shallows and dry machinery chambers. Do not grab an unfamiliar “root” for balance or follow a large sound through the reeds. Stay on the path your guide recognizes, leave yourself time to return before river levels rise, and be willing to spend the night in a known safe place rather than search for a shortcut in the dark.
