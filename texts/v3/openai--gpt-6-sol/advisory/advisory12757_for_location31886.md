Treat this as an active volcanic construction site, not a mountain plateau. The ground is being made and broken while you stand on it. A surface that held your weight on arrival may tilt, split, or be lifted out of reach before you return.

**The safest preparation is to scout without entering.** Send a drone or remote instruments first to measure air pressure, oxygen, temperature, and volcanic gases, and to watch how quickly the ground changes. If an ordinary human must go, make it a short, daylight-equivalent visit with a companion, a tested exit, and a firm turnaround time. Do not plan to camp.

Bring a self-contained breathing supply if the atmosphere has not been verified breathable. Thin air can incapacitate you, and a filter mask will not supply missing oxygen. Carry a gas monitor as well: fumes may emerge from cracks or collect in quiet hollows even when the wind is strong overhead. Wear a helmet, eye protection, heat-resistant gloves, sturdy high-ankle boots with puncture-resistant soles, and clothing that protects against sharp glassy rock. Take independent lights, water, first aid, communications, and a way to navigate back without relying on visible landmarks.

Place your return point **off the rising plateau**, if possible, and verify that you can reach it after nearby ground shifts. Do not anchor essential equipment to loose slabs or trust a route simply because you just crossed it. Travel slowly, test footing, keep well clear of glowing fissures and the edges of apparent escarpments, and avoid standing downhill of moving ridges or blocks. The warm slabs are not necessarily solid; the quiet ones are not necessarily still.

The old walls and doorway may be compelling, but they are especially poor shelters. Their foundations are being displaced, and masonry that has survived this long can collapse in the next small lift. Observe them from a distance rather than passing through or leaning against them.

Expect an unsettling lack of ordinary cues: no paths, plants, watercourses, or dependable horizon, and perhaps no useful morning light. Mark your position with instruments, not memory. If vibration strengthens, a crack changes, stones begin to lean, gas readings rise, or the way back becomes uncertain, leave immediately. The chief danger is not a dramatic eruption; it is discovering that the ground has quietly rearranged itself around you.
