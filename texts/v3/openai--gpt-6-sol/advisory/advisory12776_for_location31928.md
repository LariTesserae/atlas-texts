Prepare for the summit first and the green chamber second. The mountain narrows to a blade, the air is thin, and a misstep could be more dangerous than anything inside the housing. Go with a partner, establish a safe route and a retreat point below the summit, and turn back if wind, visibility, or altitude symptoms make your footing uncertain. Wear a helmet, warm layers, grippy boots, and eye protection. If you need a rope, anchor it to sound rock—not to the worn iron housing.

**Treat the chamber as live machinery.** The steady hum felt through your feet and teeth, the violet glow, and the buried handwheel are reasons to keep your distance until you can assess it. Do not assume that dry surfaces are electrically safe. Check for hazardous voltage, radiation, and unusual air before approaching the doorway; use a pole, camera, or other remote means to look inside rather than leaning into a cupboard-sized space. Do not turn the wheel, pull apart the growth, or step onto the floor above the hum.

Bring a reliable light, independent communications, and enough breathable air if you cannot verify the local atmosphere. The mineral taste and thin air are warnings, not a way to identify what you are breathing. A particulate respirator may help with dust, but it cannot fix low oxygen. Gloves and sealed eye protection will also keep the pale grain from a broken blade off your skin and out of your eyes. Avoid making more dust or carrying fragments away: the green growth may be harmless mineral-like material, or it may not.

Expect an unnerving visit. Outside, wind works the iron and the slopes vanish into darkness; inside, even the dust is still while the floor carries that continuous note. Set a short time limit, agree on a clear signal to withdraw, and leave the chamber exactly as you found it.
