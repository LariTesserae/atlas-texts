At first I would look for myself among the birds. They have the best view of the hollow, and one of them knows how to ask a seam in the stone for food. But there is not much to see from the branch that cannot be known more closely from below.

I think I would be a springtail beneath the moss: pale, smaller than a bead of seepwater, grazing on the fine threads of fungus where they reach into fallen leaves. The rock would not feel smooth to me. Its old seams would be passages, its thumb-sized basins wide pools. I would spend most of my life within a few inches of the place where I hatched.

If a frog's foot came down nearby, I could spring clear. For a moment I would be out in the open, perhaps on the skin of a pool, held there by the water I could not enter. Then I would find the moss again.

I wouldn't keep a twig or leave a mark. What I ate would make room for what grew next. In that crowded hollow, that seems like a way to belong.
