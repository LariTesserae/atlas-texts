At first I think I would be under the low roof, sorting the stones: lifting one, turning it, setting it beside another whose shape almost answers it. But I would not stay there all day. I would keep going out into the square to look at the shallow channels, especially at the places where they cross a seam in the paving. Those are easy places for grit to catch.

I would be the person who clears them.

In dry weather, this would scarcely look like a task. I would walk the square with a narrow piece of wood, easing dust and grass roots out of the joints. Someone opening a shutter would hear me and know where I was without looking. I might stop to speak, with the tool still in my hand, and then remember a spout I had meant to check.

When rain came, I would watch from a doorway at first. I would want to see whether the water took the route we had left for it or found a better one. If it ran around a stone instead of across it, I would remember that stone for a dry day. I would let the basin fill. There would be no sense scraping it clean while the roofs were still sending water down.

I think I would like this work because it never makes the square wholly mine. The roofs, the slope, the people who carry loads across the paving, and the rain all have a hand in it. On the morning after the basin emptied, I would scrape out the silt and carry it beyond the last wall. By then the stones would be drying, and the sound of my footsteps would be ordinary again.
