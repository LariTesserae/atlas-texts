You would find me where the open shelf almost becomes a crack: in a shallow gully that holds a line of shadow after the rest of the stone has gone bright. By day, there might be nothing to find. A pale shape against pale rock, still enough that even a bird looking down would have to decide whether I was worth a closer pass.

Toward evening, I would move.

I would be one of the six-legged grazers, though most of my life would be spent with all six feet tucked close beneath me. The wind’s note would be as constant as the stone under my belly. I would know a useful place less by its shape than by what it kept: a little damp grit, a strip of lichen the sun had not dried too hard, a narrow way back when the light changed suddenly.

I think I would travel farther on a cloudy night. Not bravely—just far enough to reach a patch the others had not scraped clean. Then I would work my mouth slowly over the stone, leaving a trail too faint to see until morning.

At the first bird shadow, I would flatten. At dawn, I would return to the crack and press among the others, backs outward, soft undersides hidden. From above we might look like loose pieces of the valley wall. Underneath, in the dark, there would be moisture, young, and the small movements of lives continuing while the shelf appeared empty.
