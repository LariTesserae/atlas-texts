At first I would look for myself among the wedges. They are the parts that can be seen from above: dark, firm, holding apart a weight that ought to close. I would want to be one of those.

But I think you would find me lower down, in the hanging rows of unfinished bricks. I would still be soft enough to take the shape of the older one sheltering me. When the floor shifted, its hard parts would close around me—not to keep me from feeling the weight, but to let me feel it without being crushed.

I would learn the place by pressure. Three knocks would travel through a plate and arrive through several bodies before they reached mine. I would know a basin had risen because the weight above us changed hands. I would know rain was near because an elder returned cold and heavy with clay, though the rain wall might look no closer than before.

For a long time I would mistake becoming hard for growing up. I would practice widening a seam by the smallest measure, pleased when the plates eased apart. Then I would brace myself beneath a join that looked sound from above. Its clay would be dry and smooth; each time we lifted it, a hairline crack would run farther than we intended. The others would take the weight while I drew back.

After that, I would work where the surface was still wet. I would press a shallow groove into it and leave room beside the groove for water to enter. I would wait through its slow stiffening, touching it now and then to learn when it could bear another layer. Most attempts would break. I would carry the pieces below.

Perhaps, much later, someone crossing the plates would notice my wedge in a gap. They would see a ridge settle gently instead of falling. They would hear one knock beneath it, and then another, as I passed its weight on.

I would stay there only until the clay could hold. Then I would withdraw, leaving just enough space for the next rain to find its way in.
