I would first look for the fitted ring on the rise. It is the closest thing to a room, and I would want somewhere to put down my pack. But I don’t think I would stay there. A ring whose distance never changes is a poor place to sleep.

I would live near the edge of the crowded basin, beside a bird with a chipped beak. I’d begin by thinking I was a guide. I would learn which grazer had dust caught in a particular fold of its hide, which long-backed animal carried a ridge of white crust, and how to reach the next shelf without relying on the cuts in the stone. When a traveler came, I could walk with them as far as the scarred grazer. After that, they could keep it in sight until they found the bird.

That work would leave a great deal of time. I would notice a bird turning its head. I would come back days later and notice that it had not turned again. I would learn not to call either thing sudden.

Eventually I think I would be less a guide than a witness to the gaps between the animals. I would know the narrow space where the young grazer first stood beside its elder, though I might not have seen it arrive. If another traveler tried to pass through, I would ask them to go around. There are plenty of ways across the basin, even if the pavement cannot always be trusted to show them.

I would keep my pack by the chipped-beaked bird. Some mornings it might be facing me; on others, the empty shelves. I would let that be enough to tell me where I was.
