I would be just inside the wall, where a repaired brick meets an older one. At first I might mistake myself for the brick. I would feel the noon heat reach my outer face and, hours later, the deep note of a chamber reach my back.

But the brick is too solid an answer. I would be the narrow gap beside it: a fault in the mason’s work, no wider than an insect’s leg.

At dawn, dust would settle in me. An ash-colored insect, leaving the shelter of a rolled blade, would squeeze through on its way into the wall. By noon the air would be moving too hard for anything so small to cross the open court. I would feel the wall’s note through both my edges, while the pale cords pressed nearby, taking the shape of hollows they had never seen.

One day an insect would come through carrying a fleck of resin. It would lose the fleck in my dark, and nothing more would happen for years. Grit would round my edges. A cord might reach me from below; a new blade might rise from the seam above. Or a mason might lift the brick, scrape me clean, and press fresh clay into my place.

For a little while I would be gone. Then the clay would dry, the chamber would sound, and somewhere along the repair a space too small to notice would open again.
