At first, I think I would live on one of the bare swells, where I could see the rain coming. But the swells offer no shelter. I would be down behind a wall with everyone else, holding a stone on the edge of a hide while the wind tried to lift it.

I would be old enough to carry a basket and still young enough that someone checked my route home. After rain, I’d go to the channels for the pale creatures. I’d learn to keep the basket low, so they couldn’t scatter back under the rock before I reached them. I’d learn not to hurry, even when I could see other people filling theirs.

What I’d be good at, perhaps, is remembering the sounds. There is one hollow near our wall that makes a note I can feel through my pole before I hear it. For years it has sounded the same. Then, one afternoon, with rain still falling beyond the nearest swell, the note begins to break.

I test the stone twice. Nothing shifts under my feet. I go back anyway and tell the others.

We spend the last of the light moving stones from the channel above the hollow. I carry the small ones; the heavier slabs take two people. The water is cold enough to stiffen my hands, and for a while the note only grows louder. Then it settles, not quite into its old pitch, but into one that holds.

That night, I lie awake listening. I cannot tell whether we have saved the line for a season or only for the next rain. In the morning I will go out with my pole and listen again.
