I would be down near the floor, though I might spend my first few nights trying to climb. From above, the crack would look like a place made of exits: a seam of sky, birds departing through it, bats pouring after them. I would follow a ledge until it ended in smooth stone and find that the air grew drier with every inch.

Eventually I would settle beside a rain cup. I would be a frog small enough to fit beneath its overhanging lip, with my belly against the damp rock. The cup would not always hold water. Some nights I would wait for a drop from the warm joints above and turn toward its sound before it struck. Other nights I would have to leave the cup and find moisture elsewhere in the crack.

At dusk, wings would cross so thickly overhead that I could scarcely distinguish one from another. Then the noise would thin, and a beetle might come through the grit. I would hold still while it passed the reach of a spider on the ledge. If it came close enough to me, I would move once.

I would know very little of the mountain around us. I would know which hollow stayed damp after rain, which stone kept its warmth longest, and how far I could go from water before I had to turn back.
