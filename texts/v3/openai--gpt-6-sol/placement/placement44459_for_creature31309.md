I would be beneath one of the stones a child has been meaning to lift.

Not deep beneath it. There is hardly any depth here yet: a little red earth pressed into a seam, a dark layer of leaves someone covered before the wind came, then the black stone holding the day’s warmth. Rain reaches me through all of it. When the uphill ditch runs high, I move toward the grass; when the ground begins to dry, I follow the coolness down.

I would know the town less by its streets than by what passes through them. Water seeping out of a garden bed. The faint tremor of a hooked stick striking grit from a channel. At dusk, the weight of someone crossing the court on the way to a cistern. Their work brings scraps and leaves into my narrow reach, and mine leaves the earth a little more mixed than I found it.

For a while I might go undiscovered. Then a small hand would turn my stone, and daylight would fill the seam. The child would call for an adult—loudly, in this quiet place. I would draw back from the air while they looked at me, glad, perhaps, but careful too. They would put the stone down with room beneath it.

I would be a worm. Not the soil they are trying to make, nor any promise that it will last: just one small creature able to live in a few inches of it. When the stone settles overhead, I would begin again among the wet leaves.
