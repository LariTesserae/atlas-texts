At first, you would find me where the stone looks dry: under the lip of a shelf, beside a mineral ring left by water that vanished before sunrise. I would be still enough to pass for a flake of the ledge.

Then six legs would unfold.

I think I would be one of the low, ridged creatures, though not one that spends much time at the enduring basins. I would follow the water that almost arrives—the dampness beneath a stone, the green line that stops halfway across a shelf. I’d put my head to the rock and wait. If there were coolness underneath, I would scrape away a little grit and leave the opening for whatever came after me.

For a while I might believe my work was finding water. Over many dry weeks, I’d learn that I was also keeping its small passages open: a seam under a tree root, a groove in an old ring, a hollow where morning beads could collect. I would not know who made the arches. I’d tend one because water ran there, then wander off to tend a place no one had made.

When rain finally crossed my shelf, I would stand with the others and open my back to it. By morning I’d be gone again, somewhere out on the bright stone, following a stain too faint to see from above.
