At first I think I would be one of the drops gathering on the ceiling. I would hold a broken piece of the bright floor inside me, grow heavy, and fall. But a drop spends so little time here. It leaves almost as soon as it arrives.

Perhaps I would be the mark it leaves: a thin white edge around a hollow, made a little wider each time the water retreats. That would keep me in the cavity. Still, I think I would belong where the edge is not quite dry.

I would be a film of microbes along the cooler side of the hollow, close to the fallen chip. Water would bring me dissolved iron; I would take what I could from it and leave a few reddish grains behind. When the hollow emptied, I would become too thin to notice against the stone. When it filled again, I would stir.

The springtail might pass over me with its antennae, grazing at the wet line before a falling drop sent it under cover. I would have no reason to follow. My place would be that narrow margin the water visits and abandons: neither pool nor dry floor, but a small living trace of its return.
