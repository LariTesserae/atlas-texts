At the entrance I might mistake myself for the beetle. I would feel the traffic over my back and watch the last light go out on the water. But the beetle can wait through a falling drop without thinking of the houses behind it. I would turn at the warning tremor.

So I would be farther in, where there is almost no light: one of the small inhabitants, with a length of hollow stem tucked under my arm. I am young enough to be trusted only with a shallow groove. All afternoon I have kept it open, lifting stray moss aside with my fingers and setting it back along the banks. Then the floor rises. Not much—just enough to tilt the stem and send its thin trickle toward a house.

I put my palm flat against the moss. Something long passes beneath it. By the time the worm has gone, my neat groove is lost, and I can hear a drop swelling overhead.

There is no time to restore what I made. I pull the stem free and carry it two houses over, where the moss is higher. Someone inside feels me at the threshold and folds the sedge flap shut. The warning comes along the fibre. The drop strikes the old lane, and water runs into the hollow stem instead of through the wall.

For a while I stay beside it, holding its open end in place. My feet grow cold. When the trickle stops, an older neighbour comes out with a plug of moss, and together we press a new bank around the stem. In the dark our hands meet there. Neither of us needs to point out where the lane must bend.

By morning, others will walk this way as though it has always been a lane. I will remember the worm beneath it. I will be listening for the next one.
