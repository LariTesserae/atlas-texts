I would be on the stairs beneath the streets, where a footstep can arrive after the foot has gone.

At first I might take myself for an echo. I would follow the schoolchildren down in the morning, always a little wrong: late to the first step, early to the last. They would know better than to look for me. By afternoon I would know which child had been kept back and which one was trying to make it home before the bread cooled.

But an echo ought to repeat what it hears. I would begin to notice that, sometimes, my second step came before theirs.

I would have no shoes and no wish to be seen. I would live in the small delay the stairs allow, where a sound has left someone but has not yet reached anyone else. Most days I would do nothing useful. I would hear a mason carrying fresh stone down to an older pavement, or two neighbors discussing a leak they had both meant to report. I would learn their weights and their careful places. I would learn that the woman from the clinic pauses on the third step, not because she is tired, but because the handrail is wet there.

Once, she would come down with someone whose steps kept falling farther behind. The street above might be a short one for her. I would hear her stop and wait. My last footfall would finish climbing in the quiet between them, and then the other person would reach the landing.

I could not shorten their way. I think I would be glad, though, that she had heard how far away they still were.

When they set off together, I would follow badly, as I always had, until they reached the right door. Then I would let my final step sound and leave them to hear it shut.
