I would find myself near one of the working lamps, though its light would have nothing to do with why I was there. The curb beside it has a sheltered crack, and water reaches that crack from below. On the surface, I would be easy to mistake for a line of dirt left by a retreating puddle.

I think I would be part of the black growth: not the whole of it, and not even a fringe long enough to trail across the curb. Just a small reach of filaments rooted in the seam. I would know the machinery first as pressure. Before a valve’s report crossed the plain, the water in my crack would press against me. I would fold into the grit that held me, then open again when the rush passed.

Cement dust and rust would lodge among my strands. Slowly, I would draw them inward. Some days the flow might scour the curb clean around me; on others it would leave a little more material within reach. If my shelter wore away, a piece of me might break loose and travel through pipes I could never enter by growing.

There would be no way to tell, from the lamp, whether I had survived that journey. Farther down the channel, behind a flake of rust, a thread might catch and open in the slower water.
