I think I would first go to the workshops. I would like the steadiness of the belts, the work that can be checked with a fingertip. I might spend years learning which knock means a stem is settling into its braces and which means it is beginning to bear against them.

But I would keep finding reasons to walk beyond the last buildings, where the new streets are still round at the edges. Eventually that would become my work. I would be the person sent out after a route has been marked, to see whether it is still possible.

I would carry very little: a soft probe, a roll of cloth markers, something padded to kneel on. I would follow the proposed street slowly, testing places where the root skin had risen since the surveyors passed. Sometimes I would find that a straight line could still be kept if a young wall were given more room. Sometimes I would take up every marker and bring them back.

The animals would be better at this than I was, though they would not be helping me. I would watch where a runner crossed without slowing, and where it stopped to find another way. If a vent had opened ahead of the street, I would wait beside it. The opening might be temporary. It might also be the first sign of a passage the plants meant to keep.

People would call me a surveyor, because they would need a name for my work. I would be less certain. Most days I would come home with no new line to show them—only the places we ought not to close yet.
