At first I think I would live high in a tower, where I could watch the lights run through the bridges after dark. But I would spend most of my time lower down, in the passageways where the walls are thick enough to muffle the wind. I would know the town first by touch: the slight give of a floor, the warmer patch of wall around an active route, the grit caught in a doorway that closed too late.

I would be human. I would be the person sent to listen when a bridge sounds different.

An hour before the wind, I would go out with one hand against the tower wall. The vents would start knocking above me. I would wait through the familiar notes for the new one: a thin rattle from a bridge that used to hum evenly. There might be no danger tonight, or for many nights. I would still stay until the gust passed, then cross under the bridge and look for a pale seam, a place where the living surface had begun to pull away from its older layers.

I could not mend it before morning. The tower takes time to answer, and sometimes it answers by moving a route somewhere no one expected. I would mark the place and come back with others. We would keep asking it to strengthen that span, while making sure it did not close the passage beneath.

On the way home I would pass a doorway I had been watching for years. The child inside would be nearly grown now, and each season the opening would have become a little less convenient for them. Their family would already have begun asking the wall to widen. Perhaps my own childhood doorway would be nearby, its pale edges still showing where it had once made room for me.

After the wind I would hear the seed-catchers ticking across the folded leaves. I would stop and let them pass. Then I would put my hand to the wall once more, to learn whether the bridge’s rattle had changed.
