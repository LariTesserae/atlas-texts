At first I would go to the rail yards. It seems important to see a birth while it is still bare: clay lifting the ballast with the shapes of the stones pressed into it. I would stand with the crews, trying to feel what they feel through their boots, and probably mistake a distant wagon for the ground moving.

But I don’t think I would stay a surveyor. I would be more useful after they had marked the edge.

I’d be a fitter who goes back to things: the bridge shoe that has slipped a fraction overnight, the pipe bend that has used up its spare room, the door whose bottom catches again though I trimmed it last month. My cart would carry wedges, bolts, a short level, and pieces of timber too odd-shaped to throw away. People would tell me which windows had begun to stick. I would learn to ask whether the cup on their table moved too.

I’d live above a narrow workshop near a glasshouse, where water drips from the iron ribs even when it hasn’t rained. From my window I could see a chimney mark and a stand of willows the yard cuts back every spring. I would keep my own measurements of both. The mark would tell me how far a wall had gone; the willows, how long it had been since anyone last had time to clear the path.

In the evening I would put my tools away without fastening the workshop door too tightly. If the pale birds rose from the vents all at once, I’d set down whatever I was holding and wait. Then, if nothing else moved, I’d go downstairs and leave a little more room around the latch.
