At first I think I would be the person on the ledge, watching the floor move and trying to decide whether the bridge is safe. Then the wings rise all at once, and I find myself looking for a shelf to cling to. But I don’t think I could live where a single crack sends everyone into the air.

I would be one of the broad-backed animals, though a small one, not yet strong enough to lift a plate alone. I’d follow the older ones at a distance and learn which slabs merely rock and which ones settle hard enough to trap a foot. When they opened a seam, I’d wait for them to take the soft bodies underneath. There would still be growth along the stone’s hidden edge, and grazers caught where the plate touched down.

I’d sleep in the uphill opening of one of the fitted-stone enclosures. Its walls wouldn’t keep out the noise or the grit, but rainwater would run past it instead of through it. From there I could feel a deep crack begin before I had to choose where to run.

The basket would frighten me less than the bridge. I might nose it each day until I knew how its handle smelled after rain. The bridge would rattle even when the floor was still, and I would never quite learn to trust it.

After a slab fell, I’d go with the others to the new break. I would be hungry enough to go near it, and careful enough to keep one wide forefoot on stone that had already stopped moving.
