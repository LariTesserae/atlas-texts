At first I think I am one of the loose pebbles. I belong close to the ground, and there is no reason for anyone to notice me. But a pebble could be rolled across the hollow by a hard rain. I cannot be moved so easily.

I am the dark-leaved weed between the stones of the old wall. From above, there is only a small cluster of leaves. Most of me is in the soil that has gathered in the gap, where my roots follow its cool, narrow pockets. One root reaches toward the damp beneath the trough. Another stops against stone.

The toad lives lower in the wall. I would not know it by sight from here. I know when it presses back into the seam on a dry day: a faint loosening of soil, then its weight held still against the stone. When rain comes, that weight leaves. My leaves take the water; the toad goes out onto the bare floor.

I will never follow it. I stay where the old work and the bank have become difficult to tell apart, holding a little of the soil in place. When the bead falls from the trough, I feel nothing of the sound. Later, if the rain lasts, the cool reaches me.
