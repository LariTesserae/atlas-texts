At first I think I would be one of the pale marks on a pool’s rim: something a traveler could look straight at and not recognize as alive.

I would live beneath a low lip of stone, close enough to a vent to feel its warmth but far enough away that the shiver in the rock was gentle. My shell would follow the curve of the mineral above it. From the open basin, there would be no reason to distinguish one pale edge from the other.

But I would not experience the place as still. A drop gathering overhead would change the weight of the stone by the smallest amount. I would wait beneath it, my soft edge extended into the wet film. When it fell, I would take what remained along the lip. The deep roar would be there throughout—not a warning or a message, simply the condition under which I held fast, as constant as the warmth.

For a while I might mistake that constancy for safety. Then a tremor would come through the vent differently, and I would draw inward with the others. Nothing visible might follow. I would stay inside my shell until the familiar shiver returned. In a place with so little to hide behind, I think I would trust differences more than appearances.

I would cross a shelf only after the bright sky had gone. The journey to another hollow might be no farther than the length of a hand, but I would make it slowly, keeping one part of my underside against damp stone. By morning there would be a clean arc behind me. Someone might notice it and know I had moved, though they would not have seen me go.

That is where I would find myself: under the wet lip, neither part of the stone nor separate from it for long. Small enough to be passed over, alive enough to wait for the next drop.
