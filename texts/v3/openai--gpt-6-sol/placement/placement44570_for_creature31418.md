At first I’d look up. The bats have made a second ceiling of themselves, and it would be tempting to join that restless dark. But I don’t think I’d belong to anything so numerous or so free to leave.

I’d be under a fallen block, just beyond the line of pools: a pseudoscorpion small enough to stand on a limestone chip. The stone above me would shelter a pocket of dry dust; a few steps away, the edge of the drip would stay damp. I’d have pincers but no tail, and little use for sight. I’d learn the chamber through contact—the tremor of an insect picking through droppings, the faint disturbance of one crossing my hiding place.

I might begin each day thinking the cave was still. The blocks would not have moved. The water would strike the same pool. Then something edible would come near, and for a moment my whole world would be the space between two grains of stone.

I’d live on what happened elsewhere: bats returning overhead, a root finding a crack, rain reaching the seam. I would never see most of it happen. I’d only meet the small lives it brought past my block, and wait there when they were gone.
