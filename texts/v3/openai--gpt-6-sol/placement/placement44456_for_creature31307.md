At first you would have trouble finding me. You would look toward the birds, then toward the cloud when the larger voice called. I would be under a metal body that never touches the ground, beside the little fan of grass growing in its shelter.

I am a beetle. I carry a dry scrap almost as large as my back: part of a grass blade blown up from the colder slopes. There is nowhere to put it yet. The rock beneath me is warm and wet and so new that nothing catches there.

When the light above me pulses, I stop. The note comes up through all six of my feet. I lift them in turn as the seam opens, and steam folds around my body. The stone rises slowly enough for me to wait. I have waited before. The birds cannot bear the note and go crying into the air, but by the time they return I am crossing the closed seam.

I would not follow them onto the fresh edge. Their feet can test it, and their wings can save them. I go the other way, toward a shallow pool that has emptied and left a pale ring. My scrap catches against the ring. I pull; it slips free; I pull it back again. When the next wash of water passes over us, a few grains of dust stay caught beneath it.

For a while I remain there, holding to the wet stone. Flies settle on the pool. A pale hunter comes out from between the instrument legs, and I keep still until it has taken one and gone. Then I drag the scrap another short distance, looking for a place where it will stay.
