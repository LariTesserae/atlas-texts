At first I would look for a place to stand: perhaps beneath the loading crane, out of the cart’s way. But there is no useful place for a person to stand here. The cart knows when to move without me. The gate knows when to settle. Even the dripping water has a route.

I think I would find myself in a narrow space behind one of the hottest vents, where a counterweight hangs on a short chain. When the seam overhead begins to leak, the shutters pull the chain taut and lift me a little. When the stone dries, they let me down. I never rise far enough to see the chamber. I know the rain only by its weight arriving through iron.

At first I might think I was a keeper, making certain the shutters moved. But the shutters would move without me; I give them resistance, and the right amount of resistance lets them stop where they should. Over many wet and dry spells, my chain would polish a small arc into its guide. That mark would be the nearest thing I had to a path.

I would come to know other parts of the district through their effects on that small movement. A gate settling would tremble through the wall. A cart taking its load would draw heat from the pipe beside me. Once, a drop would get past the shutter and hiss on the vent, and for a long while afterward I would hang a fraction higher, until someone—or some patient adjustment elsewhere—put the balance right.

I would be a weight, then. Not the thing that keeps the district alive, but one of the things through which it notices a change and answers it.
