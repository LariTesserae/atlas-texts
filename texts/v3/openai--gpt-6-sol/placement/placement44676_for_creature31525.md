At first I think I would keep water. I can picture myself going out before breakfast with a cup and a folded cloth, knowing which window ledge fills first and which pipe joint stops sweating when the engines slow. But I would be a poor water keeper. I would linger too long after the cup was full, watching a drop gather without falling.

So perhaps I would be given the maps.

Not the large map with the workshops and stairs. I would carry a smaller one, creased soft enough to open with one hand. On it I would mark the places that change: a tread that stays slick into the afternoon, a warm vent crusting over, the hairline crack beside the young tree. My marks would be made in pencil. Ink would suggest a certainty the district does not have.

Most days no one would need to ask me anything. I would walk slowly, hand on the rail, and tell the kitchen when the foundry’s back doorway had grown too narrow for a hot mold to pass safely. I would tell the engine tenders when the usual tremor could no longer be felt through the western stair. If a bucket appeared in a new place overnight, I would find out whether someone had put it there or a drip had.

Eventually I think my work would have less to do with maps than with empty space. I would be the person who argues against filling the last gap beside a foundation, even when a shelf would fit there neatly. I would know how much farther that root might go. I would help lift a stair tread and set it down an inch away from the wall, leaving room for a thing we planted to keep growing.

At night I would sleep near that stair. In the morning, before reaching for my pencil, I would put my hand against the wall.
