At first I would take a room near the top of the city, one with a view across the vacant stone. I would mark which courts drew near in the dim hours and which arches could no longer be reached by morning. For a while I would think I was making a map.

Then I would notice how much better the small animals knew the place. I would follow one down a stair that had gained a step overnight. At a blank wall I would wait while it held its forelimbs to a crack. It might wait longer than I could. When I returned, the wall would have changed, and I would have learned only that my map was late.

So I think I would be found lower down: at the foot of a wall, among the fine lines of dust drawn around a clean patch of floor. Not one of the animals. Not quite part of the city. I would be the little warmth making its way through the stone, so slowly that a room could be cold for years before I reached it.

I would have no way to call the animals to me. Sometimes a pale, soft-shelled one would settle against the floor just as the warmth arrived; sometimes I would pass through an empty chamber. The city would go on changing the distance to the next door.

If you wanted to find me, you would have to stop trying to find the room I was in yesterday. Look instead for a small animal that has stopped at a wall and laid both its delicate forelimbs against it. I might be there, not yet through.
