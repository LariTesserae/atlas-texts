I would look for myself first in the hollow left by a foot. The ground gives way, water rises into the pressure, and for an instant the plant has a shape it did not have before. But the hollow closes. I wouldn’t be the mark.

I would be farther along the same passage, where the displaced water reaches a narrow vessel beneath one of the woody chambers. I would be a small region of that chamber’s membrane: a place that lets water through, holds salts back, and adds them, slowly, to a sheet of fibre. There would be nothing to distinguish me from the membrane beside me except the work passing through me at that moment.

From there I would know the plain without seeing it. A pressure at the roots would arrive as a slight change in flow. The steady charge would persist through the walls. I would hold one edge of a chamber under tension while its other edge held me. The green light at our seam would be the only outward sign of where I was.

I might spend a long time expecting the chamber to open. Everything about it seems made for an opening: the tight walls, the fresh fibre, the seam. Yet my life here would be in keeping it closed without letting it cease to grow. The sharp ribs outside may have formed in just this way, one dense layer after another, until they rose through the skin of the ground.

If someone passed beneath the leaves, I would not turn toward them. Their step would reach me as water, and then, perhaps, as a minute alteration in what I laid down. By the time the surface was smooth again, I would still be there, holding my narrow part of the seam.
