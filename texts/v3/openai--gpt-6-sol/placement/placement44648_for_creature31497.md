At first I think I would live inside one of the shells, in the narrow place its teeth never close. I would know the plateau by heat: which roots had arrived overnight, which pale edge would bead with fluid, how long to cling still while a black wall moved past. Six wings would be useful in a world where a seam can rise into daylight without warning.

But I would fly too readily. I would mistake every new opening for a way through.

So I find myself under the ferns, four feet on warm gravel, waiting in a line of narrow-bodied grazers. I am not the leader. I am the one immediately behind her, close enough to see the skin of her muzzle flatten against the stone when she stops.

Ahead of us is a strip of bare ground. Yesterday there were moss and roots across it. Today a pale crack runs its length, and on the other side the fronds are wet. We can smell the water. The youngest grazer shifts its weight; somewhere behind me, another keeps chewing. Our leader holds her muzzle to the ground for so long that vapor gathers on her neck.

Nothing happens that I can see. Then she takes one step.

I put my foot into the print she leaves. By the time the last of us crosses, the crack has narrowed enough to hold a fallen frond. Perhaps it was narrowing all along. Perhaps her weight told the two sides how to meet. I would not know the difference, and I would be alive because she waited.

In cold weather I would sleep above the shells, legs folded against their slow pulse. I would wake when the great thing passed below the cloud, lift my head with the herd, and feel its call arrive after its dark back had gone. Once, as a calf, I would lose a pool I had known all my life. I would stand at the tipped ledge and call without opening my mouth. An answer would come through the stone from someone I could not see.

When I grew older, I would remember that answer more clearly than the vanished pool. I might be the one who gives it next time.
