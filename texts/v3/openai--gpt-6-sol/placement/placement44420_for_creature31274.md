At first I think I would be one of the settled stones. They seem to know how to stay here: lie low, let the water spend itself overhead, offer the current nothing it can carry away.

But a stone cannot feel the narrow space beside it empty and fill again. I would be in that space, small enough to mistake its opening for the whole sea.

I would be one of the soft animals, tucked upstream of the hollow where the crab searched. Not the one it found. My shelter would be less secure: a flat pebble over clay, with grit forever slipping in at the edges. I would fasten grains to my back as they arrived, though the next current might bury me all the same. When it did, I would fold my threads, grip the pebble, and ease upward by almost nothing.

Most of my life would pass in waiting for the water to become useful. A cloud would lift; I would stay shut. The grains would settle; I would stay shut a little longer. Then, in the brief flow after the swirl, I would open my fringe and take what came to it.

I would not know the plain stretched for miles. I would know the two places my limbs could hold, the feel of gravel ticking through them, and the moment when an apparently empty gap could open and feed me.
