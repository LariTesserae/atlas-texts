I would live beside a basin on one of the newest spans, where the bolts bead before the rest of the steel. I might have been assigned to the freight rails at first, with a grease pencil and a watch, recording departures and arrivals. But I would keep finding reasons to stay by that basin.

My work would be easy to mistake for cleaning. Each morning I would lift the grate, remove fallen sealant and the larger scraps of grit, then set it back without scraping the walls. There would be fish in the water, though I might see them only when a drop fell and they corrected their positions beneath it. I would learn how much clear space they needed and how much of the faint blue film they could not do without.

Eventually the other workers would send me to places that looked clean but had begun to hold moisture too long. I would carry a brush, a lens, and sealant, and spend most of my time deciding which of them *not* to use. A thicker line of film beneath a bolt might mean a seal had shifted. A dry line where it ought to be blue might mean water had found another way through. I would mark the fitting, not its location; by the time someone came to repair it, the stair might lead elsewhere.

I would still wear a watch with more than one hand. Near evening, I would note when the first drop formed on the three familiar bolts, then listen for it to strike the basin below. If a loaded cradle entered the wall while I was there, I would mark its departure and leave the arrival for someone on the other side. My part of the route would be to keep the water flowing where the fish could meet it.
