At first I think I would live in one of the green seams near the top of the ridge, where there is just enough light to see the new roots taking hold. I would be small enough to slip through the walls. I would know the terraces below only as wide, dark openings I was warned away from—or drawn toward, when an older swimmer passed beneath me and set the whole wall faintly trembling.

But I would grow too large for those passages. My back would darken. One day I would have to leave a seam by the outside, with the drop in view, and swim down to a terrace where there was nothing to hide behind for a hundred paces.

I think that is where I would find myself: halfway down the ridge, with my fingers in a column.

I would be one of the swimmers, though swimming would be the least exact thing I did. I could cross the empty water easily enough, but I would spend most of my life nearly still, learning what the wall felt like from within. A hollow stem carrying the current would press steadily against my hand. A pocket full of grazers would answer with small, irregular clicks. A failing strand would yield before I could see anything wrong with it. I would draw that strand out slowly, holding its loose end in my mouth while I guided a living shoot through the same turn.

There would be places I knew well enough to reach in without looking. There would also be parts of the ridge I never visited, tended by swimmers whose names—if we had names—would be sequences of pressure rather than sounds.

Once, while I was working, I would feel a sequence come through the column from far along the terrace. I would stop with a fiber between my teeth and put my other hand against the wall. I would know which swimmer had sent it by the little pause before the last press. I would answer. The water would go on roaring, and the fish would go on crossing the open floor one by one.

I cannot say exactly what we would have told each other. Perhaps only that a passage was narrowing, or that the grazers had returned to a hollow left empty for a season. Perhaps, after years of sending them, those particular presses would also mean: *I am still here.*

In time I would become slow enough to spend days at one column. Younger swimmers would pass through the dark beyond it, pale at the edges, not yet sure which strands could bear their touch. I would show one where to place its fingers. Then I would let it feel the current for itself, because the wall could tell it more than I could.

I would not expect to leave the ridge. When I could no longer push away from it, I would settle in some curve of the terrace. The small animals would come, and the roots afterward. I like to think a hollow stem might grow through the space my ribs had held, carrying water where I had once lain listening.
