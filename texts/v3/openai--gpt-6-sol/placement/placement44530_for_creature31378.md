At first I would think I was a loose stone in one of the rings: dark, worn nearly level with the floor, holding a little shelter against the evening draft. I would know the cleft by what passed over me—the fine insistence of young hooks, the heavier pressure of an adult shifting to let another through.

But stones do not shift back. I think I would be one of the larger animals, old enough to have helped worry that stone into place. My shell would be dull with grit. I would spend most of the day in a passage above the deep holes, feeling for the small tremor of a scraper coming out. Hunger would make me wait there; the crowded ring below would make me give the place up when another adult needed to pass.

Near dusk, when the heat rose sharply, I would leave the passage and put myself along the ring’s low wall. The youngest would gather inside it, not yet able to hold to the exposed ledges. I could not cool the stone or stop the updraft. I could only keep my grip while they found theirs.

A visitor might look down and see no room for a foot. I would feel their weight arrive through the ledge and, very slowly, make room.
