At first I think I would be one of the flyers, tucked into the crease with my face turned up. It would be good to know the deeper note through the bodies of others, and to wait with them for water.

But I keep looking toward the corner where the vine grows. There is a narrow channel just below its roots, dry for a while, then suddenly full. I think I would live there: a small, soft-bodied animal that carries a sleeve of gray ceramic grit around itself. In the dark, I would be nearly the same color as the silt. Only a pair of feelers would show when I leaned out to test the water.

The deep note would tell me when to pull back. It shakes loose the drops caught on the ribs, and a little later the channel runs. Most of the time I would let it pass, taking what it leaves against my sleeve. Sometimes a larger fragment would lodge there. I would work it free before the scavengers found it, turning it until its rough edge fitted the place where my shelter had grown thin.

I would not go as far as the sill. I would know it by the current drawing harder through the channel, and by the sudden absence of scavengers ahead of me. My world would end a short way before the open air.

In time, the grit caught around my sleeve might raise a small bank beside the vine’s roots. Water would pause there between drops. I would drink from it, and the vine would hold the bank in place. Neither of us would have made much of a home alone.
