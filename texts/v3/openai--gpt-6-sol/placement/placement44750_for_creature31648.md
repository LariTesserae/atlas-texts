At first I would look for myself in the obvious motions: a numbered plate turning toward its mate, a pipe extending across the gap. But those seem too settled. Each has a place to be even before it moves.

I think I would be found in the narrow passage the city makes when it needs to move water out of the way of a new joint. Not quite a pipe—only a temporary route through a seal that has not finished closing. When two ceramic rims approach, I let the water between them pass into a chamber growing behind the wall. I am present for a few seconds, as a clear thread of current and a slight delay between one knock and the answering knock. Then the rims meet, and that route is gone.

The next time the city needs such a passage, it may open somewhere else. I would not travel there whole. I would be the same small accommodation made again: room for the water to move, without ever making room for nothing.

From the courtyard, there would be little to see of me. Construction dust would briefly stream sideways near a seam. The grass might bow in a direction it had not bowed before. Then a ceramic click, and the current would find another way around.

I would live in the city’s unfinished moments—not holding it together myself, but allowing its parts to meet without breaking the one thing they must keep continuous.
