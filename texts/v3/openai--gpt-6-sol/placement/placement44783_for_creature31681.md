From the ridge, I would look for a way down. In the valley, I would have to learn that a path is not something to trust. I might follow the white creature for a while, watching where it places its broad toes, but it would be gone through a gap before I had decided whether the gap was wide enough for me.

I think I would find my place beside a fallen column, where its weight had pulled two root systems apart. There would be a narrow strip of wood exposed to the air for the first time in decades: damp at its lower edge, cold and silvering above. At first I might mistake the silver for frost. Then I would notice that it remained after the frost had gone.

That would be me—not the column, and not anything that could save it. A fine growth working slowly into the newly opened wood. I would take what the rain left in the split and what the living core could no longer use. In a dry afternoon I would be almost invisible. After a shower, I would hold a little water along my threads while the larger leaves drew theirs inward.

The white creature might pass over me to reach the soft place where a belt turns. I would feel its weight briefly, then the slow pressure of the roots moving again. Perhaps the opening would close before I had spread far. Perhaps the column would settle and expose another length of wood, and I would go on there.

I would live in that interval: after the break, before the valley grows across it. From the ridge, no one would see me. Up close, I would be one of the ways an old piece of the valley makes room for something new.
