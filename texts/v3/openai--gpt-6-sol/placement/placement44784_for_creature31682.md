I think I would begin by mistaking myself for a drop of water.

I would arrive before dawn on the underside of a pipe, held there while the stone below was still dark. Then I would fall into a gutter and be carried past the clear-backed grazers, past a hunter waiting with its feet wide on the rim. For a while, that would seem like the best way to know the town: to go where gravity and the old channels took me.

But I would want to stop. I would notice a reach of gutter where the current had thinned overnight, and the grazers had gathered under one last wet lip. I would find myself kneeling there with a person from the town, watching them lift a screen of grit from the inlet. Water would come through again, not in a rush, but enough for the little bodies to loosen their grip and feed.

So I would be one of those people eventually. Not the keeper of a great pump, perhaps, but someone who knows the small routes: which terrace stays wet after rain, which seam can shelter a swimmer until evening, where to set a scrap of shade without blocking the morning condensation. I would carry a thin tool for clearing channels and a cup for moving stranded animals. Most days I would have no reason to use the cup.

At noon I would work beneath an awning, listening for a change in the drops falling behind the wall. At evening I would sit by the open gutter with everyone else. Something small would dart between my feet. I would leave my feet where they were and let it find the stair’s shadow.
