I think I would begin in a wall without knowing it was a wall: a fig seed left by a bat in a joint above a cistern. Rain would wake it. Its first root would follow the damp mortar downward, while its first leaves found a little light over the rim.

Years later, you would find me there still, though not all in one place. My roots would enter the cistern wall and pass through the floor of a room below it. My branches would cross a narrow street to rest on another roof. In the dry months I would hold my leaves above the dark water; after rain, drops would collect at their tips and fall long after the sky had cleared.

Geckos would wait on the stone beside my trunk. Bats would come for the figs and carry their seeds farther down the terraces. Before dawn, the long-bodied animal might use my branches to cross the street, its young pausing where the wood bent under them.

I would never see the whole city. I would know the reach of one crack, one cistern, two roofs—and, each season, send a little of myself beyond them.
