At first I would put myself above the street, among the bats. From there the district would be a narrow strip of warmth and moving water, and the pump would be something felt through the rock before it was heard.

But I think I would come down. I would be the person who clears the sump screen before the first carts arrive.

It is a small job, mostly lifting out grit, grain husks, and the occasional moth. I would learn to wait for the pump’s first stroke before reaching into the water. I would know which railing rattles and which patch of pavement stays dry enough to kneel on. The attendant might know me by sight, though we would seldom need to speak.

One morning I would find a fish caught against the screen. I would tip it back into the gutter, upstream of the rush, and watch it turn to face the current. After that I would look for them each time I cleared the sump. Not because they were mine to keep, but because they had found a way to live in a street we had barely finished making.

I would be one of the people who keeps the water moving, and one of the creatures who has begun to follow its route.
