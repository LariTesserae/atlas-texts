At first I think I would be a lizard beneath a bridge. I like the exactness of that life: knowing which plank will bead first, waiting with my mouth open while moths pass close enough to catch. Then I imagine being the bright moss in the new seam, taking hold of stone that has only just entered the air.

But I think you would find me above that seam, on my knees in the old garden. I would be one of the people who comes out at dusk with a bowl in each hand.

I would have placed one beneath the hanging roots the night the crack appeared. The next evening I would move it a finger’s width. I’d know from the first drops whether to leave it there. I might reach into the house for a larger bowl and come back to find the bird on the wall, still waiting, with the insects gathering below it. We would have our separate reasons for staying.

In the morning I would measure the opening against a mark I’d made on the garden wall. Some days there would be no change I could see. Some days I would have to move the bowl farther from the edge before I emptied it. I would try to save the exposed roots if I could, though I would not pack the seam with earth. The cold air comes through there now, and by evening the moths have found it.

There is plenty to do before dusk: wash the bowls, set a wire repair right, carry water to a neighbour whose lintel has stopped dripping. I would keep one small plank unfastened beside the garden, ready if a crossing became useful, and easy to take away if the stone moved again.

You could probably tell which house was mine by the bowls drying outside it. You might also find me standing in the lane after everyone else had gone in, trying to hear a drop land somewhere I had not thought to put one.
