At first I think I would be one of the little birds. There is something enviable about arriving from below, finding a flower in all that stone, and stepping back into the air as though it were solid.

But I would be too curious about what remains after the birds leave. I would want to know where the meltwater goes, and which crack the pika trusts when the larger bird passes overhead. I would stay close to the wall. By evening I would be too cold to go anywhere.

So I think I would be a stone in the windbreak—one of the low ones, with grit caught along its underside. I would have come loose from the wall in a frost and been set here by hands that have since rubbed my upper face smooth. I would not shelter anyone very well. A person would still have to crouch, and the pika could pass behind me without slowing. But the wind would lift a little at my edge. Water would have time to sink into the grit. Perhaps, after many short summers, a few green stems would take hold there.

The visitor would come back, rest a hand on me, and look out over the drop. When they left, I would remain: a small part of the ledge where something can pause without being seen.
