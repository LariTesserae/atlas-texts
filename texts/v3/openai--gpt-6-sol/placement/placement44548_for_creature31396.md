At first I would think I was a traveler. I’d leave a mark in chalk beside a doorway, walk until the rain stopped, and return to find my mark opening onto a court I had never seen. For a while I would keep trying to learn which streets belonged together. I would carry a cup to test the gutters: pour water in here, wait for it to appear somewhere else.

Then I would stop carrying the cup. There are gates in the streets, small enough to lift with one hand, though no hand comes to lift them. I think I would find myself at one of those gates—not the timber, but the narrow space beside it, where water slips through even when it is shut.

Most days I would be dry. Frost would whiten the paving above me. A few thick leaves would hold their water in a crack nearby, and nothing would pass. Then a storm too far away to hear would fill the channel. For an hour, perhaps, I would connect a roof under rain to a court under clear sky.

A root would grow into my opening. It would make the passage narrower and keep the stone damp after the water was gone. On one side of the gate its leaves might open in warmth; on the other, the same plant might lose a shoot to frost. I would not know whether the root had come from the court or from somewhere beyond the wall. It would be enough to feel it hold fast when water arrived.

I had wanted to be the one who understood the city’s changing distances. I think I would be this instead: a place where a little water gets through, and where a root, finding it, stays.
