I think I would first take the shelter for my place. I’d sit beneath its ribs during a storm and watch the old pool rings darken around my feet. But the shelter seems made for someone who comes and goes, and I don’t think I would want to leave.

Perhaps I’d be one of the clear threads in the silt. I’d know the plateau by touch: the grit that shifts as the pool rises, the cooler seam that will stay wet until morning. I’d draw myself into a knot when the water went away.

But I keep returning to the third post. I would live in one of its tiny moving spaces—not the post itself, and not quite a creature you could lift out of it. When water reached me, I’d close. As the pool deepened, I’d open higher up, carrying a little silt with me. At dusk I’d let it go. Some would settle in a fracture; some would be there when the flies hatched.

A person watching might never pick out my particular opening. I would know the hour by where the water touched the post, and tomorrow by the first small patter at its foot.
