You would find me above a turbine, in the space between its concrete ceiling and the stone laid over it. I would sleep with my feet against the ceiling. Even asleep, I would know when the river rose: the ordinary pounding would grow heavy and close, as though the chamber had become smaller.

I would be one of the small animals the crews look for.

They might think I had left because of a failing bearing. Sometimes that would be true. A bad bearing puts a little catch in the floor, turn after turn, until there is no comfortable place to curl up. But I would also leave when water found a new seam overhead, or when the warm pipe beyond the door cooled. The crew would listen to the machine while I waited in the next chamber, where the concrete still felt right.

I would know the people by what came through the walls: a cart arriving, tools set down in their fixed order, two blows on a housing before someone opened it. Their voices would reach me through the speaking tube, changed by its length. After the work, I would wait for the cart to go. Only then would I cross the chamber and test the ceiling again.

Once I would follow a loosened root out into the marsh. Underfoot, the stone would have a smooth groove I had never felt before, packed at its edges with torn stems. I would go along it as far as the service wall, where a narrow gap led back inside. Something would move in the shallows beyond me. I would stay at the gap for a long time, feeling for another step.
