At first I think I would be the bird: small enough to cross the whole town in a moment, wary of every footstep. But I would leave when someone came near, and I want to know who tied the fresh cord on the gate.

Then I picture myself at the upper stair, fitting another step against the raw stone. By midday my hands are sore, and I have made only a little room for a foot. I like that work, but when I hear the jar ringing behind the wall, I put down the hammer.

I think you would find me there, shifting the jar an inch so the drip falls cleanly into it. I carry its water to the seedlings among the house stakes, then set it back before the seam runs dry. On wet days I clear the mouths of the clay vents and turn the cut stems under the eaves. On clear ones I help with stone. I know which thresholds have been swept because I have swept some of them.

I would be one of the few people living here, with no grand charge beyond noticing what needs doing next. The town has six stalls and not enough sellers, steps that stop halfway up, and a plot where a house may stand someday. I would water that plot anyway.
