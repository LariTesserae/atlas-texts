At first I think I would be in the water, where a movement can travel farther than a sound. But I would want to know when the water had gone, too. So I find myself at its edge, in a hole in the soft bank, scarcely wider than a thumb.

I am one of the crabs. Not the first out after dark. I wait until the birds have settled into their stillness and the heavy thing beneath the vines has passed. Then I climb onto the bank and work among the wet leaves. A fallen scrap can be pulled home and eaten slowly. A stranded fish is worth the risk of staying out longer.

My world has a small circumference, but it changes shape overnight. Rain can put the mouth of my hole underwater. A dry stretch can leave it high above the nearest run. I learn which silt holds when I dig and which will fall in behind me; which roots give my tunnel a roof. The distant engines reach me only as a faint tremor, too far away to alter what I do.

By morning, I am back below the bank. What remains of my journey is a set of narrow tracks crossing the sheen of clay.
