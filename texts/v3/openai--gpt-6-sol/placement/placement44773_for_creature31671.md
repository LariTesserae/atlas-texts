At first I think you would find me beneath one of the trees, in the loop of an exposed root. It looks like shelter. A bird’s shadow could pass over the stone, and I would have somewhere to go.

But I would not stay there. I would be out after the note from the posts, when the beads first gather and the rock is still warm enough to dry them. I would be the dull, narrow-mouthed creature following the shallow cut. To a bird I would be no more than a moving mark on the stone. To a lizard, perhaps, something too small and troublesome to chase.

I would touch the water as it came. Not because I needed much to drink: the fern seam holds enough for me. I would want to know whether it had begun to move. Some beads would swell in place for a long time; then one would slip, and the next, until there was a thread I could follow without lifting my mouth from the stone.

I would know the hollow by that thread. The hot rims would belong to the lizards, the crowns to the birds. I would have the nearly level passage between the posts and the ferns, a way across the bare floor that appeared only for a little while. When the water reached the seam, I would go down among the roots with it. The beetles there might stir aside. Above us, the cut would empty and become hard to see again.

I do not think I would know why the posts sound, or whether they call the water out of the air. I would know the low note through my feet. Afterward I would wait at the edge of the cut, where the first bead usually forms.
