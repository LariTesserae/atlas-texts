At first I think I would be one of the birds. From above, the pools must appear and disappear as the cloud moves, and the whole ridge would fit into a single turn of the wings.

But I keep coming back to the ledge that appears dry until you wait beside it.

I would be a springtail beneath its lip, small enough that a drop of water arrives as a change in weather. My place would not be the open pool but the pale seam leading to it: a narrow run of grit, moss threads, and damp growth I could feed on. When the stone dries, moisture would remain there a little longer. When it freezes, the deep part of the crack might still hold water.

I would know the birds mostly by interruption. Calls overhead; then a shadow at the pool’s edge, a tap of feet, the sudden loss of some other tiny moving thing. If a beak reached my seam, I could spring from it—but not choose where I landed. Wind and water would have more say in my travels than I did.

The deep report beneath the ridge would mean little to me at first. Later, a trickle might enter from a new direction, carrying grit across the place where I fed. By morning, ice might bar the way out. I would wait under the stone, where the mountain is still wet, while the birds searched what remained open.
