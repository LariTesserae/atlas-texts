At first I think I would be on one of the warehouse roofs, among the gulls. From there I could see the whole town take the tide in and let it out. But I would grow restless waiting for something to happen below.

Then I think of the mud: a crab at the foot of the quay, working through shell grit until a truck sends every crab underground at once. I like the thought of knowing the town through its tremors. Still, I find myself following the water farther in, beneath the roads, to the gates that decide when it can pass.

So I would be the person who tends those gates. My boots would never quite dry. I’d carry a ring of keys, a stiff brush, and a notebook gone soft at the corners. Before rain, I’d walk the wide roads to clear shell and rubbish from the grates. Twice a day I’d check the hinges and watch for the first dark push of seawater in a drain.

I would know the channels by what came through them. One brings leaves from somewhere inland, though there are no trees on my route. Another shelters rats in its upper ledge. At the last gate by the sheds, little fish sometimes hold against the current; I would wait a moment before closing it, if I could.

People might call me when a road flooded or a gate stuck. Most days they would not need to. They’d see me only crossing an empty apron in the heat, or crouched beside a drain while rain made fresh water over the old salt line. I would belong there by the habit of paying attention: to a hinge beginning to fail, to the hour the tide turned, to the small lives using every opening we left them.
