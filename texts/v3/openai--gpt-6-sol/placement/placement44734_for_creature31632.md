I think I would begin on the walkway, beside the leaf rubbed shiny by passing shoulders. I would mistake its clear bead for plenty of water. I might even reach out to keep it from falling.

But I could not stay there. The leaf is only the visible part of something that goes down through the red soil, across the stone, and into cracks too narrow for a hand. I would follow it past the shelf, where the roots gather around a cool seam. There the roar would still pass through me, but so would the occasional trace of water.

I would be one of the fine roots at the edge of the basin—not the thick cord gripping the rail, only a thread pressed into the silt beneath the pipe. Most days I would have nothing to draw from it. I would hold my place while the damp spot shrank and the pipe above me warmed through another long afternoon.

When rain came down the old stair, I would know it first as a change in the stone. For a day, perhaps two, I could send a little of what I found upward. I would not know which leaf received it: the broad one at the grate, or the curled shoot near the floor. I would only feel the silt dry again, and remain where the water had passed.
