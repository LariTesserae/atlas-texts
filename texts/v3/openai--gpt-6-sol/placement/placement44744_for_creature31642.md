I think I would begin as something easily mistaken for the basin itself: a short tube of grit caught under the raised channel. Water would run over it. Silt would gather against one side, then vanish overnight.

Only when the tube moved would you see the caddisfly larva inside.

I would have made my case from what I could find—sand grains, a flake of rust, perhaps a thread of dead reed. I’d keep to the shallow compartments, where the water is cool and there is enough fine matter to eat. I would not know where the water came from. I would know when it was about to rise: the floor beneath my case would tighten, and a dark fold would press up through the silt.

At first I might retreat from it. Later, I’d learn that the fold never tries to take my case. It needs the same wet seam I do. If the seam dried, I would drag my little shelter toward the pipe, grain by grain; below me, the creature would be opening some longer way around.

You would find me most often at the edge of a pool that seems to be emptying. By the time you crossed the concrete to look, it would be filling again. My case would have turned to face the new current, and I would be inside it, holding on.
