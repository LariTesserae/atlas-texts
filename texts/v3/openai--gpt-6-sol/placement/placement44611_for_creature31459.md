At first I would look for myself in the dust. It goes wherever the plain sends it, and for a while I might mistake that for freedom. But dust has no way to choose a seam, or to stay when something living needs the shelter it makes.

Perhaps I would be one of the black stems. I like the thought of holding my leaves edge-on to the fierce light, of breaking without quite dying. Yet I think I would grow impatient beneath the tiles, waiting for a gap wide enough to try again.

So you would find me where a dozen hexagons sit slightly too high.

I would begin under a loose rim, nearly transparent, my limbs already curled around something older and harder than I am. For years the plain would carry me. I would learn its openings by warmth and its grip by night. Eventually I would become heavy enough to lift one tile a little, then another—not to get above the plain, but to make room within it.

Small animals would come to the grit I disturb. I would feel their quick feet at my edge and, now and then, the faint weight of one riding on my back. A seed might catch in the dust there. For a few days I would carry a black stem, until a closing seam took it cleanly away. Its root would remain somewhere behind me, or perhaps part of it would travel on.

I would be easy to miss at midday. That would suit me. If you wanted to know where I was, you could come back after a season and find the patch of raised tiles beyond the groove where you last saw it. Or you could wait until the glare was strongest and look for the thin shadow that should not be there.
