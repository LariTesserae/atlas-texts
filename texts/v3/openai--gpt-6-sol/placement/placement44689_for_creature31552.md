At first I think I’d want to live beneath one of the great trees, where there is shade and company and a clay bowl to check each morning. But I would keep wandering out beyond its basin. The open reaches only look empty from far away; up close, every few steps ask you to notice something.

I would be one of the people who walks ahead of the others after rain, finding out which paths still hold. I’d carry a coil of fresh fiber and a few flat stones, though I wouldn’t set a stone down just because a place frightened me. Some hollows need a crossing. Others need everyone to leave them alone until the new growth has joined over the old.

I’d learn to stand still when the birds rise. Perhaps then I’d see the seam beside my foot become an animal, and know I had nearly mistaken a life for part of the path. I’d learn, too, what a herd feels like through the soles of my feet before it comes into view. If it reached a weak stretch of ridge, I could wait where it might see me, then take the longer, firmer way home. The herd might follow. Or it might know a better way.

My screen would be under a tree, patched so often that little of the first building remained. In the morning I’d drink what the bowl had caught. In the evening I’d come back smelling of resin, with bits of dry leaf caught in my clothes, and tell whoever shared the shade where the weave had begun to sag.

I wouldn’t be a keeper of the plateau. I’d be someone trying to remember where it had changed—and willing to find out the next morning that it had changed again.
