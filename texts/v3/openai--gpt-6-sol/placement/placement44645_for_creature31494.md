At first I think I would live at the rim, where the wind has room to speak without being interrupted. I would go out each morning with the others and look down into the haze, as though I might learn something from it.

But I don’t think I would stay there long. I would turn back because someone behind me had begun the mill, and its stone sounded slightly out of true.

I would be a mender of small things. Not the person called when a whole roof gives way, but the one who notices that a slab has begun to rock underfoot, or that a vine has drawn a wall’s loose earth into its roots. I would keep a few stones beside my door, sorted by thickness. Children would know me as the person who sometimes stops them in an alley and asks them to walk a step twice, to tell me whether it has changed.

My house would be in a hollow where the light arrives late. A thorn tree would grow over the roof. I would trim the branch that catches sleeves and leave the one that shades the flour jars, though every year I’d have to decide again which was which.

Most days I would learn more by listening than by looking. I would know the herd had taken the outer path from its bells, and that a neighbor was setting a roof slab from the careful pause before each blow. When a familiar sound failed to come, I would put down what I was doing and go see why. Often there would be no trouble: a child had lingered with the goats, or the miller had slept a little longer. I would be glad to have walked over anyway.

I would still cross the worn rock at the edge in the mornings. I wouldn’t be especially good at judging the weather. But from there I could hear the town beginning behind me, and on the way back I could test the old steps with my feet.
