At first I think I would be one of the pans: shallow, chipped at the lip, useful only when put beneath the right drip. But a pan has to wait for someone to move it. I would want to be the one who hears that it needs moving.

You would find me beyond the third arch, where the rainwater groove passes under a threshold. I would be young enough that people still send me for things, but old enough to know that a soft tick from that bay means water is collecting where it should be running. Most days I would carry jars, scrape the pale crust from dry pans, and try to learn pot-mending from a woman who tells me I use too much wire.

When rain begins, I would leave whatever is in my hands. I know which pans nest together and which one must be carried alone because its old repair will not bear another knock. I would set them out with everyone else, then go back to listen along the grooves. If water crosses the threshold, I have missed something; someone will have to lift a shelf to dry the jars beneath it.

I think I would like that no one could do the work alone. A voice from the next bay would tell me the cistern was nearly full. I would call for the broad pan, and it would arrive through two doorways in hands I could not see. Later we might argue about where it belonged.

By evening, when the dripping slowed, I would put my ear close to the brick once more. Not because the court was quiet, but because I was beginning to know which of its sounds meant everything was going as it should.
