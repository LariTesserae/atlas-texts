At first I think I would work in a mill. There is comfort in a wheel that can be heard from the same distance, even on a morning when the street insists on becoming longer. I could learn one belt well enough to hear the width of a fingernail going slack.

But I don’t think I would stay inside. I would be the worker sent out with a covered vessel, a narrow knife, and no promise of getting back before dark. I would stop at the rain basins to lift a folded rim and make sure the little creatures underneath still had room to work. I would cut only the stems that had grown across their channels. Then I would go down the terraces toward the exposed roots.

Some mornings I would see the rim plainly and spend hours reaching it. I’d have to learn to sit down when the mill grew faint, instead of walking faster. While I waited, I might notice an old boot scuff appearing in the new paving beside mine. I think that would trouble me for a long time.

At the edge I would pour water from the vessel a little at a time. Most of the roots would stay dry. I would crouch to brush grit from a fine tip, careful not to mistake its worn outer band for the end of it. Up close there would be a pale point extending into the air, where, from the ridge, the whole city seemed to stop.

That is where you would usually find me: late in arriving, with water still left to carry farther along the edge.
