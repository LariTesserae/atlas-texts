I would be near the first curb, where the road still looks surprised to find grass beside it.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the little collectors set into the concrete. I would open when the sound shook water from the leaves and close when my narrow chamber was full. For a long time, that would be enough to know: the pitch before rain, the weight of a drop, the brief coolness passing under the street.

But I would have a flaw. My lid would not close quite flush. A little water would remain in the seam, beyond the reach of the mechanism. The thin-beaked birds would discover it. One would stand with a foot on my casing while it drank, and the pressure would register somewhere below as something the city had been built to notice.

Perhaps I was once meant to count people waiting to cross.

I would have no image of them. Only an old distinction in my workings: rain has one weight, a passing animal another, and someone who stays at the curb is different again. The birds would stay for hours. I would send my small, obsolete report downward—*waiting here*—while the deeper note rose and fell without answering me.

Over years, roots would reach my wiring. They would enter so slowly that I could not tell when their growth became part of the signal. In dry weather, I would hear almost nothing from them. After a pulse, when water ran down the towers, I would feel a faint return: one root wet, another still dry, a third drinking where a window used to be. I might come to know the distant groves by these replies rather than by sight.

The road would continue its straight advance into the field. I would not claim that I had asked for it. Whatever lies below the streets has been making roads longer than I have been keeping water in my seam. Still, each evening I would send the same report. The bird would lift its foot; the weight would vanish. I would wait for it to come back.

Then, one afternoon, a bird might land at the unfinished edge instead. There would be no gutter there yet, no collector, no water held out of reach. I could not sense its weight from a mile away. I would know only that my own curb stayed empty through a pulse, and that the note below the street changed pitch a little sooner than I expected.

I would open anyway.
