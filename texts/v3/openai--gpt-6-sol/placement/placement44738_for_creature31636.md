At first I think I would be somewhere in the pondweed, where the water is green and every passing shadow matters. I would know the lake as a maze of stems and bright openings, and believe that disappearing quickly was the same thing as being safe.

But I would grow too broad for those hiding places. I would find a cobble beside an old stream channel and learn the current there: its usual pressure against my face, the small lull behind the stone, the change that means another fish has passed upstream. In time I would move farther down, to a shelf where the light still reaches but the plants do not.

I think I would be one of the mottled fish, old enough to have a favorite gap beneath the rock. In spring I would guard eggs there. It would not look like bravery from above—only a blunt head emerging from darkness, a sudden rush at anything that came close, then a struggle to settle my fins against the stone again.

The lake would never quite let me keep my place. Rain would send brown water over the shelf. The pumps would begin their low vibration beyond the cliffs. Some days I would return to my gap and find its entrance half-filled with gravel that had been on a distant beach the week before.

I would push what I could aside. Then I would hold there, feeling the new current find its way around me, until I knew the place again.
