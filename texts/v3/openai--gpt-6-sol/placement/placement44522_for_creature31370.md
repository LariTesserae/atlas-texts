I would find myself at the lower edge of the square, where the overflow comes out from beneath the paving. Not because I had been sent there. I would have stopped to listen: the water usually runs with a hollow, steady sound, and this morning it has begun to tap against stone.

My house would be one of the smaller ones, its back wall dark with damp. I would keep a short iron hook by the door and a stack of flat stones that other people had decided were too awkward for steps. At first glance I might look like a mason. But most days I would take nothing apart. I would lift a grate of woven hazel, draw out a clot of leaves, and wait to hear whether the water had found its old route again.

After the shower, a thin stream would appear between two paving stones where feet need to pass. I would follow it uphill, past the wicker worker’s awning and the child still waiting at the spring. The drinking trough would be clear. In the overflow, though, a root would have caught fallen leaves and turned the current sideways.

I would not cut the root. I would pull the leaves free, then set one of my awkward stones where it would guide the next rush of rain beneath the square. The child would ask whether the water was fixed. I would say, “For today,” and let them hear it running below us.

By then my sleeves would be wet to the elbows. I would go home for a dry pair, leaving the hook beside the door where I could find it when the sound changed again.
