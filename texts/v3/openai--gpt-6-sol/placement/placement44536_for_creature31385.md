I think I would begin in a place too narrow to see from the street: a seam behind a retaining wall, where water arrives one drop at a time. Above it, boots pass along the dry steps. Below it, a drain runs warm toward the canal.

At first, the seam is only shelter. Then a leak begins somewhere uphill, and each drop brings a little of what has escaped the covered basins. I move out when it starts. There is food caught against the grit, and a current gentle enough to sort through. When the rain comes hard, I turn sideways and go back into the wall.

So I would be one of the pale crabs. Not an old resident of a picturesque ruin, but one living beneath a grate at a pumping station built only a few seasons ago. Its ledges are still mostly bare. A bird has begun carrying scraps under one brace; I have found the gap the builders left where the drain meets the stone.

Someone comes after rain with a hook to clear the grate. I retreat at the first scrape. The hook pulls up a clot of shells and basin film, and water runs freely again. The worker sets a chalk-white boot beside my gap, not over it. Whether they have seen me or simply know what lives in such places makes no difference to the water.

When the boot goes, I come out. Farther down, fish hold still beneath the outfall. Above, the birds call before light reaches the street. I can go only as far as the grate and back, but food keeps arriving from well beyond it.
