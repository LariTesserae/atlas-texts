At first I would look for somewhere to stand. The steps are already doing the work of walls; the shelves end against other shelves. Even a blind doorway has been filled with stone. I might put my hand against the second lintel, the one fitted beneath an older, failing span, and feel the chord through it.

That is where I would be: not the lintel, but the hairline space between the two.

I would have begun when the lower stone was set in place. Perhaps it fitted perfectly then. Perhaps a chisel left one shallow ridge that kept the stones from meeting. For a long time I would be no more than a flaw too small to notice, warm on both sides, holding a little pale grit.

Then air would find me.

It would not pass through easily. It would worry at one end of the seam and come out the other as a thin sound, almost lost among the clay throats. I would not be another mouth of the chord, exactly. A hand over the nearest opening would leave my sound unchanged. Nor would I be separate from it: the same unseen force would have reached me through the stone.

I think I would live by remaining narrow. If I widened, the air might rush through without a note; if grit sealed me, there would be nothing to hear. As I am, the two lintels hold their weight around me, and I give the chord a faint, rough edge it did not have before.

No one crossing the courtyard would hear me. They would feel the great sound in their feet and move on. But down inside the wall, where the passages join, my small note would arrive among the others. I would be a crack the old repair failed to close, and a part of what the place now sings.
