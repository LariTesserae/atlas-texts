I would live near the eastern wall, though not on the new ground beneath it. I would have a room close enough to hear small stones come down at night, and a path to my door that changed after every hard rain.

In the morning I’d clear leaves from the channels with my hands. At first I might think the work was to keep the water out of the houses. Then I’d learn which thresholds needed water passing beneath them, which garden beds dried when a channel was blocked, and which bit of wall held because its roots were wet. I’d become someone neighbors called over before they pressed mud into a gap—not because I knew how to stop change, but because I remembered where the water had gone last time.

I would keep a reed stem laid across the path below the eastern fall. If a stone moved, I’d shift the stem farther back. If seedlings took hold, I’d leave them their patch and help feet find another way around.

By evening there would be clay under my nails and cold in my ankles. I’d sit in the last long daylight with the others, listening when a bird crossed the roof. At home, I’d duck beneath the branch growing over my door. I might tie it aside for another season. I wouldn’t cut it yet.
