At first I think I would live at the rim, where the tram line disappears into stone. There would be room there to look over the steam and imagine where the traffic went.

But I don’t think I would stay. The rim is too exposed, and nothing in this city survives by having a wide view of it. I would be farther in, beneath a walkway, at a valve in a pipe that carries hot water from the pumps. The valve closes every few hours, but not quite. A grain of mineral has lodged against its seat. When the rest of the line goes still, one drop continues through.

I would be that small failure.

The water would strike a cooler joint in the wall and spread into a band no wider than a hand. Pale film would grow there. At night, the hard-shelled animals would come out from under the rusted bolts and graze across it, leaving tracks that close by morning. When the pumps started, the valve would shudder against the grain; for a moment the drops would come faster, and the animals would hold tight or retreat into the stone.

I would have no way to know any of this. I would only fail to close, through each rain and each dry spell, while mineral slowly gathered around the gap. One day the valve might seal, or break open. Until then, there would be a little water on a cool wall in a city whose machinery was still doing more than it was built to do.
