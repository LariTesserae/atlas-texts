I think I would live close to the landing, though not on it. At first I imagine a room with a view of the stream, then find I want a doorway onto one of the narrow passages instead. From there I could hear water coming from several directions before I saw any of it.

I would be one of the older children, nearly grown, sent under the landing each morning with a hooked stick. I’d know the stone mouths by touch: the one with a chipped lower edge, the one that catches rush stems, the one that sounds clear until you put your hand into it and feel silt banked behind the lip. I would leave a basket of wet blue clay beside whichever wall needed it most. Someone else would press it in.

For a while I’d think I was good at this because I could hear a clog before the younger children could. Then a doorway would flood while I was listening elsewhere. Its occupants would clear it, and afterward ask who had let the water through. I would have to go back and learn that sound too.

In the afternoon I might carry roof ties to a neighbor, squeezing sideways past the fish racks. On the way home I’d set the smooth white stone beside its threshold; it would have shifted when the step sank. No one would have asked me to do it. I’d only have noticed it was out of place.

By dusk there would be clay on my wrists again. I’d be sitting in our passage, pressing it into a seam while someone on the other side of the wall did the same. We might disagree about where the water had entered. Between us, the wall would hold for the night.
