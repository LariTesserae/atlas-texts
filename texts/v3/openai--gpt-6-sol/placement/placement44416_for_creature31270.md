I think I would live near the end of the last street, in a house that has already been moved once.

At first I imagine myself among the young people who go out along the ridge, following bare rock into the green. But I would turn back often. I would want to know whether the line I’d tied could still be found in clouded water, whether a hand coming from town would recognize its knots. By the time others were ready to build on the next rise, I might know the route better than its view.

My work would be to fasten things, and to notice when they ought to be loosened. I would cut mineral crust from the bindings of a house whose ledge had begun to shift. I would carry its sound poles to another shelf and help bind them there. Some fibers would break under my fingers; others, though pale and stiff, would prove worth saving. I would be slow to throw anything away.

On a day when silt darkened the streets, I would find my way to the square with one hand on the line. At each marker I would pause long enough to know where I was. I would want the youngest children to learn those shapes, too, before they went beyond the houses where no markers stood.

There would be a guide rope out there that I had tied myself. I would check it after the others came home, feeling along its length for a frayed place or a knot pulled tight against stone. If the rock beneath it no longer held, I would untie it and look for another place. The next day, someone setting out would find the rope a little changed, but still leading onward.

I would be one of the people who keeps going back: from the new rise to the last house, from the last house to the square, carrying news by hand until everyone knows which part of the way is firm.
