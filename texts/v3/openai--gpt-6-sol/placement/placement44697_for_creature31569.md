At first I would put myself on the walkway, with a lamp clipped to my coat and a wrench in my hand. Someone has to keep the drains clear. I would know which stair lights are slow to wake and which stretches of rail leave water on your palm even when the stone looks dry.

But I think I would spend most of my shift with the lamp turned off.

There is a service door halfway around the chamber that opens onto a space barely deep enough to stand in. From there, I could hear the machinery without hearing my own footsteps over it. I would keep a notebook there: the time of each heavy rush, how long the rattle took to pass, which drain had begun to drip faster. The system would announce a fault for itself eventually. My work would be to notice it sooner.

I would begin by marking the dark curve on the far wall as damage. On another shift I would find it farther along the groove. After that, I would wait for the air to move and watch it leave the stone.

There would be a grate below its resting place that needed clearing. I could reach it from the walkway with a hooked rod. Each time I drew the rod back, the metal would shiver; once, the creature would flatten itself above me. I would finish the work more slowly than usual, then remain there until the next rush carried it away. It would not need to know I had waited.

So I would be a maintenance worker, probably not a remarkable one. I would go home through the streets fitted into the chamber wall, carrying damp gloves and a notebook full of ordinary measurements. Before I left, I would look once across the unlit middle. If a blow sounded below, I would stay long enough to see where the drops fell—and whether the narrow body reached the other side.
