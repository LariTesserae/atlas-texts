At first I think I would be one of the pebbles, newly loosened from the pale wall: sharp, still, waiting for the next gust to send me down. But a pebble has no reason to stay with the old bank. I keep finding myself beneath it instead, in the dry earth the roots have caught.

I would be a fungus there. Not a mushroom rising where anyone could see it, but fine threads running between grains of soil, touching an old willow root, passing under a cushion of flowers. My body would have no single place to stand. Part of it would be sheltered deep in the turf; part would reach the exposed edge, where a little earth had fallen away and left the threads bare.

The wind would reach me only when the plants above bent together. I would feel a sedge root pull, then settle. The turbine would be harder to place: a steady shiver in the stone beneath the soil, unchanged when the wind stopped. I could not know what made it. I would know the close crack of a slab shifting, because afterward some passages through the earth would end in air.

I would grow along what still held, taking what I could from scraps becoming soil and living among roots that fed me in return. I would not hold the bank together by myself. When a piece broke free, the threads crossing it would tear. Some would go down with the roots and crumbs; others would remain in the turf, beside a gap that had not been there before.

By morning, if the edge still held, I would be growing into the grains on its sheltered side.
