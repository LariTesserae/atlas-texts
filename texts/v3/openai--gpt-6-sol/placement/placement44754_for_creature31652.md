I would be near the drain, at the last rib the water crosses before it falls away.

At first I might take myself for the water held there: a narrow bright line in the lamp’s yellow patch. But the line empties whenever the valve turns, and I remain. I am a raised burr in the steel, left where the rib was cut. Too small to trouble a boot or change the work of the pumps. Just enough to hold back a little water, and now and then a thread or a grain of grit.

The thing in the seam would know me by reach rather than sight. When the flow is thin, one of its limbs can find what has stopped against me. When the pumps climb, nothing can hold there; the water covers us both and takes everything on. I feel the rail shiver through the floor, though I cannot tell which of the many sounds began it.

I would not be its shelter. The seam and the grass do that. I would be a small place where something nearly lost stays for another moment—long enough, sometimes, to be taken.
