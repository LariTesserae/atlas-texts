At first you might take me for one of the upright stones. I spend a great deal of time beside them, pressed flat against the rock, waiting for the wind to ease enough that I can lift my head.

But stones do not hurry into the leaves when a shadow crosses them. I do.

You would find me between the shelter with the carved bowl and a green seam half a morning away. It is a poor crossing: a long stretch of bare rock with only three markers. The middle one falls often. Its foot rests in a shallow crack, and rain washes the grit from beneath it. I have wedged it upright with pebbles more times than I can remember. I could move it to firmer ground, but from there the last marker disappears behind a swell. So I keep coming back.

I know the water at each end of that crossing. The bowl fills slowly, with a drop that strikes near its rim and runs to the center. At the far seam, water hides under a cushion of leaves. You have to put a forefoot into the dark soil to feel where it is cool. After rain I carry damp soil across the bare places in the seam and press it around the roots that have worked loose. Sometimes the wind takes it away before morning. Sometimes a vine catches there.

Before a storm, I go to the opening beneath the western edge. I cannot hear the first change in its note as clearly as the others can, but I feel it through the flat of my feet: a tremor that comes and goes, then stays. That gives me time to reach the shelter, if the middle marker is standing.

Today it is not. I find it lying pale side up in the grit. The rock has begun its warning, faint but steady. I set the marker in its crack, gather the pebbles that rolled away, and push until it holds. Then I put my belly close to the warm stone and follow the line home. Behind me, the marker is still upright when the first rain strikes it.
