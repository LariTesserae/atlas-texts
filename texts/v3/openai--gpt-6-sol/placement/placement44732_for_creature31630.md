At first I think I’d live near the upper bridge, where the strip of sky is widest. But I wouldn’t stay there long. I’d keep going down, past the freight berths and the stalls, to a ledge where the two walls nearly touch and the fresh stone is still bare.

I’d be the person who checks whether the crack is growing.

There would be small marks on opposing teeth of rock, made when someone first dared to measure the gap. I’d visit them with a lamp and a rule, carrying my notes in a pocket so they wouldn’t blow away. Most days I’d record no change. On other days a mark would have shifted by so little that I’d measure it twice before telling anyone.

I’d learn the motors through my boots, as everyone does, but I’d listen for what continues after they stop. The birds settling behind a cabinet. Grain ticking down through the platforms. A fall of stone too deep to locate. I wouldn’t know whether the pale hunters could sense a shift before I could, though I’d find myself watching them when they held still.

When the red lamp came on, I’d clear the bridge with the others. Once the cage was safely above us, I’d go back down and check the marks. My work wouldn’t be to keep the mountain closed. It would be to notice, in time for someone to move a bunk, a walkway, or a child’s usual place to sit.
