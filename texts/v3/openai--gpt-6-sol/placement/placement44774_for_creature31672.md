I would be found where a corridor has begun to disagree with its stakes.

At first, I would be easy to mistake for a worker repairing a tile. I would kneel with one hand on the floor, wait through a turn of the leaves, and feel the slow pressure of a root beneath my palm. I would carry spare sockets and a cord to anchor myself in the bright hours. My shoulder plates would be old, with one kept long after it ought to have been replaced because a six-legged animal sleeps under its loose edge.

But I think my work would be less about fixing the corridor than learning what it has become. Once a tile has settled into a new socket, an aisle that took you outward yesterday may bring you home today. I would walk it from both ends, leaving time between journeys for the leaves to turn. Sometimes the animal would appear ahead of me, insulation in its jaws, having crossed a seam I had not yet dared to trust.

When I was certain, I would move the black stake. On some days I would set it down where it had stood before. The floor changes slowly enough that this, too, would feel like work: finding that a passage still leads where someone expects it to lead.

I would live beside an old row near the shadowless strip, close enough to hear the loaded frames pass at night. I would know their rhythm before I woke. In the morning, I would put my hand to the floor again, then choose which way to walk.
