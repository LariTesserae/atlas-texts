I would come down from the ridge. The view is too good up there: it makes the valley look settled.

At first I think I’d belong on a stair, somewhere between streets, with a key to a shop and a habit of clearing the grate outside it. I’d know the rain by where it appeared next. But I keep following it downhill, past the patched wall and the old bridge, until I reach the canal.

I’d be an alder on its bank, planted close to a gate. My roots would hold what soil they could while the water took some away. Leaves would catch in my lower branches when the gate rose, then pull free and show how fast the canal was moving. Someone watching from the quay might use that to decide whether to stay a little longer.

I would not be old enough to remember the first bridge. There would be brick and buried iron beneath me older than my roots, and a strip of newer paving above them both. Each year, crews would cut back a branch that reached too far over the working path. In a wet spring I would grow it back, though not in quite the same direction.

The heron might stand in the narrow opening beside me until the gate began to move. I’d know it was there by the sudden weight lifting from a branch. Most days, that would be all: water passing, a train sounding briefly from somewhere higher up, and my roots finding room in a bank the town was still trying to keep.
