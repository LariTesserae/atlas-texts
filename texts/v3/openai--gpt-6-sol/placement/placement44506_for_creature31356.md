I would begin at the eastern basin with the crews, watching them draw the morning line around the warm hollows. I think I would want a measuring cord, a place on a tram, some way to say where the city may build next.

But I would be looking into the hollows instead of at the line. When a small, dust-marked hand appeared among the roots, I would know the streets better from beneath them than from any map.

I would be one of the canal animals.

By day I would sleep under a low bridge, where the last water holds a little warmth. At dusk I would follow the pale roots into the shallows and visit the stone mouths below the planting beds. Seeds collect there. So do leaf stems and bits of grit too large to pass through. I would nose them aside for what is edible, leaving the water a way through. Above me, people would walk on paving that still looks newly laid.

After rain, I would feel the city travelling. The current in the canal would scarcely move, but through the stone at its edge would come a finer movement, steady and eastward. I would not know how much of a bridge or road had passed beneath my claws. I would know when a mouth had more brick dust than it did the night before, and when a tree had sent a new root down to drink.

When the canal dried, I would go to the basin. The crews would be at work on another slope. I would push into the dark soil left around our hollows, turning once before sleep, while the ground above me settled softly into a new shape.
