At first I think I would be one of the silver seam-runners. I like the thought of stepping into a drop without making it fall, of finding a way upward through water no deeper than a film. But they seem to know where they are going. I would spend too long at each crossing, feeling the current change against my feet.

Perhaps I would live in a leaf vein instead, asleep beneath the ice that forms around it. Yet I don’t think I would sleep through the floor rising. I would want to feel the moment a familiar place became unfamiliar.

So I find myself at the edge of a raw patch of mineral, where the moss has not quite covered the stone. I am a young root tip, no thicker than a thread, growing out from the lower mat. The rock is smooth here. I press against it for a while, searching for a hold, and withdraw. Then I try a little to the left.

The blue points reach the stems above me before they reach my root. By then the fronds are folding away from a seam of water that has not opened yet. I feel a pull from the older roots: move, make room. I do not move immediately. There is a hollow in the stone beside me, fresh from another root’s retreat, and a small sleeping animal has settled against its lip. If I tighten now, the moss will close over it.

The pull comes again. I hold where I am until the animal stirs and crawls into a supple vein. Then I let the water through.

No one in the hollow would see much happen. A drop would swell under a leaf. The seam-runners would arrive beneath it, ticking. Farther in, the curtains would loosen for the heavier creature to pass. I would try the stone once more after it had gone by, and perhaps this time find a place that yielded.

I would be very small in a place that keeps growing. But I would have one useful gift: I could take a little longer to answer.
