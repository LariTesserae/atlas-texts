I would live at the far end of the saddle, where the last shelters shine at dusk and look nearer than they are. I think I would begin by carrying bowls for the workers. There would always be one more to fetch, and carrying a bowl would give me a particular shelter to walk toward when the mountain tried to become my destination.

In time I would be given the empty bowls to tend.

They would stand under cracks that had stopped dripping years before. I would clear grit from them after windstorms and set them upright when the scree shifted. I might complain that there was nothing to collect, until an older worker showed me a bowl with a pale ring inside: the height reached by water on the one morning its drip returned. No one had been there to see it. The bowl had waited.

My shelter would be one of those that stays dry while water crosses its threshold. I would leave the chosen wedge beside the door each night, but I would also keep a place there for a stranger’s bowl. If I saw someone crossing the saddle with their eyes fixed on the far ridge, I would point to my roof and wait outside until they reached it. I would not call. They might hear me from behind them and turn away.

I would not become especially good at finding the hidden steps. I would know a smaller part of the saddle: which empty bowls had stayed empty, which had acquired one red grain, and which must not be moved even when they appeared to hold only light.
