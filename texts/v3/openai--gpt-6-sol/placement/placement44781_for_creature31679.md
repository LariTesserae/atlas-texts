I would live near the last houses, where a lane ends against bare rock and the lagoon is visible beyond it. Not quite at the landing: a few steps above, beside a channel so shallow that a stranger might take it for a crack.

As a child I would have been sent to clear shells and grit from that channel. It would seem like a small job in a town full of boats and nets. Then I would learn how much water a handful of grit can turn aside. I would learn to taste from a hollow before filling a jar, and to wait after rain has stopped, when the stone begins giving up water from underneath.

I think I would grow into the person people call when a cistern tastes faintly of salt. I would walk the lanes with a stone point, lifting silt from the cuts in the rock, checking where a wall has shifted or a threshold has shed its shells. I would know the houses by their water: the one whose roof drips long after the others, the one whose doorway stays wet in dry weather, the one where children always come out with jars before the morning birds leave.

I would still go down to the landing when the boats returned. Everyone would. Someone would need a hand with a skin boat or a place cleared for the catch. But I would look back up the steps before climbing them, to see whether the crabs had come out again and whether fresh water was threading between the wet stones.

The boat crews would bring food into the town. My work would be to notice what was already arriving, quietly, through the rock—and help it reach everyone.
