From the pass, I would probably look for myself among the birds. They can read the valley from above, then land at exactly the dark seam that still holds water. I like the thought of knowing where to return when every stretch of stone looks dry.

But I think I would be lower down than that.

I would live in a crack beneath one of the weathered fins, where the sun reaches the entrance for an hour in the morning and never reaches the back. I would be small enough to turn around inside it. Most days, the only evidence of me would be a fan of damp grit pushed out onto the pale rock.

After rain, I would work at that fan while the chute above still ran. I would know the sound of a grazer placing its feet on the ledge, and the lighter, quicker touch of birds at the seam. If one came near, I would withdraw until its probing stopped. By night the seep would have shrunk to a stain, but the grit beneath it would still be soft enough to move.

I might first have chosen that crack because it seemed safe. Over time, I would come to know it by other things: the cool that persists at the back, the insects that wander in after the pipework beads with water, the particular scrape a pump makes through the stone before it starts. I would not understand the machine. Its vibration would simply be one more change worth waiting out.

When rain came hard enough to fill the old chute, I would stay inside. Later, I would clear whatever the water had left at my entrance and push a new little fan into the open. By morning, tracks might cross it—mine first, then a bird’s, then none that could be told apart.
