At first I would choose a drop on the clamp. I like the long wait before it falls: the slow gathering, the tremor when the motor starts, the moment its weight becomes too much. But a drop passes through this place only once.

I think you would find me on the underside of the lamp bracket, where the fitting crew left a burr. I would be that little raised edge of metal—not part of the design, not worth filing down.

Most days I would hold nothing. Sometimes a film of water would spread over me and draw back. The lamp would warm one side of me; cold air from the seam would find the other. I would have no way to follow what happened along the green threads.

Then a bead would run down one of them, carrying the clear animal. For a few minutes the water would catch on me. Its legs would open and close in the bead, too small for me to feel. When the water thinned, there would be just enough edge beneath its claws for it to climb back.

Afterward I would still be a burr. Another drop would form overhead. Nothing in the factory would register what had happened, and the green mark beside the lamp would remain green.
