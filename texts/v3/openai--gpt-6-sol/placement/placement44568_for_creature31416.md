At first I would think I belonged to the chip of basalt.

It hangs beside the corner it broke from, close enough that the fracture still seems like a thing that might be undone. I would wait there for a long while, expecting the chip to fall, before understanding that waiting is not what keeps it in place.

Nor am I the tower. The tower can preserve a difference, but it cannot choose one.

I would find myself in the last ceramic house before the mountain drops away. My boundary reaches a little beyond its open frame into the street. If you entered, you would feel the sea’s weight lift from the back of your neck, then gather gently around one hand. Deeper in the house, I have made a narrow place where the pressure is nearly even on all sides. It is not shelter from the water. There is no other substance to take shelter in. It is simply how I have learned to give a room a shape.

I cannot see the slope as a creature sees it. I know the edge by the enormous, uninterrupted weight beyond my walls. I know the other residents by the ways their thresholds differ from mine. We do not visit. To cross a street would mean ceasing to have the boundaries by which I know myself.

The jointed visitor comes by the last house after tapping the other doors. Its limbs touch my frame in a quick, uneven sequence. I never know whether it intends a message. I know only that it has come all this way over new stone and will soon go down again.

Once it has left, I take a little weight from the doorway, no more than a creature might mistake for the easing of its own effort. The tower holds that change. By its next visit, my answer is still there.

I am the resident of the last house. I have spent a long time learning how little can be changed, and how long that little can remain.
