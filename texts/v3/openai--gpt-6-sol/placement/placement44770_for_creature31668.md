At first I would look for a place in one of the open halls. From the rim, they seem like the only places where a person could stand and watch a storm come in. But up close, I don’t think I would want to stay. The roof would be filling above me, the columns taking its weight, and every sound would mean something changing.

I might go down to the shining floor instead, following a seam where rain gathers. There, beneath the noise of the gutters, I could feel water passing through ribs too thick to see around. I would begin to understand that the hall and the floor were not separate things, and that I was standing on something alive.

I think, in the end, I would be part of it: a small joint high in a gutter, just before the water enters a chute. Most days I would hold two green edges together. During a hard storm I would feel the gutter bow and the water press against me. If the chute below had tightened too far, I would open.

To anyone watching from the rim, it would look like a failure—a sudden spill down the outside of a roof. But the water would land on broad leaves waiting below and run toward another frame. When the weight had passed, I would close slowly, still dripping long after the sky cleared.

I wouldn’t know the whole valley. I would know the pull of my neighboring ribs, the heat that rises after rain, and whether the route ahead can bear what is coming. I would be one small place where the great plant can change its course.
