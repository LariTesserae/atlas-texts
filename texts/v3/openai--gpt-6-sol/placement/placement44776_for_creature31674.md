At first I think I would live under the pots. There is room there for a beetle, and work that can be done without anyone noticing. But I keep coming back to the narrow shelf above the doorway, where a vine has found a crack in the roof stone.

I would be one of its roots—not the thick root still held in the sealed pot, but a fine new one feeling its way into the ridge.

From there I would know the courtyard by what reaches me. A little water arrives from the pot, never quite enough. Dust settles in the crack. When someone repairs the roof edge, packed earth closes a gap and opens another. The hand mill turns somewhere below me; feet cross the hollow in the court; a bird lands hard on the slab and sends a brief tremor through the stone.

I would not know who brushes the ants away each morning. I would know that they return. Their road follows a seam close to mine, and now and then their traffic carries a grain of soil farther into the crack than wind could have put it. Above us, the vine spends what I find on leaves and pale flowers. Bees come. A few seeds go missing from the drying baskets. The people gather the rest and give water again the next morning.

No one planted me in this stone. Someone planted the vine and has kept its pot sealed and tended; I am the part of that care that went farther than intended. I would be found above the doorway, almost invisible, holding to a place scarcely wide enough for me.
