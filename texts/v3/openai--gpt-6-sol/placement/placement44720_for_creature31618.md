At first I would be a station tender. I would have chosen the work because it asks for things I know how to do: keep a motor running, count my steps, write down what a gauge says even when it says the same thing for three nights. I would tie a wrench to my wrist and learn the other tenders by their knocks on the stairs.

I would be assigned the walk between two stations whose logbooks never agree.

For a while I’d blame my counting. I’d go out along the porcelain feet, touching each cable support as I passed, and come back with stones in my pocket to mark every hundred steps. On the return I might reach the stair with one stone still in my hand. I would leave it in the margin of the book, beside the hour, rather than force the numbers to match.

Then, one night, everything would stop. The fish against my legs, the small pressure of bodies at my boots, the beaks sounding across the shallows. I would stand with one hand on the cable and feel water rise through my old footprints. When the sounds began again, I would walk on and find the next station much nearer than it had been.

Its tender would already have heard my knock.

That would trouble me more than the distance. I’d have to sit on the concrete stair for a long time before I could climb it. Eventually I’d ask to hear the knock described, and I’d recognize the small pause I make before the last blow—the one I learned years ago so a sleeping person would know it was me.

I think I would keep tending the pumps. I would still write pressures and volumes in the proper columns. But in the margins I’d begin recording where I was when the plain went quiet, and which bank had risen when I started walking again.

I might never learn what becomes of the missing ground. If I found a new gravel strip, I would test it with my weight before trusting it. I would look for fish moving their gills among the clean stones and carry them to water if there was water close enough. Then I would go on to the station, knock on the stair, and wait to learn whether anyone had heard me coming.
