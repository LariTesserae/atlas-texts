At first I think I would be on the service walk, looking down and trying to decide whether the basin is a machine that has begun to resemble a valley, or a valley someone has tried to make obedient. But the walk is too far from the water. I would keep watching one particular step, where the pumps leave a dark ridge of grit each time they run.

I would be under that step: a crab no wider than a thumb, with one leg shorter than the others. My shelter would be the space behind a bolt that had not been driven quite flush. The bolt would be cold even when the lamps warmed the wall, and just beyond it a stamped number would hold a little moss.

I would not know the whole basin. I would know the blow through the concrete before the pumps began, and how long afterward to wait before putting a foot outside. I would know that the ridge sometimes held a torn reed, sometimes almost nothing, and that a hard drop from the roof meant nothing was hunting me—but I would run from it anyway.

Once I grew large enough, the bolt would no longer hide all of me. For a while I would stay there, exposed at the edge, unwilling to cross the clean concrete. Then the pumps would stop, the water would draw back, and I would go looking for another shelter. I might find a drain whose opening was just wide enough. I might have to find it twice, before I trusted it.

From the walk, I would be difficult to tell apart from a fleck of wet slag. I would live in the short quiet the machines left behind, carrying what I could pick from the seams back under the grate.
