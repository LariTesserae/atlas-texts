At first I think I would stay in the broad square, beside the hollow. There is enough sky there to watch the weather arrive, and enough empty stone to see the little bird cross it. But I doubt I would remain in the open. After the first rain I would follow the water toward a lane, then lose it where it disappears beneath the paving.

That is where I would find myself: inside the last step before the square, in a seam too narrow to admit light. I would be one of the dwellers in the wall.

I would know rain by its weight before I knew its sound. It would reach me through several stones, carrying grit and the softened remains of moss. While the passage was wet, I would put out threads and hold a little of what had come down from the mountain. When it dried, I would draw back. A thin skin would remain across a gap that had been open the season before.

For years, no one crossing the lane would have reason to notice me. Water might linger at the edge of that step, though, even after the paving above had gone pale. In time the step would lose its sharp corner. A visitor might put a hand there while climbing and find the stone unexpectedly smooth.

I would not want to close the passage entirely. The rain must still reach me. Somewhere above, a doorway would keep whistling when the wind came through it, and I would hear that note through the masonry. If the sound changed, I could not tell whether I had altered the city or winter had opened it elsewhere. I would wait for the next rain and continue along the wet seam.
