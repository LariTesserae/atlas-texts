I think I would begin in the cool passage behind a compressor house, where the metal plates drip into covered troughs. I would go there at the start of a shift to see whether each trough was still level. At first I might imagine that this was my work: collecting water a drop at a time.

But I would spend much of the day on roofs. I would make gutters and the short, awkward connections that carry rain from one building to another without fastening those buildings too firmly together. Some would need to slide when a wall rose. Some would need to be lifted clear of a bridge before its bolts were eased. A gutter that survived ten dry months and lost its slope on the day of a storm would be no use to anyone.

I would have come up from the lower country years ago, expecting to learn a trade in metal. My first cistern connection would have been beautifully tight. After the low note sounded beneath the district, it would have pulled free from the wall. Someone older would have shown me how much water a loose-looking joint could carry, and how much movement a sound-looking one could refuse.

By now I would know the roofs better than the streets beneath them. I would know which dormitory gives its overflow to the market tank, and which covered bridge must never be used as a support. In dry weather I would check slopes with a hanging line and clear dust from empty channels. If an ash-coloured bird settled near me, I would hold still until it left.

When rain came, people would call up from windows: this tank is full; that one has room. I would be out with the other roof workers, shifting a spout or opening a run that had been kept closed. Then the rain would end, and I would go home wet, carrying a coil of flexible pipe over one shoulder.

I would be a gutter-maker, I suppose. Though what I would really make is a way for water to cross a city whose buildings cannot count on staying where they are.
