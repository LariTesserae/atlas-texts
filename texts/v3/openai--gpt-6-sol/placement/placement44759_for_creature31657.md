At first I would want the crest. I would be one of the six-legged grazers, running with the band while the new root is pale and tender, turning when a seed case cracks because every body around me turns. For a little while, I think, speed would feel like knowing the valley.

But the crest is occupied almost at once. I find myself looking below it, into the spaces that seem too narrow to lead anywhere.

I would be one of the broad-bodied creatures. My back would give against the stalks instead of breaking them. Dust would cling to the oil on my skin; after I passed, the powder behind me would be pressed into a smooth, dark-sided channel. I would know a rising root first as a pressure along my whole length. There would be time to leave, though less than I wanted. I would feel for the suspended cords, fold myself around them, and keep going.

I think I would live near the stone enclosure—not inside it, where the floor is scraped bare, but beneath the roots entering its wall. The hands that cut there would seldom see me. They might find one of my polished passages when their old way uphill had closed. It would be too low for them to use without crawling, and even then only until the next ridge lifted.

When the broad root rose in the middle of the valley, I would not reach its open crest. I would be underneath, waiting for enough of its weight to pass that I could ease forward. Above me the grazers would already have come and gone. Dust would sift through the new shoots onto my back. Somewhere beyond the crowded stalks, the makers of the enclosure would be choosing another patch of floor.

I would leave them a passage without meaning to. By the time they found it, I would be farther on, feeling along the next root for a way through.
