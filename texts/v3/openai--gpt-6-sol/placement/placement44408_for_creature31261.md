I would be down where the plain is least level: in the shallow hollow beside a sedge root, where a finger’s depth of water lasts after the rest has drawn back.

At first I might mistake that hollow for a permanent place. The dark stone at its bottom stays wet; the sedge holds the bank together; a little current brings scraps past it. But by afternoon the water thins, and the hollow becomes a chain of damp impressions in the clay. I would have to leave it then.

I think I would be the small thing that drags the wavering line between pools: a mud snail, carrying a shell no larger than a seed. I would move under water so shallow it hardly seems to count as water, grazing the film left on stone. When it receded, I would follow the dampness beneath a bank, slowly enough that the birds’ shadows could pass over me before I reached it.

By morning, the wash would have taken away my trail. I would come out and make another—not to mark the plain, but because somewhere beyond the next low rise, an inch of water was still finding its way through.
