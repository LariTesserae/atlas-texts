I would find myself beneath a folded wing, where the stone is warm and the wind cannot quite reach. At first I might mistake the steady rasp above me for the mountain breathing. Then my shelter would shift its foot, and I would have to grip a feather until the rock was still again.

I would be one of the pale many-legged cleaners. I would know a ledge by its bodies rather than its shape: the tight passage between two resting adults, the young one whose feathers stay damp, the place where a drop forms reliably after a long, still night. I would eat what the wind caught and a mouth missed.

For most of my life, the opposite wall would be only calls. I could see its ledges when the sky was clear, but no route along the stone would take me there. Then, tucked under the wing of a young creature making its first flight across, I would feel my familiar wall let go. The calls would come from every direction for a moment. I would hold fast until claws struck rock again.

On that side, the pale seam would look the same, but none of the bodies around it would be known to me. I would begin with the nearest wet feather.
