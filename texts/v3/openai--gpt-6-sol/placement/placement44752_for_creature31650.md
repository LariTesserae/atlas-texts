At first I think I would stand on the rise, watching the dark edge of rain come across the plain. There would be time to see it arrive and no reason to hurry. I might walk toward one of the walls, more for the company of something built than for shelter.

But I don’t think I would remain standing. The humming would begin under my feet, and I would put a hand to the stone. By the time the reeds drew near enough to count, I would be lying flat, trying to tell whether it was the ground or the distance that had moved.

Perhaps I would be one of the pale creatures after all: not one of the smallest, which can pass over a full cup without disturbing it, but one grown too broad to leave the seam entirely. I would know the plain from its edges. A pressure rising far away would reach me before the sound did. I would flatten at the first low note, then wait to learn whether it climbed. Most days I would take grit and drowned things from the water, letting my folds carry me along a crack no walker would notice.

I would return to a wall. Not because it kept the rain off, but because its stones had been moved and set down again, leaving narrow ways through them. I could cling to the worn inner face while water passed around me. During a storm my back would fill, ring by ring. I would hold that small portion still.

The others might be too far away to see. Now and then one beyond the wall would turn as I turned, though I would not know how we had agreed. In a loud hour I might stretch across a gap that had become briefly narrow. By morning the cups would have drawn apart. What remained between them would not be a path for me, only a clear thread on which smaller lives could gather.

When the humming stopped, I would empty the rain I had held. It would join the water moving under the pavement and go somewhere I could not follow. Then I would follow what came the other way.
