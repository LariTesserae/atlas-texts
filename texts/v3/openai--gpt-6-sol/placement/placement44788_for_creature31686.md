I would be easy to miss. For most of my life I’d be under a leaf in a warm channel, holding to its underside while rainwater lifted it and let it settle again. Above me, the water would be too hot; farther down, the current would carry me away. I’d live in the narrow stretch between.

The vent would reach me first as a tremor. Then the water might cloud, or grow suddenly warmer, and I’d loosen my hold and find another leaf. What looked like a fixed bank from above would be a changing place to me: silt laid down overnight, a root newly exposed, a cool trickle reaching a channel it had missed yesterday.

Eventually I’d leave the water as one of the tiny flies hanging in the steam. For a little while I’d see the basin from above it—the pale lips of the spring, the moss-dark channels, perhaps a frog still waiting at the place where warm and cold meet. I wouldn’t go far. A bird might take me before the cloud closed over the ridge.

That seems right for me here: not the creature that knows the whole mountain, but one that spends its life learning a few inches of its warmth, then rises briefly into the air it made.
