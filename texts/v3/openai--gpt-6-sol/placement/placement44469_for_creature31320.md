At first I would look for myself among the pale wings. I like the thought of being small enough to rest beneath a shelf, letting a drop gather above me for a long time before going to drink. But I think I would grow impatient with wings in a country where the air hardly moves.

Then I might imagine myself as one of the heavier animals, known mostly by the sound of my back brushing through a passage. I would learn which basins held water longest and which shelves carried my weight. Yet even that would make me only a visitor to the places I knew best.

I think you would find me at the edge of an arch: a pale root tip, barely visible against the raw stone.

I would belong to one of the immense plants, though I could not tell where that plant ended. Above me, its stems would hold open a room. Far behind me, other roots would reach a seam in the roof and take in water. I would work a little of it into the grains beneath the arch, fastening them before they could shift. Most days I would advance no farther than the width of a wing.

A drop would fall into the basin nearby. A winged animal would drink; later, a broad foot might leave a print in the pollen without pressing hard enough to hurt me. I would feel neither one as a guest. The arch was there before them, and the tender shoots beside it would grow well because it remained open.

From where I was, the room might look like a way through. I would be one of the small reasons its edge held.
