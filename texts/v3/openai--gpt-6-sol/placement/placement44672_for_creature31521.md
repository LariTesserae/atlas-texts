I would be one of the small six-legged grazers, old enough to leave the seam behind the housing, though not so old that I would cross the bare stone.

Most of the ledge would have no meaning to me. The cloud, the distant ridge, even the ferns would be too large to take in whole. I would know the underside of one leaf, the sheltered side of an iron foot, and the particular stretch of concrete where warm water lays down a new film. I would follow that film slowly, feeling for it before each step.

The turbine would shake through my legs all day. I might scarcely notice its roar. But when the valve hissed, I would flatten against the concrete with the others and wait. Then, after the sound was gone, I would unfold one leg and test the surface again.

Perhaps I would be found at the farthest pipe support on a clear afternoon, grazing where the stain had only just begun to darken. Beyond it, the iron would be dry. Behind me, a wavering line in the fresh film would lead back to the moss. I would turn there—not because I knew how high the ledge was, but because I knew where the water stopped.
