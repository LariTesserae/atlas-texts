I would be at the ditch’s narrowest point, where the water has to turn before it enters the culvert.

At first I might mistake the pump’s beat for something inside me. It comes up through the wet ground and holds its pace whether the workers are asleep or crossing the yard. Then a frog drops from the pipe, and the ditch trembles differently. A swallow cuts across the grey light. Someone’s sandal scrapes grit from a higher stone into the water.

I would be the reed growing there: one green stem in a place still mostly brick, iron, and concrete. My roots would hold in the silt the rain brings down from the yard. Each morning the pump would send water past them; each hot afternoon a little would dry from my leaves.

I could not keep the workers’ rooms dry. I would not even stop the ditch. But grit would catch around my roots, and a frog might pause in the shade of my stem. If the water rose overnight, I would bend with it and stand again when it passed. That seems like a way to belong to a yard that has not finished becoming itself.
