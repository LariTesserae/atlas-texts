At first I think I would be one of the long-legged grazers, able to look across the plateau and see where it falls away. But there is too much to attend to underfoot. I would keep lowering my head.

I find myself instead inside a chimney that has already fallen once. Its walls are thick at the base and open to the sky at the top. I am one of the builders living in it: small enough to carry a damp grain of dust in my mouth, strong enough to press it into place before the morning warmth takes the moisture out of it.

We do not rebuild the whole chimney. The old walls give us shade, and rebuilding too high would cost more mornings than we have. We line the passages below, where the young wait, and close the cracks that let hot dust drift in. Before sunrise I go out with the others. The stone is cool then. I can feel moisture in a grain before I can see it.

By noon the broken rim is full of pale animals. Their backs cover our work so completely that a grazer looking down might take the chimney for a low mound of stone. I stay inside. Through the wall I feel its narrow feet settle beside us, and later the lighter movement of animals following where it has fed. None of them knows which grains I placed this morning. I could not find them myself.

At dusk I climb to the rim. The pale bodies are making their slow way toward the shelves, and for a little while I have room to stand. Beyond them the heights are still lit, though our doorway has gone dark. A hunter waits against the far side of the chimney. I know it by the pressure of its body on the wall. I go back down without trying to drive it away. Tonight it has the shade outside; we have the shade within.

In the morning, if there is moisture, I will carry up another grain.
