At first I think I would live near the downhill works, where the windows cloud over and the pumps can be heard through the walls. I would know how to tighten a wandering belt and how long to wait before asking an old piston for another stroke. But I don’t think I would stay there. The machines already have tenders who understand them better than I would.

I would find myself on the stairs beneath the sheltered square, carrying a lamp and a length of chalk. My work would be to walk the older passages after a change in the weather, listening at the gates and marking the water level beside marks made by people long before me. It would be modest work. Most days I would come back with nothing to report except that a familiar drip had moved a little farther along the ceiling.

I would live in a narrow room above a covered lane. A bowl beneath one seam would fill slowly enough that I could hear each drop while mending my coat. In the morning I would empty it into the household jar, clear the leaves from our grate, and then go down to see whether the mountain had gathered anything more.

Over time I think I would become less certain that I was measuring water and more certain that I was learning where it chose to linger. I would know the stair on which a call comes back clear when the cistern is low, and the place where roots have changed the sound of a wall without yet breaking it. In one of the oldest chambers I would hold my lamp still after taking the level, until the pale fish came near the surface. I would not put them in my notes unless something about the water had changed. But I would wait for them.
