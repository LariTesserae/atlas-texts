You would find me beneath the stand that looks, from the far end of the hollow, like a fine dark comb. I would be sitting beside a pool with one hand in the water and the other holding a cup under the drip. I am not washing anything. I am learning whether the leak has slowed.

As a child I thought the people who came to the pools were caretakers of water. Then I thought they were mapmakers, though none of their maps survived a season. Now I think we are listeners. A drip tells you what the roof is doing. A leaf tells you what the air did after the roof moved. Sometimes those are the only warnings you get.

I carry a rolled screen, a cup, and a length of cord. The cord has knots in it, but they do not mark distance. One was tied after the crack that dropped ice into the eastern fans. Another marks the day a leak stopped and we found its water two stands away. I can no longer say which knot came first without feeling each one. That is enough for me; when I return to a place, I need to know what to look for.

Today the cup takes longer to fill than it did yesterday. Above me, a bead swells on the pale roof, hangs, and falls wide of the pool. I move the cup and wait for the next one. It falls wide too. The old screen is still pinned there, catching leaves for water that is beginning to land elsewhere.

I take the stones off its corners. The fibres resist when I fold it. By warmer hours they will loosen. Somewhere higher up, the pale animals are working at a fresh break, so I keep my eyes on the joints while I pack. I could climb the old ladder for a better view, but its top rung has disappeared into a branch. I remember when there was room for a person to stand above it.

A crack sounds overhead. I put down the screen and wait. Across the hollow, the curtains begin to move, one stand after another. A few leaves turn in the bright air. I watch where they go.

When the motion passes, I pick up my things and follow the new drops.
