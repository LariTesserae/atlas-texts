At first I would look for myself in the empty air: somewhere above the square, listening to the roar pass through the roots. But air leaves by the seam. I think I would remain.

I would be a small crystal of salt in the powder beneath the smooth floor, where a fine root has found me and held fast. From inside the town there would be no sign of me. Someone feeling their way across the square would find only its old, worn surface. Below it, the root would grow a little thicker at my edge.

I would not know when the square was cut, or whose hand held the stone. I would know its passing only as a faint pressure overhead, altered whenever the wood shifted. Years might go by between one change and the next. A doorway would narrow; a roof rib would bow; a root would take a little more of me.

For a while I would be a hard place the plant could grow against. Then I would be less distinct: salt drawn up into living wood, perhaps into a bud that spends years opening across a door. I would never see the town. I would become part of the thing that keeps it, and slowly closes it.
