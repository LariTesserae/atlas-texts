I would live halfway down the eastern slope, behind a stack of paving stones that no one ever set. The basin there is long and narrow, but one end was pressed a little lower while the mortar was soft. Even after the last water has gone over the lip, a spoonful remains.

For a long time, I would think I was that spoonful.

In dry weather I would draw myself into its deepest part, where grit lies in the thumbprint of the person who finished the basin. Rain would loosen the grit and carry most of it away. I would learn to tell how hard it had fallen by what was left: three pale grains after a brief shower, none after a storm. I could not count the rain itself. By the time it reached me it would already have passed through too many hands’ work.

Above me, a row of threshold cups empties quickly. Below me, the water enters a covered cistern. I would know its resident only by a drop that sometimes sounds against stone and sometimes does not. For years, perhaps, that would be enough of a conversation. When my basin filled, I would keep the first water at the lip a little longer. Eventually a sheet would slip over, travel out of sight, and the drop below would change its note.

One season, the unused stones would be moved. Light would reach the low end of my basin for the first time, showing me that the thumbprint is not its deepest place. There is a smaller hollow beside it, no wider than a nail. I could stay there through a dry spell and let the spoonful vanish.

I would not do it at once. I had mistaken the water for myself for so long that seeing it leave would be frightening. But one clear morning I would draw back into the hollow. The remaining water would creep toward the lip and fall. For a while I would be alone with the damp clay. Then the stone would dry, and I would still be there.

When rain came again, I would let the first thin sheet take me. I would find out what kind of life a covered cistern allows, and whether the drop I had heard all those years could hear me arriving.
