I would be beneath the eastern grille, though at first I might mistake the whole chamber for where I lived. The knock would arrive through the iron, the ceramic, the water. After it came the rattle, and after the rattle a rush carrying silt through the trench. Those would be the boundaries of my day.

Closer in, the world would narrow to the underside of the grille. Something would hold there against each stroke of the tide. An arm would pass along the rows fixed to the metal, clearing grit from the spaces between them. When the water slowed, it would pass again.

I would be one of the dark points inside an egg: small enough that the crab crossing the plates could not see me, close enough to feel my mother grip the grille when the shaft caught. Light from the roof would reach me only after passing through the sea and the room and the thin wall around me. I would know her first as a change in the water.

Then, one stroke, the water would get in. I would leave the row and tumble past the pale tip of her shorter arm, out through the grille into the open middle where the fish gather. For the first time the knock would come from somewhere behind me.
