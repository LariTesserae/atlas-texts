At first I would put myself on the flywheel rim, in the narrow strip the snail crosses. It must be one of the few places where iron still meets daylight. I could watch the snail arrive so slowly that, for a while, we would seem to be keeping pace with the wheel.

But I don’t think I would stay there. The rim is exposed; everything lasting in this yard has found a way inside something else.

I would be a pocket of air caught in a broken valve, between a swelling root and the iron that holds it. Most of the hour I would grow smaller. Water would press from below, the root would thicken, and the casing would give me nowhere to go. I could hear the drops on the housing and the leaves tapping overhead, though I would never see them.

Then the valve would bark. I would rush through the gap with the water, perhaps as a scatter of bubbles among the fine roots, and lose myself for a moment in the pools. When the pressure eased, another little pocket would gather in the casing. I wouldn’t know whether it was me. In this yard, that hardly seems a question the stems could answer either.

I would live where the pump and the growth leave just enough room for one another to move. Not an empty place for long—only the brief, stubborn bit of space that lets the wheel advance a tooth.
