At first the darkening of a basin looks like water spreading over ceramic. Watch it closely, though, and the edge does not retreat quite as the heat would make it. One small part holds fast. Another extends a hair’s breadth into the dry street. By the time the basin is empty, the shape is gone.

The inhabitants are thin enough to be mistaken for a finish on the stone. Each lies against a terrace or the underside of a bridge, a black film with a faint copper grain. In dry air it tightens and becomes indistinguishable from the ceramic beneath it. When a drop lands nearby, fine ridges open across its surface and draw the water toward the warmer edge. The drop may disappear at once, but a trace of it travels through the film before the film closes again.

There are many such patches, though most of the plots are bare. The largest occupy the raised lips at intersections, where heat comes up from below and a basin sometimes keeps water for a few seconds. Smaller ones cling beneath the elevated line. They need both routes: warmth rising through the city and moisture falling into it. Too much of either would leave them with nowhere to feed.

Their young are a pale dust caught in the fresh mineral seams. A cooling draft can loosen them; a falling drop can carry them across a street. Most settle where they cannot live. A few lodge in a warm crack and begin to lay down a dark skin, drawing minerals out of the little water they receive. Over time that skin becomes another lip, another shallow channel. The city has been growing this way since the ridge opened—each patch making a slightly better place for the next one.

They do not pass along the streets. Even seen over days, they scarcely seem to move. Their work is in the edges: holding a bead of water just long enough, thickening a crossing by a grain, leaving a basin with a new, almost imperceptible fall. When a bridge clicks in the heat, the film beneath it opens along the joint and closes in its new position.
