At first the ledge seems to belong to the plants. They keep so close to the stone that even the wind cannot get beneath them. Each cushion has its own small weather: damp grit at its center, frost at its edge, an hour of sun in which a flower can open and an insect can find it.

Then a piece of rubble moves.

The animal that emerges is rounder than the country allows. Its ears are short, its fur the gray-brown of old stone, and it pauses with a flowering stem held crosswise in its mouth. A pika. It lives somewhere under the broken wall, beyond the reach of the evening glaze. In the brief wet hours it cuts stems and carries them into a sheltered crack. Some it eats now; some it leaves to dry. Over the season, a small, fragrant stack gathers there: leaves, flowers, and grasses brought up from lower slopes. When the snow closes over the ledge, that stack will be its field.

It knows the windbreak as another patch of fallen stone. It passes behind it when the larger bird goes over, while the moths close their wings and the little birds disappear below the lip. Once the shadow has passed, the pika comes out again. Its call is a single thin note, so brief that the open air nearly takes it.

The smooth-topped stones belong to someone else, someone who comes often enough to rest a hand on them but cannot stay through the frost. For now that visitor is absent. The pika crosses the hollowed slab, chooses another stem, and vanishes into the wall. A moment later the birds return to the flowers.
