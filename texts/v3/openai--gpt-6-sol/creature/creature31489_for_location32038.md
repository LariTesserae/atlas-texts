The hollow is inhabited in layers. Moths sleep beneath the fern fronds with their wings folded to the shape of the leaves. Beetles work slowly through softened bark. Pebble-backed frogs wait where seepwater crosses the stone, and pale, narrow animals lie among the submerged roots, moving only when something smaller drifts within reach. Fungi take what has fallen; roots take what the fungi leave.

The birds are the most visible residents, though at first they look like a single dark growth along the branch. They roost shoulder to shoulder, feathers touching, conserving warmth in the cool air. Now and then one lifts its head. A neighbor shifts without waking. The bird on the shelf holds a twig under one foot and slips its tip into a seam in the rock. It draws out something minute, swallows, and keeps the twig. It has no use for the twig beyond this moment, but it knows the seam.

Nothing here has much room to chase or flee. The frogs survive by looking like stone; the moths by resembling wet leaves; the creatures in the water by resting where roots break their outlines. Even feeding is mostly a matter of reaching into the nearest gap. They are not quiet because the hollow is empty. They are quiet because nearly every surface is occupied, and every movement passes close to another life.
