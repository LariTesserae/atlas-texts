The largest thing living on the shelf has no clear boundary. A trunk disappears into a neighbor and rises again several yards away; a root feeds a branch that once belonged to another tree. In places a fallen vault remains green because the trees on either side have grown through it. The wood is not one creature, exactly, but it has had centuries to become less than many.

Among its branches live the long-tailed walkers. They are about the size of foxes, with broad, gripping feet and fur that sheds water in fine silver lines. They rarely descend to the peat. Instead they travel the joins between trees, pausing where rain has collected to drink and to pick pale insects from the moss. At rest they wrap their tails around a branch and tuck their faces beneath them. A traveler looking up may take one for a hanging knot of roots until it opens an eye.

The pools have their own occupants. Small frogs lay their eggs in water held high above the ground, where a branch has grown around a hollow. Their young spend their first weeks in those little cups, feeding on softened leaves. Lower down, larger frogs wait at the edges of the dark pools and vanish into them without a splash. Most of the shelf’s birds hunt by sight. They move through the dimness in short flights, stopping often, and seem to have little use for song beneath the steady sound of falling water.

There are heavy animals here too, though they are harder to see than the walkers. They have low shoulders and wide feet that spread their weight over the yielding floor. At dusk they nose through the wet leaf beds for roots and grubs, following routes worn into the moss over many generations. When rain fills their tracks, the frogs come to them. By morning the tracks are green again.

No one tends the old stone circles. Seedlings have divided the blocks, and one pool has grown so deep with leaves that its bottom is beyond reach. Yet the animals treat these places like the rest of the shelf: a walker drinks from the pool, a bird nests in a gap in the wall, roots pass slowly through both. Whatever once lived among the stones is gone. What lives there now has made no distinction between the ruin and the mountain beneath it.
