The first inhabitants you meet are the ones that do not move aside. They are small, jointed things in the mud, no larger than a thumb. They press against a boot and continue pressing until the boot lifts. Afterward they gather in the print, feeling along its walls while water wells up around them. By the time the print is full, they have gone somewhere beneath it.

The fish are less easy to distinguish from the water. Their bodies are warm and soft-sided, and they turn together in the shallows. If you stand still, you can feel them passing one way along one leg and the opposite way along the other. The station tenders say this is how a new channel begins. They have also found fish stranded on fresh, dry gravel, alive and quiet, their gills moving in air that smells of rain.

The birds keep wider spaces between themselves. In the dark a person learns their arrangement by sound: two beaks clicking near a cable, a feather brushing the reeds farther off, then several careful steps through water. They feed without stirring much silt. Sometimes a tender coming back from a station finds a bird standing where the outward journey took place, its feet sunk into a bank that had not yet risen. It stays while the person feels for the cable and passes.

The tenders live out on the plain for weeks at a time. They know one another by the way each knocks on a concrete stair before climbing it. In the station rooms they hang wet clothes over the warm motor housings and sleep with a hand against the floor, where the pipe’s slow beat can be felt. Their logbooks record pressures, volumes, hours, and the number of steps taken between stations. In the margins are notes they do not put in the official columns: *birds silent at second cable*; *stones under return path*; *heard Mara at north pump before she left this one*.

No one likes to be walking when everything falls quiet. The fish cease brushing the ankles. The little mud-dwellers release their hold. Even the birds stop setting down their feet. A tender caught in such a pause stands still too, with a tool tied close to the wrist, and waits for the sound of water entering water. Once the beaks begin clicking again, there may be fewer steps to the next station. There may be more.

The tenders have never found a creature responsible for this. They know only what is left living on the changed ground. Small bodies find the new mud first. Fish follow water into the cuts. A bird lowers its foot onto a clean-edged bank and takes another step. Far behind it, a tender reaches the end of a cable sooner than expected and writes down the hour.
