The people who live there keep two addresses: the one painted on the building and the one that will still get a visitor to their door. Children learn the second kind first. They can tell a tram stop by the pitch of its rails underfoot, a cistern by the moths gathered on its warm side, a neighbor’s block by the interval between two whistles. A child sent for bread may come home late, but seldom lost. Adults are more likely to insist that a familiar street ought to be shorter.

Many residents work at making things fit. Tram crews carry lengths of spare rail and find themselves using them on routes laid only a year ago. Masons leave a dark space beneath a new wall and come back in spring to see how much of it remains. The surveyors sleep poorly. They go out with painted stakes and notebooks, and return with measurements that disagree without any stake having fallen. In the evenings they compare figures at the depot, then choose the figures that will let the trams run tomorrow.

The cistern beds have their own population. Among the moss live crowded, quick creatures whose feet spread wide on wet brick. They emerge after the shifts, when the yards are warm and quiet, and nose along the gutters for trapped insects. They know which tanks are full before the gauges do. Workers who care for the pipes sometimes leave a shallow dish beside a drain; by morning it is empty, with a little fan of delicate tracks around it.

Above the rails, the birds nest in such numbers that a person walking a span can hear the young calling through the metal. Their flocks return to particular wires even when the distances between pylons change. At the unfinished cliffs they settle on bare ledges and face the smooth rock, waiting, it seems, for cracks. Each year a few more find places to nest.

There are people, too, who spend whole shifts out beyond the last paving stone. They put down stakes, clear loose rock from the new road, and mark the place where a culvert will have to go once there is a slope to carry water through it. They take their meals in sight of the city, though it may take an hour to walk back. At dusk they count one another before leaving. A voice can sound close on that height when its owner is far away.

No one has claimed the whistle out there. It sounds irregularly, sometimes in answer to the foundries, sometimes after they have gone quiet. The outdoor crews have looked for its source and found only their stakes, the fallen rock, and a little more ground than they remember crossing. When it calls, the fox-footed animals stop at the edges of the yards. The birds rise from the wires all at once.

By morning the crews are at work again. Someone will have painted a new number on a wall. Someone else will have found an extra step beneath their front door and will use it on the way out.
