The dark ones are easiest to notice when they turn. Their backs are made of close-fitting plates the color of old bolts, but the joints between them flash a dull silver as they bend. Most keep their heads down, working grit through their jaws and leaving clean, narrow trails in the pale dust. Where a trail meets a groove, others join it. Soon the stone seems to have acquired a moving seam.

The pale ones live in that traffic without quite belonging to it. They slip under the armored bodies to take particles loosened by the jaws, then retreat into cracks too thin for a shell to enter. Seen against the stone, they are little more than quick interruptions in the dust. One can disappear beneath a flake and emerge several body-lengths away carrying a dark crumb almost as large as itself.

Along the rim, the hunter moves with an economy the others lack. It has a low, broad body and forelegs held just clear of the ground. Its shadow stops the traffic before it reaches a groove. For a while nothing moves there except dust falling from the toothed metal overhead. Then a pale animal breaks cover, and the hunter's forelegs close on the place where it was going.

The hollow belongs to none of them for long. The armored animals wear passages open; the pale ones clear the finer debris from those passages; the hunter follows both. When wind makes the track hum, grit shakes free and settles among them. All three kinds pause or shift beneath that long note, then return to their work. By evening the trails have changed, though the hollow, from a few steps away, looks exactly as it did in the morning.
