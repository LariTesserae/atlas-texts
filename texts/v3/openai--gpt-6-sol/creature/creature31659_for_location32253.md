The largest animals are easy to miss at first. They stand no higher than the vegetation in places, with broad feet that spread their weight over the thin soil. They move slowly across the open ground, cropping the tough leaves one mouthful at a time. From a distance, a group of them looks like a few patches of grass darkening and shifting. Up close, their coats are full of seeds, and small insects climb through the hair around their shoulders. They drink deeply when they find water, then go on without it.

Beneath them is a much more crowded country. Burrowers live in the soil held among the roots, keeping several entrances open so they can retreat whichever way danger comes. They emerge to cut stems and carry them below, leaving little heaps of drying grass at their doors. Their whistles pass from one hollow to the next faster than a running animal can cross the ground. When the alarm goes up, the plateau seems to swallow its inhabitants: the moving lines stop, the whistles cease, and only the insects remain audible.

The insects are everywhere, though most are seen only when the plants are parted. Some spend the cold nights clinging to the undersides of fleshy leaves, where dew collects on their bodies. Others live in the shallow pans while they hold water. After rain, they rise all at once, and the birds come down to meet them. The small birds fly low enough to brush the grass, their turns so sudden that a flock seems to change its mind as one creature. Larger birds wait on the wind. They watch for a burrower caught too far from its door, or for one of the grazers to leave a weak newborn behind.

Much of the plateau’s life gathers around water without staying there. A pan may be surrounded by tracks in the morning and dry by evening. Plants crowd its rim, their leaves scarred by feeding; insects lay eggs in the wet soil; birds bathe in the last muddy inch. By the next day, the animals have moved on, and the ground is quiet except for what has retreated into it.

People come through in much the same way. They use the old paths to reach the pans and sheltered hollows, patch the stone windbreaks, and leave them for the next travellers. At night, a low shelter may hold several voices against the wind. Before dawn its occupants can hear the burrowers begin calling beneath the grass, then the birds, until their own voices are only one part of the noise.
