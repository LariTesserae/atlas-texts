At first the town seems to be inhabited chiefly by birds. They stand in the open channels on legs too long for their bodies, lifting their feet high out of the warm water and disputing each new patch of mud. Others nest in the walls, where the broad leaves meet but do not quite close. A house can be full of their noise before anyone inside has woken.

The people are harder to spot from the rim. They live low among the stems, in rooms that admit wind and weather in measured amounts. In the morning they step over sleeping frogs on their thresholds and check which paths have opened. They know that an errand may require a different route home, so a visit to a neighbor is often allowed more time than the distance seems to warrant. Children are sent out with baskets for dropped fruit and come back with fish as well, caught in the shallow run of the street. They learn which edges of a path are firm without testing them, and which narrow bridges are still drawing themselves together.

Much of their work concerns water, though they do not try to keep it out. They clear blocked channels, lift young plants above a season’s expected flood, and make small repairs to doors that have grown too heavy to fold. Some tend the instruments set among the roots. The instruments record a cart’s passage as pressure spreading through the mat, or a bridge tightening strand by strand beneath a traveler. Their keepers can show these records to anyone who asks. They still walk home by the routes the roots provide.

The roots support more residents than the houses do. Silver fish slip through the streets after rain and remain in pools no wider than a footprint. Insects hatch in the gutters, leaving their wings for the next rush of water to carry away. Small animals travel the high paths while they are open, pausing at a crossing until a stem has finished moving. The trunks have their own occupants, too: birds by day, and creatures that come down cautiously in the evening to drink.

The town makes room for all of them, though not in any way that stays fixed. A balcony opens into a bird’s usual landing place; a channel cuts across a foraging path. The people move a basket or wait for a roof to empty itself, and the other residents find another way around. By nightfall, mist rises from the water and each house holds its own pocket of warmth. Calls carry between them. Under those sounds the roots continue to shift, keeping the streets passable for whoever will need them in the morning.
