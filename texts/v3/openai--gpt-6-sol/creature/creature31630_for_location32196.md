The people here learn the town first through their feet. A steady tremor means a motor is running well. A stutter means someone will soon be called from a bunk with their boots half-laced. Even asleep, they know the difference between a freight cage settling into its berth and a stone shifting somewhere beyond the bolted walls.

They keep their belongings close. A repairer may have a bed, a bench, and a cupboard on three different ledges, connected by stairs too narrow for passing. Coats hang beside doorways because the walk to supper crosses open air. Children grow adept at carrying things one-handed while keeping the other on a rail. The oldest residents still speak of the crack as an event rather than a place: where they stood when it opened, which road was lost, who first lowered a line into it.

No one mistakes the birds for wild abundance. They live on warmth borrowed from the switch cabinets and on food that arrives in cages. The people leave the nests alone, partly because the birds catch what flies and partly because their sudden silence can tell you something is wrong with a machine. The beetles have less standing. They are swept from the meal stalls, then reappear beneath the freight platforms, where spilled grain collects in the heads of bolts.

The pale hunters are harder to account for. They slip from pipe to pipe on legs fine enough to seem jointed from wire. Some people call them spiders; others insist they have counted too many legs. Either way, a child who finds one in a sleeve is told to put it near the crates, not crush it. In a town with no soil and no water of its own, even a small appetite has its work.

When the red lamp comes on, the residents clear the upper bridge before the cage rises. The birds clear it after, at the last possible moment. For a few seconds everyone waits together—the people behind their doors, the birds wheeling under the strip of sky, the hidden creatures holding still among the pipes—while the load comes up from a world with fields in it.
