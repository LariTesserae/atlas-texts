The things that live here are easiest to see in the sheltered cuttings. The pale stems are not rooted in soil; they rise from narrow seams in the stone, each joint fitting closely over the one below it. A stem may stand for years at the same height, then add a joint overnight. No one has seen it happen. Around the warm pipes, moss covers the places where the stems enter the rock, making a bank soft enough to press a hand into. The stems do not bend away from a hand. They remain still until the hand is gone.

Each leaf holds a bead of water. The beads grow in the rain and diminish in clear weather, but none falls while the machinery is running evenly. When a turbine briefly loses its pitch, drops strike the stone all through the cuttings at once. Then the stems lean—not toward the water, but toward the sound of the turbine coming back.

They live in the works and nowhere else. A few stand behind a tank in a basin miles from the next district; a dense stand can fill the space beneath a service bridge. At its outer edge, where the shelter ends, the last stems are short and widely spaced. Beyond them there is only wet rock. They have never crossed it.

There may be something living below them as well. In the pumping houses, the pressure gauges make small, regular movements that do not match any change in flow. The same movement appears at the next house hours later, then at the next, proceeding along a fold of the plateau. It passes beneath bare stretches where there are no pipes. Engineers once left instruments there, drilled deep into the stone and fixed behind locked covers. Their windows are lit; their paper rolls have long since filled. Through the glass, the needles can still be seen moving.

Nothing on the plateau pursues a visitor. The streets stay empty, and the bridges hold. But in a cutting, after standing still long enough to hear the turbines separately from their echoes, a person may notice one bead of water growing larger than the rest. Far below, a gauge needle turns. The drop stays on its leaf.
