The people who live here are used to leaving room. Their houses sit back from the road, with vines climbing the brick and damp gathering on the sills before anyone is awake to wipe it away. In the morning, workers cross boards laid over the unsettled gravel, carrying their boots into the workshops before the first blow of the press. By evening they know, from the sound of a cart on the drainage bridge, which plank has worked loose again.

The open ground has more residents than it first admits. Starlings possess the power lines and announce themselves over the motors. Swallows make quick circuits of the yards, turning around a crane as readily as around a willow. Under stacked timber, beetles keep to the cool, wet dark; among the nettles, small creatures have routes that seldom bring them into sight. The weeds are not filling vacant lots so much as occupying every interval the buildings leave them.

The youngest inhabitants are the willows on the river shelf. They have taken root in ground the town has only just begun to use, and each year they make the distance between workshop and water a little harder to judge. Nothing here seems entirely established: not the gravel paths, not the riverbank, not even the division between yard and thicket. The people go to work and come home by the boards; everything else finds its own way through.
