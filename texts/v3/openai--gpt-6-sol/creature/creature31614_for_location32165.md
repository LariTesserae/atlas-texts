At first, the long-legged birds seem to be the only inhabitants. They step over the channels without making ripples, lower their bills into the leaf-lined pools, and stand so still afterward that their legs become two more pale stems. They feed on things too small to see from the bridges: clear, quick bodies that shelter beneath the tiled leaves and scatter when a shadow crosses the water.

Closer to the pools, the leaves themselves prove less uniform than they looked. Some are held slightly apart by small, soft creatures with flat backs the color of wet seed husks. They spend hours at a rim, drawing loose fibers together and pressing them into the young crust. One works where a channel meets a wall; another tends the lip of a hollow root. Their movements are slow enough to miss, but a gap seen in the morning may be closed by afternoon. They seem to favor the places where water is about to take a new course.

The largest inhabitant is harder to count. The widely spaced trees put down roots into the same braided mat, and those roots pass into the vaults and the trunks below. A pulse of water felt against one pool wall can be heard, a little later, in a tower across the meadow. New growth does not arise evenly: a wall thickens beside a swelling root; a bridge begins where two groves have reached toward one another. Whether these are many trees living in close accord or one life with crowns set far apart, its body is the plateau.

It makes room for the others. The birds find firm edges on which to stand. The small builders find damp seams and fresh material. In the thin water among the moss leaves, nearly invisible swimmers graze and breed. Beneath them all, water travels through living wood, and the long, unchanging notes from the vaults are the sound of that passage.
