The tall animals look alike from the service bridge: pale backs, narrow heads, legs spaced carefully in the shallow basins. At their own height, they are less uniform. Some have smooth, nearly hairless flanks that hold the light like wet tile. Others wear a short cream-colored coat, darkened to gray around the knees. Their feet spread when they put weight on them, making a soft seal against the basin floor. They can stand for a long time without seeming to rest, but every so often one lifts a foot and sets it down a few inches away, as if the water beneath it has grown uncomfortable.

They do not move as a herd. A disturbance passes through them slowly: one turns its head, its neighbor makes room, and a line of bodies opens around a particular joint in the paving. A young one may follow that opening well after the reason for it has gone. The youngest still hurry when a feed slot opens. They arrive with their heads low and find the older animals already standing there, chewing. In time they learn where to wait, and how little sound the mechanism makes before the block appears.

Other inhabitants live at the edges of this standing crowd. Small, shelled animals fit themselves into the warm seams between brick and steel, withdrawing when a hoof comes down nearby. Fur-bearing ones sleep above the basins, tucked along pipe brackets in loose overlapping rows. At feeding time they descend by routes so familiar that each seems to have its own rung and patch of wall. A few remain underneath, watching the movement below until the space around a slot clears.

The quiet is not a lack of attention. Ears turn toward valves. Heads dip to taste a fresh run of water, then rise when its flow changes. Animals standing shoulder to shoulder adjust to one another all day, yielding a foot’s width of room, reclaiming it, settling again. By daylight their bodies cover much of the careful geometry of the works. At night, when the pipes stay warm and the trickles continue, they are still there: breathing above the basins, sleeping under the racks, waking in ones and twos to find their places unchanged.
