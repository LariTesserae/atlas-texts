At first, the inhabitants look like the places where nothing is happening. A patch of moss stays wet when the rushes around it dry. Black silt in a stone’s hollow gathers into a shallow crescent and holds that shape against the rain. In a pool, an old reed stem trembles a moment before the wind reaches it.

Under the silt, each of these places joins others. The fine threads in the clay are not one creature spread across the plain, nor quite a multitude. A body can end at a dry ridge and begin again where seepage reaches the next pool. Two bodies can share a stone for years, their threads growing alongside one another, until a bank slips and makes them impossible to tell apart.

They have no visible faces. Their nearest thing to a gesture is a change in the water’s tone. One draws a ripple along a pool’s edge without disturbing its centre; another lets a low note pass from stone to stone until the reeds on a distant ridge shiver. Most of the time they are occupied with smaller work. They hold a little moisture in the clay around a new root. They take up the loosened grains beneath a weeping seam. Where rain cuts a channel, they grow into the exposed bank, slowly enough that the cut remains, but does not widen as quickly as it might.

The plants live among them without appearing to notice. Shrub roots grow through their threads. Algae spreads over the pools in sheets so thin that a thread can lie in one layer and its growth in the next. Small animals pass across their bodies and vanish into cover. Once, a bird lands on a ridge and all the nearby pools give a faint, uneven knock; when it flies off, the sound takes several breaths to cease.

Toward evening, they become easier to hear and harder to locate. The lower tones rise from across the marsh together, though rain has reached only the far edge. In the pool at your feet, its reflection arrives first: a darkening sky, reeds bent under drops that have not fallen here yet. Beneath it, in the clay, the threads are already taking up the new water.
