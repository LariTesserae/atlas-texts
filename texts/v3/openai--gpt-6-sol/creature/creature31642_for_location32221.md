At first the basin seems to have many small inhabitants. A dark line draws itself through the silt in one compartment. In another, a patch of foam moves against the current and comes to rest beneath the overhang of a pipe. Near the shed, a shallow pool puckers in the middle, though nothing has fallen into it.

The lines belong to the same creature.

Most of its body is below the concrete. It has grown through the old drains and the gaps around their joints, following water into spaces too narrow for roots. What shows aboveground is only its soft working edge: folds of dark, close-grained flesh that can flatten under a film of silt or swell enough to lift it. Water passes through those folds. They take from it the fine matter washed off the banks, then let it go on, a little clearer and a little slower.

It cannot cross an open stretch of dry concrete. Instead it waits below until a compartment fills, and extends itself along the wet seam. A new passage may take a whole season to reach. An old one can close in an afternoon if grit lodges in it. This is why the basin sometimes seems to change its mind: the creature opens one route to draw water through its body, then another to keep a stranded fold from drying.

There are places where it has come close enough to the surface to be touched. Beneath the raised channel, its skin is as cool as the water and faintly ridged. Along a rusted pipe it is rust-colored too, and a bead left on the iron will slowly disappear into it. It withdraws from a footstep, though not quickly. By the time the silt has settled, the part that felt the pressure may already be several compartments away.

The bird was in no danger. Its landing sent a small weight through the channel, and the creature tightened beneath it. The water rose over the lip; the bird flew; the creature held still for a while afterward. It has learned the difference between that brief disturbance and rain, which can arrive everywhere at once.

In the shed, behind the motionless flywheel, one of its oldest folds grips the inside of a pipe. When the basin is nearly dry, it remains damp there. When the rain returns, that fold loosens first, and water begins to find its way across the concrete.
