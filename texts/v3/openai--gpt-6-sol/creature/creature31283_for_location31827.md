The oldest resident is difficult to separate from the garden. What looks like a ring of vines may be several plants, or one plant that has gone around the bowl many times, putting down roots wherever rain has made the iron thin. Its stems are thick at the arches and finer where they enter the seams. New leaves turn toward the open sky; old ones press against the metal and hold damp there long after the clouds have passed.

The vines keep to the rim. The middle of the bowl is too warm and too dry for most seedlings, though seeds arrive constantly. Birds drop them. Wind drives them against the rivets. After a cloud has gone through, tiny green points appear beside the path, and for a few days the garden seems ready to cross its own floor. Then the iron dries, and almost all of them vanish.

The birds know this place in short seasons. They build in bolt holes, carrying grass up from the mountains until each nest has a small, improbable weight. In the roar, a returning bird may land beside its mate without either hearing the other. They touch beaks; they feel the arch tremble under their feet. The moths stay lower, under the ferns, where the air is still enough to fold their wings. Beetles move through the rust beneath them, making use of what the water and roots have loosened.

No one living here remembers the bowl without the furnace. Its heat brings the ferns through cold nights and empties the gutters in dry weather. When the machinery changes pitch, the leaves shake. When a cloud enters the arches, they grow heavy and quiet. For a little while the whole rim drinks, while the bare path remains open across the iron, leading from the lift to nothing but air.
