At first, the district seems to belong to the little jointed thing that sometimes crosses it. It knows the six streets, pauses at the blue-marked doors, and taps each frame in turn before going back down the slope. But it never stays. It is a visitor, or perhaps a messenger that has forgotten whom it serves.

The residents are harder to notice because they do not displace the water.

Each occupies a building and the dark space immediately around it. You can find one by passing slowly through a doorway: the pressure changes, though nothing moves to meet you. In one house it eases around your shoulders. In another it gathers at your fingertips. These differences are not produced by the tower. The tower holds them where they are, just as it holds the chip of basalt; the residents made them.

They have no bodies with which to arrange a room, so they arrange its weight. One has kept a narrow passage of almost effortless water from its door to a corner of raw rock. Another lives among several small regions of immense pressure, set apart like furnishings no hand could touch. The blue lines mark the places where their boundaries meet the street. They are faint because there is nothing there meant to be seen from a distance.

They were here when the stone was newer still. The mountain’s sudden rise gave them edges to live against, and the ceramic frames gave each an address. Whether they built the district or were brought to it is difficult to tell. Nothing has worn enough to answer.

They do not come out when the jointed visitor taps. Sometimes, after it has gone, the water in one doorway bears a little less heavily than it did before. The change is so slight that a passing animal might mistake it for its own movement. It remains that way. In this district, even a reply can last a very long time.
