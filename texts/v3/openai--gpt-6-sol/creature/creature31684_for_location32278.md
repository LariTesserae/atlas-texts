At first, the city can seem to have no inhabitants at all. Its doors stand open on empty rooms, and a person crossing a bridge may be the only moving figure in sight. Then a patch of paving beside a dry channel begins to stir. Small, pale animals unfold from its seams and feel along the stone with their forelegs. They are no bigger than fingernails. They have come for a dampness too slight to darken the floor.

The people notice them. A woman on her way to mend a bank will pause where they have gathered, press her palm to the channel bed, and decide whether to bring her stones here or farther down the street. She may still get it wrong. Water can enter through a crack that has been dry for years and pass within a few steps of a waiting basin. No one in the city speaks of owning a channel. They tend a course of water for as long as it lasts.

Most households occupy only a few rooms of houses built for many more. They keep their stores behind stone shutters, out of the dust, and sleep in the inner rooms when the air turns cold. Their repairs are easy to recognize. An old wall is smooth with wear; beside it, a newly set block still shows the short, uneven strokes of a chisel. The youngest residents know which stairs remain safe, though an elder may remember when those stairs led somewhere else.

When water does run, the city grows crowded at ground level. The rust-coloured film thickens along its margins. Tiny grazers collect there, and longer, quick-bodied hunters follow them from the joints of the masonry. Fish slip out of connected pools and into basins that stood empty the day before. Children watch for them, but are taught not to scoop out a pool simply because it is full. A basin left undisturbed may feed the cracks around it long after its water has vanished from view.

The people eat little that the cavern itself provides. Supplies come down from above and are kept carefully; the small wet places belong, for the most part, to smaller lives. Still, everyone watches them. A procession of pale legs across a doorstep may be news of a change underground. A hunter waiting motionless beside a channel may mean that water has already passed through.

By evening the moving air has brushed dust over some of the tracks. Someone carries a cut stone wedge to a bank, lifts two loose blocks, and sets them down where the trickle has turned. Nearby, the little animals reach the new wet edge before the work is done.
