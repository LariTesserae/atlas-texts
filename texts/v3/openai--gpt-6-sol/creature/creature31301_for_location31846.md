The most numerous inhabitants of the plateau are the ones a traveler first mistakes for the ground.

Each upright leaf belongs to a larger life below it. The dark cover is not quite a forest and not quite a single plant: neighboring growths meet, exchange charge and minerals, and sometimes grow across one another, but they also compete for light and for a hold on the stone. A hollow rib may belong to a growth older than the town beside it. Its living surface is thin; beneath that surface lie seasons of its own compressed remains, still gripping the rock.

The towers are this life given a particular shape. They close a vent against grit, strengthen a bridge where it flexes, and send a route around a failing wall. From outside, such changes look purposeful enough to suggest a creature thinking slowly. People who live in the towers are less certain. They know the structure can keep itself alive; they also know how patiently it must be persuaded to make room for anyone else.

Those people seldom stand in the open when the sky is bright. They travel under low roofs or along the tower walls, carrying little that cannot be fastened down. A household may remain in one chamber for generations while its doorway shifts a finger’s width each year. When a child can no longer pass through it comfortably, someone begins the long work of asking the wall to widen. The pale seams of the town are records of such requests—of new children, crowded rooms, abandoned rooms, a bridge needed where none had been before.

The wind hour belongs to smaller lives. Many-legged seed-catchers wait in the low streets with their feet hooked into the mat. As soon as the worst gust has gone through, they scatter beneath the walls, feeling for seeds lodged among the folded leaves. Their hard backs make a faint ticking against the ground. The hunters appear a little later: long-bodied animals that can flatten themselves almost to the height of a leaf, then lift enough to spring. A seed-catcher that reaches a hollow column may find the hunter too broad to follow.

When the towers begin to hum, their human residents listen as well. The sound tells them which vents have opened, whether the approaching wind is carrying grit, and where a bridge has changed its note since the last storm. Much of the town’s work can wait until morning. A new rattle in an old wall cannot.

Afterward, everyone comes out for a little while. The seed-catchers search, the hunters search for them, and people cross the streets to see what the wind has left behind. Beneath their feet, points of light keep moving through the living walls and out under the flats. By dusk the leaves fold over those routes. The animals vanish among them, the doorways darken, and all those crowded lives become, from a distance, an almost bare plateau.
