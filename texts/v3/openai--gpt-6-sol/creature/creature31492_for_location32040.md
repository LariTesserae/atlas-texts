The dark coating on the stone is the beginning of most lives here. It thickens where warm rock meets a cold trickle and thins wherever a band of pale grazers has passed. Each grazer is scarcely larger than a fingernail, with a blunt scraping mouth and a nearly transparent body. You can see the dark meal moving through it. They travel so close together that a band seems to have one edge, flowing around a dry patch or dividing cleanly at a crack.

The broad-backed animals follow the bands, though not only to eat them. They work their noses beneath plates loosened by the moving floor and pry them up. Their shoulders are low and heavy; their forefeet spread wide when a plate begins to shift. Beneath it they find damp growth, trapped grazers, and the soft bodies of things that have never been exposed to the light. A successful animal feeds quickly, with one foot braced against the stone. The others wait just beyond the plate’s reach. They have learned that some openings close.

Most of the winged creatures spend the bright hours on the shelves. Seen at rest, they are little more than folded wings and hooked feet, crowded so tightly that a shelf’s rough outline appears to move. In flight their wings are broad and thin enough to show the pillars through them. They descend on fresh wet seams, snatch up grazers, and carry scraps back to the shelves. Their young cling farther inside, beyond the reach of the daylight, calling whenever a returning shadow crosses them.

A crack in the floor interrupts all this feeding. Before the sound reaches the ledge, the broad-backed animals put their feet flat and the grazers lose their orderly edge. The shelf-clingers let go almost at once. For a moment the chamber holds more wings than air. They settle again after the slab falls, but not on the same shelves: the fresh break has changed where the water will run.

There are hunters that wait for that moment. They lie in the narrow, dark gaps between upright slabs, their bodies thin enough to be mistaken for a deeper seam. They seldom chase anything. When the grazers spill across a new surface and the broad-backed animals press in behind them, a hunter catches what passes too near. It retreats into the gap to feed, leaving the crowds to close around the break.

The fitted-stone enclosures stand above these shifting routes. Nothing is in them now. After rain, small tracks sometimes lead through their uphill openings and back out again; the driest corners offer a pause while the hollows below fill and empty. The bridge serves the living animals less well. They go under it, or spring away when a loosened peg rattles, then return to the wet stone once the noise has stopped.
