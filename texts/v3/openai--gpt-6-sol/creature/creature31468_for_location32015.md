The first settlers are too small to see one by one. They live in the rust-colored film along the seep, taking what they need from iron in the water. The film is thinnest where the current runs hard and thickest in the sheltered corners. It gives the new stone its first soft edge, though a shell can scrape that edge bare in a moment.

The shell-bearers stay in the fractures. Some are no bigger than a fingernail; others have grown broad enough to seal a crack with their backs. They extend pale, busy mouths into the flow and withdraw them when the silver fish press near. The fish have almost no room to turn. Each follows the movement of its neighbors, so a single flinch travels around the basin and brings every flank flashing into view. Transparent animals occupy what space remains, hanging between bodies and stone with their feeding parts spread wide. When the shoal moves, they fold themselves narrow and let it pass.

The loudest residents are small. Snapping hunters hold stations beside the grazed patches, where a shell-bearer or a drifting animal may be caught away from cover. Their strikes crack through the water; then they go still again. Larger hunters seldom enter the basin. Their shadows cross it from above, and the crowded fish make room by driving deeper into every notch.

One of the larger fish keeps close to the seep. Faint pulses pass along its sides as it noses over the rock. Behind it, the bacterial film darkens in fine branching marks. The marks linger after the fish has gone, making its route visible for a little while before the rust color returns. Nothing in the basin seems to attend to this writing. The grazers scrape across it, the fish close ranks, and beneath them the rock’s slow thump goes on.
