The first residents a visitor notices are usually the birds. They give away the presence of a garden long before it comes into view, scolding from a vine above a closed workshop or shaking grit onto the steps from a nest tucked behind a gutter. They know which troughs will still hold water by evening. The lizards know which vents stay warm after the lifts stop.

The people know these things too, though they describe them differently. They give directions by the sound of a pump, the smell of a particular bakery’s exhaust, the landing where a pipe drips even in a dry week. An address is a level, a crossing, and a few landmarks that may have grown since the last time you passed. Children learn the climb in portions: the steps they can run, the railings they can slide a hand along, the shaded courts where they are expected to wait for a slower relative.

Most households keep something alive outside the door. A row of herbs in cut-down containers may belong to one kitchen; a much larger vine, trained over three balconies, belongs to no one quite so clearly. Neighbors argue about its roots when a drain backs up, then take cuttings from it in spring. The gardens nearest the condensation pipes are thick and untidy. Elsewhere, someone must carry water uphill, and the plants show the limits of what can be spared.

There are people whose work is to understand those limits. They clear the mouths of channels, listen for a pump changing pitch, replace a length of rail before a loaded lift reaches it. Some remember repairs beneath repairs: which brick face hides an old valve, which steel collar has been widened twice for a tree. They can look across the valley and pick out a failed drain by a stripe of leaves gone suddenly dark. Their work is conspicuous only when it has been left undone.

Not everyone is patient with the climb. Porters take the lifts when there is room, and riders on the bridge rails arrive windblown and glad to be off them. Older residents arrange errands so they need descend only once. People on the upper levels complain when water pressure falls; people below complain when an upper garden sheds soil into a channel. The whistles passing between levels are sometimes warnings, sometimes greetings, and occasionally a message too particular for anyone outside the exchange to understand.

After dark, the bats travel the service courts without regard for those divisions. Moths gather at the high windows, sparrows go quiet in the leaves, and voices carry farther between the slopes. From the opposite side of the valley, the lit rooms appear to be set in a mountain of trees. Inside it, someone is still washing a filter, easing a root away from a seam, or making space on a landing for a neighbor coming home.
