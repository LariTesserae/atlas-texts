The people of the hollow know one another first by sound. A loom worker can be recognized by the quick, uneven tap of her shoes on the root street, even beneath the belt drive’s clatter. The woman who tends the dew pump carries a ring of copper keys that chimes against her hip. Children learn to stop talking when the machinery stops: in the sudden quiet, everyone listens to find out what has caught.

Morning begins at the tank. The queue is short enough for conversation and long enough for disputes about whose vessel was set down first. The dew keeper draws off a cup before she opens the taps and holds it to a lamp, looking for insect wings or a film of soil. Her hands smell faintly of copper. On nights when the ceiling gives little water, households leave their largest pots at home, and no one needs to say why.

In the workshops, people make things with an eye to taking them apart again. A cabinetmaker fits pegs rather than nails wherever he can; a child sent to fetch him knows to wait while he checks a pale shoot emerging beside his bench. He might cut it back, move the bench, or mark the doorpost for next year. The oldest workers carry measurements in their heads that the youngest still have to read from the wood. They disagree about them, too. A root does not always grow where it did last spring.

The animals know the town just as closely. Beetles settle on an engine casing before it is fully warm. Moths lay powder on the green lamp glass and turn a clear pane cloudy in a week. The little pale runners overhead have paths as worn to them as the streets below are to the people. Children watch for their sharp-eyed faces in the cracks, then blame them for small things gone missing.

At dusk, the people draw back from the court. Someone fetching a last bucket of water ducks as the first bats come through; soon the open space is full of them, banking between the lamps and the dark above. From the dwellings, families watch with doors ajar. They know the bats keep the tank clear, though they still cover their bowls.

There are inhabitants no one sees whole. A fine root appears one morning through a join in a wall, tests the air for a few days, and goes on growing. Someone ties a thread beside it to see how far it reaches. Someone else moves a shelf. By the time the thread is taken down, the change has become part of the room, and the person who lives there steps around it without looking.
