The largest animals are easiest to mistake for parts of the terraces. They lie crosswise in the fast water, broad and low, with pale backs that have the same shallow pitting as the blocks. Their bodies yield a little to the current and then hold. Along their downstream edges, rows of soft openings spread to catch what the flow brings: minute swimmers, scraps of flesh, the fine living dust that never quite settles. When one leaves its place, it does so slowly. A dark gap opens beneath it; then the whole broad body lifts and is carried to another ridge. The smaller animals rush into the space it has vacated.

Most of them live close to the stone. Clear swimmers gather where a broken course throws off a steady eddy. They face upstream and beat almost invisible fins, darting out only when something edible passes. Pale, many-legged creatures travel the undersides of loose plates, their limbs finding purchase in pits too small to see from above. Some have hard, rounded heads with which they prise food from a joint. Others are soft enough to enter a seam and emerge from another several blocks away. At a knock, every exposed surface seems to empty. Slowly the limbs appear again.

In the deepest terraces, where less food arrives from above, the animals are thinner and more patient. Long feelers extend from openings in the paving and remain there for hours, trembling when the rising water changes. A few creatures follow the silt curtains as they fold through the basin, taking what the current has lifted from the older floors. There is no meadow here for anything to graze. A meal is caught, taken from a crevice, or found in the wake of another animal.

The builders are harder to describe. In quiet joints there are clusters of small, soft bodies edged with new pale material. One presses itself against a block and leaves a lip; beside it another lays down a lip of darker grain. A loose plate comes to rest against their work and, over time, ceases to be loose. They do not appear to know the shape of a terrace. They close a gap, raise an edge, make a sheltered place in which to feed. Yet above them stand course upon course of such places, worn down and built over by lives no longer visible. The basin is inhabited all the way into its walls.
