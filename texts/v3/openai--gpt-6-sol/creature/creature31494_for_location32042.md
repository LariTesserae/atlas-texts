The people who live on the shelf know their town by touch as much as sight. A shoulder brushes the same cool wall on the way to the mill; a foot finds the shallow step beneath a veil of vine. Children learn the alleys by following goats, then learn which shortcuts are meant for goats alone. Visitors look down to place their feet. Residents tend to look ahead, listening for whoever is coming around the next turn.

They keep small things close. Flour is stored in sealed jars, loose stones are set aside for the next roof repair, and water is carried home without spilling. A shrub beside a doorway may belong to a household as surely as the doorway does. Its leaves give shade, its roots hold a little soil in place, and someone has to cut it back when it begins to catch at passing sleeves. On the roofs, people work around the thorn trees rather than remove them. From below, a branch may look wild; from above, its growth is full of old decisions.

Much of the town’s company is heard before it is seen. A woman grinding grain knows when the goat herd has passed the lower alley by the order of its bells. Someone mending a wall pauses to let a neighbor’s hammering finish, so they can hear whether a child has answered a call. Names travel over roofs and through leaves. So do complaints, offers of help, and news of a slab come loose. Privacy is found less in silence than in everyone’s long practice of letting an ordinary sound remain ordinary.

The goats know the outer paths best. They walk them with an assurance that makes their keepers look cautious, stopping to reach for leaves above the drop. Lizards move into the patches of sun left by a shifting branch; birds scatter husks onto the roofs; the pale insects go on working in the shaded bark. Each makes use of the narrow shelf differently, and each is noticed most when it is absent.

In the morning, people cross the worn rock at the edge one or two at a time. Some judge the wind, some search the haze below, some stand there while the town wakes behind them. Then they turn back toward the alleys, where a mill has begun to turn and someone is already shifting a stone into place.
