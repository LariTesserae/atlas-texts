The walls are crowded with lives, though most of them never leave the places where they began. Each shell holds a soft body behind its lip. In the warm current, those bodies reach out: fringed mouths, combs of fine hairs, pale fans that catch the falling food. A shadow passing overhead closes a whole stretch of wall. Moments later, one mouth tests the water, then another, until the ridge looks soft again.

The oldest shell-builders are buried deep in the walls. Their descendants live at the surface, fastening themselves to every sound edge. Where the current presses hardest, they grow low and tight together. In the sheltered courts they rise into fragile shelves, leaving gaps large enough for other animals to inhabit. A passage that a fish can swim through now may narrow around its route as new generations settle there.

The fish know these changing ways. Small, dark ones spend much of their time suspended in the openings, facing the flow. At a tremor in the water they disappear so quickly that the wall seems to swallow them. Others move in close groups above the roofs, turning all at once to show a brief glint of flank. They feed where the current sheds morsels against a corner, and avoid the broad passages when a larger body has passed through.

Below them, the mud is busy. Long-legged scavengers pick through the soft deposits at the feet of the walls. Some carry a faint light beneath their bodies, invisible until they step over a ridge in the floor. Thin-armed animals live farther in, in cracks the fish cannot enter. They extend a feeler into open water, then draw back with whatever touches it. After feeding, they leave small, clean patches among the settled silt.

The larger visitors do not belong to any courtyard. They come along the outer ridges, feeling their way through the porous shell with slow movements of fin or limb. The small fish retreat ahead of them; the mouths in the walls close. Long after a visitor has gone, suspended mud marks where it turned.

Today the warm current keeps the exposed sides of the walls open. Food drifts in, and nearly every reachable surface is feeding. On the sheltered sides, animals wait with their soft parts drawn close. When the flow changes, they will extend into it. The fish will turn to face the other way, the scavengers will work new drifts of silt, and openings that looked empty will fill with reaching bodies.
