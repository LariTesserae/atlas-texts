At first, the people here seem to be hurrying. They step off a crossing just as a freight deck begins to lower, slip sideways through a market queue, and take stairways a visitor would swear lead away from their destination. But they are not all in a rush. They are making small allowances for a place that moves: leaving room for a bridge to rise, listening for a change in a pump’s beat, choosing the dry side of a street before the water appears.

Most carry little that cannot get wet. Deliveries travel in sealed cases, babies sleep in slings above the flood line, and market stalls can be folded shut in the time between a warning tone and the first surge. People keep spare shoes at work and know which neighbors will have a floor to sleep on when the lower rooms fill. An address includes a level as well as a street. Directions often include the weather.

There are workers whose faces nearly everyone recognizes but whose names few know: the gate crews in marked coats, the people who test crossing joints at dawn, the drain scrapers who move through the dim lanes with long-handled tools. They do not command the water. They keep it from surprising anyone. When a bridge shifts badly, conversation stops nearby until one of them has crossed it.

Children learn the warning tones before they learn the names of distant districts. They race small crabs along the gutters and dare one another to look down through the broad street grates. Older children are sent on errands by routes that change with the rain. An elder may no longer travel far, yet can tell from the sound under a floor which channel has opened and where someone coming home should turn.

Not everyone agrees about the city’s constant repairs. A shopkeeper argues for raising a threshold; the neighbors below argue that this will send the next flood through their doors. Crews work while customers complain around them. By evening, the new stone is wet, the argument has moved elsewhere, and feet have already begun to wear a path across it.

At night the long-backed animals come up beside the food markets. Someone always leaves scraps where they can reach them; someone else always curses the practice. Above them, in sleeping chambers and rooms stacked to the roofs, people lie down to the pounding of pumps. Some can tell when a familiar machine stops even in their sleep. They wake, listen for the next sound, and decide whether to get up.
