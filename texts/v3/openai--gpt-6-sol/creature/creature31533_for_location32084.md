At first, what lives here seems to be all the ordinary things that disappear when approached. Beetles turn in the damp roots. Frogs sit with only their eyes above a pocket of rainwater. A small bird drops into the sedge and, without leaving it, becomes impossible to find.

Stay long enough, and the floor begins to seem less like a covering over the hollow than its chief inhabitant. The roots and moss are not joined into one body, but they hold one another so closely that a footfall passes through them as a single, slow yielding. A pressed place rises again. Water caught in one depression darkens the moss around it; a few days later that dark patch has shrunk, and green has advanced into it from every side. Nothing travels far. Everything makes room.

The creatures that live among the stems know the small differences in this surface. Beetles take the drier passages beneath old roots. Frogs return to hollows that still hold water after the others have emptied. Birds arrive at places that look, to a person standing above them, exactly like everywhere else. Their comings and goings give the clearing its brief, scattered motions. Most of its life is slower: roots gripping roots, moss taking up dampness, sedge bending down and lifting again.

Beyond the trees, the works keeps up its immense, even sound. Here it is only sound. Beneath it, the old river bend goes on holding what little water it receives and sheltering what can live close to the ground. No current crosses it now, yet the life in the hollow still gathers in its shallowest lines, following the shape the river left.
