At first, the clean panels seem to belong to the machinery alone. Then a grey line breaks away from a seam and travels against the current. It is an animal, flat enough to disappear beneath the lip of a plate, with fins spread wide against the floor. Others come loose after it. From above they look like scraps of the film gathering around the bolts.

They live where the floor interrupts itself. Their mouths rasp at the weed on sheltered joints, and they pick small animals from the silt caught in drain slots. When the pumps run steadily, they are difficult to find. Each holds to the metal with a broad underside, its body trembling in the flow. The larger fish in the trenches sometimes pull one free; more often they pass over them without noticing.

The seam-dwellers notice the gates long before the warning lights come on. A change in vibration sends them out across the panels, not away from the opening but toward its edges, where the first rush of water will scour food from the floor. They cross open ground in brief, low bursts and settle again. A swimmer who follows them can watch the coming current travel as a succession of small bodies lifting and fastening down.

Their young shelter in the empty mounting frames and among the untidy first growth on new welds. Some frames fill with them for a season, then go bare when a crew installs the missing part. The adults return to older seams, though those are worked clean more often. After a gate closes and the silt settles, they emerge one by one into the lamp light, feeling along the bolts for anything the water has left behind.
