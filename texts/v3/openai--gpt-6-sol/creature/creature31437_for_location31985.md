The largest inhabitants stand beneath the western cliff. Their fronds are black and taller than the little slate walls, but scarcely rigid. Each leaf is divided into threads so fine that the current passes through without tearing it. When the distant fall grows stronger, the whole stand bends in one direction; when the ice cracks, a shiver travels through it though the plants have heard nothing. Mineral grains catch on the threads. Older fronds carry a faint, rough crust, and eventually their weight draws them down against the stone.

The plants at the warm fissure live closer together. Their round leaves overlap, and new stems push up through gaps left by old ones. From a distance—when the ice briefly gives enough light for there to be a distance—they look like one low, dark mound. They are not one plant. Their roots compete along the same narrow source of warmth, and at the mound’s outer edge a stem can spend years extending toward bare gravel without finding enough to sustain its next leaf.

The pale columns elsewhere are roots. They rise from the floor and divide again and again on their way to the ice, where their leaves spread flat against its underside. A leaf may remain there through many seasons of cracking and repair. Sometimes the roof takes it in as it thickens; sometimes a change in the water peels it away. It makes a long descent, recognizable all the way down.

Nothing moves between these territories to tend them. A new stand begins when a fragment catches in a seam where the mountain has started releasing what it needs. For a while it is no more than a dark thread in the rock. Then the current shapes it, and the bare place slowly acquires an edge.

The sprig in the third stone bowl has found such a seam, or perhaps only the small store of minerals the bowl has gathered. Its leaves extend over the rim into the passing water. The other two bowls hold silt. Whatever stacked the slate walls and set the stones toward the drop-off is not among the living things here now. The path remains, ending where the shelf ends, while the little plant grows across a doorway too low for anyone to enter standing.
