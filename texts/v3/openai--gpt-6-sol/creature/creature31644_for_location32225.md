The people who live here know one another first by sound. In a passage too narrow to see around the next turn, a particular scrape of basket means the fish seller is coming; a double tap against shale means someone is testing a loose step. They call before entering the landing, not out of ceremony but because there may already be three people there.

At first light, someone in each house takes a hooked stick to the gutter. They lift out the blue silt while it is still soft and carry it to a wall that needs filling. Children learn the stone mouths under the landing by counting them with their fingers. An older child can tell from the sound of water in a passage which mouth is beginning to close, though the younger ones still run to look. If a doorway floods, its occupants clear the gutter themselves, then ask who upstream let theirs fill.

Their hands are seldom clean for long. One woman dries fish in a recess made by two leaning houses; when the air is too wet, she moves the racks a hand’s breadth at a time, following the little draft. A man keeps spare roof ties tucked above his door so he can reach them from outside when the rushes come loose. In the evening, people work clay into seams while they talk to whoever is working on the other side of the same wall. Sometimes they argue about whose repair made a shelf tilt. Often neither can be sure.

There is little room to keep anything that has no use, but people do. A smooth white stone sits beside one threshold, moved back into place whenever the step sinks. Someone has hung a string of pierced shale under an eave, where it gives a faint rattle when the air changes. In the sleeping recesses are dry rushes, folded cloth, jars set where no one will kick them in the dark.

The long-legged bird lives here too, in its fashion. It follows the shallows beyond the last house, stopping where the stream leaves small things exposed in the mud. People give it room when they go to cut rushes. By morning its tracks have often appeared beside their own on the new bank, all of them still holding water.
