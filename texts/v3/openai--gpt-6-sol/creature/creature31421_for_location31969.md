At first, the crack seems to belong to the bats. They hang so thickly in the upper hollows that their bodies soften the stone’s outline. A wing opens to let another bat pass, then folds; a head turns toward a falling drop. Even at rest, the colony is never quite still. Those nearest the warm joints press into them, while others edge along the wall looking for a place that holds the heat as well.

The dark birds keep closer to the pale strip of sky. They cling to holds too slight to see from below and quarrel in short, rough calls. At dusk they launch into the opening just as the bats begin to descend. For a few moments both kinds of wing cross the narrow air. Then the birds are out over the mountain, the bats follow into the dark, and the crowded walls look almost bare.

That is when the smaller residents become easier to notice. A beetle pushes through the grit beneath a roost. A cricket stops moving at the faint scrape of a spider above it. Beside a rain cup, a frog no larger than a thumb waits with its belly against damp stone. It may stay there long after the cup is nearly dry. When an insect comes close enough, there is one quick movement, and then it is waiting again.

None of them has much space, and none lives only here. Wings carry food in from beyond the summit; rain briefly fills the cups; warmth and moisture rise from deeper in the rock. What lives in the crack has learned its brief openings and dependable seams. In the long dim hours between departures, the place is full of small adjustments: a bat shifting its grip, a bird defending a perch, a frog turning toward the sound of a drop.
