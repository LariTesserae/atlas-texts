The people here know the sound of water changing course. Someone carrying kindling stops at a new trickle across a step, sets down the bundle, and pulls a handful of leaves from the channel with two fingers. No one remarks on it. At the spring, a woman fills a jug from the drinking trough while a child waits with a cup, watching a water strider work the quieter surface of the overflow. Down in the square, wicker is mended beneath an awning whose posts are living hazel; the worker has to reach around a branch that has thickened since the awning was built.

There is little keeping the other inhabitants out. Hens know which doors open onto kitchens. Small birds take fruit from the tree and shelter under the eaves between raids. Swallows make quick, perilous passages through the lanes; beetles blunder into rooms and are turned loose again. The frogs in the wall are seldom seen, but their calls travel through the wet stone even after the rain stops. Beneath the flat paving, roots and small burrowing things occupy the ground the townspeople walk on.

No one seems surprised by all this company. They guard the clean trough from fouling and the seedlings from hens, but leave the fruiting tree full of birds. When a stair shifts, they mend its footing; when a root grows across the way, they step over it until the path needs changing. The town’s residents are busy with their own work, yet much of that work is making room—for water to pass, for a roof to hold, for everything rooted or winged to go on living close by.
