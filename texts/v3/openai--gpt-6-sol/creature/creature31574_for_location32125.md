At first, it is easy to count the plateau’s inhabitants incorrectly. There are the hoofed animals, the lizards, the birds, the hunter at the basin’s edge. Then a patch of shade comes loose from an upright stone and walks away.

It is a low, broad creature, no larger than one of the silver shrubs. Its back is pale and ridged like rock left bare by a receding pool. Beneath it, six narrow legs carry it across the shelf with a deliberate, almost soundless gait. In full sun the ridges lift slightly, exposing dark folds of skin; in shade they close again. It can remain still for hours beside a ledge, and often does.

What brings it out is water too slight for most animals to drink. Near dawn, it presses the soft underside of its head to a damp hollow and holds it there. Later it follows a mineral stain across dry stone, stopping where the stain seems to end. There it scrapes gently with its forefeet. A lizard will sometimes hurry over to the place it has opened and find the coolness first.

Several live within reach of each enduring basin, though they seldom gather at the water. One may spend a morning clearing grit from the grooves of an old ring while another works beneath an arch far across the shelf. Their movements are so slow that the insects walk over their backs. Now and then one rises on its legs and faces another across the open stone. Nothing audible passes between them, but after a while one turns aside, and the other continues along the track.

They do not appear to have made the arches. They attend to them as they attend to natural seams: removing sand, easing loose roots away from a groove, pressing a damaged edge with the damp pads of their feet. A young one, small enough to fit within a hoofprint, trails an older creature through these tasks. It has no patience for the work yet. It stops to watch birds and reaches into pools after insects, withdrawing its foot each time with evident surprise.

When a column of rain crosses the valley, the creatures leave their separate places. They arrive at the shining shelf from every direction and stand beneath the runoff, backs open, while water streams between their ridges. The hoofed animals come down to drink among them. By the next bright morning the gathering has dispersed. A little new mud has been tucked against the roots of an exposed tree, and one fine groove beneath the arch runs clear again.
