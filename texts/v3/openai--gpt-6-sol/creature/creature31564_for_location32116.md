At first, the city seems to be inhabited by people who measure it. Before the first trains, surveyors stand in the rain with their hoods drawn tight, checking marks on steps and walls. They record a building that has lifted a finger’s width, a door that has begun to catch, a new seam in the road. By breakfast, someone will have adjusted the footings or shaved the threshold. Most residents never see this work. They notice only when a familiar route feels slightly different underfoot.

Others spend their mornings looking for water. A wet patch beside a tram shelter may come from rain that fell there an hour ago, or from a basin miles away. The water crews keep maps of its known passages, annotated with dates and guesses. They know which grates attract the grazers, which hillside seeps freeze first, and which dry-looking cracks should not be crossed after three days of rain. Their maps are useful and never quite current.

People live in the districts much as people do anywhere: above shops, beside relatives they visit too seldom, within hearing of a neighbour’s early alarm. Children ride the covered trains to school and learn to judge distances from travel time rather than sight. From a classroom window, they can see a tower in another district; on a field trip, reaching it takes most of the morning. They dare one another to go out to the survey fences, where the new marks make an orderly path across ground their parents still call unsettled.

The wide spaces are inhabited too. The white birds crowd the viaduct ledges so closely that the stone appears edged with frost. They have learned the train timetable and rise just before its rush reaches them. Grazers move through the open tracts in small, enduring groups, nosing grass from pockets of soil and waiting at the water grates. A fox may trail a herd for hours, then leave it to hunt among the sheds. People who work at those sheds find tracks at their doors in the morning, though the animals are seldom visible while anyone is watching.

In the newer districts, residents speak of a block as young even when its buildings are already occupied. They watch how it fares through its first winters: where ice persists, whether a floor stays level, whether rain returns from beneath it. Some keep a pencil line low on a wall to mark the slow change in height. Others dislike making a record of what they cannot feel from one day to the next.

After dark, lit rooms hang far apart across the stone. A person waiting for the last tram can see many windows and know no one in any of them. Then someone else arrives under the shelter, shaking rain from a sleeve. The birds stir above the line; water ticks from the roof. For a few minutes, that small patch of ground is where the city lives.
