The people live close together, but not for warmth. The air is warm, and their houses hold damp rather than heat. They live close enough to share a wall, a cistern, a call for help across the noise. In the lanes, someone holds a fish steady on a flat stone while another draws its skin away in one piece. Children carry shells to the channels and learn which pools remain fresh enough to drink. No one steps through a doorway without looking down: water gathers there, and the shells set in the threshold are slick.

Their work has made them quick with things that will not last. A fish is eaten, dried, or cut for bait; its bones become points and pins. Skins stretch over boat frames and roofs. Cord is checked whenever the wind rises, and a sound roof is noticed by how little it lets through when rain begins. The stone lasts longer. People fit it to their hands, to the lanes, to the shallow routes by which water reaches the cisterns. They know which hollows collect rain and which wet places are fed from below.

The birds are the town’s other great population. They dispute the roof edges, foul the slabs, and watch every cleaning place for scraps. A person can wave them back without stopping work. Farther out, seals crowd the spit, lifting their heads to bellow above the water. Crabs withdraw beneath the landing steps when the boats come in, then feel their way out again among the wet stones.

At low water, the boat crews wade a long distance before they can float their craft. From the houses, they remain visible as small moving figures against the pale lagoon. Those at home keep splitting, tying, scraping, carrying water. By the time the crews return, birds will be waiting along the roofs, and someone will have made room on the stones for whatever they bring back.
