The papery growth is easiest to find where a rib has turned recently. It clings to the side now facing away from the sun, though no sheltered side existed there yesterday. Its leaves are thin enough to show the stone beneath them. In the brief warmth of midday they lift a little; when powder passes overhead, they fold flat before any grain touches them.

The pale animals feed there. Each is scarcely longer than a hand, with broad feet that spread its weight over the packed powder. On the ribs they move quickly, putting their noses into the dark growth. On an open flat they proceed a few steps at a time and wait. They are not listening for a crack—the air carries too little sound—but feeling through their feet for the first slight looseness in the ground. Some freeze for hours. One may be visible in the same spot at noon and near dusk, while the rib it was travelling toward has quietly come closer.

They make their nests in narrow seams, lining them with scraps of the papery growth. A seam may close with a family inside and open again farther along the rib. The animals emerge hungry, powder-white, and apparently unsurprised. More dangerous are the seams that open only partway. The ribs sometimes hold a small pale body as firmly as they hold the dark leaves.

There is something else at work beneath them. You can see where a rib has separated and settled: its broken ends become blunt, its pale face weathers, and the low branches regain the spacing of branches that have lain undisturbed for centuries. The movements are vast, but they seldom pile stone high or leave a hollow for long. Across distances too great to watch at once, the plateau keeps making room for itself.

Perhaps this is how its inhabitant grows. Not upward, where there is too little air and almost nothing to catch, but outward through the old bands of mineral, easing one length beside another. Its changes travel as cracks. Its healed places look ancient because most of it is ancient. A pale animal can spend its life on a single rib without ever crossing the whole of the thing it lives upon.

The black masts are harder to account for. Powder gathers against a fallen one, then passes on without filling the space beneath it. When the horizon bends between two standing masts, the animals stop even on firm stone. Afterwards they test the ground afresh. The papery leaves, which usually know the sun’s direction, may remain folded until morning.

At night the flats show no sign of occupation. There are only the low ribs, the leaning masts, and occasional tracks running out into emptiness. By the next evening the tracks are gone. The powder has not blown into them; it is packed as tightly as before. What lay beneath those little feet is no longer quite the same stretch of ground.
