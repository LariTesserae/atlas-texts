The large animals are difficult to count because they spend so much time resembling the rock. A feeding one may hold the same posture through a change of weather, its broad feet spread over a seep, its head lowered until the jaw plates touch the crust. Only the slow working of those plates distinguishes it from a boulder. When it lifts its head, a dark, damp mark remains on the stone.

Up close, its hide is not smooth. Shallow seams run from the throat along the flanks and down the legs. Moisture darkens them before it appears anywhere else on the body. Beneath them, fluid moves under pressure, and a faint pulse passes from one wet seam to the next. The animal takes in lichen, grit and brine together. Much of what it swallows comes out unchanged; the useful fraction is small enough that feeding occupies most of its life.

The herds are loose even at water. Each animal leaves room for the next one to set down its pads without breaking the thin crust over a seep. An old grazer will wait a long time behind a smaller one. The smaller animal, in turn, may leave its mouth against the rock after it has stopped grinding, taking in the last film of water. Nothing drives the waiting animal forward. Its feet remain planted, and the pulses under its hide continue at their slow interval.

The young are easier to notice. Their pads are pale and too large for their legs, and they place them carefully in the prints of an adult. They cannot make much use of dry stone. For years they travel between the sparse pockets that stay wet, scraping lichen softened by older mouths. In a line crossing open ground, each youngster keeps close behind one body, though several adults may be passing. If that adult stops, the youngster stops too.

A ridge tremor can travel farther than a call would in the thin air. One animal rests a pad flat against the stone; another, far down a channel, raises its head. The response may take hours to become visible. A line that was heading toward a dry hollow gradually bends toward water, each animal turning where it reaches the same stretch of rock. At other times a whole basin goes on feeding while rain falls beyond its lip. By the time the first animals arrive there, little may remain but damp cracks.

They are numerous because they live far apart and make very little haste. Across miles of bare ground, one slowly empties a pocket of brine while another reaches one that has had a season to fill. Their paths cross and separate over years. From the rim the lines can look purposeful, almost made by someone who knows the plateau. On the ground, there are only animals finding enough to keep going, and stone that holds the trace of where they went.
