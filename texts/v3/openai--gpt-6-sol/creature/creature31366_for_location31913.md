What lives between the walls is difficult to see because most of it is not in one place at once.

A narrow body lies on the unseen surface midway across the gap. It is clear and flattened, with a fringe of fine hairs along either side. The hairs catch what the current brings—clay grains, shed leaf skins, the smaller things that feed among the moss—and pass it slowly toward a dark seam underneath. At the far ends of the gap, the same body touches both pebble walls. There it is no thicker than a root hair. Near the middle it has room to bend.

It does not swim. Once in a while it draws itself up from the surface, and the leaves on one side lean toward it. Much later, leaves on the other side lean away. This is how it travels: not across the water, but through it, letting one part of itself settle while another releases its hold. A clay grain may come to rest on its back for hours. By morning the grain has moved the width of a stem.

The pebble walls are its work, though they were not built to keep anything in. Each flat stone marks a place where it once reached the edge of what it could feel. As the distance grew, it laid another stone farther on. The roots found their way through the courses afterward. The creature leaves them undisturbed; their tips turn when they meet its body, and sometimes follow its fringe for a little way before finding clay again.

The dull tap comes from deep in one wall. The creature tightens at that sound. All along its length the hairs lie flat, and the dark seam closes. It remains so while the suspended grains descend through the stems. Then the hairs lift, one region after another, until it is feeding again.

It has never reached beyond the two walls. From above, its whole country would disappear beneath a fingernail. Between the stones, it has yet to find the other side.
