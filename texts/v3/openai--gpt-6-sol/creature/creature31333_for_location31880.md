The people of the ridge are easiest to see when they stop swimming. In motion, their narrow bodies resemble the shadows of the long leaves; at rest, their hands give them away. They have broad palms and fine, strong fingers, well suited to teasing a stem free of its neighbors without uprooting either one. Faint gill folds open and close below their ears. Children’s folds are pale and conspicuous. On older people they are darkened by years of silt and sun.

They do not spend much time inside their houses. A house is a place to sleep out of the open water, to keep tools where they can be found, or to sit with someone who is ill. Most work happens in the round clearing. There, one person splits fibers while another holds the strands taut; a third watches the baskets and sorts the shoots that must be planted from those that can be eaten. They talk as they work, striking a doorpost or stone when they need to be heard across the ridge. Each household has a familiar rhythm. Even the youngest children know whose knock is whose.

They are careful gardeners, though little of their care looks like tidying. They remove a stem when it blocks a lane, and train its replacement toward a wall or roof. They leave snails on the houses unless a wall has grown thin. When a lashing loosens, someone usually notices the crustacean feeding there before they notice the knot. Repairs begin with a new strand of fiber and a patient search for somewhere living to fasten it.

The spring belongs to everyone. People passing over the square dip into its cool flow and let it run across their gills. Those who have been working in the thick beds beyond the lanes linger there longer, washing grit from their mouths and the joints of their hands. Baskets are counted at the edge of the clearing, where nothing set down can vanish among the leaves.

They know the heavy fish by their routes, and give way without alarm. A broad shadow crossing the ridge may interrupt a conversation but seldom ends it. What worries them more is a patch of plants that has stopped growing, or a lane that must be cleared twice in one season. Then several people go together to look, following the stems down to the silt with their hands.

From above, none of this is visible. The dark stitches are roofs, doorways, people pausing to speak. Between them the lake keeps growing, and the people keep making just enough room to live in it.
