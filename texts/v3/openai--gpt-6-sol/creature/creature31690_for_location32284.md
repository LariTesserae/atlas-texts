The frogs live closest to the channels. Their backs are the color of wet mortar, so that a frog beneath a reed bridge may look like an extra knot in the binding until it blinks. They sit with their throats drawn tight and their feet spread against the clay. They do not call, though now and then one swells as if preparing to. A person following the water may see the same frog at several crossings. It always appears settled, with no sign of having hurried.

Bright insects live in the reed knots and under the raised edges of tiles. Some are no larger than the thumbprints in the clay. They test the channels with their forelegs and climb down to drink, their colors briefly doubled in the water. Long-legged birds inhabit the garden baskets. They step between crowded stems without bending them and stand for hours with one foot hidden among the leaves. When they open their beaks, their bodies make the familiar shape of a bird calling, but the sound does not arrive.

The plants are the place’s most restless inhabitants. Vines take the ladders provided for them, then fasten themselves to the backs of the ladders as well. Roots enter mortar, reedwork, and the narrow dampness beneath paving. A shoot under one tile has spent longer than memory pushing it upward. Its pale tip is always newly exposed; the tile is always lifted the width of a fingernail. Elsewhere, leaves continue to uncurl and water continues to gather at their points. None of it looks stunted. The gardens are lush with growth that has found no need to end.

Someone else lived here recently enough to leave a cord beside the bowls. Whoever made the paths pressed clay around reed bindings with bare thumbs, fitted moss into upturned pots, and set the bowls where roof water would fall. The work is careful but not yet worn by use. There may be a maker somewhere among the distant gardens, out of sight behind the leaves. Nothing here proves they have gone. Nothing suggests they are on their way back, either.

At the central platform, the inhabitants gather without quite meeting. Insects cross the bark roof. A bird watches from a basket rim. A frog waits in the channel below. Overhead, water hangs from a shingle toward the bowl that holds only one drop. The drop grows heavy, and for a moment every small creature seems to inhabit the space before it falls.
