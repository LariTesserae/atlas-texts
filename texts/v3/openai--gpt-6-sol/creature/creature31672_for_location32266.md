The first inhabitant you are likely to notice is the small six-legged animal. It stops at each seam and tests it with two delicate forefeet before putting its weight down. Its mouth is narrow enough to reach the soft wrapping of an old root without damaging the root itself. When a tile shifts, these animals arrive quickly, though it is hard to see how they know. They disappear into gaps too shallow to admit a finger and emerge several rows away with pale insulation caught in their jaws.

The people of the plain are harder to notice. A worker standing still among the leaves can be mistaken for a loose bundle of stems: tall, jointed, and the same dull green beneath the glare. Their shoulders carry overlapping plates made from the season’s growth, replaced as they wear. Their hands are narrow and remarkably strong. One can hold a rising tile in place while another eases a root into the socket beneath it, then lower the tile so gradually that the root is not bruised.

They spend much of their time listening. A worker will kneel with one hand on the floor and remain there through several turns of the leaves. They are feeling for strain in the courses below: a panel about to lift, a channel beginning to close, a root pressing where no socket was prepared. Repairs are seldom urgent. The floor gives warning years in advance, and the workers have learned to answer at the floor’s pace.

In the bright hours they fasten themselves to shaded root beds by thin cords at their wrists. They rest there while the leaves turn, drawing a little charge and salt through the cords. Even asleep, they wake when a loaded frame passes nearby. Its soft tracks have a rhythm they know as readily as a voice.

Their black stakes are not warnings in the usual sense. The workers use them to remember which passage a corridor has become. Before entering an aisle marked by one stake, a worker touches its dark side and looks along the leaves, checking the direction of their slow turn. Someone who ignores the mark may walk safely all afternoon and arrive behind the place where they began. The six-legged animals seem untroubled by this. They are often found at the far end of an aisle before the workers who set out to follow them.

At the shadowless strip, the workers make the newest part of the plain. They carry panels outward and take old leaves back to the grinders, leaving no bare course open longer than they must. Seen close, their work is full of small accommodations: a socket moved aside for a widening root, a worn plate kept because something nests beneath it, a new row set a fraction off true so that the leaves above it have room to turn. From a height these choices vanish into the enormous worn floor. Underfoot, they are everywhere.
