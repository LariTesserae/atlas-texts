At first there seems to be nothing in the recess but the grass. Then the valve turns, and something draws itself out of the seam beside it.

It is narrow enough to fit where the concrete meets the floor. In the lamp’s light, its back looks like a fold of wet grey cloth; outside the light, it is difficult to distinguish from the joint. It has many fine limbs, though it rarely uses them all at once. Some hold fast to the rough edge of the concrete while others reach into the water crossing the ribs. It catches what the drain would otherwise take: a grain of grit, a thread shed from someone’s sleeve, the husk of an insect carried down from above.

When the pumps climb to speed, it presses itself flat. The grass goes down with it. For a while the two look like part of the same small accident of shelter and dirt. When the rail stops shivering, the blades rise first. Then the grey back lifts, slowly, until its limbs can feel the water again.

It has lived through the pouring of the walls. There is no old ground for it to return to, and it has never tried the open ledge. Here, between a working drain and a seam that keeps a little grit, it has enough room to turn.
