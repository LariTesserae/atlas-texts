At first, the inhabitants seem to be the tall frames. Each stands apart like a plant that has grown into the shape of a building, with broad leaves below it to catch whatever falls from its roof. But the water that leaves one frame is caught by another, and the ribs beneath your feet run on toward both. It becomes difficult to tell where one plant ends.

Perhaps there is only one.

Most of its body lies open to the sky. The shining floor is its skin: leaves fitted together so tightly that rain cannot reach anything beneath them. The ridges are thickening ribs; the channels and high gutters carry water across distances too great for any single stem. Its halls stand empty because they are not shelters. They are organs held up in the weather, built to receive a downpour and pass it on.

When rain arrives, the valley changes shape around it. Roofs bow under the weight. Chutes tighten and pour. A column splits, and both halves reach neighboring ribs rather than falling; an opening appears where there was none before. After the storm, as heat lifts vapor from the seams, water continues to travel through hidden joints. Some gutters close while still full, sending their contents down a different route.

There are no calls from the open halls, no movement crossing the spaces between them. The movement belongs to the place itself. By afternoon, a passage you walked through in the morning may have narrowed or vanished. The broad empty stretches remain, though. Whatever lives here leaves room for the next dark band of rain to cross the valley—and for its own great frames to catch it.
