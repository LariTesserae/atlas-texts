The people here learn the mountain first by its sounds. A full cistern has a different echo from one that is merely deep; a pipe beginning to fur has a thin whistle at the bend. Children sent to fetch water pause on the stairs and call down into the dark, listening for the answer. By the time they are old enough to work, many can tell which gate has been opened without going below to look.

They are not all keepers of the water. There are bakers who rise while the roofs are still dry, seamstresses whose windows mist from within, and carters who know exactly where a loaded wheel will slip on moss. But nearly every household has some small duty to the system: a grate to clear, a gutter to turn, a mark on a wall to watch. When a bank of cloud approaches from below, people step out onto footbridges to judge its height. They speak briefly from bridge to bridge, then go in to move what needs moving.

At the downhill works, the tank tenders wear wool under waxed coats. Their hands smell of iron even after washing. They know the mills by temperament—one belt that wanders in damp weather, one pump that must be allowed its pause between strokes. In the older houses, families still keep shallow bowls beneath particular ceiling seams. The drops are clean, and it would seem wasteful to let them fall on the floor.

Other things live in the town more quietly. The little trees in the yards grow around abandoned hooks and axle rings, holding them in their trunks. Pale insects gather beneath cistern lids, safe from the wind. Moss advances across a doorstep over many years, then is cut back in an afternoon, leaving a green outline that shows where the stone has stayed wet. No one mistakes these lives for part of the machinery, though the drains would behave differently without them.

And below the streets, in passages few people enter now, there are fish in at least two of the oldest cisterns. No one agrees how they arrived. They are small, nearly colorless, and come to the surface only when a lamp is held still for a long time. The workers who check those chambers lower the lamp gently. They take their measurements, note the level, and leave the fish to the slow dark water.
