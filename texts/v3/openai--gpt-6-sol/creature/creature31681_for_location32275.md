The broad columns are not trees in the usual sense. Each is a place where the valley has gathered itself upright: wood laid over wood, with a living core that draws water from the tangled floor and sends it along pale veins beneath the bark. Some columns stand straight for centuries. Others lean until their crowns meet the ground, take root there, and continue growing in a new direction. The fallen ones are seldom dead all the way through.

Most of the valley lives between them. Dark leaves overlap so closely that frost can remain beneath one while sunlight warms the next. Fine stems bridge the hollows, die, and become a bed for finer stems. At the edges of a newly opened gap, shoots that have waited for years begin to straighten. By dusk, the light may belong to something else.

The belts carry more than seed husks. A split cup releases its stored rain into a descending vein; elsewhere, pressure lifts a little of that water uphill. Seeds with thick shells travel until a belt frays or a column falls, dropping them into the bare, damp interior of a break. What looks like tireless work is also a way of surviving damage. The valley is always losing pieces of itself, and always growing across the losses.

In the brief spaces between moving roots lives the white creature. It is no bigger than a hand, with a blunt face and toes broad enough to spread its weight over the stem mats. Its pale coat is dense and dry even in rain. It drinks from leaf cups and eats the soft tissue exposed where a vine belt turns around a pulley. It is quick, but seldom has far to go: when one gap closes, another is opening nearby. Sometimes its tracks end at an unbroken wall of roots, as though the valley had put them away.

There are other inhabitants too, though many are difficult to distinguish from the place that feeds them: translucent threads inside the wet bark, minute dark bodies gathered in leaf cups, silver growth on wood newly exposed to air. None occupies the valley for long without becoming part of its movement. A resting surface rises. A shelter folds shut. Even the largest columns, so regularly spaced from the ridge, are only pauses in a living shape that never quite holds still.
