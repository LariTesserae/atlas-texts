At first it is reasonable to think the shelled animals are visitors. They appear only at the drains, and never for long. But one, if left undisturbed, will come all the way out. Its shell is flat enough to pass beneath a chip of rock, dark as wet shale except at the rim, where years of rubbing have exposed a pale band. Beneath it is a soft, almost colorless body with two short feelers and a pair of fine limbs. It pauses at every crossing, pressing its shell against the stone.

There are others in the walls. They live behind the fitted slabs, in spaces the streets were built to connect. The open chambers are workshops, not homes. At certain hours they emerge to shave mineral from a drain, clear grit from a groove, or wedge a bridge more firmly into place. They work slowly, carrying a flake of stone between two of them when one cannot manage it alone. The cups turned upside down keep their hollows clean until they are needed to catch a drip or hold a pinch of scraped crust.

They know the town by its sound. Against a wall, a shell receives the roar from below and the smaller notes made by water passing through the streets. A blocked channel changes a doorway’s pitch; a loose bridge adds a rattle. One of them may remain motionless under an arch for a long time, listening, before turning toward a drain that has only just begun to narrow.

They rarely gather in the central passage. There is room for so few at once, and they prefer to meet where a wall can carry the faint taps of their shells. The town seems empty because most of its life takes place on the other side of what can be seen. Now and then one crosses an opening, stops at the wet edge, and goes back to its work.
