At first, the only living things seem to be the green threads on the youngest ridges. They have no leaves. Each is a narrow filament fastened to a seam in the stone, making what it can of the light the water brings down to it. When a ridge rises, some are pulled apart. Others remain attached and climb with it, bent almost double in the current.

Farther out, something harder to recognize persists in the empty water.

A pressure wave passes through a region where two currents meet. Most of the wave travels on, but a little of it turns back. Light takes the same bend. For a while, that patch of sea keeps a shape: a broad, wavering fold with no boundary you could touch. It moves without drifting away. When the currents tear across it, the fold comes apart—and forms again a short distance downstream.

There are many such folds, though they are seldom alike for long. One holds beside a rising wall of stone until the wall splits. Another follows the cold sheet thrown off by the fracture, changing shape as it goes. They draw nothing from the sparse growth on the ridges. What sustains them is the continual meeting of water, light, and force. In a calmer sea they would fade.

They do not call, and there is little reason to think they know one another. Yet a fold can alter the current that reaches the next one. Across miles of apparently unoccupied water, small changes accumulate. A pressure wave from below may arrive at a ridge having been turned many times, striking its new edge gently or with enough force to strip it bare.

The threads live on the stone. These other things live only while the sea is moving. For now they are as unfinished as the mountains: recurring shapes that the water has not yet learned to keep.
