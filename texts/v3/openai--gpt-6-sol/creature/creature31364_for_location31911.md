The white grazers are the easiest inhabitants to see. They travel pressed flank to flank beneath the fronds, their bodies so soft that each one takes the shape of the leaf above it. A line will stop all at once, though no grazer at its rear could have seen what halted the first. After a pause, they turn and feed along a different vein. Behind them the leaf is thinner, but not stripped. New tissue shows through it within a day.

The silver seam-runners are less patient. They work through the films of water on jointed feet, vanishing under a root and emerging higher up on a hanging leaf. Their feet make much of the hollow’s faint ticking. When a drop swells too large to hold, several gather beneath it. They touch the water, and its fall is delayed; one by one they slip through its surface and leave the drop hanging.

Something heavier lives among the curtains. Its outline is never still enough to follow. At times it seems narrow and many-legged; at others, the leaves bow around a broad, slow back. It does not tear through the growth. It waits while roots loosen their hold, then passes into the space they make. The rasping of the grazers grows louder after it has gone by, as if they have found a newly exposed place to eat.

But the hollow’s most numerous inhabitants may be impossible to separate from it. The dark sheets between the roots tighten when the cold deepens. Blue points travel through stems and water films, and fronds fold before a fresh seam opens beneath them. The low pulse in the root beds quickens where rock has been exposed. Not every leaf moves with every other leaf; a patch of moss will resist the change, then slowly join it. Whatever is being passed between them takes time, and sometimes meets refusal.

There are small animals asleep inside the supple veins, each enclosed by a leaf that keeps ice on its outer edge. There are young roots feeling their way into stone and withdrawing from places that will not yield. There is the larger creature, somewhere in the green, settling its weight onto a floor that has risen since it last slept.

Nothing here appears finished with its neighbors. The grazers feed and leave room for regrowth; the roots shelter them and take the warmth of their crowded bodies. Even the hidden animal seems to belong to the hollow’s slow rearrangement. When it turns, leaves shiver for a long while afterward, and a blue point begins moving, stem by stem, toward the place where it will rest.
