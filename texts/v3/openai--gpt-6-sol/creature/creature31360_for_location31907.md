At first, the city seems to belong to the small shelled things. They occupy every warm surface the people leave exposed. Along the cistern walls they fit edge to edge, dark backs shining with condensation, their bodies hidden except for the thin working parts that rasp at the mineral seams. A person can tell whether an outflow is running by the sound they make around it. When the water stops, the scraping spreads outward, becoming faint and irregular as they search.

They are not always slow. A collector shedding its load sends a vibration through the shelf, and the nearest shells tighten against the stone at once. Later, when a little of that water reaches their wall, they begin moving toward it. Their tracks persist on a dry threshold long enough to guide the next procession across it. People step over these lines even when no animals are in sight.

Beneath the bridges live the folded ones. For weeks they could be mistaken for fittings: jointed shapes tucked into the undersides of spans, their claws hooked around metal. Wind makes them tick. A change in that ticking can send a bridge worker looking for a loose brace, though often the creatures have only turned themselves toward a warmer beam. No one knows from the street what they eat. Occasionally one unfolds in the cloud below a bridge, and those above hear its claws catch somewhere farther along.

The broad-winged animals are seen least clearly. They work their way around the collector towers without landing on them, banking into the cloud each time the shutters open. People crossing the upper walks sometimes stop when a pale shape appears beyond the guide marks. It may be a wing held level for a breath, or two passing at different distances; then the dark closes again. Whatever the animals find out there, they return to the towers.

The people live among these movements with a practised attention. They keep their hands off a cistern wall unless they have looked at it first. They know which grilles freeze early and which doorways the shelled processions favour after a shutter opens. On the hanging streets, they walk close enough to the amber marks to see the next one before leaving the last. Freight travels sealed away, but people still carry small things by hand, pausing for one another on stairs that disappear into cloud.

Their work shows in the details of the place: a cleared margin beside an outflow so an animal can pass without entering a doorway; a warm pipe guarded where it crosses a bridge; marks scratched into a crystal at a pier’s base, one winter above another. The marks are not measurements anyone needs that day. Someone returns to them because the pier is still growing, and because a change too slow to witness is easier to believe when it has a date beside it.

At night, a resident waking to a sudden quiet may lie still and listen for what has stopped—the cistern wall, the claws under a span, or a collector that ought to have opened. Then they get up to find out.
