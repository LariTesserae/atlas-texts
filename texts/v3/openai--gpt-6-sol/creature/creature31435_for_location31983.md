Most of the plateau’s inhabitants are rooted. Their holdfasts have worked into the warm stone so deeply that a frond torn away in a hard current may grow back from what looks like bare rock. The pale fans feed where water passes quickly between them. The rust-colored stems do better at the still edges of the old channels, where sand settles long enough to cover their lower branches. When a cold current crosses the plateau, both kinds bend flat, and for a while the lanes of bare stone grow wider.

The creatures among them are difficult to count. Some are no longer than a finger and nearly as thin as the leaves they shelter under. They have clear, jointed bodies with dark sediment visible inside. They travel along the undersides of fronds, stopping whenever the growth above them lights green; in that brief glow their outlines vanish. In the old channels they dig shallow pits, eat what gathers there, and leave before the next sheet of sand erases the pits.

Larger ones keep to the broken walls. They are low-bodied animals with several pairs of limbs, some made for gripping stone and others so fine they can comb food from a fan without tearing it. Their backs carry small gardens of living growth. An animal at rest can be mistaken for a slab whose joints have filled with weeds. They seldom leave an enclosure in daylight’s faint remainder, though daylight itself means little here. They come out when a cold current has passed and the stems are still bowed, moving from wall to wall across the exposed rock.

No one has seen them fit a slab into place. They do move the smaller stones. At one enclosure, a loose piece is always returned to the foot of the eastern wall, however often the current carries it away. Sand piles there as well. The animals burrow into it and emerge with their combing limbs full, while the ground on the other side of the wall stays clean.

They cross the plateau’s odd distances without apparent difficulty. A wall-dweller will disappear into a stand of fronds visible through a narrow gap and reappear, much later, from growth that seemed to lie only a few strokes away. Whether it has traveled farther than the eye could follow or found a shorter passage is hard to tell. Others linger at the mouths of channels before entering, feeling along the stone with their foremost limbs, then choose a direction.

Far beyond them, over the rim, the broad shadow lives in open water. It visits without descending. Its passage dims a long strip of fronds, which brighten faintly as the water disturbed beneath it reaches them. The little creatures hold still until the green fades. Then the combing, feeding, rooting, and quiet shifting of stones begin again.
