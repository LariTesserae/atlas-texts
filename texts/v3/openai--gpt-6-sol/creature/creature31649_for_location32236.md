The people of the basin are numerous, though from the slopes they can be mistaken for part of the stonework. They move along the lanes with a practiced turn of the shoulders, carrying water jars or bundles of thornwood close to the body. In a yard, someone kneels to reset a wall stone while someone else sorts the smaller pieces for a roof. A good fit matters more than a quick one. The walls must hold through wind and frost, and they must also be possible to take apart when the ground changes.

Children learn routes by more than their names. They know which corner stays icy after the others have thawed, which roof gutter spills in a hard rain, and which stretch of open scree is safe only while the sun is high. Older people remember lanes that no longer exist. They may give directions by them anyway—past the old turning, where a wall stood before the last fall—and a child who has never seen that wall knows where to go.

Water brings people together more reliably than a square or a meeting house. At the drinking cisterns, lids are lifted carefully and vessels are set down so grit will not fall in. Neighbors notice when a roof is failing to drain, or when a household has had to draw more than usual. Before the thaw, work parties clear the channels from one court to the next. Their arguments concern gradients, leaks, and who last repaired a particular joint; by the time the snow begins to run, most of them are working side by side.

Beyond the houses, shepherds accompany the sheep and long-haired goats over ground that offers little shade and less certainty underfoot. They return with weather on their clothing and news of the upper slopes: a new crack in a face of rock, a snow patch lasting longer than expected, a gully that should be avoided. The animals know the yards by smell. Goats push at gates; sheep stand packed together in the last warmth of evening. Roof birds quarrel over nesting places, while the small creatures of the rubble live among gaps too deep for a human hand.

At dusk, people come out briefly to feel whether a wall still holds heat, then go in and close the low doors. The basin seems quiet, but the quiet is full of attention: to a bell beyond the houses, to water ticking into a cistern, to the faint shift of loose stone somewhere above. They have made a crowded home here without ever expecting the mountains to leave it alone.
