At first, the ledge appears to belong to the ferns. They have claimed nearly every hollow deep enough to hold grit. But beside the turbine, where the pipe passes over its iron feet, something smaller lives among their stems.

It is no longer than a fingernail. Its back is dark and rough with grains it has gathered from the seep; its underside is pale enough for the faint movement within it to show through. Six short legs carry it across wet metal and moss, but seldom onto the bare stone. There, a body so small would dry quickly, even in this damp air. In the fern hollows, it can stop beneath a leaf and disappear.

Several live along the concrete footing. They feed on the thin green-brown film that grows where warm water runs over the mineral stains. Their paths are visible only when the film is new: narrow, wavering tracks that end at a crack or under a mat of moss. The youngest are nearly transparent and keep to the sheltered seam behind the housing. The older ones venture as far as the pipe supports, crossing the iron slowly, feeling ahead with their front legs.

The turbine passes through them as a continual shiver. They pause when its casing trembles harder, then go on. The valve is another matter. At the first sharp hiss, every one in sight presses flat to its surface. For a moment the footing seems empty. When the sound ends, a leg unfolds, then another, and the slow grazing resumes beneath the unchanged roar.

They have never spread across the whole ledge. The black gravel shifts too much, and most of the sharp new slabs offer neither food nor cover. After rain, a few are washed out among the stones. By the next clear afternoon they have either found the older, mossed rock or vanished.

The bird on the cable may see none of this. From above, the inhabitants are only flecks beside a pipe. Their part of the mountain is a few damp inches wide, warmed from below, shaken day and night, and renewed wherever the water leaves a stain.
