The people who live here are difficult to count from the road. There may be only a dozen in the yard on a working day, and fewer still when the weather turns dry. They spend much of their time out of sight: under the press-house floor listening to a bearing, on a ladder freeing a window frame from a new lip of wood, or down in a cistern with a lamp, clearing leaves from the intake. Their clothes are patched at the knees and pale with fibre dust. Most carry a knife, though they use it as often on their homes as on the crop.

The worker with the wrench returns from the cistern and pauses beneath the roof before going inside. A shoot has found the valve’s warm pipe and curled around it. She loosens it with her fingers, then ties it to a cooler branch. Cutting would be quicker, but in a few weeks that shoot may help hold the gutter where last winter’s weight pulled it loose. She goes in to the motor with rainwater still on her sleeve.

People here know the buildings by their tendencies. The press house closes its west windows first each spring. One of the smaller dwellings puts out roots beneath its own threshold, making a step that has to be shaved down every year. At the far end of the works, a length of roof has never thickened properly; in storms someone props it from below. These are not faults anyone expects to finish repairing. The oldest residents can point to work their grandparents did that is still alive, though it has moved several inches from where they put it.

The buildings live here too, less peacefully than they appear from the bank. Their leaves draw water from the channels, but their roots also meet brick, buried rail, and one another. A wall thickens and steals light from the drying racks. A gutter reaches toward the cistern but sends half a storm down the wrong side of the yard. The people trim, brace, and sometimes give way. In return they have shade over the rollers, walls that mend small wounds, and foundations that stay firm when a brief hard rain comes through.

In the dry months the workers leave more stems standing and take longer over each bundle. Evenings are quiet enough to hear the motor stop: the belt gives its last soft slap, and someone sets a cup upside down over the steam valve. The bird under the roof calls once or twice after that. By morning there may be drops on the gravel, and everyone will look up to see whether the newest shoots have reached their ties.
