The people of the hollow are seldom still, though they rarely appear hurried. One carries a plank under an arm on the way to fetch water. Another pauses mid-step to watch a dark line spread beneath the ice. Children learn which stones are safe by the sound they make underfoot, and are taught to leave a route for water before they are trusted to cross a new one. An elder can look at a wet threshold and name three houses that will need warning.

Their houses are small because the hollow is small, but much of their work happens between them. Neighbours keep one another’s spare wedges, help lift roof weights, and pass word by pointing from lane to lane. A bridge is not quite anyone’s property: its far end may belong somewhere different by evening. Drying racks stand high above the changing channels, hung with fur, cord, and things that must be brought indoors before the damp takes them.

The other creatures know the settlement just as closely. The white-throated birds follow newly opened water, stepping into a channel almost before the people have finished cutting it. Round-eared animals shelter where the house walls give off warmth. Beneath the pools, beetles gather in the dark; above them, a stoat hunts the roofs. The people make room for most of this life, but watch where they put a hand.

When a channel breaks toward a house, the nearest people come with planks and wedges. They work quietly, reading one another’s gestures as well as the water. Afterward, someone tests the new crossing and someone else marks the old one before snow hides it. No one expects the repair to last forever. It only has to hold while they live with what the hollow does next.
