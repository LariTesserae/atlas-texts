The first thing a visitor learns about the people is that they seldom put a full cup near the edge of a table. They leave room around it without seeming to think. A child born here learns to steady a saucer before learning why the saucer moves.

Most adults can feel a birth coming through their soles. It is not a gift so much as practice: a faint change in the pressure under one heel, or a tremor that lasts longer than a passing train. Yard crews stop their work and look at one another. Inside the mills, someone sets a hand against a wall. When the earth rises, they go out with stakes and notebooks to find its edge. They do not try to guess what the new patch is *for*. They need to know whether it will hold a wagon by morning.

They keep old measurements carefully, though few expect them to remain true. Surveyors’ children know the chimney marks by number. Masons can point to courses of brick laid by three generations of their family, each correcting for a different lean. The best builders are willing to return to a job: to loosen a joint, shorten a step, or raise a door that they once fitted perfectly. A house that has never needed repair is regarded with some suspicion.

Other residents are harder to count. Foxes sleep beneath loading platforms that shift too often for anyone to store goods there. Hares feed in the narrow green places along the electric lines, ears upright at sounds the workers cannot hear. In warm vents, pale birds bring up broods while starlings watch the yards from survey poles. When they all rise at once, people notice. A flock can give several seconds’ warning.

The plants make use of every interval the city leaves them. Willow roots enter a gap between paving stones and thicken until the stones stand apart. Ferns live on water gathered from factory breath. Moss grows over mortar, bolts, and the names stamped into old iron. Workers cut it away where they must see a crack, then let it cover the rest. Against the cold metal it is soft; in winter, small birds pick through it for insects.

No one has found a seed or a root in the gray clay when it first appears. It comes up bare, preserving the imprint of whatever lay above it. For days afterward, very little will grow there. Rain pools on its smooth surface, and even the foxes go around. Then the first dark specks gather in the hollows. A film takes hold. By the time willow seedlings arrive, the crews may already have laid a line across it, and the new ground has joined the crowded life of the yard.

At dusk, the people going home step over hoses and roots and the raised lips of old paving. Some carry bread, some tools, some a cutting rescued from a glasshouse trough. Behind them, a shift begins in the foundries. Ahead, their windows come on among the thickets, and the streets are nearly where they were that morning.
