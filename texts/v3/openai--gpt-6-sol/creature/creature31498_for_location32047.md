What lives here is easy to miss from the ridge because most of it has not grown tall. Moss occupies the hollows in the stone, dark with water; sedges stand in close, uneven company wherever a little silt has stayed put. Between their roots is a finer life still—a film on submerged gravel, green threads in the clear pools, the organisms that feed on them and return them to the soil. The valley’s thin ground is being made and held in the same places.

The plants do not cover it evenly. A cushion of moss swells around one sharp rock and leaves the next bare. Sedges lean with the flow where water passes through them, then rise a hand’s breadth away. Seeds caught among their stems may take hold there, sheltered from the current. Others wash on. From a distance this looks like a continuous green floor; close up, it is full of edges and small chances.

In the pools, tiny creatures move where the water is quiet enough to keep them. A larva clings to the underside of a stone. Minute swimmers vanish into the glare when a shadow crosses them. Small flies find the damp air above the sedges, and a spider can stretch a few strands between stems, though the next hard rain may take both web and anchors. Almost nothing here can count on its particular patch of ground remaining as it is.

The streams pass through all of this before they reach the grate. Water threads beneath the moss, emerges bright among the grasses, and sometimes carries a little of the living floor with it. Near the intake, the roar overwhelms the slight sounds of wings and running water; farther out, those sounds are still there beneath it. The people who maintain the track and machinery come and go. The valley’s other inhabitants stay close to the ground, holding, feeding, spreading, and beginning again wherever the water lets them.
