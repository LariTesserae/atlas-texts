The largest animals are easy to mistake for parts of the plateau. At rest, their backs lie low and level, plated in overlapping bands the color of old hematite. Salt collects in the seams. Wind lays black grit against their sides, and smaller creatures cross them without provoking a movement. Only the steady opening and closing of vents along the flanks gives them away. With each breath, a dark line appears in the dust; with each exhalation, the line vanishes under a little fall of warm, altered powder.

Their heads are narrow because they rarely need to look up. They press the lower jaw into a seam in the rock and advance by fractions, working it with a sound like a heavy door being pushed against its frame. What they take in is not always what they leave. Red shelves pale behind them. Salt crusts acquire a hard, bell-like underside. In some places they have made stone so friable that a footstep breaks through it; in others, they have sealed old cracks more firmly than the surrounding rock. They do not appear to choose between these results. A beast finds a seam and follows it, sometimes for months, until its body has described an entire slope.

The little animals under their plates are quicker and more particular. They fit inside a joint of the armor with their limbs folded close, then emerge to pick at the residue caught there. Their knocks can sound like loose machinery, but they are also how one announces itself to another before entering a narrow gap. When the great beast moves, they retreat upward, fastening themselves beneath the edges of the plates. A procession may carry whole clusters of them across a shelf; an animal working alone will have only a few small, patient shapes keeping pace in its shadow.

Others live out of sight in the pits. Their work is audible through the soles of your feet: a brief scraping, a pause, three hard taps. Dust sometimes rises from an empty hollow hours before anything emerges. At dusk a narrow head may appear there, followed by enough plated back to show that the pit is much larger than its opening.

None of them seems troubled when noon puts a sound in the wrong place. A small one will tap beneath a plate while its answer comes from across a gully; the great bodies continue to feed while their shadows move on a distant wall. Perhaps the animals have always known the plateau this way. They attend to the seam under their mouths, the weight of grit against their sides, the knocks that come after a tremor. By dark, when the shelves themselves are difficult to distinguish, those sounds are enough to tell that the place is crowded.
