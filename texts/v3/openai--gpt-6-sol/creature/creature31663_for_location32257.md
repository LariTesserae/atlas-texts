The town belongs to the plated animals, though at first they are easy to mistake for a pale crust left by the rain. Under an eave, their backs overlap like roof tiles. Along a pipe, the smallest fit so tightly into the opening that the drain seems to have grown teeth. Only when one lifts its head do the others become distinct: a row of flat mouths loosening from the brick, a ripple of legs making room, then every plate settling back into place.

They live at the edges of things. The long-legged ones wait where condensation gathers in the gutters, stepping delicately over one another to put their feet in the water. Others hold to the warm pipes and work at the scale with their teeth. The blunt-headed young stay in the drains, where water and shelter arrive together. They do not cross the streets often, but they watch anyone who does. Their attention follows a walker from one roofline to the next without a single animal needing to give chase.

Their rattling changes when rain begins. Plates lift, water reaches the dry seams beneath them, and a sound that seemed to come from the roofs passes down the walls. Afterward they crowd the troughs. One drinks, yields its place, and fastens itself nearby; another comes forward. Their pale waste hardens below the ledges built to receive it. In places it has been worn smooth again, though no shovel has touched it in years.

They have inherited more than the houses. The pumping hall gives them warm water, the brick gives them minerals, and their accumulated banks were once fed to the foundry. Now the wheel turns without workers, and the animals remain close to its pipes. Whether they notice its steady beat is hard to tell beneath their own noise. When it pauses between strokes, the roofs are still full of rattling.

On the outer houses, generations of passing shells have hollowed the doors at the same height. The animals that brush them today are small beside the town, but there are thousands of them, fitted into every place the town provides. From the open street, the buildings look deserted. From beneath an eave, there is scarcely room for one more.
