The longest-lived inhabitants hold on without being seen to hold at all. Cushion moss fills the shallowest hollows, swelling when cloud passes through and drawing tight again in sun. Ferns grow from cracks that look too narrow to admit a seed. In the wet seams, pale roots run along the stone until they find somewhere to turn downward. None of this growth covers much ground. Each patch has its own small boundary with the bare rock.

Beetles live on the drier side of those boundaries, under flakes of lichen and in the grit caught behind stones. The wet side belongs to tiny flies and other things that rise as soon as the cloud lifts. Small birds know the seams well. They come down lightly, take what the water has brought out, and vanish into a cleft before the wind has finished changing direction. The swifts belong more to the open air than to the hollow; their cries arrive and are gone almost with them.

A goat visits when it can. On the rim it looks substantial, with its weight set carefully over four small hooves. Then it steps down the cliff and becomes part of the stone again. It feeds where the sedge is thickest, lifting its head often—not always at a sound a person can hear.

Someone else has known this place closely enough to build a windbreak where drinking is easiest. The scoop remains, but there is no sign of how often its owner came, or whether they still do. Nothing has made a dwelling of the hollow in the way a person would mean it. Its residents occupy a crack, a tuft, a brief interval of shelter. When weather comes over the crest, the birds withdraw, the insects settle, and even the goat lowers itself out of the wind. Afterward, the moss opens again.
