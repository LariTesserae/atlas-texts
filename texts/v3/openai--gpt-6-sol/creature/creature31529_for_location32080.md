Most of the district’s living inhabitants are small enough to be mistaken for bits of moving silt. Pale prawns gather where the warm water comes through the grilles. They hold themselves in place with their hind legs and comb the current with their forelegs, turning slowly as a wheel turns behind them. In the quieter stairwells they grow darker, marked with the green of what they eat. A prawn crossing a gauge may pause on a number for an hour.

The weed has its own inhabitants. Nearly transparent snails travel along the ribbons in the mortar, leaving narrow clean paths that are soon overgrown. The oldest plants are down in the cable conduits, where their roots thicken around the wires and brick ties. When a pump starts to strain, its vibration passes through those roots. The leaves nearest the street tremble before anything audible changes.

People come to tend the pumps, but they are seldom visible from one street to the next. They move slowly, carrying their air with them, and work with a hand against the wall so they can feel which bearing has begun to roughen. They clear weed from a belt, free silt from a grille, or fit a thin new piece into a housing worn too wide for its wheel. Much of their work is done by touch. A loosened bolt clouds the water; a correctly seated one is felt as a steadier hum through the tool.

They know where the prawns gather, and leave those places alone when they can. They know, too, which doorways will close with weed if the current weakens. Toward afternoon they make their way to the return pipe. Its warmth reaches them before the pump house does, a change against the bare part of a wrist. After they have gone, the district continues its slow passage of water. The little clean paths on the leaves disappear, and silt settles over the marks their hands left on the brick.
