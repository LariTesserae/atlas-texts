The first animals a traveler notices are the little grazers. They are no taller than the sedges, with narrow muzzles that part the stems rather than tear them down. In rain they feed openly. In sun they disappear beneath the small trees, leaving the moss pressed into paths only a few hoof-widths wide. They return to the same crossings for seasons, though the paths never quite become permanent.

The larger animal uses those crossings without fitting them. At night it moves between the ridges on wide, soft feet, placing each foot beside the path so that the grazer’s line remains visible in the morning. Its body is long and low, its coat dark enough to lose its outline among wet roots. Pale flecks on its shoulders might be old scars or bits of mineral crust caught in the fur. People who have only glimpsed one at dawn disagree about how big it was.

It hunts by waiting where a path narrows against stone. Its ears are small and held close, but it can feel a footfall through a shelf of rock, or hear sedges spring back after something has passed. It does not call. A bird giving one short alarm in a thicket is often the only sign that it has moved.

In the warm clefts, its young shelter through the first frosts. They are born with thin coats and spend much of the day asleep beside the rising water, while their mother ranges over several ridges. By the time they begin to follow her, they know the sound of those clefts: the steady pressure beneath the rock is a way home when rain and wind have made every slope look alike.

Most lives on the plateau are smaller and less easily distinguished from their surroundings. Beetles graze the pale deposits at the edges of seeps. Birds pull them from beneath fallen leaves. Roots hold open cracks that carry water to lower shelves, where moss thickens and the grazers find food. No creature tends this country, but each uses what the others leave passable. Even the hunter’s faint, broad footprints fill with water; by morning, a beetle may be drinking from one.
