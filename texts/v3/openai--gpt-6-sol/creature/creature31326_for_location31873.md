The birds are the most visible inhabitants, though they rarely stay visible for long. They arrive low over the plates in loose, quick-moving flocks, settle where water has collected, and begin searching the edges. A bird pauses with one foot on dry stone and the other in the shallows, tips its head, takes something too small to see. When the flock lifts, its calls go with it; the pools look empty again.

Much of what feeds them is nearly as fleeting. Small insects shelter under the lips of stones or collect beside the meltwater. Some have been carried up by the wind. Others have spent their whole brief lives in a crack that remains damp after the open rock has dried. They disappear beneath a film of water, emerge onto a pale seam, and are gone at the touch of a beak.

In the deeper cracks, moss holds on. Its green is dark with rain and pressed close to the stone. The few other plants keep their leaves low, out of the wind; their roots follow the narrow places where grit has gathered. Around them, life is thinner still: minute growth in the wet seams, barely distinguishable from a stain. None of it softens the mountain from a distance. You have to stop, and look where a drop repeatedly falls, to find how much has taken hold.

The birds know these small places. They return after the mist has hidden them, sometimes to the same pool, sometimes to a ledge that seems to offer nothing at all. Beneath them the ice gives a distant crack, and a trickle changes course. By evening, a rim of new ice has closed part of their feeding ground. The flock works the remaining water until the light goes, then rises together into the cloud.
