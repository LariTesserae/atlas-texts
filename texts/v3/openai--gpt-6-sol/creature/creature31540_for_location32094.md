The people here learn the valley by its levels. A person can live above a mill, work below a railway, and reach home by a stair that passes through the backs of three different streets. Children know which walls stay wet for days after rain and which shortcuts become streams. Older residents remember when a bridge carried trains instead of pedestrians, though the trains now run across a newer bridge above it.

They live with water without treating it as scenery. Someone clears leaves from a grate before opening a shop. Someone at the canal watches a gate rise and judges, from the pull of leaves against the quay, how much higher the water may come. In the street with the lifted paving, the crew moves its barriers twice: once for the work it planned, and again for the runoff it uncovers. Their repairs never quite erase what was there before. A patched wall holds an old drain; roots find both.

There are lives here that need no address. Moss thickens in mortar sheltered from the wind. Ferns take the spray behind the transformers. Willow roots hold the canal bank while water works soil away beneath them. The vegetables in the yards are tended closely, their leaves washed by rain and marked by grit from the roads. The heron knows an opening along the crowded bank that is hard to see from the ridge. It feeds there, then leaves without a call when the gates begin to move.

By evening, people come back through the folds of the town as the trucks and trains continue outward. Lights appear on one slope and vanish under cloud on another. From up here, no single movement seems to change much. Down among the walls, each one matters: a gate shut in time, a drain cleared, a young tree given room to grow beside a stair.
