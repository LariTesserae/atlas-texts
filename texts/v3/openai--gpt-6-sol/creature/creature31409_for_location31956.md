The leaves give the range its dark color, but the roots do most of the living. They enter cracks no wider than a hair, swell when rain comes, and hold on through freezes that would split a larger trunk. A plant on a crest keeps its leaves close and hard against the wind. The same plant, growing beneath an overhang, sends down pale, flexible blades to gather the water that forms there. What looks like a continuous covering is often many generations of growth: new roots gripping old roots, dead leaves caught and slowly turned into the only soil the mountain has.

The small animals live inside that covering. Some are scarcely seen except when a leaf jumps against the direction of the wind. They eat soft root tips, insects, and the thin films that grow in sheltered wet places. At a water cup they arrive separately, wait in the folds overhead, and descend in a sudden rush when the larger animals have gone. Their calls carry strangely well along a ridge. Across a gap, an answering call may come from a place the caller has never seen.

The grazers are built for narrow footing. They travel with their weight low, setting each foot down before trusting it, then moving so quickly that a watcher can mistake three animals for one. They know which new leaves will come away cleanly and which will pull up a whole rooted mat beneath them. After feeding, they leave a ridge visibly lighter. The plants recover, though not at once; the grazers must range far enough to give them time.

Hunters follow the grazers through sound as much as scent. A cry cut short in the foliage tells them where to climb. They wait near water when the wind makes pursuit dangerous, and sometimes lose a meal because a ridge they crossed yesterday has shed a slab in the night. No animal here can treat the ground as settled.

The birds alone cross the broad gaps without making a journey of it. They launch from the windward edges, disappear below the cloud, and return to stone with wet feathers and seeds caught against their feet. Their nests are tucked behind hanging leaves, where rain reaches them as drops rather than needles. On clear nights their calls come from far across the range, briefly making the empty distance seem occupied.

The people live here more lightly than their shelters suggest. They tend the branches that will become frames years from now, and cut only some of the growth around a water cup, leaving leaves enough to shade it. They know a route by its sounds: the hollow knock of a particular slab, the pitch of leaves at a narrow pass, the shelter’s creak before it appears. Children learn to test a step with their hands as well as their feet. Anyone returning after a long absence has to learn parts of the route again.

Their fires are small, fed with what can be carried or dried without stripping a ridge bare. On cold evenings people gather close to them to mend fiber lashings and sharpen stone, while calls continue in the leaves just beyond the light. In the morning they may find a skin of ice beneath a water cup, a grazer’s fresh bite marks beside the steps, or a seed rooted in the crack of a tool left outside. There is always something else making use of the shelter.
