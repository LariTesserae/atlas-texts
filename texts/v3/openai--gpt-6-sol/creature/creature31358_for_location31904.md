What lives in the pass is mostly out of sight.

Inside a piece of quartz near the wall, a thin green film occupies a hairline fracture. It is not moss. It has no leaves to flatten against the light, no roots to hold the stone together. Its cells lie where the rock admits a little sun but keeps off the wind. From outside, the quartz looks empty.

For weeks, perhaps months, the film does almost nothing. Cold holds it still. Then the crack above it begins to drip. Water finds the fracture, wets the cells, and stays liquid for an hour. In that hour they take what they can from the light and the minerals dissolved around them. By evening the water is ice again. The cells endure another long pause.

Their world is smaller than the shelter and older in a different way. A few grains carried through the fracture may cover part of them; a split caused by frost may expose another part to air it cannot survive. They do not know the crescent of stones was made by hands. The people who made it could have sat with their backs to the wall, looking across the drop, without seeing the life within a hand’s breadth of their shoulders.

There is no sign of it on the pass floor. No green edge to a stone, no softening of the bare rock. Only that narrow place inside the quartz, where light and water meet briefly enough for something to live, and not long enough for it to grow beyond concealment.
