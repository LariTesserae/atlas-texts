The pale animals in the basins are easiest to mistake for part of the stone. They settle one inside another, fastening their jointed feet to the inner walls until a basin looks lined with overlapping shells. In ordinary rain they scarcely move. When warm water pushes up through a seam, they open all at once. For a few breaths the basin has a second, living rim: a pale fringe combing the water for fine matter brought from below.

They know the turning stone by touch. A certain low thud makes them draw in their feet; a run of metallic clicks sets their feeding limbs working faster. Their shells are thin, and a hard pulse sends a clatter across the plain. It is not a warning they mean to give, but other animals answer it.

The seam hunters are long enough to reach beneath several neighboring basins without showing themselves. They travel against the flow, bracing their bodies between the moving faces of shale. Their mouths are narrow when they swim and wide when they strike. A hunter can pull half a layer of pale animals from a basin, but the catch is seldom clean: the rest grip the stone, the hunter twists, and the whole rim shakes. Sometimes a loosened shell goes spinning down into the machinery of the strata. The hunter waits for it to come back up.

Farther out, in the channels too deep to see the bottom, the dark creatures drift. Their weight is difficult to judge. They rise without a splash and sink without much movement of their bodies, as though the water grows denser or thinner around them. Scar tissue lies in pale bands across their blunt heads, where they have pressed themselves against vibrating stone. They have no eyes that can be found. Along the edges of their mouths are soft folds that open toward the loudest cracks and close upon the cloudy water expelled there.

They follow a sound for a long time before feeding. When the deep turning thud slips out of rhythm, several may gather over the same fissure, hanging nearly motionless while the water rattles around them. Small hunters pass beneath their bodies unmolested. The dark creatures are attending to something farther down.

The quiet drives all three kinds into shelter. The pale animals seal their shells. The seam hunters flatten themselves under the lips of stone. Even the dark ones leave the noisy fissures and settle against channel walls. Nothing comes for them. There is only the cessation of a pressure they have lived inside since hatching, and then the difficult moment when it begins again.

After such a pause, the young pale animals sometimes reach before the water arrives. A hunter may dart toward a basin a moment before its shells begin to clatter. The dark creatures are hardest to watch: one will turn its blind head to an empty seam and hold its mouth open until, several thuds later, warm water rises there. Whether they sense a motion carried through the buried stone or have learned the plain’s strange order, they survive by trusting what has not yet reached them.

None of them can travel far over the rims. Each life is spent in a basin, a seam, or a flooded channel, among neighbors close enough to strike or shelter beside. Rain supplies them; stone shakes them loose; the water brings them together again. As the rims wear lower, new passages open between their small territories. A basin that held pale shells for generations becomes a hunter’s route, then part of a deep channel where the blind creatures wait with their mouths turned down.
