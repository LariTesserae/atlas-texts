The birds are easiest to find. They nest in the highest breaks in the masonry, just below the cracks in the hollow’s roof, and drop into the streets to pick insects from the dusty plants. Their calls carry strangely. A bird calling from the next courtyard can sound farther away than one perched across a district, where the wind has found a clear run through the ruins.

Lower down live the lizards. Some are small enough to vanish into a fitted joint without stirring its dust. Others are as long as a forearm, with broad feet for crossing loose stone. They spend the gray hours pressed flat beneath the shrubs, taking insects that come to the seed cases. When the wind rises, they retreat into the walls. Often the only sign of them is a succession of pale tails disappearing ahead of a visitor.

There are larger inhabitants, though they are seldom seen in the open streets. At first they look like slabs left standing against a wall: squat, gray-backed animals with long narrow faces and ears that fold close to their heads. They come out under the deepest shade to nose through fallen seeds, insects, and the leaves that have dropped from the vines. They move slowly, stopping whenever the wind changes. A sudden whistle overhead will make every one of them lift its head at once.

They keep to the old doorways. In some districts, several generations have worn a shallow path between a sheltered room and the nearest patch of growth, rounding the stone more surely than weather alone. Their young lie in the cool dust behind collapsed roofs while the adults forage. They get what moisture they can from leaves and prey; in the long dry spells they barely venture out.

Much of the city belongs to creatures no visitor can pick out from its noise. Insects live inside the dry seed cases, birds quarrel above them, and lizards hunt in the seams below. When the wind crosses the cracks, their separate sounds seem to become one voice. Then it shifts, and the inhabitants are only small bodies in a very large, quiet street.
