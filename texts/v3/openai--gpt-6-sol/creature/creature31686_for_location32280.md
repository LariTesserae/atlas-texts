The hottest water is almost bare. Along the lips of the spring, where it has cooled enough to touch, thin rust-colored and green films take hold. They make the pale mineral terraces slippery. Farther down, moss closes over the stone, and the warm channels fill with insect larvae that cling to the undersides of leaves when the current rises.

Life gathers in bands around the water. Tiny flies hang in the steam without seeming to move. Frogs sit where a cold trickle enters a warm one, their throats pulsing above the silt. They retreat beneath roots when the vents begin to rush. Birds follow the flies through the lower branches: quick, dark shapes that stop just long enough to turn a leaf over, then vanish into the wet green.

The broad tracks belong to a heavy, shy browser. It travels through the basin mostly at night, parting ferns with a short, searching snout. The mineral crust draws it to the springs, but it keeps clear of the hottest pools. Sometimes it stands for a long while in a rain-fed channel, water washing around its legs, before climbing back into the trees. By morning there is little to show for its visit beyond a broken stem, a patch of flattened moss, and the deep prints filling slowly with water.

Nothing here stays in quite the same place. Larvae are swept into cooler streams; seeds lodge in cracks opened by heat; a frog abandons a bank when a spring shifts its course. Even the trees grow around a slow change below them. Their roots hold the old rock while water finds new ways through it, and the small lives along each channel move with the warmth.
