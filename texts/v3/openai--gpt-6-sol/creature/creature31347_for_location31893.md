Most of the town’s inhabitants keep close to an edge: the seam beneath a drain cover, the underside of a loading ramp, the thin strip of mud exposed when the tide falls. The crabs come out first. From above they look like scattered shell grit until their claws begin to work, turning over fragments too small to see from the quay. A passing truck sends them backward into their holes. Minutes later, one appears, then another, and the mud seems to move again.

Underwater, the pilings are thick with mussels. Small fish hold in their shelter when the current runs hard, then nose out into the cloudy water where the sheds discharge. Rats know the same routes as the water. They travel the channels after dark and sometimes in broad daylight, crossing open concrete quickly, as if surprised by the distance.

The gulls spend long hours on the roofs, facing into the wind. They have learned the sounds of work: an engine starting, a shed door rolling up, a catch arriving. A few lift off at each noise, but most wait to see whether it means food. When one bird finds something in the shallows, the others become suddenly loud.

People live here too, though an empty road can make that easy to forget. They appear in rubber boots to clear a gate or guide a truck, their clothes damp at the back before the rain starts. Some know which drain will push seawater up first and which stretch of quay stays slick after the rest has dried. They watch the water for practical reasons, but they notice its other residents as well: the new scatter of crab holes beside a wall, a rat vanishing under a tank, the brief turn of fish below the pilings.
