The largest animals are difficult to count because they rarely stand apart. A herd looks like a single dark rise in the vegetation until one section of it leans forward and the rest follows, six legs at a time. Each grazer has a narrow head for reaching between the stone blades and a broad, flat back on which pale moss takes hold. The moss thickens around old scars and along the spine. Young animals, whose backs are still bare, walk in the middle of the herd, where they lose less heat to the wind.

They eat the thick leaves without stripping a patch clean. Their mouths take the soft edges; their blunt chins rub dust from the surfaces left behind. Seen from above, a herd’s passage is a slow change in color: pale backs advancing, newly polished leaves shining in their wake. Smaller creatures hurry through the spaces that open beneath their feet. Some gather the crumbs of leaf. Others pick at the damp moss on the grazers’ legs, clinging on even when a foot lifts clear of the rock.

The shell-backed animals belong to the cracks. They are no longer than a finger, but their files can stretch across a ridge. At a wet seam each one stops, seats the rim of its shell against the stone, and waits. Water beads along the rim and runs into a groove underneath. A full animal moves on; the next takes its place. On dry days they follow the same routes to cracks that have yielded nothing for weeks. Their worn paths make the safest crossings for the smaller soft-bodied things, which can slip under a shell when a bird comes down.

The birds seldom stay aloft for long. Flying above the plateau exposes them to the full cold, and from the air there is little to see but a confusion of similar leaves and backs. They land close together on the ridges, bright-eyed and braced against the wind, then take short, quick steps through the traffic. One may spend an hour beside a hollow no wider than its foot, waiting for the translucent creatures there to open. In sunlight those creatures spread thin lobes over the warm stone. At the sweep of a wing, they draw themselves back into the fissure, leaving a film of water that the bird drinks instead.

Much of the plateau’s life remains beneath the mats. Burrowers no thicker than roots move through the packed soil, pushing damp grains from one sheltered hollow toward another. The low vibration in the stone comes partly from them and partly from the grazers shifting their weight above. It never quite ceases. When the distant rain finally reaches the plateau, the sound changes first: clicks soften, the leaf-chatter falters, and thousands of small bodies turn toward the wet cracks. By the time the fine curtain has passed, the exposed stone is dry again. Under the leaves, water is still moving.
