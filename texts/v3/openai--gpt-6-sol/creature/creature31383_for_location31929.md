At first, the shelf seems to belong only to the birds. Then, near sunset, some of the pale stone begins to move.

The creatures that come out of the cracks are low and broad, about the length of a hand. Their backs are dull as the rock around them, but their undersides are dark and soft, kept close to the stone. Six short legs carry them with a peculiar care: they cross a stretch of open shelf quickly, then stop for minutes where a shallow gully gives even a finger’s width of shelter. Seen from above, a still one could be a chip loosened by the weather.

They feed on the lichen that the midday light makes almost invisible. Their mouths work slowly against the stone, leaving clean, faintly brighter trails. After rain, they visit the little basins, pressing themselves against the wet rims rather than entering the water. Most of their drinking is done inside cracks, where damp grit persists after the basins have dried. There they crowd together until it is difficult to tell where one ends and another begins.

Birds take them, when they can. A passing shadow sends every creature flat against the shelf; a sharper change in light sends them running for the nearest seam. They do not startle at the wind’s deep note. They were hatched within earshot of it, in narrow places where their young can reach moisture and a bird’s beak cannot.

By morning, the shelf appears bare again. Only the fine scraped paths through the lichen show that anything spent the night upon it.
