The people on the raised stones are easiest to find after rain. While it is falling, they keep low behind their slab walls, their shelters pegged down with rock. When it passes, they come out carrying shallow baskets and go straight to the channels. The fresh water has stirred the pale creatures from under the stone, and there is only a little time to gather them before they retreat.

They walk slowly, though they know the plain. A route that was sound in the morning may be covered by evening, and shallow water gives no warning of a hollow beneath it. Each person carries a pole and tests the stone ahead. Children learn to do this before they are allowed beyond the nearest wall. They learn, too, which of the great notes rising through the ground are steady and which have begun to change. A new pitch can mean that water has found another opening. Then the stones laid across a channel must be moved, or the next hard rain will take the whole line.

The walls are not houses. They break the wind enough to make a fire possible, and mark the places where people can wait out the mist without stepping into a pool. On the bone-and-driftwood rack they dry strips cut from the long-backed swimmers. Smaller catches are eaten at once. Nothing is thrown far away: a useful length of bone becomes a peg, a worn slab goes into a wall, and even a failed water barrier leaves stones ready for the next one.

The birds share these places without growing tame. They will stand among the people at the pools, intent on the same movement in the water, but rise crying if someone approaches a resting swell. In mist, those cries help the people locate the bare rock. No one relies on them entirely. Sound travels strangely here; a bird calling from across a hollow can seem close enough to touch.

At night the shelters fill with the smell of wet hides and cooked catch. People speak near one another rather than calling across the open stone. Beneath their beds the water goes on rushing, whether anyone is awake to hear it or not.
