At first the town seems inhabited only at the scale of a drop. Moths wait beneath the roof scales with their wings folded so tightly they resemble loosened flakes of bark. When rain reaches the cups above them, they come down to drink from the iron rollers. Pale beetles follow, feeling along the metal with their forelegs. Some carry a bead of water under their bodies as they leave; others are caught there by the long-legged hunters that stand among the stems of the walls.

The hunters are difficult to count. One may be visible beneath a press while its legs are still crossing the square. They do not seem troubled by this. They wait where beetles will gather, and when a roof scale lifts, they move into the brief strip of light it makes.

Larger animals keep to the roads in the leaf. Their backs are low and dark, their feet spread wide enough to grip a vein’s sloping sides. One can cross the whole square without once appearing on its black surface. In the morning, a vein it used may lead directly into a wall; by evening another has swollen beside it. The animals learn the new routes quickly, though now and then one pauses at an old crossing and puts its nose against the stems.

The workshops shelter another kind of life. Filaments occupy the rollers’ grooves, and small translucent bodies cling to the belts. When a flywheel turns, they travel with it for a little way, then remain where they are as the iron moves on. In the cool interval afterward, they draw moisture from the metal. No one tends the machines, yet their slow motion opens a press just wide enough for a moth to pass beneath it, or carries a drop to a place where roots can take it.

All of them live inside something much larger. The square leaf drinks what falls across it. The walls thicken at their joints. Roots hold the bearings and work farther into them without breaking the wheels. Under the basin, ridges rise through grit that was not there the day before. Whether these are parts of one body or many bodies growing close together is hard to tell from the streets. A roof may close early while the wall below it is still wet from yesterday’s rain.

At the windows, the smaller creatures use both sides. A moth moves through a frame into the bright thicket visible from within, then can be found outside again, settled on the dark leaf. Beetles drink from drops left by rain that has yet to reach the square. The hunters wait there too. Whatever order the town keeps, it gives them water, shelter, and one another.
