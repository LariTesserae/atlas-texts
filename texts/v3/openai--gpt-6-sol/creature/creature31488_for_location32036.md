The first inhabitants you notice are the leaves. They turn a fraction at a time, holding their undersides to the cool air until a drop forms, then tipping it toward a gutter. A shadow passing over a street travels on after the cloud has gone. At the mills, thick stems swell and release in sequence, keeping the toothed wheels turning. The city does much of its work without anyone in attendance.

Its workers appear singly. One may come around a tower carrying a coil of pale fiber, stop to listen to a pulley, and spend the next hour tightening a belt by the width of a fingernail. They wear broad, dry collars to keep the dripping leaf-roofs off their necks. Their tools are few and well wrapped against the damp: hooks for lifting the paving, little wedges, knives whose blades have been worn narrow by years of trimming new growth from old joints.

They know the streets by sound. A worker setting out across a square will pause when the mill’s wheel grows faint, even if its tower is still plainly ahead. Sometimes they sit down where they are and wait. The paving lengthens under their boots so gradually that there is nothing to see but a slight gathering of grey-green stems at the seams. By afternoon, when the wheel sounds near again, they continue on. They do not trust a distance merely because they walked it yesterday.

There are others whose labor is harder to spot. In the rain basins, small translucent creatures cling beneath the folded rims, keeping the channels clear with their mouths. Fine roots grow around them but leave their resting places open. Dark birds visit the drying sheds for loose fiber; now and then one carries a strand to the rim, where it catches on an exposed root and flutters for days.

The oldest workers tend those roots. They go down the broad terraces with water in covered vessels and pour a little along each parallel band. There is seldom enough to darken the whole length. They examine the fine tips and clear away grit with a thumb, taking care not to pull. From above, the bands look spent, their surfaces almost rubbed through. Crouched beside them, a worker can find the new, pale growth extending past the old edge into the air.
