At first the plateau seems to belong to the rails. They are the only things that cross the open ground in straight lines. Then a line of prints appears beside one: shallow pairs of crescents, disappearing wherever the basalt is bare and beginning again in the dust. The animals that made them are well ahead of the train, travelling at about its speed without ever appearing to hurry.

They are broad and low, with overlapping plates the colour of old iron. When one stops at a fissure, it lowers the front of its body into the crack and remains there for hours. Something passes through the dry stone that it can use; the animal leaves a pale mark around the opening when it goes. Its file may stretch over several miles. The young are small enough to shelter under the adults, though they often stray out to inspect rail joints and return dusted white.

Smaller plated creatures make their living on the surface. They arrive at the edge of a shadow as it lengthens across a rock, setting their mouth-combs against the fine deposit there. A dozen can work along the shadow of a single pier without meeting. If someone approaches, they close their combs and become little dark stones. By the time the person has passed, the stones have shifted.

The pale slag-pickers are less easily mistaken for anything else. At dusk their limbs unfold from beneath the piers one joint at a time. They test the discarded pieces with the tips of their legs, choosing the ones that still hold warmth. Workers sometimes leave a clear strip along the slag heaps so the creatures can pass without going near the furnace mouths. No one remembers an order to do this. It is simply where the creatures go, and where a worker, carrying hot tools at the end of a shift, would rather not put a foot.

The workers themselves live mostly out of sight from the flats. In the lee of the ridges, they build low rooms with thick walls and doorways that can be lifted when salt banks against them. Their clothes and exposed skin carry the same fine red powder as the roads. They speak little while crossing between districts; thin air makes conversation a deliberate thing. Inside the workshops there is talk, the sound of tools, and the low vibration that comes up through every floor.

They know the plateau by changes too slight for a traveller to notice. A seam has grown poor. Dust has begun to settle on the other side of a wall. The animals are taking an old road again. Some workers record these things; others learned them from people who are gone. Beyond the last dwelling, a road continues through the bare country, still firm underfoot. By morning there will be fresh crescents beside it.
