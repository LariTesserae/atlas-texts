The people of the shelves are easy to miss from across the valley. They work in the shade of walls or kneel among the gardens, and they tend to pause when a bird passes overhead. Up close, there is always someone at a small task: turning a basin to catch the morning’s beads of water, pressing moss back around an exposed root, testing a bridge plank with one foot before trusting it with a cart. Children carry cups from parapet to garden. They spill some, but there is usually more to collect the next morning.

They know their neighbors on the other shelves by the pale shapes of their roofs and by the sounds that arrive when the air is clear. A hammer ringing twice may draw a glance across the valley. Visitors learn to wait at the edge of the market square; its empty space is where people stop to talk without blocking anyone on the stairs. The mason there has patched the same tread more than once. He will show you the fault if you ask, though after he has set it right, you may not be able to find it.

The birds make fuller use of the town than the people do. They claim the roof ridges in daylight and the sheltered cracks at night. Lizards take the warmth the walls have stored; the little browsers take leaves from the outermost gardens, where the beds are planted thickly. People shoo them away from young shoots and let them have the rest. Beneath the trees, roots and moss keep hold of the damp places, while out on the bare shelves a gardener can spend a morning tending a patch no wider than two hands.

No one appears anxious about the exposed edges of the mountain, though everyone watches them. A child is taught which seams will take a foothold and which open into air. An adult crossing a timber bridge listens to it. Life here has a great deal of such attention in it. The walls need fitting, the cups need filling, and by evening the basins are empty and the birds have settled again on the pale roofs.
