The little pale things are crabs. Most are no wider than a thumb, with shells so thin-looking that the dark line of the gut shows through them. They wait beneath the grates while the pumps run, wedged into corners the current cannot reach. In the quiet afterward they emerge one at a time, feeling across the concrete with their feet. They stop at each damp seam and pick at what has collected there. A loud drop from the roof sends them sideways into the nearest opening.

They know the basin by its intervals. The pumps leave wet grit and scraps of reed against the lips of the steps; the crabs have only a short while to search those little ridges before the water comes back. The smallest can fit into bolt holes. Larger ones shelter under the narrow ledges of the retaining walls, where a claw or the edge of a pale shell is sometimes visible in the amber light. A few have lost legs. One carries a dark new shell, still soft enough that it keeps close to the drain.

The reeds stand farther out, wherever a pocket of slag stays wet between surges. Their roots hold the loose grains in place. There are not enough stems to make a bed, only separate plants with a few clear spaces around each one. Moss occupies the stamped numbers on the wall, and a slick, nearly invisible film grows where water repeatedly drains and returns. The crabs graze those places too, leaving small clean marks that are hard to distinguish from the marks of running water.

The bird lives above the basin. Its call comes from the cable trays, but it is seldom seen until it moves: a brief shape crossing between lamps, then a landing somewhere among the ducts. When the machines fall silent it descends to the service walk and looks down. Once, it drops to a shallow step. The crabs vanish before it reaches them.

There may be more living things under the grates than can be counted from the walk. Each time the pumps stop, the concrete seems empty for a moment. Then feet appear at the edges of drains, a reed shifts where there is no current, and the bird gives its thin call overhead.
