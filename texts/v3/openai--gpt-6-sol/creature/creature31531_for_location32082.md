The most numerous animals in the basin are seldom seen whole. They live beneath the moss, pressed between root and stone, and feed with small, hard mouths that leave the green surface intact. When a bed swells with warm vapor, they move with it. A tremor passes under the leaves; then the bed settles, and the feeding begins again. They are pale and nearly blind, with broad feet for gripping wet rock. To cross a bare stretch they wait for mist, when the stone is slick enough to travel quickly and their bodies will not dry.

The blue points under the leaves belong to the roots themselves. They brighten as a hollow root fills, showing through the moss for a few moments before going dark. The light draws thin-winged hunters down from the cliff rim. These cling flat against the rock by day, their wings folded to the shape of old fractures. At night they follow the points from bed to bed, feeling for movement below the moss. A grazer caught near the surface disappears with scarcely a sound.

Other hunters do not follow the light. Among the fallen blocks live squat, dark creatures whose backs are the color of stone after rain. They wait beside the shallow pools, sometimes through several cycles of mist and frost. They drink when water arrives, but they are waiting chiefly for the animals that must drink. Their tracks are easy to mistake for cracks until the frost outlines each print.

Much of what lives here is older than any one night’s activity. The moss beds advance so slowly across the bare stone that their edges seem fixed. They shelter the grazers, hold the moisture that brings the winged hunters, and grow over roots that pulse beneath them. In the coldest hours everything draws close: leaves against leaves, wings against stone, small bodies into the narrow warmth of a seam. Then vapor rises again. One patch lifts, a blue point appears, and life begins moving across the basin without anything tall enough to cast a shadow.
