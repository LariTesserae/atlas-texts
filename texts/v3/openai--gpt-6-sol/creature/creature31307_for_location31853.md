The first inhabitants of the shelf are the ones that can leave it. Birds pack the rim shoulder to shoulder, their feet dark with moisture. They feed on flies, quarrel over the warmest patches of stone, and drop into the cloud whenever a larger shadow passes through it. They are also the first to return. Each time the deep note sounds, a few land on the fresh rock and walk its length, stopping wherever steam escapes.

The small pale hunters stay farther from the edge. They have wide, soft feet that hold to wet stone and narrow mouths made for picking flies from the surfaces of pools. Most live among the legs of the instruments, though they retreat whenever a metal light begins to pulse. Beetles are less easily disturbed. They cross the seams carrying crumbs of windblown matter, pausing with their feet raised when the floor moves beneath them. Nothing here has had time to make a path, but the animals have already learned the timing of the stone.

There is something under the shelf, too. It gives the low note that passes through every plate and brings the birds crying into the air. Whether it hears the instruments or the instruments have been set to hear it is hard to tell. Their lights flash; a seam admits another measure of dark, hot stone; water hisses; and the gap closes gently around whatever happens to be crossing. The metal bodies never quite touch the ground. Grass has begun to gather beneath them anyway.

The shelf’s residents live by that brief succession: heat, steam, flies, hunters, birds. The larger caller in the cloud seldom shows itself, but its voice can empty the rim in an instant. When the cries settle, the birds come back to find a little more room than they had before. On that bare addition, the first pool is already forming, and flies are arriving at it.
