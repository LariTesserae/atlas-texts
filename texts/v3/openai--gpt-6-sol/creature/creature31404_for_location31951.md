The largest living things here rarely rise above a traveler’s shoulder. A thread-crown may spread wider than a house, its roots sunk so deeply into stone that its center seems fixed while generations of outer branches grow, blacken, and fall away. Its silver tips face the prevailing wind. After a dust veil passes, they are faintly pale with what they have caught, and grit gathers in the lee of every stem. A forest builds its own ground this way: first a foothold in a crack, then a mound, then a sheltered crust where another seed can remain.

The bare distances have their inhabitants too. On the shaded sides of stones, dark colonies cling in thin, close-set patches. They are nearly invisible until fresh dust settles; then their edges advance across it, leaving a darker shape where there had seemed to be none. Tiny things live among the grains and fallen threads, moving most when the wind is still. A stone turned over reveals their narrow tracks in the powder beneath it.

The pale animals are few, and each has a long way to travel. Their coats catch loose seeds as they pass through a crown’s low branches. In a forest they linger, nosing through the old growth and the sheltered crust; in the open they cross steadily, their coloring lost against bright rubble except when they move. Some seeds fall along the way and come to nothing. Others stay tangled until an animal reaches another green province—or pauses beside a shaded seam where dust has begun to gather.

Much of what lives here is occupied with holding on, or with crossing the space between places that can hold it. The wind carries most of what the rooted things need. The animals carry what the wind cannot reliably place. Across the high country, those small acts of catching, carrying, and settling slowly change where a forest can grow.
