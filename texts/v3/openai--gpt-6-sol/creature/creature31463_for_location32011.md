At the edge of a grate, a black fringe moves against the current. It might be a strip of cable insulation, caught where the water narrows. Then a valve opens somewhere underfoot. The water surges, and the fringe draws itself flat between the bars. When the surge passes, it rises again, each fine strand combing a different part of the flow.

There are more of them. They line the mouths of drains and the lips of joints where water wells up through the concrete. Some are no longer than a fingernail; others trail across a whole seam. From above they look like accumulated grime. Up close, the strands are translucent at their tips, darkening toward a dense, gritty base. They catch the finest particles the water carries—cement dust, rust, whatever the floor has surrendered that day—and work them slowly inward.

The fringe is only the part that shows. Beneath it, something soft occupies the spaces between slabs. It has grown through the old packing around pipes and into the roughened concrete, spreading along routes kept wet by the pumps. A strand at one drain may be joined to another near a housing, though nothing between them is visible. Where the water is still, it waits with its filaments open. Where it runs hard, the filaments close before the rush can tear them away.

It cannot move far under its own power. A change in pressure takes it across the plain a grain at a time. Small pieces break loose in the pipework, pass through the machinery, and return in water spilling from the housings. Most wash into the broad channel and disappear. A few lodge where a cap has lifted or a rail meets worn concrete. They fasten there and begin to grow.

The mist gives away some of its older sites. Above certain drains it hangs lower, beading on filaments too thin to see until the drops make them bow. Around one amber lamp, the curb is edged in black for several yards. The lamp has not warmed it; the curb merely offers a long, sheltered crack.

When the machinery roars, the fringe does not startle. Pressure reaches it first through the water in its channels. It folds down, holds fast, and opens again after the valve has shut. The pumps bring it food and carry its young away. The same water wears the floor that shelters it, widening some seams while scouring others clean. At a bare grate, there may be nothing living now. Farther along the channel, where the current slackens behind a flake of rust, a new dark thread has caught.
