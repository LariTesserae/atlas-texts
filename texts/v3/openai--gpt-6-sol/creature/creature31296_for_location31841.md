The smallest inhabitants are difficult to separate from the wet stone. They gather where a crack gives off its sharp smell, pale grains with legs that become visible only when they move. Their mouthparts leave faint, clean strokes through the film on the rock. Behind them come animals large enough to cast shadows: flat-shelled crawlers that can wedge themselves between two ridges and scrape a seam from end to end. Their shells are chipped along the edges. In a crowded trough they climb over one another rather than turn back.

Not everything feeds on the stone. A thread of water may deliver a blind, soft-bodied swimmer from somewhere deeper, and the trough comes alive around it. Waiting animals unfold jaws they usually keep tucked beneath their heads. Others find the disturbance too late and feed on what remains. There is little room to hold a catch unseen. A hunter on the ridge can feel the struggle through its long legs; it steps down with care, placing each foot where the stone is bare.

Above them, the winged creatures keep to the moving air. They are small and almost soundless alone, but there are so many that their turns make a noise like cloth being shaken far off. Some settle upside down on ledges in the dark. Others never seem to land while anyone is near enough to hear it. Fallen wings and bodies reach the floor, where they vanish quickly among the shells.

The deeper blow changes them all. Before the first droplets fall, the shell-bearers stop scraping. The long-legged hunters lift their bodies clear of the ridges. Then water finds the troughs, and creatures that had been crowded into a few damp seams spread across the basin. Some arrive with the flow. Some are washed out of hiding. The crawlers work at fresh deposits on the stone while the hunters follow the confusion, and the flock overhead turns toward the sound of running water.

Afterward, there are wet trails crossing ridges that were dry before, and new shells lodged in the narrow places. The animals continue moving long after the flow has sunk away. By morning—if morning has any meaning here—the trails will have crossed one another so often that no single journey can be made out.
