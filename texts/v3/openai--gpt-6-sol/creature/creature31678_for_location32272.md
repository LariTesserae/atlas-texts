The trees live without asking the stone for water. Their braided trunks lie along the wind-cut channels before rising, as though each has spent years learning which way it can afford to stand. When a gust strikes, a crown does not bend all at once. Its leaves turn edge-on in ripples, shedding the force across their broad surfaces, then open again to the light and dust. From the path they look fragile. From beneath them, where the air is briefly slower, they seem to be holding their ground.

The glittering bands are six-winged grazers. They pass a crown again and again, skimming its leaves so closely that the green vanes tremble after they have gone. In a lull, hundreds drift apart and become visible as separate animals, each correcting its position with quick, unequal beats. When the towers change pitch, they gather into bands so dense they look like something poured into the air. Some go over the rim with the wind and return minutes later on a rising column. The dark hunters follow them out of sight, then come back singly, their wings held still until the last moment.

Underfoot, the pale colonies inhabit cracks too narrow for a finger. A step sends them deep into the rock. They reappear first at the edges of a footprint, feeling through the fresh grit before the next gust carries it away. They are easy to miss beside the great noise of the towers, though in a rare still interval their dry, collective ticking can be heard from the path.

People live here less certainly. The scratches inside the shelter record waits rather than stays: short marks, crowded marks, lines begun and abandoned when the air changed. Someone has learned where to stand when a tower begins to turn and which tree will fold lowest; someone else has left a fresh mark among the old ones. At the height of the noise, none of them can call to one another. They watch the leaves instead. The crowns tighten, the grazers scatter, and everyone who knows the bowl moves before the grit arrives.
