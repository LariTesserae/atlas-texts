At first, the town seems to belong to the water. The little grazers in the gutters are nearly invisible until they move: clear bodies braced against the current, dark meals showing through them. They crowd the cool underside of every lip and retreat into the narrowest seams when the sun reaches them. After rain, they spread across the terraces in a slow, glittering tide, feeding while the stone stays wet.

The basin swimmers are larger and less hurried. Their pale joints fold beneath them as they turn through the shallows, stirring the dark film from the bottom. Long-legged hunters wait above with their feet planted far apart. A hunter may stand so still that droplets collect along its limbs; then it strikes, lifts a swimmer into the heat, and swallows it before the water has finished dripping from its mouth.

The people of the town keep to shade almost as faithfully. They cross the courts early, when the roofs are dull and wet, and work under awnings through the bright hours. Their hands are often cool from pipes and valves. They know which wall will bead first in the morning and which gutter will run after a dry night. A blocked channel brings them out quickly—not only because a workshop needs its water, but because a whole crowded reach of small lives can perish on bare stone.

By evening they come out again. Some sit beside the open gutters, where the air is a little cooler. Small things dart between their feet and the shadows of the stairs. Above them the tanks begin to gather moisture, drop by drop, and the town fills with the quiet sound of its inhabitants drinking.
