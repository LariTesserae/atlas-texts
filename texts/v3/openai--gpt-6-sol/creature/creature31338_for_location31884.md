The people who live here know the district by what needs doing. A pump keeper can hear a change in the motor before the gate sticks. Someone in the timber yard keeps a narrow path swept between stacks of boards, though seedlings appear in its cracks every spring. At the end of a lane, a household has trained a vine over its doorway and cuts it back from the lock twice a year. The work is repetitive without being futile. It leaves the place passable, and leaves most of it green.

Not everyone who tends the district lives in it. Workshop doors stand open onto small, crowded interiors: pots and lengths of pipe, sawdust on a bench, boots drying under it. People cross the yards with armfuls of stock or call out to someone they cannot see through the leaves. Those who do live above the workshops and warehouses learn which steps stay slick after rain and how high the canal can mark the wall. Their windows look into foliage at close range. A branch brushed aside in the morning may be back against the glass by evening.

The plants occupy the intervals people make for them. Ferns hold to the shaded brick; moss darkens the mortar that stays wet longest. Saplings take a firmer grip behind the curbs until someone decides whether they can be left there. The nursery stock outside the workshops is watered and moved, but its loose soil feeds other growth too. Roots follow joints, drains, and the soft edges of old repairs. They are cut away from hinges and cables, then return where there is room.

Much of the other life keeps out of sight. Small insects gather beneath wet leaves and in the seams of timber stacks. Spiders use the cables overhead. Birds come down when a yard is quiet, then retreat to the wires as the saw starts again. At the canal wall, the water reaches into gaps no hand can easily clear; what lives there is noticed mostly by its movement when the surface is still.

No one here lives apart from the pumps for long. Their beat travels through floors and railings, through the damp air behind the leaves. When it changes, heads lift in the yards. Then the gates move, the canal settles against the brick, and work goes on.
