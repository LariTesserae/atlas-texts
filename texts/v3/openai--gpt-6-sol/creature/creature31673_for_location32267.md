The people are easiest to find by what they have left loose. A plank bridge can be lifted in one hand. Bowls wait beneath lintels and at the mouths of cracks, some glazed, some repaired with wire. No one has pulled down the vines over the empty lanes, though they sometimes have to duck beneath them. At dusk, a few residents come out to move a bowl by the width of a finger or test a bridge with one foot. They know where the water will fall tonight, and which stone has shifted since yesterday.

The smaller inhabitants know those things too. The flat lizards spend daylight pressed into joints in the masonry, so close in colour to the rock that their eyes give them away first. When the cold air rises, they gather under the bridges. A drop strikes a tongue; another runs over a jaw and into the crack below. They stay there while the moths arrive, though insects pass close enough to catch.

The moths settle on the vine cords and the pale lichen until the ceilings seem thicker than they were at noon. Some descend into the fissures, where beads of water cling out of reach of the bowls. Birds come after them, stepping from roof to wall with their wings half-open for balance. They have learned the town’s narrow paths: along a coping, behind a store shed, around the square rather than across it. One bird can clear a length of wet moss of insects without making a sound louder than its claws.

There are lives here that scarcely move at all. Moss takes the cold, sheltered faces of new stone; ferns root in old mortar and hold the evening water longer than any bowl. In places where a stair has ceased to lead anywhere, their leaves have filled the space a person would have occupied. The walls look crowded from a distance. Up close, there are small intervals between everything—a lizard’s passage behind a stem, a gap left in a vine curtain, a dry foothold beside a wet one.

At the new seam in the garden, these arrangements have only begun. The roots still hang exposed, and the moss below them is bright and thin. Insects collect in the opening as the air cools. The bird on the wall waits for them. Beneath it, the new bowl waits for the drip. By morning the insects will have dispersed, the bird will be gone, and someone will come to see whether the bowl is full—and how much wider the seam has grown.
