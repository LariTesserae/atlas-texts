The people who live here learn to look up when someone speaks. They read a face and a pointing hand together; from across a square, a raised palm means wait, and two arms held wide mean a load is coming through. Children acquire these gestures before they can make themselves heard over the wheels. They also learn which paving stones tremble underfoot, which stairwells leave a sleeve wet, and how long it takes a spill in the north market to reach the mill drains.

Many households keep a dry shelf just inside the door. Papers, bread, spare shoes, and anything with a fine working edge go there. The rooms behind it may be damp, but they are not neglected. People have lived with the seepage long enough to notice changes in it. A thin new stream down a wall brings a mason to look at the stone above; water that arrives warm calls for someone from the works. The drain crews know the city from underneath. At supper they describe a district by the grit they have cleared from it, or the sound a wheel made before its bearing failed.

Work leaves its marks on them. Press operators have broad, scarred fingertips. Mill hands often stand with their weight shifted, as if still making room for a belt in motion. Older residents speak quietly, even outdoors, having spent years saving their voices for the person beside them. In a quiet room they may lower them further. Those rooms are where children hear a parent sing without leaning close, where an argument can proceed one sentence at a time, and where someone newly arrived sometimes sits for a while before going back out.

The birds over the ponds seldom stay, but other creatures do. Small dark fish hold in the sheltered channels below the bridges. Moss-colored insects cling to the wet stone, and pale moths gather at lamps near the covered gutters. Children know where to find them, though most adults pass those places without looking down.

No one thinks of the roar as a single sound. A resident waking in the night can tell when a tram is crossing two streets away, when the eastern presses have stopped, or when a wheel beneath the house is turning out of true. In the morning they open their doors, test the weather against their faces, and listen for what needs attending to.
