At first, the place seems to belong to the plants. Each stands alone on its chosen crack, a dense dark crown above a great deal of bare stone. In a lull, its blades fold inward and it looks almost sealed. Then a gust turns it silver, and the crown opens to the dust. Fine grains lodge beneath the leaves; what the wind does not take away becomes food for something else.

The clicking near the wall comes from animals small enough to vanish among the loose scales of stone. They have low, close-fitting bodies and feet that can grip an edge while the rest of them hangs in the wind. A gust sets the colony ticking—not quite together, but in quick replies up and down the rim. Under cover of that noise, some dart across the swept floor carrying scraps of husk or a grain too large to eat where they found it. When the wind drops, they flatten themselves into seams, and the floor appears empty again.

Higher up live the scrapers. They cling beneath folded blades, sheltered by a plant that seems to have closed around them. As silver surfaces unfurl, they follow the opening edge in a slow, crowded line, rasping the powder from it. A larger creature sometimes shifts inside the same crown, making the whole plant tremble. Its long sawing cry rises over the fronds, breaks when the wind strikes, and begins again from a different perch. For all the noise, these creatures are seldom seen whole: a hooked foot, a pale working mouth, the movement of a back behind green-black leaves.

The ruined structure shelters another part of their traffic. Its shade holds husks and shed scales that the wind has not yet found, and narrow bodies slip between the stacked slabs. When the notched paddles knock, the animals move. The sound does not seem to summon them; it gives them a few beats in which the open stone is safer to cross.

Nothing here appears to live apart from the gusts. The plants catch what arrives in them. The scrapers harvest what the plants catch. The little stone-dwellers wait for the fronds and paddles to make enough noise to hide their feet. In the rare pause between winds, the enclosure does not fall silent. From deep in one closed crown comes a faint, deliberate rustle: someone still at work while the rest wait for the air to turn.
