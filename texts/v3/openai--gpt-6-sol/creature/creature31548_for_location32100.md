The shallow prints belong to an animal that seldom touches the open floor. It has two broad hind feet and a long body the colour of a broken stem: dark on the outside, almost white underneath. In the hanging growth it moves sideways as readily as forward, gripping a curtain with its forefeet while its hind feet feel for the next hold. It comes down for water. At a drip pool it drinks quickly, ears flattened against the sound of falling drops, then climbs until its body is only another dark line among the leaves.

Others live higher still. Small pale things shelter in the joints where the great fans meet the stems. They feed on the soft new growth there, and on the wet faces of fresh breaks. When the ice cracks, they vanish into those joints before the hanging curtains have begun to sway. Afterward they emerge to work over the fallen leaves. From below, their movement looks like frost slowly loosening.

The woven screen was left by people. They visit the dependable leaks, though none has made a lasting dwelling beside one. The screen catches leaves before they foul the pool; in warmer hours its fibres thaw enough to be lifted and emptied. The ladder once reached a shelf where someone could gather the pale growth near the roof. A branch has since closed over its top rung. Its makers will find another way up, or another shelf.

They travel with little they cannot carry. A drip may shift when the ice opens, a passage may narrow as stems thicken, and a stand that was still yesterday may be shedding branches today. They know the hollow less by paths than by intervals: how long water takes to fill a cup, how far a fallen leaf drifts before the air quiets, which cracks give light and which have begun to sound. When a report runs through the roof, they look up with the animals. For a while, every living thing in the hollow waits to learn what has changed.
