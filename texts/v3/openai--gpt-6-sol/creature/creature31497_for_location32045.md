The narrow-bodied grazers travel in lines, each placing its feet where the one ahead has stepped. They can cross a crack too fresh to have collected dust without looking down. On uncertain ground, the leader stops and presses its long, bare muzzle to the stone. The rest stand still behind it, chewing. They will wait hours for a route to become passable, though a person looking on might see nothing change.

Their young learn the paths before they learn to feed. A calf that strays toward a familiar pool may find it gone, its ledge tipped into the cloud. It calls with its mouth closed, sending a tremor through the ground. Usually an adult answers from nearby. Sometimes the answer comes from much farther away than the calf can see.

The six-winged creatures live closer to the plateau’s heat. They hang in clusters inside the turning shells, safe in spaces the great teeth never quite close. Their hooked feet comb shreds of root from the rims. When a shell sheds a tooth, they crowd its pale new edge and drink the warm fluid beading there. In flight, their six wings make so little sound that a whole swarm can pass through a fern grove like a change in the light. Their eggs are left in damp seams; a new ridge may rise carrying an old seam, and with it a brood ready to hatch.

The shells themselves are alive, though little about them invites the word. A shell takes years to turn once. Roots find the openings in its black surface and grow inward; the shell closes around them and feeds on what it presses out. Its worn teeth loosen and fall into the clouds. New ones grow underneath, pale at first, then darkening in the air. Where several shells meet below a cliff, the stone holds their slow, uneven pulse. Moss grows thickest above them. Grazers come there in cold weather and sleep with their legs folded against the warm ground.

Whatever calls from below the lower cloud is too large to come up through the openings. The grazers know its voice. They lift their heads together when it passes beneath them, while the six-winged things cling tight to the shell rims. Once in a long while, a dark back shows through a break in the cloud, moving alongside the cliff for miles. Vapor streams from it. Then it is gone, and the call reaches the plateau a little later, trembling through pools that did not exist when it began.

None of these lives has a fixed territory. A fern grove can remain where it is while the ground beneath it rises; a nesting seam can open into daylight a season before its insects emerge. The grazers keep to their paths, the six-winged things follow fresh roots, and the shells turn toward whatever the plateau gives them. When a new pale split appears across the shade, the first creatures to reach it are usually the smallest, feeling along its warm edge for a place to lay eggs.
