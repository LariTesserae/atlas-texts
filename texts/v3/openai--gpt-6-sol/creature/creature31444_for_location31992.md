The dark grazers are easiest to see when they cross a patch of new growth. They run close to the ground on long, splayed toes, their bodies narrow enough to slip into the lee of a ridge. They do not eat the silver surface once it has hardened. Instead they follow the faint darkening that precedes a split, arriving to crop the soft leaves as the seam opens. A herd may spend most of a day in motion and leave no visible mark behind it.

At the great trunks, pale-winged animals live in the wind. Their wings are stiff and narrow, useful for holding position beside a clattering crown but awkward on the ground. They catch seeds as the blades shake dust into the funnels, then retreat to the quiet grooves of the trunk to eat. Those grooves hold old nests, shed wing-cases, and seeds that escaped them. When a gust is strong enough, dozens of the animals rise at once. They seem, for a moment, to be fragments of the crown breaking away.

Most of the animals are less often seen. Beneath the silver rind, burrowers follow the softer layers between living roots and dead fiber. Their passage lifts a hump that travels for several yards and subsides. Sometimes a narrow snout breaks through to take fallen seeds or the fresh tips the grazers have missed. Around an old waste cone, where a trunk has deposited centuries of crushed dust, the skin is thin and often disturbed. Burrowers gather there; so do small animals that wait at their openings.

The noise gives them cover, but it can warn them too. Grazers pause when a traveling snap passes under their feet. Winged animals leave a crown before its blades begin to shed. A burrower that surfaces during a lull may remain still, its back dull with silver grit, until the drums inside the nearest trunk begin again. Then it disappears, and the plain looks uninhabited.

Nothing here can afford to stay exposed for long. Young grazers lie under the overhangs of ridges. Eggs are tucked into trunk grooves or sealed beneath loose folds of the living floor. The animals drink little openly; they get what they can from tender growth and seeds, and keep to shelter when the wind draws moisture from everything it touches.

From a distance they are almost lost in the size of the country. Up close, their paths cross everywhere: clipped seams, worn holds in the dry trunks, small openings that heal around their edges. The plain makes room for them as it grows, closes over them when it can, and goes on clattering in the wind.
