The town’s oldest inhabitants are difficult to count. A trunk may begin as one plant and vanish into three others; a stem that seems dead may leaf out again two courts away. Where bark has pressed against bark for years, some of the stems have joined. They carry water through one another, so that a tree rooted in a dry crack can survive on seepage caught higher up. No single plant occupies the town. Each is crowded, bent, and partly supported by its neighbours.

The young plants live in the streets. Their roots take hold in a spoonful of mould between larger roots, or in a pale fracture where wind has lodged a seed. For a few seasons they remain low enough to step over. Then their shoots reach the opposite wall, and a narrow passage becomes an arch. In the courts, saplings grow beneath the older crowns with their leaves turned toward the small pieces of open sky. Many will die there without ever reaching the light. Their stems stay in place, holding leaves and more seeds.

Other residents are easiest to find by what they have done. Tiny pale animals have eaten winding tracks inside fallen leaves. Fungi soften the stems that no longer carry sap, though those stems may stand for years, braced by living growth. In the wet hollows, small insects shelter through the cold; a bird can come down from the crowns and pick among them without ever landing on the ground. When it is gone, the leaves close over its route.

Nothing living here has much room to withdraw from anything else. Roots grip the stone and one another. Crowns take the full weight of the wind while the lower leaves barely stir. Water passes from leaf to leaf, through the joined wood, and back into the fractures below. At the last arch there is cloud and a drop no root can cross. Behind it, the youngest shoots are already reaching across the opening.
