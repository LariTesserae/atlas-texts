At first the chamber seems to belong to the bats. They hang shoulder to shoulder on the dry reaches of the roof, tightening into a dark, furred mass when a pump starts or a footstep passes below. At dusk they leave by the breach in a long, uneven stream. Their droppings stipple the stone beneath them and wash, little by little, into the water.

But at floor level the inhabitants are smaller and harder to separate from what they live on. A thin, slippery film has begun to take hold where the slow water crosses limestone. It catches grains of silt, then more grains. On the sheltered sides of the new banks it is faintly brown; under a lamp it shines for a moment when the water shifts. Insect larvae graze there. Crayfish feel along it with their claws, retreating beneath sharp-cut blocks whenever the pump’s vibration changes.

The fish have no fixed part of the chamber. Schools enter when the river rises, turn silver in a lamp’s circle, and vanish between the pillars. Some feed in the aisles where falling water troubles the surface. Others hang almost still behind a concrete pad, facing a current too slow to show itself except by the direction they hold. The frogs stay closer to the machinery, where a ledge remains warm and a lamp brings moths down within reach. Their calls sound large in the chamber, though the frogs themselves fit easily into the seams of the concrete.

None of them has been here long. A flood can move a bank overnight, fill a crayfish’s shelter, or leave a stranded fish in water that will soon sink into mud. The quarry still has the open, unfinished look of a place being entered from many directions. Yet every sheltered edge is beginning to collect evidence of life: a clutch of eggs under a stone, a casing left by a larva, tracks pressed into fresh silt and blurred by the next drip. Between the pillars, while the pumps run and no one is watching, the residents make use of whatever holds still.
