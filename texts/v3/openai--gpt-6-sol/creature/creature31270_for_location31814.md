The people of the summit look, at first, as though they are always trying not to fall. They keep one hand on a line or a ledge, and their long toes curl around whatever they find. But falling is not what troubles them. A person who loses hold rises slowly above the street, trailing loose cords and a little cloud of silt, and must pull themselves back down.

They have broad, work-worn hands and soft folds beneath the jaw that open and close as they breathe. The edges of those folds are delicate; the fingers are not. Years of cutting mineral crust and fastening gardens to bare rock leave the fingertips pale and rough. Their eyes are large enough to catch a neighbor moving at the far end of a street, though in dark water they trust a familiar knot more than a distant shape.

They grow much of what they need. A household tends the weed fixed beside its door, taking a few ribbons for food and leaving the strongest to thicken around the stone. Fibers for bindings are stripped from other plants and twisted while still supple. Children are given short lengths to practice on. Their first knots are easy to undo, which is fortunate: children also tie them around one another’s ankles, across doorways, and to the markers in the square.

No one passes those markers carelessly. Each has a shape that can be known without sight, and hands pause on them when the water clouds. The people meet on the cleared rock to divide work, to settle quarrels, and to hear news from anyone who has gone beyond the last house. They face one another closely there. A change in a hand’s pressure can answer a question; a story takes longer, traveling through gestures, touches, and the occasional knock against stone.

Some of the older inhabitants remember when a particular shelf was too bare to shelter a home. Now they can push aside its garden growth and find walls behind it, bindings hardened by years of mineral deposit. They know which ledges have stayed firm and which have shifted enough to tear a house loose. When the rock changes, they move the house. They complain while doing it, then save every sound length of pole for the next frame.

The youngest go farther along the ridge than their parents like. They return from the green distance with bare stone under their nails and accounts of places where a line might hold. For now, the adults let them tie one more guide rope, then another. No one calls the empty rises a town. Still, the ropes remain, and hands have begun to learn the way.
