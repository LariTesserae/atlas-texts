At first the middle looks unoccupied. The water is too shallow to hide anything, and the pale aggregate can be seen through it almost everywhere. Then a patch of the floor moves against the current.

The creature is low and long, no bigger than a hand. Its back is the color of concrete that has stayed wet for years, with a little green along the spine. Six narrow legs spread its weight across the floor. Where its feet touch, the water gathers into small clear ridges and slips around them. It has eyes, but they are hard to find until it turns its head: two dark points set beneath a transparent fold of skin.

It spends much of the day beside the inlet grille. Bits of leaf and fern root catch there, and it pulls them free with the two delicate hooks at the ends of its forelegs. It eats the soft growth on the metal and leaves the larger scraps in a loose pile beneath the pipe. Now and then it abandons the grille and crosses the vacant square, stopping so often that the journey takes an hour.

The pumps pass through its body as a continuous shiver. A falling drop scarcely troubles it, but when a bird lands on the upper rail, the creature feels the contact travel down the wall. It goes still, head raised toward a visitor it cannot see. After the bird leaves, it waits a little longer before moving again.

There are smaller ones in the maintenance troughs. They seldom come out into the open. This one has grown old enough to cross the whole floor, from the water’s entrance to its departure, though it has never found a reason to follow it through the grille.
