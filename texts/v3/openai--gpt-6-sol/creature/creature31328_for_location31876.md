The people who live in the valley can be recognized by the things they carry: grease pencils, sealant, small brushes for clearing grit from glass housings, and watches with more than one hand. Most dress in layers they can take off as they descend. The air stays cool, but an hour spent beside the bright walls leaves a person warm on one side and damp on the other.

They know the towers by their fittings rather than their positions. A certain stair has a split handrail; a certain wall has three bolts that collect water before any others. Such details are more useful than an address. Children learn the freight routes early and are warned not to follow a familiar bird through an unfamiliar crossing. The birds can afford to be wrong about where they come out.

There are more birds than nests suggest. They spend much of the day examining the mouths of pipes and the rims of condensation basins, taking the tiny creatures that gather there. The lizards wait near lamps that give no warmth anyone can measure, but reliably bring out small, soft-bodied things from the seams. In the dry troughs, the many-legged animals lie packed beneath flakes of old sealant. When water returns, they disperse so quickly that the trough appears to change color.

The basin fish are almost invisible until a drip strikes above them. Then each gives a short correction with its tail and settles again, facing the falling water. Some basins have no apparent inlet for food, yet hold fish for years. The workers do not move them when clearing the drains. They lift out what has fallen in, leave the fine film on the basin walls, and put the grate back.

That film is the valley’s most abundant inhabitant. It grows in places that look clean from a few steps away: under bolt heads, along the inner edge of a steel plate, in the narrow line where a pane meets its frame. In one light it is colorless; in another it has a faint blue cast. It catches dust and residue, holds moisture after the metal around it has dried, and feeds things too small to see without a lens. Birds, lizards, and fish all depend on it by way of other mouths. The people depend on it less directly. Where it thickens unexpectedly, they look for a changed flow of water or a failing seal.

Near evening, someone usually walks the newest spans to note which bolts have begun to bead. Below, the lizards leave the walls, and the birds make room for one another inside the beams. A worker may stop to watch a loaded cradle disappear into solid white, mark the time, and go home before it comes out.
