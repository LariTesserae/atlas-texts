The smallest inhabitants fit beneath the lip of a stone step. They have flat, jointed shells and feet ending in hooks fine enough to catch on mineral grains. Most have no eyes. They find a warm crust by touching it, then settle over it for hours, working at it with mouths that make no more sound than dust moving over rock. Their pale undersides show only when a stronger updraft loosens their hold.

The larger animals follow them. From above, it can be difficult to tell one from another: ash-colored backs overlap in the narrow passages, and each body yields a little when its neighbor moves. These have eyes, though they use them less than their feet. A scraper withdrawing from a hole puts a faint tremor into the ledge. The nearest hunter feels it, shifts aside, and waits where the smaller animal must pass. Sometimes the scraper stops for so long that grit collects on its shell. Sometimes the hunter gives up its place to another without either making a sound.

The fitted stone rings belong to the larger ones. They cannot lift a stone far, but many feet can worry a loose piece along a ledge. A ring takes a long time to make. Within its low wall, the floor stays clear when the evening draft scours the rest of the cleft; the youngest animals gather there, packed beneath the adults until their hooks are strong enough to hold the bare rock. Some rings have been used so long that their stones have worn level with the floor.

Near dusk, the whole cleft changes its grip. Scrapers leave the deep holes before the cracks grow too hot. Hunters make room for them, or seem to; then the passages close again. The movement passes from ledge to ledge with scarcely a tap. In the last light under the shelf, a visitor might mistake the dull backs for part of the caldera wall—until the place slowly rearranges itself around their feet.
