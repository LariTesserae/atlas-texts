The district’s most abundant resident is easy to take for a stain. It lives along the old tide mark on the square’s retaining wall: a dark, close-fitted skin that begins below the water and climbs a little way above it. When the pumps lift the water, it wets. When the water falls, its outer surface dries to a dull crust while the threads underneath remain damp. The mark has held its height for years because the pumps have held theirs. Within that narrow range, the living part has grown thick.

It is in the pale repair mortar, too, and in the joints where the old mortar has gone. Fine green threads enter the softened brick; darker growth fills the sheltered hollows beneath the walkways. A little of it takes hold on the roots that hang into the canal. There it forms a loose screen, catching silt and slowing the water until reeds can establish themselves. Farther up, leaves and vines occupy the places it cannot reach. The plants are separate from it, but they keep one another wet.

Each afternoon’s runoff scours the lanes before a foothold can last. Along their edges, where the current slackens, the dark growth returns in thin lines. It is thickest inside the pump houses, around warm outlets and perpetually damp seams. A loose valve shakes fragments free; the discharge carries them across the square; most wash away. Some lodge at the waterline and remain.

Nothing living in the district crosses a walkway or goes to ground when the pumps start. Its residents hold fast, drink what passes, and put out new growth into the space left by worn brick. If you come back after a long absence, a doorway may have more reeds in it, a pipe may carry a heavier rope of vine, and the old mark may look unchanged. Close to the wall, though, it has acquired another fine edge.
