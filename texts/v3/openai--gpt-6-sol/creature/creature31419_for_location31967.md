The first inhabitants are the ones that can hold fast. Small bivalves grip the raw walls by their threads; barnacles seal themselves to the sharpest edges. They arrived as drifting young, found stone, and stopped. Now their shells face one another across the crack. When the tide brings plankton, they feed in the same current, each taking a little from the water that reaches its neighbor.

Below them, tube worms live with only their crowns exposed. They catch suspended grains and bind them to their tubes, raising a rough shelf around themselves. An amphipod shelters under its overhang. A shrimp works along its edge, picking at what the current has left there and pushing loose sand back into the passage. The shelf is shelter to one animal, an obstruction to another, and a foothold for the next larva that settles.

Others live by the changes these bodies make. Small crabs wait where two ledges nearly touch. A brittle star reaches an arm through a gap without bringing its body after it. Deeper in, where the blue light fails, translucent feelers test the water from holes in the terraces. The cloudy seep at the far end supports a thin, pale coating of microbes; tiny grazers gather on it when the water clears enough for them to feed.

No inhabitant knows the whole crack. Each knows the reach of a feeler, the pull of the tide, the passage that was open yesterday. A shell closes and sends a shrimp sideways. A tube gains another rim of grains; beneath it, a sheltered pocket becomes dark enough for something new to stay. The city is made this way—not all at once, and not by any creature trying to make it.
