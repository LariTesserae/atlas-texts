At first they look like a coating on the iron. Each has a low, black back made of overlapping plates, slick enough to return the bulb’s amber light in a brief curved flash. Beneath it, fine legs find purchase on mold ridges too slight for a fingertip to feel. Along the tray lips they lie side by side; under the trays they cling back to back, until it is hard to tell where one animal ends and the next begins.

Their noise comes from those crowded bodies. A leg draws across a neighboring shell. A shell presses against iron. The rasp passes through the packed animals and into the plates behind them, where it acquires its deep note. Close up, the sound has countless small interruptions: a scrape stops while an animal shifts its grip, then starts again. From the door, none of those pauses can be heard. The room seems to have one unbroken voice.

They do move, but seldom far. One climbs over several backs to reach a bolt head and drinks from the drop hanging there without dislodging it. Another emerges from below the waterline, legs working slowly through the surface film, and fits itself into a space that did not seem large enough to hold it. Smaller ones shelter in the seams, their new plates dull rather than polished. The few moss threads are left largely alone. Here, water is plentiful and a dry foothold is precious.

Nothing about them makes the chamber look old. They have not ground paths into the grit or dulled the sharp iron edges. Even where they are thickest, they appear less like settlers wearing down a place than occupants holding it exactly as they found it: gripping the ribs, touching the trays, sounding them. When the service door shuts, the last thing visible is a scatter of amber points trembling on their backs.
