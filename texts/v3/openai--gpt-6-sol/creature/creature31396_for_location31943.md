The most abundant residents are low enough to miss from a doorway. They fill the seams between slabs with thick leaves and tight, dark roots. On the open plateau they grow in islands, holding whatever soil has gathered in a hollow. In the city they are less easily counted. A bed beside a dry stair may be joined through a crack to roots under a court where rain fell that morning. What looks like a hundred separate patches may be one plant, or may have been one until a doorway changed.

There are taller plants where the drains empty: moss that lifts in soft banks around a stone plug, vines that take hold in damp mortar, broad-leaved growth that shades a cistern. None has much use for a flower that must be visited. New growth travels by creeping stem, by broken root, by small pieces carried in the water. After a storm, green appears along gutters that have been bare for years. A few weeks later the opening that fed them may lead elsewhere, and the new patch must live on what it can keep.

Their lives are not quite as still as they appear. A vine will extend through a threshold into wet shade and, when that shade is lost, leave its far end to dry while thickening nearer the root. Leaves along one wall face a pale morning sky; along the same stem, beyond a door, others fold against frost. The plants do not need to know where these places are. Water reaches a root or fails to. A leaf warms or does not. Growth follows, slowly enough that the city can change around it.

There may once have been many people here. The gates and drains still bear the shapes of their work, and some of the plantings began in courts deliberately built to hold them. Now the cisterns fill for no one. The living growth keeps parts of the old system clear and seals others with roots. It takes no care of a district as a whole. Its concern, if it can be called that, is the next stretch of moisture.

From a roof the city can look deserted, its green courts too far apart to make a garden. From below, beside a drain in an empty square, a fine root can be seen crossing the lip of a stone. It is growing toward water that has not fallen here.
