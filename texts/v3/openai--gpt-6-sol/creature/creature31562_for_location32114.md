The living things here are easy to mistake for buildings, especially from below. Their lower stems rise in close ranks, dark and polished where other stems have rubbed against them. Higher up, each one divides into hundreds of thin, pale branches that hold a crown together by pressing against its neighbors. A tower stands because the towers around it stand. When one gives way, the others bow into the space before its leaves have reached the ground.

They compete most fiercely for water. Broad leaves shade the root mat through the hot part of the day; smaller leaves grow inside the shade, cupped beneath joints where condensation collects. Roots thicken around these cool places. A stand may draw so much moisture into itself that nearby shoots yellow and loosen their grip, leaving room for the stand to spread. Its new growth is supple and light green, though the ribs inside it are already strong enough to change the course of a passage.

Some roots can be followed from one trunk to another. Others enter the dark soil at the edge of a court and rise, still continuous, beside a spire that could not be reached on foot. They do not look strained or twisted. Fine root hairs cover them all the way along, and new stems push up from them wherever they find light. The plants use the strange joins in the plain as readily as they use a gap in the canopy.

A falling crown often reveals how densely they have filled a place. It tears away a wall of green and exposes layers of old leaves, live roots, and narrow shoots that have grown for years without ever standing in open air. Then the neighboring crowns shift. Leaves pour across the opening. From a distance it looks as if the stand has healed; close up, there may be a clear view through touching trunks into a court that was not there before. Thin stems find that opening first. They pass through while they can, branch on the far side, and pull their leaves toward whatever dampness gathers there.

No one moves along the corridors. They are the temporary spaces among the inhabitants, not paths made for passage. By evening, even a freshly fallen tower has begun to disappear beneath reaching leaves. Its uprooted earth grows a soft green nap, and the living ranks lean over it, creaking as they take its weight.
