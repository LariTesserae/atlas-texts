The inhabitants look, at first, like the workers their buildings imply: people in pale, close-woven clothes, with goggles pushed up on their foreheads and dust caught in the seams of their hands. They walk without hurrying. At this altitude a hurried person has to stop sooner, and a person carrying water is given the clear side of the lane.

Closer to, they are difficult to sort by trade. A foundry hand may spend the first part of a shift reading the painted marks on a dormitory wall. Someone selling cloth may know which bridge needs its bolts eased before evening. The shelf does not move often enough to interrupt ordinary life, but it moves often enough that noticing is ordinary life. Children learn the old positions of doors the way children elsewhere learn street names. They can tell a settling pipe from a shifted support by its tick.

They live above their work where they can, and store little below it. Beds and tables have adjustable feet; cupboards are fixed to walls at the top and left free at the bottom. In the markets, a seller weighs out screws, salt, or dried food beneath a hanging line used to check whether the stall has begun to lean. Nothing that can split is sealed tight against the rock. There is an established trade in making things fit again, though its practitioners are seldom called repairers. A stair that has gained a step is not broken.

Water brings out a less measured side of them. When rain strikes, doors open all along the bridges. People who have spent weeks passing one another with brief nods call across roofs about which gutter is running and whose tank has room. They set out clean pans for a few mouthfuls beyond what the cisterns will catch. By the next day the pans are put away, and the plates behind the compressor houses resume their patient dripping.

Some residents were born on the shelf; others came up from the lower country to work and stayed. The newcomers tend to notice the dark noon sky and the absence of anything growing. Those who have lived there longest notice the birds. A single ash-coloured visitor on a rail can draw a glance from every window of a covered market, not because anyone expects it to remain, but because for a little while it does.

When the low note sounds beneath the district, no one stops. A cart continues over a joint; a hand closes around a warm pipe rail. Later, somebody checks a survey mark on the way home. They inhabit a place that cannot promise to keep its shape, and have made a city out of leaving room.
