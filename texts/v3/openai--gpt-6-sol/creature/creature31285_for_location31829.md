The animals are easiest to tell apart by what collects on them. Dust settles in the seams of the six-legged grazers’ hides, tracing a pattern that changes only when they bend to sift the pavement. The upright birds have pale grit along the edges of their folded wings. Mineral crust thickens on the long-backed creatures until a young one, walking beside its elder, looks almost like a different kind of animal.

A visitor may spend an afternoon beside a grazer and see it lower its head once. Its lips close around a patch of stone. After that there is no visible chewing, though a faint rasp can sometimes be heard with an ear close to its jaw. The birds make smaller movements. They turn toward a sound, then remain facing it long after its source has gone. Where they have stood for years, their feet have worn shallow paired hollows.

The herds have no clear edges. An animal stands apart in an empty stretch of pavement, and beyond it are three more, then hundreds gathered so closely that their backs nearly touch. They seem to leave room for the young ones without anyone giving way. A calf can be found in a gap that was occupied the day before; beside it, an older grazer has shifted just enough to make the space. It is possible to miss both changes.

Travelers trust them more than the cuts in the stone. Those who return to the plateau look for a bird with a chipped beak, or a tall grazer whose front left leg bears a white scar. Such an animal may have turned around since the last visit, but the shelf behind it will still be the shelf they remember. The fitted ring on the rise has no known owner. Sometimes a bird stands inside it. No one has seen one enter.

When the sound of teeth begins, heads lower across the basin at no discernible pace. The young one lowers its head too. Its lips are still too narrow to gather much dust, and it lifts its muzzle with pale powder clinging to it. By the time it tries again, the rest of the herd appears not to have moved at all.
