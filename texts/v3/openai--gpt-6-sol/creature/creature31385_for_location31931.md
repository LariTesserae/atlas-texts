At the covered basins, the oldest living things are kept warm and stirred. They make a dark film on the water, then a thicker one, and the workers lift it out in sheets that sag between their hands. Most of it is dried for food. A little always goes elsewhere: on a boot sole, in a spill from a cart, through a joint in a pipe. By the next reservoir it has become food again.

The people who tend the basins know this, as do the crews who clear the drains. They mend screens without expecting screens to last forever. They leave a little space beneath some grates so the water will run freely, and return later to prise out the shells. Their boots are pale with chalk; their sleeves carry the rusty marks of the walls. In the lower streets they learn which steps remain dry after rain and which railings will wet a hand on a clear morning. Children learn those routes too, along with the names of the birds that call over each stretch of canal.

The pale crabs live where those routes end, at the mouths of drains and in the wet seams behind retaining walls. Most are small enough to vanish when they turn sideways. They come out when a runoff pipe begins to drip, sorting through what the water brings with quick, delicate movements. Fish wait farther down, under the warm outfalls, broad backs barely moving in the current. A person looking into the canal may see only one fish, then notice that the dark shape beside it is another, and another behind that.

Above them the birds have divided the city less neatly than the people have. One kind nests under the aqueducts and drops into the water for larvae. Another works the loading yards for scraps. Larger birds keep to towers and bridge braces, where they can watch both. Their calls travel through the morning before sunlight reaches the lower streets. By afternoon, when vapor gathers under the raised roads, they know the city by its edges and sounds rather than its view.

The moths belong to the night shift. They gather at lamps by the works until the light seems to shiver. Birds come there too, briefly, and the workers passing beneath them keep their mouths closed. In the warm water below, countless smaller lives feed, multiply, and are eaten without ever rising into sight.

There are neighborhoods where a new pumping station can be picked out at once by its clean braces and empty ledges. Give it a few seasons. A bird will find a place the builders overlooked, rain will carry something edible into a corner, and a crab will turn up beneath a grate. Someone will come to clear the grate, hear the shells clicking inside it, and know where to put a foot.
