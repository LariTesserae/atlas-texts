The leaf moves again. Beneath it is a little animal, hardly longer than a finger, with a broad head and four feet spread flat against the stone. Its back has the dull green of a leaf turned over; its sides are the color of wet limestone. When it stops, the pale line of its throat is the easiest part to see. Then even that goes still.

There are many of them. They sleep separately in the wall pockets, pressed into places a larger animal could not enter, but come out along the same familiar routes. One waits beneath a fern while another crosses the open floor. A third clings upside down to a root, catching the drops that swell at its tip. They hunt the pale insects in the cracks and sometimes a beetle soft enough to pull apart. In the brief sun they lie together on the warm patch, their small bodies touching, until the edge of light moves away.

Rain changes them. They leave the high pockets and gather around the basin, slipping through the short streams with their heads raised. The larger ones nose through the leaf drift for fallen fruit and the insects it has drawn. The smallest stay close to the rounded stones beside the basin. In a seam behind those stones are their eggs: clear, soft clusters kept damp by water that runs down the rock and by drops still falling from the roots after the basin has emptied.

A person looking in from above would probably see only ferns and stone. The animals know the sound of a foot at the rim. They vanish without a scramble, one after another, and the hollow seems still again. But under the leaves they are waiting for the footsteps to pass, for the next drop, or for the sun to reach the floor.
