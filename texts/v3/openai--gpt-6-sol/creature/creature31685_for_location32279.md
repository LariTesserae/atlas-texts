The fish under the awning lives by keeping still. Its fins are almost transparent, and a thin line of silt rests along its back until the current brushes it away. When the tram shelter lights, it turns its head. Tiny animals rise from the shelter’s roof at the same moment, disturbed by a change in the water that arrives just before the blue. The fish takes one, sometimes two, and settles back into the shadow before the lamp goes dark.

There are others, though the square seldom shows them all at once. Small, jointed things inhabit the gaps between bricks, where the current leaves a skin of fine sediment. They work it with their front legs and withdraw at the pressure of a passing fish. Their cast-off shells collect in corners, light enough to lift when water comes through the intake chambers. In the shelter, a few cling to the inside of the glass. For those minutes of light, their bodies throw enormous shadows across the old timetable.

The white growth beneath the stair is less easily counted. It feathers across several steps and has entered the seam where the rail meets the wall. Where a pipe gives off its faint tremor, its strands grow close together; farther away, they spread thinly over the brick. Pale flakes lodge among them. The jointed animals feed there, and something still smaller feeds on what they leave.

On the landing, the fish’s movement is barely visible. Below it, the growth stirs along the stair and the weeds lie flat in their pockets of mortar. Each has found a place in the slow passage of water through the works. When the lamp comes on again, the fish turns first. A moment later, the shelter glass clouds with tiny shadows.
