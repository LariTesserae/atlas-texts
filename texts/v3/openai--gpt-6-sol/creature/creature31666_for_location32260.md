At first it is possible to cross several districts without seeing anyone. The doors stand open; no voice calls from them. Then, in a courtyard, someone lifts a hand from the water beneath the paving. They have been sitting on a submerged step, with only their shoulders and head above the surface, so still that the violet light passed over them as it passes over stone.

The people of the basin are narrow and long-limbed. Their skin is dark where it is dry and pale along the creases, as though mineral salts have gathered there. They wear loose strips of cloth that cling when the streets fill. Most go barefoot. In the shallow channels they walk without looking down, but on dry ground they place each foot carefully, testing it before they give it their weight.

They live in the close-built districts, though it is difficult to tell which rooms belong to whom. A person may sleep beside a doorway for many nights, then be seen carrying a folded mat through a square that was not there yesterday. Few things are kept in any one place. Bowls are left beneath dripping lintels; flat stones hold strips of cloth against the warm walls. In some courtyards, the thin plants have been given a little space around their roots. Someone clears the crust there whenever it grows too thick.

When the streets fill, the residents come out. They stand ankle-deep at the junctions and watch the dark water arrive. Some follow it for hours, turning when it turns, returning by another avenue with no sign of hurry. Others stay in their doorways and wait for the level to fall. They seem to know which steps will remain and which will disappear, though even they are occasionally surprised. A child who finds the bottom step gone will crouch at the edge, reach into the water, and laugh once before being called back.

Their voices carry poorly here. A conversation across a square is made mostly of gestures: a hand held level to show the height of water, two fingers drawn apart to describe a lengthening passage. Up close, they speak softly and with long pauses. They will stop in the middle of a sentence to listen to a drop fall in the next room, then resume as if the drop had offered its opinion.

They do not treat the changing streets as an emergency. When a doorway recedes, its occupant begins the longer walk home. Neighbors sometimes accompany them, especially on the first morning, to see how far it goes. If another square appears on the way to the roofless halls, people cross it and learn its paving. They give the new vacant rooms time before entering them. Not from fear, exactly. A room that has only just arrived may still be finding its depth.

There are old residents who remember routes no one else has used. They can describe a short way between two courtyards and then, on reaching the turn, discover that it has grown into an avenue. They stand there a while, considering the wet stones, before setting off along it. They are not embarrassed to have been wrong. The young wait beside them, learning both the remembered way and the one underfoot.

No one goes far into the bare mud beyond the buildings. On certain nights, figures can be seen at its edge, separated by wide intervals, facing out into the dark. They are there when the street water begins to rise and gone before it has covered the square. What they watch for is not apparent. Nothing crosses the open ground.

By morning there will be bowls to empty and steps to feel for beneath the water. Someone will have found a longer way home. Someone else will have woken in a room whose door is a little farther off than it was when they lay down. The residents take up their mats, test the floor with their feet, and go about the city they have always lived in, giving it room to continue.
