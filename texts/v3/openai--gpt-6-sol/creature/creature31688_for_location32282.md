The people in the hollow keep their boots by the door, not because the streets are always wet, but because they never know how wet they will be by morning. They are pump fitters, porters, and the families of people who came to work at the foundry and stayed after it closed. They know one another by the sound of a latch or a footstep on the iron stairs. At supper, someone may pause with a cup halfway to their mouth and say that the north valve is sticking again. Usually they are right.

They keep the working paths open. Every few weeks they cut back the ferns, lift roots from a drain mouth, and clear silt from the channels. They leave much else alone. A child learns which wall can be leaned against and which will soak a sleeve; an older person knows when the water will reach the pool’s bottom step and moves a crate without making a fuss of it. Their repairs are careful, but they no longer expect to finish repairing the place.

Something else lives here, though no one has found a place to point to it. It is easiest to notice after the shift ends. The knocks of the flywheels keep their measured interval, but between them come smaller sounds: a trickle where a pipe ought to be dry, a soft drag beneath a covered channel. In the morning a patch of moss is flattened, or the silt in an unused gear housing has settled into a new shape. These things may all have ordinary causes. The people who live here generally speak of them as if they do.

Whatever moves through the hollow does not seem to enter the square pool. Nothing feeds at its edge. Its surface wrinkles under the discharge and then grows dark again. But in the pipes and the soaked ground around it, water passes from one narrow space to another, carrying warmth, grit, and seeds. The growth along the walls follows it. So do the people, listening for a change in the engine’s knock, making room where they can.

By morning, the bottom step is often dry again. Someone goes down to see how high the water came, then opens the pump house and begins the day’s work.
