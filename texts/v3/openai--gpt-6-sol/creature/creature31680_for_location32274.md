Most of what lives here is beneath the streets. Pale colonies thread through the clay, drawing in salts and the thin air that reaches them through cracks. At the surface, each growing tip leaves a hard, curling sheet behind it. The colonies do not inhabit the rooms they make. A doorway is a gap they have not yet crossed; a roof is where two edges have met. Under a wall that seems finished, their living threads may already have turned elsewhere.

The black tufts live closer to the light. They gather along fresh seams, where the mineral still has a sheen, and hold themselves stiffly toward vents and openings. They are sparse enough that the town looks bare from a distance. Up close, they mark its newest work: a dark fringe on a keen rim, a few bristles inside a threshold that may soon close.

The jointed scrapers are the town’s most visible animals, though they spend much of their time invisible by keeping still. One emerges from a crack, works at a tuft with small, deliberate movements, then settles against the clay. Its outline becomes part of the wall until it moves again. The changing passages matter to it in a way they cannot matter to the builders. A narrow opening may offer shelter for a while, then pinch shut; another appears where a new sheet curves away from an old one.

There may be many such lives beneath the apparent emptiness. What can be seen is modest: threads that build without knowing the shape of a town, tufts taking hold on their fresh edges, and a small animal waiting beside its meal. Around them, the rooms multiply. None has been made for an occupant, but living things keep finding places in the unfinished work.
