The first residents were probably not the people. The moths found the lamps as soon as they were wired; the bats found the gaps above the street before anyone thought to seal them. Now they hang from the unfinished rock in close, shifting rows. When the pump starts, they loosen their grip without waking fully. At night they slip between the pipes and return with insects from the warmer passages of the city.

People live here too, though it is easy to mistake their district for a place where people only work. Behind one shutter, a machinist keeps a cot beside a lathe because the pump sometimes needs a part before morning. Someone cooks in the room above the grain store; onion skins and a few grains reach the gutter through a crack in its back step. The pumping-station attendant knows the sound of the first stroke well enough to keep talking through it. On the strokes that follow, he has to raise his voice.

The smaller inhabitants know the machinery just as well. Fish face into the brief rush of water after each start, then settle nearer the seam when the flow slackens. Rats come out under the grain-store door after the last cart has gone. They pause when the attendant crosses the square, but not for long. He steps around them, and they step around him, each occupied with a familiar route.

No one has lived here long enough to claim that the district used to be different. A worker can still point to the patch of concrete he poured; the fern by the sump has put out only a few fronds. But the bats return to the same recesses, the rats wait at the same door, and the people upstairs leave their windows open to the hot air. Beneath the traffic of the larger city, they are beginning to know this short street by its sounds: the hiss behind the workshops, the calls overhead, and the pump that interrupts them all every few minutes.
