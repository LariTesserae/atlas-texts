The first thing that takes hold of a boot is usually a plant. The leaves let go when the stem stops shaking, but their roots move more slowly. Under the wet mat, thick runners ease across the stone and into the warm grooves where mineral water rises. They close around grit, dead insects, anything that will soften there. A runner crossing a path may remain underfoot for months. Then one day it has shifted, and the familiar step is gone.

The small pale animals in the service housings know the change before people do. They sleep in knots above the turbines, their feet pressed to the concrete. When a bearing begins to fail, they leave its chamber and crowd into the next one, slipping through cracks too narrow for a hand. Crews look for the empty nests before they put an ear to a door.

Out in the marsh, the clicking hunters catch insects above the reeds. Each click is followed by a faint answer from stone, water, or leaf. In heavy rain they fly low enough to strike the broad leaves, which fold beneath them and set off a patter across the path. The frogs fall silent as it passes. They begin again when the leaves open.

There are people here who know the stations by their faults. They can tell a clogged intake from a loose housing by the rhythm under their feet, and know which stretch of paving will tilt after three days of rain. On the cable cart they tie tools in a fixed order, so a hand can find a wrench while the other holds a door against the water. A caller at one mouth may have to wait through several turns of the engine before a voice comes back through the tube. No one leaves until they have heard that answer clearly.

The large thing in the shallows is harder to know. Its sound comes and goes in long pulls, as though it drags much of its weight behind it. Once, after a night of rain, a crew found a newly cleared channel beside a station: silt banked on either side, roots torn loose, an intake running freely for the first time in years. Farther on, the groove narrowed under a ledge, and they could follow it no longer. By the next rise of the river, stems had begun to cover its banks.
