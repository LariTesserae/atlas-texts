The people here are easiest to notice at the edges of things: a woman kneeling where a garden ends and bare stone begins, working red earth into a crack with two fingers; a child standing ankle-deep in the uphill ditch, freeing a caught leaf so the water can pass. They do not move silently, but there are few other living sounds to hide them. A spade set down in a court can be heard across several streets.

Their houses are small because much of their life takes place outside them. They wash at the cisterns, mend tools under the eaves, and carry bowls of kitchen scraps to the plots. After rain, someone walks the length of each channel with a hooked stick, clearing grit from the places where water might leave its course. The crossings are low and frequent; no one has to go far to get over the ditch.

They are making soil. In the long grassy strips, they gather fallen leaves and stems into shallow beds, then cover them before the wind can take them out across the valley. They tend the reeds where the cisterns overflow, and the few trees receive more attention than their size seems to warrant. The open courts leave room for roots to spread and for water to find its way beneath them. From a doorway, the town can look half empty. Up close, nearly every patch of ground has been worked.

There are no birds waiting on the roofs for crumbs, and no insects rising from the grass when someone crosses it. The children look for such things anyway. They turn stones and peer into wet cracks, bringing an adult over whenever they find a worm. By evening the workers have gone indoors, but a little water still runs past each house. Someone may come out once more with a lamp, listen at the cistern, and lift a fallen reed from its mouth.
