At first it is hard to say which creatures live here and which have only come to drink. The birds arrive from elsewhere. Fish slip in when the marsh rises and remain when it falls. Even the frogs may spend half their lives beyond the rim. The basin belongs, most plainly, to the small things that know its hollows by touch.

There are shrimp that turn sideways to pass through water too thin to swim in. Beetles pause at the lip of each pool and test it with their forelegs before crossing. Under the grass, snails no bigger than seeds graze the damp stone; their paths appear at dawn as clean, winding lines through a dusting of algae. The frogs wait where a crack will keep their bellies wet after everything around them has dried. A wading bird can search the same stretch of stone all afternoon and still find something new.

The columns have their own inhabitants. Tiny black flies lay eggs on their shaded sides, just above the highest reach of the morning water. When the clear skin climbs in the heat, the larvae stir inside their cases. Some are carried over the top and down in the rain. Others hold fast and wait for another day. Small lizards come out to take the flies, gripping the smooth surface with splayed toes; they look oddly exposed there, though the birds seldom notice them against the dark, wet stone.

Nothing nests on the columns. A bird that stands upon one soon steps off again. Roots bend around their bases and enter the ground beyond, while moss flourishes on the stone beside them but not on their faces. Yet the water is sweet enough that animals cross the marsh for it. Old turtles settle under the fall and let it strike their shells. Newly hatched fish gather in the shallow rings below, where the slow drops trouble the surface without breaking it.

Those who stay through a dry spell seem to learn the columns’ hours. Before the first water reaches the tops, frogs leave the cracks and move toward the bases. The shrimp emerge from their shrinking pools. Beetles climb grass stems out of the way of the joining water. Then the rain begins, and for a little while the basin has no separate hollows: everything alive in it can travel farther than it could an hour before.

By night the lizards are gone and the birds have flown to their roosts. The snails come out across the open stone. Beneath the water, shrimp graze where the beetles walked. Nothing large keeps the place. Its residents are those small enough to live from one fall of rain to the next—and those that know to return when the columns shine.
