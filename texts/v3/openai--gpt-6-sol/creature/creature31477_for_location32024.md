At first the valley seems to belong to the lizards. They are everywhere the stone holds the sun: flat-bodied, dust-colored things that can turn almost black by noon. Seen from above, they look like chips broken from the fins. A shadow crossing a rock face sends them sideways into cracks too narrow to admit a finger. They emerge again quickly. There are only so many warm places beside a damp seam, and insects arrive there all day.

The birds know this. They are small and deliberate, with feet suited to rough stone and beaks fine enough to probe wet grit. They visit a seam after rain, then return to it the following morning, when the surrounding rock looks equally dry. Some wait beside the pump awnings, picking at the insects gathered where cool pipework has sweated. A bird can follow a pipe farther through this country than a person can walk in a straight line.

On the ledges above them live the grazers. Their weight is apparent in the slow way they place each foot, though they can cross slopes of loose stone without starting a slide. They eat the low cushions and leave the taller stems around seeps for other mouths. In the fiercest light they stand under overhangs, chewing; toward evening they descend by routes worn into the rock over many seasons. The young follow close behind, stepping where a larger foot has already tested the ground.

The deepest cracks have inhabitants that are rarely seen whole. Something small scrapes damp grit into little fans outside an opening, and after dark those fans are crossed by fine tracks. At the shed seep, these marks disappear beneath the prints of birds, lizards, grazers and the occasional human boot. The mud records visits rather than encounters. A grazer may drink at dusk from water a bird searched at noon; by night, a small animal is working over the softened edge they left behind.

The machines have joined these habits without governing them. A loaded bucket passes overhead and the lizards remain on their rocks. A pump starts, and a bird pauses until its vibration settles into the stone. When hard rain falls, all of them turn toward the old chutes and dark seams. The valley is briefly easier to see as a living place: not because its creatures gather, but because each knows how quickly water leaves it.
