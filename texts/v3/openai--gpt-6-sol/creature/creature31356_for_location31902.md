At first, the city seems to belong chiefly to its plants. Vines occupy the towers without prising them apart. Trees stand along the bridges with their trunks spaced like pedestrians waiting to cross. After a damp night, the leaves all turn by small degrees toward the palest part of the cloud, and the streets look briefly as though they have agreed on a direction.

People live in the towers. They ride the trams, tend the planting beds and measure the eastern basin. They are careful with what they drop. A broken cup can be swept up, but the fine grit from its edge will eventually travel through the paving whether anyone sweeps it or not. Children learn this by setting their palms against a bank and feeling the faint passage beneath it. The sensation is easier to find after rain.

There are animals in the canal, though they are seldom seen for long. One may surface in a warm pool as a dark, smooth forehead, leaving a ring that reaches the bank after the animal has vanished. In the shallower reaches, where water threads among pale roots, you can sometimes follow one by the slight movement of seed husks. They nest under the low bridges. At dusk they come out to nose through the stone mouths beneath the planting beds, looking for what the water has brought down. They leave the mouths clear.

Most of the city’s smaller lives have arranged themselves around that slow journey east. Moss grows thickest inside the channels, not on their banks. Pale insects gather where a drain enters the dark; a few emerge powdered grey and disappear into the trees. The canal animals are older-looking than their young ought to be, with worn, rounded claws and fur that holds a little brick dust even after they swim.

At the basin, the crews know which stretches of soil to leave undisturbed. Warm hollows appear on its slopes overnight. In the morning, a nose or a small hand may show among the roots before the hollow closes again. The animals sleep there when the canal is dry, in earth made from everything the city has used up. The crews draw their new outline around those places and take soil from another side.

By afternoon the streets are almost silent again. A child may pause on a clean black bank, feeling its steady tremor; below, an animal works patiently at a drain; above them both, a tree draws water from a seam made to fit its roots. None of them keeps the street new. They live on what happens to it.
