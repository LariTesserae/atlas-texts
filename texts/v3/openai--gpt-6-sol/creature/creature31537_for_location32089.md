What lives here is the town’s wood. It is one plant, though it is difficult to find where one part of it ends and another begins. Its oldest roots have been worn into a floor and cut into walls; younger roots pass through them, turning across an old doorway or thickening around a buried crystal. In some places a wall is only a dry shell, with a living strand running inside it.

Its buds are hard enough to mistake for knots. A bud at the edge of a doorway may spend years opening into a pale leaf, bent by the narrow space until it lies flat against the wood. No light reaches it. The plant feeds by other means, sending fine roots into the powder beneath the hollow and holding their tips against salts. The thin gas that enters at the seam passes over its exposed surfaces and through tubes left by older growth.

The roar in those tubes is not its voice. Nor are the knocks that follow a settling pillar. The plant gives way in small increments: a roof rib bows, a groove in the floor loses its course, new wood grips an old cut and slowly conceals it. If there were someone to return to the square after a lifetime, they might still know it by its smoothness, but have to feel along the wall to find the street they remembered.
