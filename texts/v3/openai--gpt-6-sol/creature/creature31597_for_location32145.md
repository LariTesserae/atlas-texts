For a long time, the only evidence of inhabitants is the workmanship. A groove ends where a finger must have lifted. A smooth embankment bears a shallow hollow, broad enough to shelter a lake, where someone pressed a palm against it while the clay was soft.

Then a wedge moves.

It does not crawl out from between the plates. It tightens there, becoming a fraction wider, and the two slabs ease apart. Far below, something answers with three slow knocks. The wedge is one hard part of a much larger creature. Its softer parts lie along the undersides of the floor, folded among the unfinished bricks and warmed by the black water. It can extend itself through a seam, or withdraw so completely that a plate closes over it without a trace.

There are many of them. They have no fixed outline. One may occupy the length of an embankment for days, holding it in its curve while it dries; later, the same one may be recognizable only by the particular spacing of its finger marks across a new basin. They work where clay is wet enough to receive a touch. They score a surface, wait for it to stiffen, and bring another surface against it. Much of what they make breaks. They take the fragments below and begin again.

They seem most attentive to a join that will not hold. Several gather beneath it, their wedges braced at different depths. The plates lift, settle, lift once more. To an observer above, a ridge has risen in silence. Underneath, bodies are bearing its weight, passing the pressure from one to another until the clay can bear it alone.

The rain draws them toward the horizon, though none crosses the dry ground to reach it. They approach by changing which edges meet. A wedge near your feet releases; somewhere beneath the rain, another takes its place. For an instant a fresh seam opens, and one of the inhabitants presses a long, blunt surface through it into the falling water. When it returns, it carries wet clay into the warm dark below.

The smallest are not seen on the plates. They remain in the hanging rows of bricks, scarcely firmer than mud. An older one shelters each of them between its hard parts while heat rises through the floor. Sometimes an entire row is lost when the floor shifts. Sometimes, much later, a new wedge appears where there had been nothing to hold the slabs apart.

They do not appear to be trying to finish the place. A completed floor would seal them beneath it, away from the rain and the soft clay they need. So they keep a little space between things. They let water in. When a basin looks whole, one of them finds the place that has dried too smooth, lays a finger against it from below, and slowly opens the line again.
