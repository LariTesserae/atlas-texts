The most conspicuous residents are the birds, though they can disappear against the pale water when they stand still. They are small waders with long, dark legs and bodies the color of dried clay. Each walks with its head lowered, stopping so often that its progress is hard to notice. A bill goes into the mud beside a sedge root; a tiny crab comes out. In the next pool the bird changes its method, running after a fish in water barely deep enough to cover its feet.

They keep a careful distance from one another. A bird that finds a good patch feeds there until another approaches, then raises its head and gives a sharp call. The quarrel seldom lasts. There is too much open ground to search, and the useful places shift as the water shifts. When an unseen alarm passes through the flock, the birds lift together. For a moment their wings show dark above and pale below; then they land among pools that look identical from a distance but are not identical to them.

Much of what lives here stays beneath their feet. Crabs make low doorways in the banks and close themselves inside when a shadow crosses the water. Thin fish hold in the faint currents, facing upstream, darting into the deeper hollows when a bill breaks the surface. Frogs remain among the sedges through the bright hours, their calls coming from places too small to seem capable of sheltering them.

By evening, fresh tracks cross the clay in every direction. The birds leave narrow, three-toed prints. Crabs score the edges of the channels, and something small drags a wavering line between two pools. A slow wash of water softens the marks overnight. In the morning the animals make them again.
