The people who live here have learned to make room without moving much. They turn sideways in the passage even when it is empty, carry baskets against their chests, and know which doorway will catch a sleeve. Their hands show pale dust in the creases. In the morning they lift the covers from stored grain, check the pots for a loosened pebble, and give a little water to roots they cannot see. By noon they have drawn the door hangings across the sunward rooms. Much of their work can be heard without being seen: the mill, a tool tapped against stone, someone calling from one house to another without stepping outside.

The plants live by a slower kind of reach. A vine that began in a sealed pot has crossed the court and found purchase in a roof crack. The jointed stems hold what moisture they can. Beneath them, beetles and ants use routes older than any morning’s attempt to clear them. The people brush the ants from a threshold; the ants return along the mortar. Neither side appears surprised.

Birds know the court almost as well as its residents. They wait for the grain baskets and argue under the slabs before daylight. Lizards take the places the sun warms first, then surrender them to shade. When the bees arrive at the flowers, the whole small space seems occupied, though a person can still pass through if they wait for the wings to shift.

In the evening, someone gathers the seeds from the hollow in the stone. Some have escaped into cracks; a bird has taken some; a few cling to the soles of feet. The court grows quieter by degrees, but never empty. Behind its nearly level roofs, people settle in to sleep while roots press through the ridge, insects travel under the pots, and the old hollow holds a little of the day’s dust.
