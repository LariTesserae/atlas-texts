The first inhabitants you notice are the ones that stop moving. A flock lifts somewhere beyond the heat haze, and a strip of mat beside your boot goes suddenly smooth. What looked like a seam between roots was an animal: broad as a hand, scarcely thicker than a leaf, with its legs tucked under its edges. It has pressed itself into the weave. When the birds’ shadows have passed, one corner rises. An eye watches the sky, then the animal slips sideways into a gap too narrow to take a finger.

There are thousands of such gaps. Beetles shelter in them through the noon heat and come out at dawn to drink from upright fibers. Longer, softer creatures follow the damp places down into the older layers, where they feed on what the living mat has shed. The pale birds know how to find them. They walk with their heads low, listening as much as looking, and turn a fold with a hooked beak. Often they find only the cool imprint of something that has gone deeper.

The herds cannot hide so easily. At a distance they look like patches of darkness moving over the plateau; up close, each is made of narrow bodies and many close-set feet. They feed as they travel, cropping the newest tips without tearing up the weave that bears their weight. The young walk inside the band. The older animals take the edges, where a foot is more likely to break through a thinly knitted hollow. One will stop and test a sagging place while the rest bunch behind it, shifting their weight until a firmer way is found. You feel their passage before you hear it: a long, irregular trembling underfoot.

At the trees, lives crowd together. Husks fall into the shade and soften slowly, drawing insects; insects draw hunters with quick hands and patient eyes. Small animals climb inside the pale trunks to escape the heat. Their calls travel through the hollow wood and come out altered, so that a tiny quarrel high in a crown seems to belong to something large at its roots. In the cooler basin below, other creatures wait for dusk, when the open ground is safe to cross again.

The people have learned to live at those edges of shade without expecting the plateau to hold still for them. They follow ridges that have borne herds for years and set stones where the weave begins to fray. Before sunrise they empty the clay bowls, taking a little of the moisture gathered overnight. A screen beneath a tree is patched rather than rebuilt; its oldest stalks are nearly indistinguishable from the roots growing around them. They know which hollows will keep a rain’s water for a day, and which ones will give way beneath a stranger.

By evening the resin smell weakens. Beetles appear on the surface, and the flat creatures unfold themselves to hunt. Far across the low growth, a herd passes between two trees that have seemed all day no nearer to each other. The mat shivers after them. Then the last birds settle, and the movement goes down into the weave, where the plateau remains crowded long after it looks bare again.
