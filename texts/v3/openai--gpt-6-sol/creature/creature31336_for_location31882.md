The people who live in the court can tell where rain has fallen by its sound before they see a drop. One woman knows which pan rings when it is nearly full; a boy knows that a soft ticking beyond the third arch means a groove has silted up. When a storm starts, they interrupt whatever they are doing to move the pans. They do it quickly, without much instruction, though afterward they disagree about who put the broad one in the wrong place.

They are pot-menders, grinders, carriers, cooks, and children who have learned to yield to a loaded basket without looking up. Their hands are marked by clay and lime. They keep things past the point at which someone from the street might throw them away: a jar with a wired seam, a mat with three kinds of reed in it, a wooden blade worn thin from scraping pans. The eldest among them remember repairs made to arches that the youngest take for original brickwork.

At noon they work in the light from the grilles, shifting their tasks as the bright lattice moves. A chipped vessel is brought to the eastern patch of floor and turned slowly in someone’s hands. Elsewhere grain is sifted on the broad step. Food goes into the ovens, and its smell reaches the sleeping ledges before the people working in the far bays know it is ready. Voices carry farther than faces can be seen; a child sent for a tool may call through two arches and have it passed along by someone else.

The court has other regular occupants. Sparrows arrive boldly and depart in a rush when a pestle strikes too near them. Lizards hold their places on the flues even as people pass. The cats spend much of the day appearing asleep beside the jars, then disappear into the narrow routes they patrol. In the dampest cracks, small green stems put out leaves so slowly that people notice them chiefly when a pot has to be moved.

No one living here has room to be unaware of the others for long. They know whose footsteps hollow a stair, who rises first to check the cistern after rain, and which neighbor will mend a basket if it is left by her shelf. They make room for one another by turning sideways, lifting a jar, calling a warning. Each day they alter the court a little to keep it usable, and each day its worn steps and close-set walls teach them how to move.
