Most of the basin’s inhabitants are out of sight. Beneath the crust, termites move through galleries that stay cooler than the air above. Workers carry up dark grains of soil and fit them to a tower’s flank; others take dried leaves down in pieces small enough to disappear through a slit. At the entrances, soldiers wait while the workers pass. A tower that seems still at noon may have been extended by evening, its fresh earth not yet faded to the color of the older walls.

The towers are not one great colony. Each has its own occupied passages, and the ridges between clusters make a crowded landscape of crossings and boundaries. Some colonies thrive beneath the thorn trees, where fallen leaves collect against the trunks. Others build among the ribbed plants or follow roots far below the new crust. Their entrances open and close with the heat, so that a district busy at dusk can look abandoned the next morning.

Other lives keep close to theirs. Lizards take stations beside the paths and shift forward only when a line of workers comes within reach. Small birds search the thorn crowns and drop now and then to the ground. Beetles shelter under curled leaves; seedlings rise between burrows and towers, sometimes only to be buried by the next load of soil. The trees and fleshy plants appear to stand on a dry, empty floor, but their roots meet an occupied one.

At dusk, the basin gives a little of itself away. A sealed opening breaks open. Grains spill over a sharp plate of crust. Leaves that lay untouched all day begin to travel, piece by piece, toward the earth.
