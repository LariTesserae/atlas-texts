At first it is easy to mistake the people here for part of the stonework. They move close to walls, step over little streams without looking down, and pause in doorways while a shower crosses the next street. A visitor waits for rain to finish; a resident watches which way it is going.

Most households keep a flat iron hook by the door for lifting drain covers. After a hard storm, people come out with hooks, baskets, and stiff brushes, clearing the grit before it settles. A blocked channel below one house can send water through another’s cellar, so this work is done quickly, sometimes by people who are not speaking to one another. Children are taught the routes the water takes: which square will fill first, which stair stays passable, where a clear run over the paving means the channel underneath is full.

Their clothes are made for damp rather than cold. Outer layers hang beneath the passages, dripping onto stone already dark with water. Shoes are resoled often. Indoors, people set stools away from the walls and keep papers high, in cupboards whose doors close tightly. There are arguments about whose washing has occupied the driest passage for too long, and invitations to eat in a courtyard when its few hours of afternoon sun have arrived.

Some work at the edges of the city, where thin soil has collected behind walls. They carry compost uphill and tend small, crowded gardens around the spring groves. Others maintain the cisterns, mend steps, lay roofs, or sell things carried in from the valleys. The oldest residents know stretches of buried water by sound. They will stop halfway along a street and listen, then tell a younger neighbor which stones to lift.

There are fewer creatures than a city of this size might seem to promise. Small dark snails emerge on the north-facing stairs after rain. Pale insects collect under loose slabs; a few quick animals hunt them at dusk and vanish into the joints of the walls. Moss grows wherever feet and brushes spare it. In the cisterns, something occasionally disturbs the surface before anyone has touched the water.

Toward evening, when a flooded square begins to empty, people cross it in ones and twos. They use the familiar high places, calling back if a stone has shifted. From above, they are small moving marks among the terraces. Up close, each knows a particular portion of the city by heart: not only its doors and faces, but the places where water rises, lingers, and goes.
