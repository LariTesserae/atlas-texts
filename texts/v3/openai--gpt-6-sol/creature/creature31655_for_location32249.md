Something lives in the fresh cut beneath the turf. It is easy to mistake for one of the stones pressed into the clay: the same pale colour, the same rounded stillness. Then a willow drop strikes the water, and it draws its feet close to its body. The stone has legs—six of them, fine and dark as sedge roots.

It is no longer than a thumb. A damp ridge of peat runs down its back, while its underside is soft and almost white. Behind its head, two clear fringes stir in the seep coming through the bank. It keeps to that thin current, where the water is coldest. If the shrew passes overhead, it flattens against the clay; if a midge touches down, it comes forward with surprising speed and pulls the insect under.

The hollow is new, but the creature behaves as though the seam was made for it. It works grains of clay loose and presses them around a small opening under the moss. Bubbles collect there. Now and then it backs into the opening and remains so still that the fringes beside its head are the only sign of it.

The engines beyond the low ground trouble the water all day. A heavy strike makes the creature grip the clay a moment before the sound arrives. It waits through the tremor. When the seep runs clear again, its feet begin their patient work.

At dusk, the pale cut is the last place it can be seen. It has gathered a little clay on its back, and soon even that faint difference is lost. A few grains shift beneath the overhanging turf; a bubble escapes from the moss. Then the bank looks bare again.
