The first things to live on the new stone are almost too thin to see. The chalk-coloured film gathers in cracks where a little mineral-rich water moves more slowly. Up close it is not smooth: it is made of short, branching threads, with tiny white crustaceans working between them. The crustaceans shelter there until the current lifts loose scraps of film. Then they come out in a pale cloud, and the fish waiting beside the towers turn silver all at once.

Those fish spend much of their lives swimming without going anywhere. In the water pouring toward a grille, they hold their places with small movements of their fins, feeding on what the current brings. Each school has a crowded inner edge and a looser outer one. A fish pushed out of position circles back; a fish that tires drifts behind the tower into quieter water. They know the loose plate. Its tremor sends a quick shiver through the school, but they no longer flee from it.

Under the clean lips of the rock shelves live flatter animals, the colour of old bruises. They cling with rows of hooked feet while their wide mouths face the flow. At rest they are difficult to distinguish from the underside of a ledge. At intervals one releases its hold and crosses a gap, folding and opening like a piece of cloth in the current. There is little to eat out on the bare slopes, so they return to the few cracks where the film has taken hold.

Farther out, the larger hunters wait below the level of the plateau. They move slowly enough to seem suspended. The noise of the pumps carries through their bodies, as it does through everything here, but they attend to the smaller disturbances within it: a school breaking formation, hooks scraping stone, the brief commotion of crustaceans rising from a crack. One comes up from the blue, takes a fish at the edge of a school, and sinks again. For a few moments the silver bodies scatter across the grille. Then the current draws them back.

Nothing here needs the whole plateau. Life has collected at bolts, cracks, ledge-undersides, and the mouths of shafts, leaving the long bright falls of stone nearly empty. Where another shelf breaks open, the first pale threads will take hold. The crustaceans will find them; later, the fish may learn that place too.
