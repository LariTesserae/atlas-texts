The most numerous inhabitants are the white grazers, though a person can cross a mile of plateau without seeing one. They are warm, blunt-faced animals no longer than a forearm, with soft teeth that strip the living roots from underneath. The mat closes loosely behind them. From above, a family appears as five or six small humps moving through the gray-green growth, stopping together whenever a stalk begins to thud.

They do not stop because the noise frightens them. A grazer can feel the stalk’s motion through its feet before the sound reaches its ears. It knows which roots are drawn tight, which are about to part, and where a mouthful of new growth will rise when water shifts below. The young learn by pressing their bellies against their mothers’ backs and following the same narrow routes. An old grazer, separated from its family, will sometimes wait beside a passage it used as a youngster, though the passage has long since grown shut.

The birds know those routes too. They cling to the pale stalks with feet made for gripping wet bark and sit with their wings hanging open, too heavy to fold neatly. When a tremor approaches a hollow, they drop. Most seize a grazer as it breaks into view. Some strike at the place where one is going to appear and come up empty, shaking water from their bills as if the water were at fault.

The dark animals in the hollows rarely hunt either bird or grazer. Their long bodies pass through the flooded chambers below the mat, where there is room for a body to turn only in certain places. They come up with pale root fibers trailing from their jaws. At a breath-hole one may rest with just its nostrils showing, while its call travels under the ground and rises from another hollow far away. The birds look toward the answering sound. The grazers do not.

There are smaller lives in every wet seam: clear larvae fastened to roots, seed-case mites that ride the gusts, threadlike things that draw their nourishment from the water rushing beneath the surface. A fallen black leaf may be crowded with them before it finishes settling. They feed the roots as much as they feed on them. In an old part of the plateau, a torn root can remain outlined in the air long enough for the smallest creatures to crawl through its empty shape.

The stalks depend on all of this movement. Their crowns turn, pulling water through buried chambers; their roots spread where the grazers have opened space. A stalk whose crown has stopped will stand for years, pale and upright, while everything beneath it slowly changes. Birds still land on it. Families still pass below. Then one season its black leaves loosen, and the wind takes them across reaches of ground that may be nearer, or farther, than they look.
