The first things to notice are the plants, once the eye stops looking for trees. Each has a crown of pale leaves pressed almost flat to the rock. The leaves are tough enough to resist the wind, but their undersides are soft with fine hairs. At night, when moisture gathers beneath a loose plate, the hairs hold it there while a root threads down through the crack. A plant may show only a few leaves at the surface and still follow the fracture for a long way below.

Little plate-runners live among them. Their backs resemble chips of the same black stone, and they freeze when a shadow crosses the ground. After sunset they come out to scrape food from the leaves and drink from the hollows underneath. A runner seldom crosses open rock if it can help it. It travels the pale lines from plant to plant, pausing where a leaf has lifted or a plate has shifted since the previous night. At the meeting of two fractures, a larger hunter can wait without moving for hours. It knows the runners will come by.

The herds make their own routes across the plateau. From far off, they seem to have no shape at all: only a loose procession of dim pulses travelling along the horizon. Closer, each animal is broad and low, with long, yielding feet that spread its weight over the loose plates. Light shows through the thin skin of its flanks when the organs beneath it fire. One pulse draws an answer from a neighbour; others pass unevenly through the herd as animals turn, slow, or find one another in the dark. Calves stay inside that shifting exchange. Their smaller lights can sometimes be seen beneath the adults’ bodies.

They graze where the roots have opened the stone enough for softer growth to take hold. A herd can feed across a step for most of a night and leave it looking nearly untouched, except for overturned plates and a few newly exposed hollows. The small hunters follow, taking plate-runners driven from cover. Larger night hunters follow too, keeping beyond the herd’s light until an animal strays. The wings overhead belong to another set of hunters. They ride the warm air rising from the step faces, then drop soundlessly toward anything moving in the open.

Life gathers at the vents as well. In sheltered seams beside the gas openings, dark films grow on mineral surfaces too hot for the pale plants. Tiny grazers work over those films while the vent thrums, retreating into cooler cracks when its breath stops. Some cling to the thin mineral vanes as they vibrate. When the pressure falls and the sound dies, they can be heard dropping back against the stone.

On mist nights, the usual routes briefly loosen. A plate-runner will risk a stretch of bare ground for a new bead of water. A grazer may climb to a step it would otherwise pass below. The hunters must leave their familiar crossings and search. By morning the hollows are dry again, the little animals are hidden, and the herd has moved on. What remains visible is almost nothing: pale lines in black rock, a plate left askew, and, far down the steps, the last slow pulse of light.
