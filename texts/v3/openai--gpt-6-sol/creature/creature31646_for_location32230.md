The largest of the cleft’s animals are nearly the color of boiled bone. They have more legs than can be counted while they move, and a soft, pleated underside they press to the hot seam. There they remain for hours, taking in the water that seeps through the rock. When the seam runs low, they climb. Their hooked feet find holds on walls that look smooth from a distance, and their bodies settle into the narrow space beneath others of their kind. An old one may carry the shape of a ledge in its back.

The smaller animals live among those folds. Some are so translucent that the dark wall shows through them. They wait below a feeding body for a bead of water to swell and fall, then crowd over the wet place before it dries. They are quick to leave when the body above begins to stir. The flat hunters are quicker still: thin enough to pass between stone and shell, with legs that fold close until only a rim of them can be seen.

At cloudfall, the ledges empty and fill again. The pale animals turn their undersides outward to catch the new water. One braces a loose black chip against the wall, and a shining line forms along its lower edge. It drinks from the line. Later another animal finds the chip in the grit and holds it somewhere else. By evening it may have slid into a crack. Nothing remains of the work except water taken into a body.

Along the band where the shadows disagree, the hunters are difficult to follow. One emerges from a fissure with its feet already closing on a small animal; then the small animal darts beneath a larger one, and the hunter is alone. At other times the strike ends as it appeared to begin. The prey move through that band as readily as they do through any other part of the cleft. They know its cracks, or find them, without pausing to look.

When mist and heat meet, the whole crowded wall glistens. Hooks shift. Pleated skin opens against the stone. A hunter eases backward into darkness, making space for a larger body to pass. In the thin air there is almost no sound to mark how many lives are moving there—only the ticking of a foot against fresh rock, and water falling from one animal to the next.
