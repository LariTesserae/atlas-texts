At first, the city’s inhabitants seem to be the small animals. Flat-bodied runners slip between the root streets and the squares, stopping so suddenly that they resemble fallen chips of bark. Pale things cling beneath the lamp housings, their folded limbs almost invisible against the stone. Larger animals sleep in the hollow stems. When a vent eases open, one will wake, step inside, and emerge two streets away with a dusting of fresh fibers on its back.

Then the people begin to show themselves. They are quiet, not scarce. In the workshops they stand close to the living belts, feeling for a change in tension with their fingertips. Out on the plain, they walk widely separated, following the rounded edge of a street that has not finished rising. Their clothes are close-wrapped to keep the bitter air off their skin, and their tools are often padded: a hard strike travels a long way through the roots.

They work by making room as much as by making cuts. A panel-cutter leaves a narrow bridge of living fiber until a cart is ready to take the weight. Someone tending a new building loosens its braces a little each day. A badly placed brace can bend a wall for years; a well-placed opening may be kept clear by the plant itself. The oldest workers can tell from a wooden knock whether a stem is pressing where it should.

The animals have work of their own, though no one directs them. Some clean salt from the seams of leaves. Some carry fine grit into hollows where new roots take hold. The vents admit them and sometimes close behind them, keeping the open streets clear. At a fresh lamp, both animals and people pause while the nearest leaves turn toward it. For a moment the whole block seems to be listening.

There are disputes over where the next street ought to go. Marks laid on the root skin can be swallowed within days, and a route that looked straight last week may now run around a broad, stubborn rise. Beyond the workshops, people are measuring another stretch of the lit plain. Farther out, small animals are already crossing it, vanishing into openings that have appeared ahead of the surveyors.
