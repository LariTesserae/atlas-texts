The high country does not end at a summit. It spreads into a broad, uneven plain above the clouds, with low ridges running through it for distances too great to judge. From one ridge, the next looks close; a day later it is still ahead. Weather gathers over separate parts of the plateau. Sunlight falls on one slope while rain crosses another in a dark band, and the ground between them remains damp.

There is little exposed earth. Moss covers the old stone in thick, cool layers, and sedges fill the shallow hollows. Small trees grow wherever they can hold, their roots spread wide through moss rather than deep into soil. They stand close together, bent in different directions by winds that change from ridge to ridge. Water is everywhere but seldom in a large body: underfoot in the moss, dripping from ledges long after rain, running clear beneath mats of roots. In the deeper cuts it gathers into streams whose banks have been worn smooth over ages.

The stone gives the place much of its shape. Its slopes are rounded, its cracks broad enough to carry roots and water, and even the steep faces have shelves where something grows. Along some of these faces, mineral water leaves dark and rusty stains. Elsewhere it deposits a pale crust over moss and fallen leaves. The patches are small, but they recur for miles. They make the plateau seem to be slowly sorting and laying down its own materials. In a few sheltered clefts, warm water rises through cold rock, and the wet ground there stays green after frost has touched the ridges.

It is crowded country, though it often seems empty at first glance. Beetles move under the leaves; birds work through the low branches; grazing animals leave narrow, repeated paths that vanish almost as soon as they enter the sedges. Something larger follows those paths at night. Nothing has cleared a space or raised a marker. The many tracks are the only lines that look purposeful, and rain softens them each week.

For all this movement, the plateau is quiet. Water ticks from leaves into water below. A wing beats briefly in a thicket. From a sheltered cleft comes the low, steady tremor of water forcing its way upward. On a windless day these sounds reach a long way, because there is so little else to cover them.
