At the edge of the city, the street ends without a drop. Its paving stones give way to a dark, level surface that has not yet decided whether it is stone. A few survey stakes stand in it, their painted tops visible for miles in the dimness. Beyond them, the new ground reaches toward a range of high, blunt peaks. Nothing here has had time to be worn into a valley. The cliffs are sheer and nearly smooth; the loose rock at their feet still lies where it first fell.

The city occupies the older side of this height. Its foundries, tram depots, and long apartment blocks are spread far apart, linked by elevated rails and unlit service roads. From a distance it looks densely built, a whole district of roofs and chimneys. Walking through it takes hours. Between one row of buildings and the next may be a field of black gravel, a stand of pale trees, or an empty square large enough for the fog to gather in its center. Engines run in the factories even at night, though night is difficult to distinguish from day. The lamps along the tramlines burn a deep amber and illuminate chiefly the damp air around them.

Water is carefully caught. Gutters lead to covered tanks; cold beads on pipes and runs down into narrow beds of moss and broad-leaved plants. Rain comes seldom, but the greenery around a cistern can be thick enough to hide its walls. Small animals live there in crowds. Moths cling to warm brick, birds nest under every span of rail, and fox-sized creatures with wet, delicate feet cross the factory yards after the shift whistles. Higher up, flocks circle the unfinished cliffs. They have plenty of ledges, but almost no cracks.

The whistles carry strangely. One sounds close enough to come from behind a door; its factory can be seen across the plateau, a pale shape beneath the peaks. A tram appears to pass between you and the factory, then takes twenty minutes to arrive at the platform beside you. People who work outdoors learn to trust the rails more than their eyes. Distance changes slowly here, without anything seeming to move. A road surveyed straight last winter now requires an extra bend to reach the same warehouse.

The newest foundations are set with room beneath them. Workers say the ground is still rising, a little each year, and leave gaps for it as they would for a growing tree. Sometimes a stair acquires another step while no one is watching. Sometimes a familiar doorway opens on the same room, but colder, with mountain air moving through it. The city measures these changes, paints fresh numbers on its walls, and goes on building.

At dusk the birds settle so heavily on the wires that the spans sag between pylons. For a while their calls overcome the generators. Then the whistles sound from widely separated parts of the city, and from somewhere out on the unpaved height, where there is no factory yet.
