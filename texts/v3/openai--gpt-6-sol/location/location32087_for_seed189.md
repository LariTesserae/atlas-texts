A hollow lies beneath the forest, though there is no soil above it in the usual sense. Its ceiling is a single broad root, its floor the underside of another. Between them is a space no larger than a room, almost entirely empty. You can stand in the middle without brushing a leaf. All the vegetation is in the boundaries: thick, pale flesh drawn taut around the air, with fine green veins showing through wherever the skin is thin.

It is warm here. Moisture gathers on the ceiling but seldom falls. A bead will swell for an hour, then travel sideways along a vein and disappear into a pore. Only after a hard rain does water reach the floor, collecting briefly in the fresh, sharp creases where the lower root has begun to fold. There is no mud yet. The hollow is young enough that torn fibers still lift from its edges, and a few places retain the clean scent of a stem just snapped.

The light is as clear as an overcast afternoon. It comes through the root itself, though the earth around the hollow ought to stop it. A hand held against the wall makes a shadow on the wall *behind* it. Shadows matter here. The small leaves growing along the seams turn toward them; a dark patch left in one place overnight will have a soft green fringe by morning.

For an empty place, it is loud. Fibers tighten with a noise like ropes being hauled. Membranes slap together somewhere beyond the ceiling. At intervals a rapid knocking runs around the chamber, so precisely spaced it seems to be circling you, though the walls do not move. Then a seam opens and the sound stops. Warm air pours out, carrying the smell of crushed leaves, and the seam closes again.

Nothing has built a shelter inside. The hollow is the shelter, and it has no occupant most days. Once in a while a long, nearly transparent grub passes through a pore. It carries a flake of sharp stone in its mouth and uses it to open the tough pods growing at the floor’s edge. The grub leaves no pile of husks: the root slowly draws the split pods back into itself. Finding the same flake again is difficult. Finding the pore is harder.

Near the newest crease, distance has not settled. From the middle of the hollow the wall looks a few steps away; walking toward it can take much longer. The knocking often reaches that crease before it is heard anywhere else. The grub avoids it. New leaves appear there most often, held out into the bare air as if the root, too, is trying to learn how much room it has made.
