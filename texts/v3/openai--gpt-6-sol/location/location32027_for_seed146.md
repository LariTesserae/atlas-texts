The floor of the hollow is warm enough to feel through a boot. It is not smooth. Fresh-edged shelves of black stone rise from it at odd angles, with narrow lanes of pale powder between them. Across one such shelf, the ceiling is only a little way above your head; beyond its lip, your voice would travel into a space too large to sound enclosed. There is no stream down there, no damp seam in the rock. The powder stays powder even where countless feet have pressed it flat.

Almost all of that distance is dark. Now and then a point of dull red appears on a far wall, holds for a few breaths, and goes out. Up close, these are small animals lodged in the stone. One loosens its grip and shows a belly like folded foil before settling elsewhere. The red comes from inside it. By its brief light, the wall proves crowded: ridges, holes, and bodies fitted among them so closely that a hand laid against the rock would touch several without finding one.

The creatures are plentiful, though the place seldom sounds inhabited. Broad, slow ones rasp at exposed mineral faces. Smaller ones gather at the grooves they leave, and thinner ones wait beneath overhangs to seize them. Some have shells; some are little more than jointed lengths of dark, tough skin. They carry what they need within themselves, feeding on the rock and on one another. Their movements come in short efforts separated by long rests. A falling flake may draw half a dozen out of their holes, yet even then the loudest noise is usually the flake striking stone.

The ledges and cracks have a newly broken look. Their corners catch at sleeves. Beneath them lie chips that still fit the scars above, undulled by falling grit. Nothing appears to be breaking now. The warm air hardly moves, and a dusting of powder on a low shelf may remain undisturbed for years unless a creature crosses it. Far down the hollow, whole walls stand at angles that should bring them down. They have stood that way longer than anyone who walks here can remember.

There are signs of those walkers, but not many. At intervals, stones have been stacked across a passage to make a low shelter, or set upright at a branching lane. The marks cut into them are shallow and plainly made by hand. Beside one shelter lies a chipped blade, its edge renewed so many times that little of the original stone remains. There are no fires, no heaps of stored food. The builders travel between known cracks in the wall, taking what they find there and leaving the larger chambers alone.

It is easy to lose the measure of the place. A shelf that seems near can take a day to reach; the red points on it stay much the same size as you approach. For hours nothing changes ahead but the arrangement of those lights. Then one goes out, and in the darkness left behind, something passes quietly across the lane.
