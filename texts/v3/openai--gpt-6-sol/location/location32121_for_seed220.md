Below the city is a chamber the size of a market square, though it has no floor in the ordinary sense. A narrow walkway follows its walls; inside the railing, darkness drops to a depth the lamps cannot establish. Streets, galleries, and service ducts are fitted into the chamber’s perimeter, one above another. From the walkway, the place feels almost empty. The city is all around you, set into the walls, and the middle belongs to air.

It is warm enough for moisture to gather on the handrails. Rain from the surface arrives hours or days after a storm, threading through the old stone in separate, patient streams. It has worn grooves beneath the drains and softened the corners of steps that were once precisely cut. Here and there, a little water rests in a tile hollow before slipping away. Nothing grows in it. The living things keep to the seams: a few dark fans of fungus behind a pipe, fine roots entering through a crack, small pale insects that withdraw when the metal shivers.

The shivering is nearly constant. Deep in the chamber, machinery moves loads between levels without cables or visible tracks. You can hear it before you can make out its effect: a heavy rushing note, then a blow like a gate closing, then a long rattle passing through the stone. At intervals the air presses briefly against your ears. Loose drops leap from the rail and fall into the dark. The system is old enough that its working has worn shallow, regular curves into the walls where decades of moving air carry grit against them.

Light appears only where a task requires it. A stair edge glows as someone approaches; a service door shows its outline and then goes black again. Most of the chamber remains unlit even when the machinery is busiest. Across the void, one lit landing may reveal another, and for a moment the distance between them seems manageable. Then the light goes out, and the noise fills it.

A small animal sometimes calls from the drains, sharply enough to be heard between the larger sounds. No answer follows. Water keeps arriving by its several routes, the machinery takes hold of it and of other things, and the old walls bear both the vibration and the slow wearing of the rain.
