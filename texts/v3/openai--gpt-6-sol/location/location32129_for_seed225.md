The hollow lies just below the surface, close enough that daylight reaches it but far enough that the weather arrives changed. A long crack in the roof admits a broad, pale beam. At noon it fills nearly the whole chamber; by evening it has climbed the opposite wall and left the floor warm to the touch. The limestone there is worn smooth, with shallow basins that hold rainwater for a day or two and then stand empty for weeks.

There is room to walk without brushing anything. A few tufts of grass grow where soil has sifted down through the crack. Ferns occupy the dampest seam. Elsewhere the floor is bare stone and fine, ochre grit. Old watercourses cross it in low, branching grooves, all leading toward a drain too narrow to enter. Their edges have softened over a very long time. Even the fallen stones seem settled, each one fitted into a shallow impression of its own making.

For most of the day the hollow sounds empty. Drops fall from the roof at intervals too widely spaced to form a rhythm. A wingbeat can be heard from one end of the chamber to the other. Yet there are animals everywhere if one stays still: pale lizards flat against the walls, beetles under the stones, moths resting high in the lit crack. Small birds descend to drink when the basins are full. They keep to the edges, leaving the bright middle oddly vacant.

Near one basin, the stone is scattered with thin flakes of flint. The lizards strike them against the limestone to open the hard-shelled insects that gather after rain. They do not keep the flakes or carry them far. The sharpest pieces remain where they were dropped, slowly becoming blunt beneath dust and passing feet. Nothing here resembles a dwelling.

When rain crosses the land above, the first sign below is not water but a cooler smell. Dark patches spread across the ceiling, and the old grooves begin to shine. Then, for an hour, the chamber has a narrow stream. By the next afternoon it is gone, and the sunlight returns to a floor that looks almost unchanged.
