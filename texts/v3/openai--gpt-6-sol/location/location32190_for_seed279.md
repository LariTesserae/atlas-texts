The basin lies low enough that water is never far below the stone. It shows itself in the seams: a dark line at the foot of a wall, a bead swelling beneath a ledge, a shallow pool where two slabs have settled apart. There is no river to follow. Water enters everywhere a little at a time, cold enough to cloud the air immediately above it.

Across the basin stand crowded ranks of stone fins. Some are taller than a person; others barely clear the ground. Their edges are sharp, their faces unworn, and broken fragments still fit against the scars they fell from. From a rise, the fins can be seen continuing for miles, though on the floor of the basin the next dozen are enough to hide the horizon. The pale stone is streaked with iron-dark bands. Rain has washed fine grit into every narrow space but has not yet softened the cuts.

Nothing grows in that grit. The pools have clear bottoms and no trailing green at their margins. Once in a while, a small white-shelled creature can be found in a wetted crack, so still it might be another chip of stone until it draws itself out of sight. Otherwise the place appears unoccupied. There are a few low arrangements of blocks, too level to have fallen that way: short walls without roofs, and passages that end against the fins. No one has cleared the fallen stone from them.

It is loud here, although little seems to move. Drops strike the deeper pools with a hollow, amplified knock. In some fins, narrow openings lead down into unseen chambers; air passing their wet mouths makes a continuous low note. One note fades and another takes it up farther across the basin, until it is difficult to tell whether the sound is travelling or the stone is answering itself. Between those tones are finer sounds—the faint crack of a cooling face, water easing through grit, a shell scraping inside a crevice. Standing still does not bring silence. It only gives the distant parts of the basin time to be heard.
