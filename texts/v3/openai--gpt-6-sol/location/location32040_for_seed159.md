The first thing visible from the ledge is distance. The chamber runs beyond the reach of a shout, its far walls divided by pillars and shelves of new stone. Above it, a long fracture in the roof admits daylight. The sun never appears, but for much of the day the cavern is bright enough to show the reddish dust on the higher slopes and the shine of water caught in low hollows.

The floor is still being made. Pale slabs lean against dark ones at angles too sharp to have lasted long. Some have broken cleanly away from the wall and carry its matching outline; others rise in buckled rows where pressure from below has lifted them. Warmth comes through the rock. Condensation beads on cooler faces and gathers at their feet, though the ledge itself stays dry enough to raise dust underfoot. From time to time rain finds the roof fracture. It falls in scattered columns, briefly turning the hollows into pools before draining through cracks in their bottoms.

There are no stems or leaves here. The wet stone is coated in places with a thin, dark growth, more like a stain than a garden, and every fresh trickle brings finer matter down from above. Wherever moisture and that matter meet, animals crowd in. Small pale grazers comb the coated rock in moving bands. Broad-backed creatures the size of dogs nose under loose plates, leaving them rocking behind. Above them, winged things shuttle between the pillars so densely that their shadows keep crossing the floor. What at first looks like a rough edge on a shelf resolves into hundreds of bodies, each holding on while the shelf trembles.

Their noise fills the chamber without hiding its other sounds. Wings clap close to the ears. Claws tick over stone. A deeper cracking travels through the floor and stops every moving creature for a breath; then the bands surge sideways, the flyers lift together, and somewhere out of sight a slab falls with a report that comes back from the far wall twice. By the time its echoes fade, animals have reached the fresh break. Some drink from the water released beneath it. Others pick at the newly exposed seam. A few are eaten there.

Along one wall stand three low enclosures of fitted stone. Their openings face uphill, away from the sudden run of water. A worn path joins them to a narrow bridge of logs and pegged stone, and a basket with a frayed handle lies where the path becomes too steep. Whoever built these things had no means to quiet the cavern. The bridge rattles as animals pass beneath it; grit falls into the empty enclosures; after rain, fresh tracks cross the old human ones.

Farther in, the daylight reaches a broad basin crowded with pillars still joined to the roof. The chamber is open there, but never still. Water starts and vanishes, crowds gather and scatter, and the rock gives a little under its own weight. On the ledge, you can feel that last motion through your soles before you hear it.
