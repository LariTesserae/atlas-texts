The ground is almost level, but it is not smooth. Low, blunt swells of dark stone rise from a plain of soft mineral earth, each with the rounded, unfinished look of something that has only just hardened. There are no gullies, no cut banks, no long seams of gravel left by a river. Water stays close to the surface instead. It beads on the stone, gathers in shallow prints made by roots, and stands in warm, leaf-choked hollows no wider than a few strides.

The air is hot enough that the dampness feels substantial. Under a ceiling of broad leaves, daylight arrives green and weak. Across the basin, the canopy looks unbroken except where pale trunks and the crowns of taller trees lift through it. Up close, everything occupies the space between: glossy shrubs against the trunks, vines laid over shrubs, ferns rising through fallen leaves before the leaves have lost their shape. Roots spread across the new-looking ground rather than disappearing into it. In places they make a floor of their own, a little above the wet soil.

Animals fill the gaps the plants leave. Small dark birds step along the roots, and larger ones move overhead with a dry, heavy flutter. Lizards hold still on the warm stone until insects pass. The hollows tremble with larvae and thin-legged creatures skating on their surfaces. There are so many wings in the understory that their separate sounds merge into a grainy hum. Beneath it come the repeated calls of frogs, a sharp knock from somewhere among the trunks, and the soft, continual disturbance of leaves by bodies too small to see.

Nothing here suggests a path, a clearing, or an old place to stand. Even the exposed stone has not had time—or perhaps has never had the force of moving water—to acquire a worn edge. Yet the basin does not feel young in the way a place feels temporary. Its shallow water, heavy shade, heat, and crowded life seem to hold one another in place. A drop falls from a leaf into a hollow; the rings reach the roots and vanish. Another drop is already forming.
