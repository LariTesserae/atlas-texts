The district occupies a cavity not much larger than a station hall. Its ceiling is low enough that the tallest roofs nearly touch it, and every remaining interval has been claimed: stairs against walls, balconies against stairs, pipes threaded through balconies, roots threaded through pipes. The rock is newly broken. Its edges still have the pale, sharp faces left by drills, without the rounded hollows that long years of dripping would make.

Heat comes through the floor. It rises from a bank of buried engines that almost never run at full speed, though the district was built for the day they might. In the dimness, their flywheels look motionless. Only a fine tremor in a handrail, or a belt advancing one tooth at a time, gives them away. Shops and dwellings crowd the machinery. A narrow foundry shares a wall with a kitchen; a rack of cooling molds forms the kitchen’s back door. There is little hammering here. People set things down carefully because a sound travels to every end of the cavity.

Moisture arrives less reliably than heat. On some days the ceiling beads with condensation and the whole district smells of warm stone and iron. On others, a person can walk beneath it without a drop falling, and the troughs beside the stairs are dry. Nothing stays exposed to chance. Roofs slope toward cistern mouths; window ledges bear small gutters; cloth is hung where it can gather dew. The larger pipes sweat even in a dry spell, and green growth has taken hold wherever a joint can be reached. Ferns fill the space under stair treads. Vines go up the foundry chimney, their leaves leathery and dark with dust. In a few courtyards scarcely wider than a doorway, trees press their roots through cracked paving and flatten their crowns against the stone above.

At first the place seems still. There are few animals to disturb it—an occasional pale moth at a lamp, something small heard once in the drains. Yet water is moving from ceiling to gutter to root, and roots are widening old seams in the mortar. A yellow crust forms slowly around each warm vent. The newest wall, built straight only a few years ago, already leans away from a tree.

The inhabitants leave narrow gaps beside their foundations rather than trying to seal them again. They know which roots will thicken and which ledges will drip when the heat changes. Their maps show streets and workshops, but also the dry places where a lamp can be kept, and the wet places worth saving.
