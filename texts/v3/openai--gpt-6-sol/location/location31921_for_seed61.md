Beneath a broad stone stair is a space no larger than a pantry. It takes a moment to understand that it is a town. Walls of fitted shale rise in close tiers around a central passage scarcely wide enough for a hand. There are doorways cut into the stone, shelves that might be floors, little steps worn hollow at their middles, and channels running beneath bridges made from single chips of rock. The uppermost roofs press against the underside of the stair.

Water comes through the stone rather than into the room. It gathers on lintels, beads along mortarless joints, and finds the same shallow grooves it has followed for longer than the stairs have existed. Everything feels cool. The walls are rounded where shoulders or water have passed them; it is hard to tell which did more of the wearing. Pale mineral crust thickens at the mouths of drains and has been scraped back with a blade again and again. Nothing green grows in the wet. The few open chambers hold only grit, stone tools, and cups turned upside down.

The place is loud. A narrow crack behind the lowest wall carries a continuous roar from water somewhere deeper underground. Close to it, the sound fills the little streets and makes the bridges tremble. Farther away, each empty doorway gives back a different, softer pitch. The drains contribute a steady ticking, drop after drop, though no channel ever seems to fill. Someone made this intricate arrangement with simple tools, adjusting stone against stone until the water could pass and the town could remain.

In the dimness, the streets look occupied before the eye learns their shapes. A dark mark under an arch is only a stain. A pale shape at the end of a lane is a scrap of worn limestone. Very rarely, a small shelled animal appears at a drain mouth, touches the wet edge, and withdraws. Otherwise the town holds its places: roofs close above roads, water close below them, the same great sound behind every wall.
