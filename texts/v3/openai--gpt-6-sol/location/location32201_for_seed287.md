The chamber lies a little way below the surface, though there is no straightforward descent into it anymore. The old access stair has worn into a sloping trough, its steps made shallow by years of grit and occasional runoff. At the bottom, the air turns hot and close. Water is scarce enough to leave a mark when it arrives: a darkening of stone, a brief line of drops along a cable, the smell of warm mineral dust becoming wet.

The chamber is about the size of a small grove. Its walls are rounded, not by excavation but by a much older passage of water. Stone shelves curve around the room at several heights, holding pockets of red soil and dense growth. Broad leaves overlap the rails of a narrow iron walkway. Pale roots grip the wall like bundled wire, crossing seams in the rock and descending toward a shallow basin at the center. There is no standing pool there. On most days the basin is a plate of cracked silt with one damp spot under a pipe.

A few structures remain among the plants: a brick pier, a switchbox swollen with rust, and three large pipes entering the wall at different angles. The pipes are still warm. Somewhere beyond them an engine runs, or did once; it is hard to tell from here. The chamber is loud with a continuous metallic roar, low enough to be felt through the walkway. Every so often a thinner note rises through it as a pipe slowly takes up heat. Nothing visible moves. A visitor can spend an hour beside the same leaf and hear the note climb only a little.

Dim amber light comes through grates high in the ceiling. It catches beads of condensation on the undersides of leaves, but little reaches the floor. The plants have grown toward those grates and around the pipes, leaving the old walkway passable only where people have broken stems back. Their foliage is abundant, almost crowded, yet their soil looks dry. They live on what the chamber gives them at its edges: moisture drawn out of heated air when a section of wall cools, or the brief rain that finds its way down the stair.

There are no insects in the leaves, no calls behind the pipes, no tracks in the silt. Fallen growth gathers where it lands and slowly darkens. When a drop finally falls into the central basin, its small sound cannot be heard over the roar. By morning the damp spot may have spread; by the following day it will be gone, and the roots nearest it will still be holding the shape of its passage.
