At the top of the lift there is a garden no larger than a railway platform. Its floor is a shallow iron bowl, its walls a ring of riveted arches, and beyond the arches there is only air. The mountains lie so far below that their ridges look pressed into the haze. Nothing here grows from the earth. The nearest earth is a long descent away.

A furnace somewhere beneath the bowl keeps the place warm. Its machinery is hidden, but not quiet: a continuous, battering roar rises through the floor, with a higher whistle that shifts when the wind crosses the arches. Speech is possible only close to someone’s ear. Even the small birds that nest in the bolt holes seem to sing by opening their beaks and trusting the sound to reach one another somehow.

The garden is lush along its edges. Thick vines wrap the arches and disappear into seams; ferns crowd the gutters; moss has covered the maker’s marks on the metal. In the middle, however, the bowl is almost bare. A single narrow path crosses it, leaving a great deal of open floor for such a small place. When clouds pass through the ring, droplets gather on the leaves and fall with a soft, invisible tapping beneath the engine noise. For days afterward the gutters remain wet. Then the warm air dries them, and the vines wait.

Everything bears the wear of a long arrangement. Rain has rounded the teeth of the drain grates. Iron has thinned where leaves hold moisture against it, and the oldest roots have found those weakened places without breaking the bowl. A maintenance ladder hangs outside one arch, polished at its top rungs and furred with rust below. It is difficult to tell whether anyone still comes up to tend the garden, or whether tending it now means leaving the machinery running.

Small moths shelter under the fern fronds. Beetles work in the rust. Birds arrive from below, stay until the next cold wind, and leave again. The vines remain, fed by water drawn out of passing cloud and by a little warmth leaking through the floor. High in all that empty sky, they have made the iron bowl their ground.
