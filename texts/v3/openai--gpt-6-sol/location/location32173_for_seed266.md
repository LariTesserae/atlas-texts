The plateau is so high that its edge looks less like a cliff than the end of a continent. Beyond it, lower ranges fade into a pale, level haze. Up here the light is ordinary and the air is warm, though thin enough that a person notices each breath. The ground keeps the day’s heat: black basalt under a powdering of red iron and white salt, all of it dry. There are no streams, no old channels waiting for rain. Where the stone is smooth, it has been polished by dust moving over it for ages.

Distances are difficult to judge. A line of low buildings may prove to be a city, its districts separated by miles of bare rock. Workshops and dwellings occupy the lee of ancient ridges. Rail tracks cross the open ground on stone piers, climbing gently for days toward furnaces at the plateau’s rim. The furnaces refine salts and metals brought up from deeper seams; their heat escapes through broad, quiet vents. From far away, the machinery is visible only as a tremor above the roofs. Put an ear to the ground, though, and the whole plateau carries its slow working hum.

There is no vegetation between the districts, but the empty ground is busy if one waits. Small plated animals gather where sun and shadow meet, taking mineral dust into fine combs along their mouths. Larger ones travel in loose files across the flats, stopping at fissures whose dry inner surfaces hold the simple chemistry on which they feed. At dusk, pale, many-jointed creatures emerge from beneath the rail piers to pick through warm slag. They are numerous without seeming crowded; each has an immense country in which to move. Most make almost no sound. The city’s workers learn to spot them by a change in the pattern of dust.

The plateau changes as they do—slowly. A ridge loses its sharpness over centuries. Salt gathers in the sheltered side of a wall until a doorway has to be raised. Old roads remain passable long after the districts they served have gone, and animals use them alongside the trains. Here, weather is less an event than a direction: the patient drift of grains across stone, carrying the mountain away without ever making the mountain seem smaller.
