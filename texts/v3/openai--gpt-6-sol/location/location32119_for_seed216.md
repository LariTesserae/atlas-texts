At this height the air is warm enough to smell of wet bark. Clouds pass below the walkways as often as above them, and the light that reaches the interior is green and poor. You can spend a day crossing this country without setting foot on earth.

Its floor is a lattice of living trunks, roots, and broad, tough leaves, joined by pegs of thornwood and lashings that have long since taken root. Nothing in it appears to have grown where it stands. The oldest platforms sag around the shape of earlier platforms; new stems have been trained across the gaps, and their joints have thickened into knees. Here and there a cut end still shows the patient, shallow marks of a stone blade. Moss has closed over most of the work, but not its intention.

From within, the place seems crowded with plants. Leaves line every passage. Ferns grow out of seams and hang in curtains across openings. From the edge, its emptiness becomes clear. The green is a thin, inhabited skin stretched over enormous spaces. A bridge a hundred paces wide may have nothing beneath it but cloud for miles. Across one of those gaps, another slope of foliage hangs in the dimness, its trees no larger than grass from here. Some hollows are so broad they keep their own weather: a warm mist rising slowly from one side while a cold rain falls along the other.

Water travels through the construction more readily than across it. Rain enters the leaf roofs, collects in cupped branches, and seeps down through miles of hollow stems. After a storm, the walls weep. Pools appear on the floors of chambers that were dry an hour before, then drain away with a faint, intermittent knocking. In older districts the wood is swollen and dark, its surfaces worn smooth by feet, dripping water, and the small mouths of grazing animals. The builders have learned which spans can bear a reservoir and which must be pierced to let water through.

Most sounds are close sounds. A drop landing in a leaf cup. Fibers tightening as the lattice takes up moisture. Something chewing steadily on the far side of a wall. There are many animals, though the open air keeps them out of sight: pale gliders that sleep in folded leaves, slow climbers that crop the walkways back to a usable width, and long-beaked things that reach into wet joints for grubs. At dusk they move through the green in such numbers that the passages stir, while the vast hollows remain silent.

Paths here are maintained as much by habit as by craft. Each season, hands bend the new growth toward the old pegs; animals carry seeds in their coats; roots follow the damp channels left by previous roots. A neglected span does not simply fall. It turns toward the light, closes its entrances, and becomes difficult to find.

Distance is less dependable. From some balconies a neighboring platform can be seen across a narrow shaft, every leaf distinct, yet reaching it requires a journey of several days. In other places, a passage turns around a trunk and arrives on a slope that had been visible far below. The joints in the wood are continuous; a traveler can trail a hand along them all the way through. Water seems to know these turns. Pour it into a channel and it may be heard shortly afterward in a basin that ought to be miles away.

The people who mend the lattice mark such places with plaited reeds. They do not try to straighten them. A shortcut is useful while it lasts, and a basin that receives unexpected water can support a garden. On the high, dim edges of the country, fresh green ties hang beside old ones, showing where someone has begun to train a bridge toward an opening no one can yet reach.
