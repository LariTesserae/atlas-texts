The town occupies a shallow hollow no wider than a grove. From its edge, you can see nearly all of it: a square, three long lanes, and a few roofless halls set far apart. There is room to cross the square without brushing anything. Yet there is no bare ground. The paving is a single tough, warm leaf, worn thin along the routes that creatures take. The walls are folded stems; their old seams have rounded over, and new growth follows the same corners year after year.

Nothing here was cut and assembled. The halls grew around pockets of air, and the lanes remain open because the leaf beneath them tastes bitter. At each intersection stands a thick stalk with a cupped top. After rain, the cups hold water for a day or two. Most mornings they hold only condensation, which slips down grooves in the stalks and vanishes into the paving. Beyond the hollow the land is low and gently wrinkled, its soft ridges fixed by generations of roots.

The noise is astonishing. A steady roar comes from inside the walls, as though every hall contains a waterfall, though the largest pool is no wider than a hand. Burrowing animals drum on the taut leaf underfoot. Insects rasp from chambers in the stalks. Flocks arrive suddenly at the square, exchange shrill calls, and depart; the roar continues unchanged. It presses against the chest and makes the little town feel much larger than it is.

There are few creatures in sight at any one moment. They travel through the living masonry, feeding on its sweet inner layers and carrying pollen between halls. Now and then a wall gives a slow shudder as something large turns inside it. Small hunters wait at the openings where the moisture runs. The town feeds them too, drawing a bitter fold shut around an unwary one before slowly opening again.

Sound has an odd habit here. A footstep can be heard at the far end of a lane before the foot comes down. Sometimes a call seems to bend a corner that the lane itself does not have; follow it, and for a moment the hall ahead stands where the square should be. The old paths always return. Their surfaces have been polished by innumerable crossings, and the animals use them without hesitation. In the warm daylight, with water drying from the cups and the walls roaring around the empty square, the place goes on keeping its shape.
