From the ridge, the city looks sparse. Its towers stand far apart on a high, nearly level expanse, with long streets of pale daylight between them. Only when you descend do you see that the streets are alive. Their paving is a continuous mat of tight, grey-green stems; the gutters are folded leaves, and the towers are grown columns whose outer layers have hardened into something as smooth and load-bearing as stone. There is no bare ground. In places where the mat has worn thin, another layer of roots shows beneath it.

The plateau has been weathering for so long that it has almost lost the shape of a mountain. Its edges fall away in broad, softened steps, and the city follows them: terraces of old growth, each one lower and wider than the last. The largest structures occupy the oldest terraces. Their trunks are immensely thick but not crowded together. Between a mill and its nearest neighbor may be an acre of open stem-pavement, interrupted by a single low basin that catches rain. Water is scarce enough to be kept there. After a shower, droplets hang for days under the leaf-roofs, and thin pipes draw condensation down into the roots.

At noon the place is bright, cool, and loud. Great toothed wheels turn inside the mills, driven by pressure in the trunks. Belts made from braided fiber slap against pulleys. A pumping house gives a slow, chest-deep thump; farther off, the drying sheds rattle as their vents open and close. None of this makes the city seem busy in the ordinary way. You can walk a whole street without meeting anyone. Once in a while a small dark bird crosses the sky, its call lost immediately in the machinery. The leaves, meanwhile, keep shifting: thousands of little adjustments that send patches of sun traveling over the pavement.

Distances are less reliable than they look. A tower visible across an empty square can take an hour to reach in the morning and ten minutes in the afternoon. The old streets do not bend, and the tower does not move. Yet some days the root mat grows an additional stretch beneath your feet, complete with the scuffs left there the day before. Workers use the mills as landmarks because a turning wheel can always be heard from the same distance away, even when its building cannot be seen.

Near the plateau’s rim, the engines grow quieter. The softened terraces open onto a view of lower country and passing cloud. There the city’s oldest roots lie exposed in long parallel bands, gripping an edge that has not noticeably changed in anyone’s memory. They look worn almost to nothing. In the cool air, their fine tips are still growing.
