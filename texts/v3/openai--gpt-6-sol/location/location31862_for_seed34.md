The city stands on a high shelf of stone so broad that its far quarters disappear into weather. From the rim, its roofs look less like an interruption in the landscape than another kind of terrain: low, close-set terraces following the old contours, their edges softened by centuries of rain. Beyond them lie pale uplands and a succession of shallow valleys. A shower can pass over one district while the next remains in clear daylight.

There are few trees. The ground is too thin and stony for them except where soil has gathered behind a wall; there, a small grove may mark the course of a buried spring. Grass grows in seams and on neglected roofs, but most of the green is moss, darkening the north faces of steps and cisterns. The city’s stone is everywhere wet. Water beads beneath ledges, runs down worn stairways, and appears between paving slabs hours after the rain has stopped. It is near the surface throughout the plateau, finding its way through layers of rock that slope almost too gently to see.

The streets were laid to make room for it. Some are narrow channels with a dry walking edge; others cross broad, open basins where floodwater can spread without reaching the houses. Stone bridges rise only a little above the paving. Walls are fitted without mortar, their joints left to drain. Nothing here seems newly cut. Thresholds have hollowed under feet, corners have rounded, and the channels have acquired smooth, dependable bends. After a storm, people clear grit from the mouths of drains and lift fallen stones back into place. By evening, water that covered a square in the morning may be slipping quietly beneath it.

The city is densely settled, though seldom loud. Courtyards open unexpectedly from the tight streets, full of damp light and the smell of cold limestone. Cloth hangs under roofed passages rather than outside, where a brief clear sky offers no promise. A door closes; someone calls from one terrace to another; far off, a trickle falls into a cistern. On the heights above the last houses, the wind is more audible than any of these sounds. Birds are scarce, and a small animal crossing an empty stair can draw the eye a long way.

In the afternoon the separate showers travel across the plateau, visible by the darkening of roofs and the sudden shine of roads. Mist rises afterward from the lower courts. The city keeps changing its surface—dry gray, rain-black, silver where water crosses stone—while its shape remains much as it has been for generations.
