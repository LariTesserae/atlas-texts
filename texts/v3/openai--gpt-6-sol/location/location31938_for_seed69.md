The ground gives before it holds. A boot sinks through a mat of wet stems, meets stone, then slides toward water running in a groove worn smooth by centuries. The plateau is high, though there is no horizon to show it. You know by the thinness of a full breath, by the pressure in your ears when the weather changes, and by the long delay before anything dropped over the rim makes a sound below.

Darkness here is complete. The rain has weight and temperature, but no color. No firefly answers the hand waved in front of your face. The broad, soft leaves that crowd the paths are not reaching for light; their roots feed among warm mineral seeps and the great black mats of microbes that grow wherever water passes through the rock. Leaves collect rain and fallen matter. Some fold shut when a footfall shakes their stems, passing the disturbance from plant to plant with a dry, rapid patter.

Water is almost everywhere, but seldom still. It threads between the roots, wells up through old paving, and falls in curtains from ledges too far away to touch. Beneath its nearer sounds is a deeper one: a continuous, uneven pounding that travels through the soles of the feet. Generations ago, engines were set into the plateau’s natural channels. They catch the descent of its rivers and send power through cables buried under the marsh. Only their service mouths stand above ground now—low concrete housings, widely separated, with doors that thrum against their seals. A warm pipe can be found by following the insects clustered on it. A failing bearing can be heard from miles away.

The machines do not command the place. Silt fills their intakes; roots lever open their joints; small pale animals nest in the dry chambers above the turbines. Crews come by cable cart when a station begins to knock out of rhythm. They work by touch and by calls shouted into speaking tubes, then leave again before the next rise of the rivers.

Sound is the surest measure of distance, though it is never quite trustworthy. Frogs call from water held in hollows of the stone. Winged hunters make brief, hard clicks over the reeds. Farther out, something large works its way through the shallows with a noise like a sheet being dragged across gravel. Rain moves across the plateau in broad bands; you hear each band arriving through the vegetation long before it reaches you. When a storm passes over an old ravine, the whole cut becomes an organ pipe, and its note can drown the engines.

The paths endure because feet keep finding them, not because anyone can see where they go. They follow the patient shapes of the rock: down around a rounded wall, over a saddle polished by overflow, past a pool that has been deepening so slowly that an abandoned handrail still descends into it. In the dark, people sometimes mistake the rail for a root. The root is more likely to move.
