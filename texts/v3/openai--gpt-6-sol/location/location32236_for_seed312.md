From a distance, the high country looks almost bare: pale ridges, dark scree, a little snow held in the folds that face away from the sun. Only as the air clears do the settlements appear. They cover the basin floor and climb the lower slopes, not as a few isolated towns but as a long succession of walls, roofs, yards, and narrow streets. Between them lie stretches of raw stone broad enough to have their own weather.

The mountains here are sharp. Broken faces show fresh bands of gray and rust-colored rock; below them, fans of debris spread across older paths. A fallen boulder may still have its pale underside exposed. Builders take stone from these falls, choosing pieces that already have a flat face, and fit them into thick walls without mortar. Roofs are low and weighted with more stone against the winter winds. Where houses crowd together, a person can pass for a long way through lanes scarcely wider than their shoulders, emerging now and then into a sunlit yard full of animals.

There is little growing beyond coarse grass, thorny shrubs, and plants tucked into the shelter of walls. Water arrives unevenly. A rain shower may darken one hillside while the next stays dusty; snowmelt runs for a few weeks in shallow channels and then disappears beneath gravel. Every district has covered cisterns. Small stone gutters lead to them from roofs and paved courts, and the cleanest ones are kept for drinking. In the morning, frost rims their lids even when the sun is bright.

The basin is populous, though it seldom sounds loud. Sheep and shaggy goats fill the outer yards and graze far up the slopes. Birds nest under roof edges and gather around threshing floors. Small furred animals live in the rubble heaps, quick to vanish when a shadow crosses them. Across the open ground, herds move so slowly that their shape seems unchanged from one hour to the next. Their bells carry farther than voices; from inside the lanes, even those are faint.

Life follows the water and the cold. People repair channels before the thaw, stack fuel well before autumn, and leave certain gullies unbuilt because stones still come down them after rain. Some streets end abruptly at fresh scree; others have been cleared and laid again a few paces to one side. The settlements are old enough to have familiar routes and named corners, but the mountains have not finished settling. At dusk, the walls keep a little of the day’s warmth. Beyond them, the stone slopes lose their color, and the immense basin grows cold all at once.
