High on the rim of an old caldera, a cleft opens beneath a shelf of black stone. It is no wider than a small hall, but its floor falls away in close-set steps, each one crowded with holes. Heat comes up through them. Outside, the plateau bakes under the sun; inside, the shelf keeps almost all light out, and the heat has nowhere to go.

The walls have had a long time to settle into their shape. Soft deposits have blown away, leaving ridges of hard, dark mineral and thin pale bands that flake when touched. Dust gathers in the sheltered corners, then vanishes in the next warm updraft. Nothing grows here. There is no damp seam, no stain left by a pool. A few low rings of fitted stones interrupt the natural ledges. Their joints are too deliberate to be cracks, though many of the stones have worn nearly flush with the floor.

The darkness is occupied from top to bottom. Small, broad-bodied animals cling under every lip of rock; larger ones fill the passages between the steps, pressed so closely together that a movement in one passage travels across the cleft. Their backs are dull as ash, their undersides pale. Now and then one turns, and the faint red of a heated fissure catches its eyes. Others have no visible eyes at all. They feel along the stone with the fine hooks of their feet.

For so many bodies, the place is remarkably quiet. There is a soft rasp when a crowd changes its hold, the occasional tap of a shell against stone. In the deeper holes, creatures feed at warm mineral crusts. Those nearer the entrance wait for them to emerge. Nothing rushes. The hunters ease forward while their prey is still intent on the rock; the prey withdraw one foothold at a time.

The updraft strengthens toward evening. Fine grit lifts from the floor, and the animals shift to new ledges before the hottest cracks begin to glow. A visitor standing still would first notice how little sound they make. After a while, they would notice there is scarcely a patch of bare stone left to stand on.
