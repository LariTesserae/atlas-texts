From the ridge, the valley looks green until the light changes. Then its straight edges appear: roofs set close as scales, retaining walls below them, bridges crossing streets that cross older bridges. Nothing exposed is quite bare ground. The slopes have been faced in brick and concrete for so long that moss has softened their joints, and trees rise from deep municipal planting beds as if from the hillside itself.

Rain has stopped, but water keeps arriving. It beads along the undersides of gantries, spills from one terrace into a gutter on the next, and shows through the seams of stairways worn hollow by generations of boots. At the bottom of the valley, a canal passes between warehouses with its surface almost level with the quay. Its banks are planted thickly with willow and alder. Up here, vegetables fill narrow yards, vines occupy fences, and ferns grow in the damp corners behind transformer housings. Every green place has a wall or a drain beneath it.

The town is packed from rim to rim. Mills, flats, depots, allotments, substations, covered markets: each stands against another or over the remains of one. Yet the view extends for miles, through successive folds of the high country, where more roofs and chimneys appear between belts of trees. Low cloud holds the afternoon dim. Windows take turns brightening and going dark as it passes.

At first the place seems still. Then a freight train emerges halfway up the opposite slope and is hidden again; the canal gates open, drawing a dark line through the floating leaves; trucks file out of a depot while other trucks back in. Crews are shifting barriers around a street where runoff has lifted the paving. A crane turns above them, slow enough to lose track of, and the rainwater changes course through their work. The valley is always being cleared, loaded, patched, and planted. Its weather gives everyone a new job before the old one is finished.

Most of this happens at a distance and makes little noise here. Wet leaves muffle the road below. There is a faint hum from the substations, a brief iron knock when a train takes the curve, water ticking into a covered drain. No birds call from the trees. Once, near the canal, a heron lifts away from the crowded bank; it is visible for a moment against the pale roofs, then disappears among them.
