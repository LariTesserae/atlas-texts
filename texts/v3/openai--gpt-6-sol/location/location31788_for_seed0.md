At night the plain has no horizon. It is broad and low, but everything close at hand interrupts the view: fans of dark leaves, reeds taller than a person, new ridges of black stone with their edges still sharp. Beyond them lie more thickets and more stone, stretching for miles. There are no paths. The animals make passages through the growth, and by morning some of those passages have moved.

The ground is young. In places, roots have taken hold in a skin of soil over recently cooled rock; elsewhere the rock is bare and still clean enough to show every fold of its forming. Small cracks exhale warmth. Water is scarce in the open, though broad leaves collect it all night and shake it down in heavy drops. Shallow pools gather in hollows after rain, then vanish into the fissures. The vegetation grows most fiercely along those hidden seams of moisture, so that a line of immense ferns can end abruptly at dry, hard ground.

The noise makes distance difficult to judge. Insects rasp from every layer of the thicket. Frogs call from pools too small to find in the dark. Above them comes the clatter of animals travelling through the crowns: branches striking branches, feet landing, fruit falling, alarm cries passed rapidly from one end of the plain to the other. On the ground, heavier bodies push through reeds with a sustained tearing sound. When one group bolts, others bolt too, and for a while the whole place seems to be running.

Little light reaches below the leaves, even before clouds cover the sky. Pale insects flash among the trunks, briefly showing a wet flank or the inside of an open flower. Warm air carries the smell of bruised greenery and fresh mineral dust. At a pool’s edge, small clever animals turn stones to break hard-shelled prey; when they leave, the stones remain where they dropped them. Nothing here has been built.

The plain changes fastest at its bare margins. A crack opens after a hot day. Rain loosens a lip of soil and a stand of reeds leans into the gap. Seeds settle there; insects arrive; by the next season, roots may have hidden the break entirely. Then a herd blunders through, stripping leaves and exposing the sharp black ground again. In the darkness it is hard to tell whether the tremendous sound is the land being inhabited or the land being made.
