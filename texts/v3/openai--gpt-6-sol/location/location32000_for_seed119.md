The place is a shallow court, no larger than a village square, enclosed by walls of stacked, unglazed brick. The walls lean inward a little. Their edges have softened over so many wet seasons that the court looks less built than worn into being, though there is no earth showing anywhere: underfoot are fitted clay plates, each one made by hand, each one settling against its neighbours. In the hollows between them, rain remains as narrow strips of black water.

There is little to interrupt the floor. A few reeds grow from silt caught in a corner, and a pale moss darkens the lower courses of brick. Most of the court is open, dim, and cool. Water seeps through the walls after rain and runs down their faces in threads; as it reaches the floor, the threads join shallow channels pressed into the clay long ago. None leads out. The water gathers, sinks slowly through the joints, and returns with the next rain.

The noise comes from the walls. Holes left between certain bricks take the wind and give it back as a low, uneven hooting. Drips strike the clay plates with different notes. Above these sounds are the animals: swifts crowded beneath the coping, frogs calling from cracks scarcely deep enough to hide them, and small, hard-shelled creatures clicking across the wet floor. At dusk the swifts pour into the empty middle, circle once or twice, and vanish through openings too narrow to see from below. The court feels vacant until something moves; then it seems to have been occupied all along.

Someone made every part of it, but nothing here is kept clean or put back as it was. A plate tilts, a seam widens, an animal finds a place in the seam. The old channels still carry water, though not always where their makers intended. In the poor light, listening to the wind work through the bricks, it is difficult to tell which sounds belong to the court and which belong to the creatures that have made use of it.
