The ridge stands high above the ocean floor, though nothing here breaks the surface. It is an enormous drowned height, its upper terraces suspended over a drop too deep for the eye to follow. The water is cool and nearly black. Looking outward, one can make out a distant row of pale uprights, then only the faint difference between open water and the darkness below.

There is no exposed stone. The ridge was raised, course by course, from the tough bodies of plants: woven roots, hollow stems, broad leaves pressed flat and grown through with new roots. Some walls are still green where they meet a seam of faint light. Most have aged to a dull brown that gives a little beneath the hand but will not come apart. The terraces are broad and almost empty. A single column may stand for a hundred paces, supporting a high, continuous canopy of interlaced growth. Between columns, the water has room to feel immense.

These are old works, though no tool marks remain. Each ledge has been worn to a shallow, even curve; each opening in the plantwork has held its shape for so long that new growth follows it exactly. Small animals live everywhere in the emptiness: silver fish crossing a terrace one at a time, pale grazers tucked into the walls, long-bodied swimmers moving just under the canopy. Their number becomes apparent only when they turn and catch the scant light.

The ridge is loud. Water passing through its hollow supports produces a steady, low roar, and above it comes the dry clicking of grazers feeding inside the walls. Neither sound seems to rise or fade. Silt rests undisturbed in the corners, and the hanging leaves point the same way without stirring. The place gives the impression of having reached an arrangement it can keep: plants holding up the height, animals keeping the passages clear, water sounding through them without wearing them away.
