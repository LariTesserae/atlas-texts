At first the plateau seems empty. The ground runs level for a long while, then falls away in enormous, shallow steps whose edges have been rounded by ages of wind. Nothing stands high enough to break the horizon. In the dark, distances are given instead by a few dull red glows along the step faces, and by the time it takes a sound to arrive.

The rock is warm underfoot, a fine-grained black stone worn smooth where the wind has kept it bare. Elsewhere it has cracked into loose plates that click when stepped on. Rain comes here, but not often, and rarely reaches far into the ground. After sunset, moisture gathers on the underside of the plates and in the shelter of low scarps. By morning there may be water in a hollow no wider than a hand, though the open ground around it is dry.

The sparse growth depends on those hollows. Pale, stiff-leaved plants follow the cracks in widely spaced lines; their roots go deep, and their upper leaves lie almost flat against the stone. Seen from above, they would trace old fractures across the plateau. Small animals know every line. They move along them to feed and to find water, and larger animals wait where two lines meet.

There are more creatures here than the empty view suggests. Much of the life of the plateau travels. At intervals, the ground gives a low shudder, and a broad herd passes somewhere beyond sight. Their feet make little noise on the stone, but their bodies carry organs that pulse with a weak electric light. One moving glow appears on the horizon, then another, until a whole stretch of darkness is marked by them. Smaller night hunters follow at a distance. Above both, wings cross the stars without a cry.

The shudder also comes when nothing is passing. Deep below the plateau, hot stone presses through a series of long faults. Gas escapes from narrow openings in the step faces, and the pressure rises and falls with a regularity that gives the place its own working rhythm. A vent will begin with a faint hiss, settle into a heavy thrum, then stop so abruptly that the quiet seems to widen. Along some cliffs, mineral deposits have grown into thin vanes. They vibrate when the vents are active, producing a distant sound like many engines running behind a wall. There are no roads or buildings, and no sign that anything has tended the machinery; the plateau has made these rhythms itself.

Warm air climbs from the lower steps and cools as it spreads across the height. On some nights it lays a thin veil of mist in the old drainage channels. The mist lasts only until the stone takes its warmth back from the air. For that brief time, drinking places appear where there were none, and the animals’ paths change. The quiet becomes full of small movements: feet among plates of rock, a wing dipping low, water lapped from a crack. Then the moisture is gone, and the vast dark surface looks still again.
