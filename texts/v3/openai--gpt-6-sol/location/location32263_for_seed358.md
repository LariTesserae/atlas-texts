Beneath the river plain is a chamber too broad to feel like a cave. Its roof rests on close-set pillars of limestone left by a quarry, and beyond them are more pillars, fading into warm, gray air. There is no clear view across it, though the floor runs nearly level for miles. Water stands ankle-deep in some aisles and disappears beneath soft mud in others. From the ceiling it falls in threads, seeps down the pillars, and gathers on every exposed edge before dropping again.

The place has only recently been opened to the river. The old quarry floor is still raw in patches, its machine cuts sharp under a skin of silt. Elsewhere, the first low banks are assembling: clay caught behind a fallen block, pale grit settling around a pillar, a shallow channel finding its way between them. Nothing has had time to wear smooth. The banks look temporary, but the water that builds them moves so slowly it is hard to see it move at all.

A few pump houses stand on raised concrete pads, linked by cables and narrow walkways. Their lamps make small, dull circles; outside them, the darkness is crowded. Roots hang from cracks overhead. Fish press through the flooded aisles in tight schools, and crayfish occupy the gaps between stones. Frogs have found the warmer ledges near the pumps. Insects collect around each lamp, while bats cling in such numbers to one stretch of roof that the stone there appears darker and rougher than elsewhere. Green things are scarce: a little moss by a ventilation shaft, a pale shoot carried down in floodwater and lodged where it cannot grow.

The noise leaves no quiet place to stand. Pumps throb through the floor; water beats on sheet metal, plinks into pools, and pours steadily through a distant breach. Frogs and bats add their calls, but even those are swallowed by the larger roar. The machines may run for weeks before anyone comes down the walkway to check them. Between visits, silt rises by fractions, water marks climb and recede on the pillars, and the chamber patiently acquires a floor of its own.
