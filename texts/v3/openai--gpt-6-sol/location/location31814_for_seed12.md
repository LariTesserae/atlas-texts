The town lies on the crown of a mountain, though there is no view of the mountain from its streets. Water covers everything. Above the roofs, the sea continues upward into a green dimness; below them, the new black slope falls away so steeply that the lower houses seem to hang over empty space.

This is a young summit. Its rock has cooled into folds and blunt, swollen ledges, but almost nothing has had time to wear them down. A ridge still bears the shape of something once soft and moving. Fine sediment has gathered only in sheltered pockets. Elsewhere, gardens take hold directly on stone: broad ribbons of weed fixed in cracks, pale fans spreading across warm faces, cords of root and frond thick enough to hide a doorway. The growth is luxuriant without being old. There are few trunks of dead material beneath it, few deep beds of soil. If a plant lets go, it drifts away whole.

Houses occupy the gentler shelves. Their frames are poles bound with fiber; their walls are woven mats weighted along the bottom with stones. Some have roofs of overlapping shells and clay tiles, though a roof here is chiefly a way to keep falling silt out. Streets are gaps left deliberately between gardens. Knotted lines run beside them from house to house, useful when the water darkens and one must find the way by hand. Higher up, where the ridge is narrow, a square of bare rock has been kept clear. Markers stand around its edge, each a different shape to the touch.

The water is warm against the skin, warmer still near the little seams in the rock. It does not boil or visibly rise. Over years, it lays a faint mineral crust on the lowest bindings, so that repairs become part of the mountain. The inhabitants cut the crust away with stone blades, or leave it to stiffen a wall. They build lightly elsewhere: a house too firmly attached to an unfinished ledge may be pulled apart as the ledge settles.

There is little to hear. A loose shell taps a post. A frond brushes another frond. Work travels through the town as small, dull knocks, with long quiet intervals between them. Fish are uncommon. Now and then one appears beneath a garden canopy, startling by how quickly it crosses a place where everything else moves slowly.

From the upper streets, the settlement can be followed along the ridge until its last ropes and planted stones fade into green. Beyond that are other rises, just visible, with no houses on them yet. People have begun tying guide lines in their direction. It will be a long time before there are streets.
