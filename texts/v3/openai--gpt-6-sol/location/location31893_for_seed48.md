The town stands on a low coast where the ground never quite dries. Rainwater lies in shallow hollows between warehouses, and the tide pushes into the storm drains twice a day. Concrete aprons slope almost imperceptibly toward the harbor. Their edges have softened over decades: steps are rounded, mortar is missing from the lowest walls, and a dark line on every piling marks the usual height of the water. Beyond the last loading shed, the shore is bare mud and broken shell.

It is hot even under the heavy cloud. Light reaches the streets as a dull reflection from puddles and corrugated roofs. There are no trees along the roads, no weeds in the cracks. Salt, paving, and the constant passage of vehicles have left little place for them. Buildings occupy most of the shoreline, but they are set far apart—long sheds, a pumping station, a row of tanks—with broad, nearly empty roads between. An engine starts somewhere behind a wall, runs for a few minutes, and stops.

The animals are easiest to see when nothing else is moving. Crabs cover the mud at the foot of the quay; when a truck crosses the bridge, they vanish into holes together. Mussels crowd the submerged pilings, fish turn in the brown water below them, and rats follow the drainage channels through the town. Gulls line the warehouse roofs so closely that, from the road, the roof edges appear white. In the heat of the afternoon even they are quiet. Now and then one drops to the water, and the others watch.

Work here follows the tide. Gates on the drains are opened and shut, catches are brought through the sheds, and wash water runs back toward the harbor. In between, the place seems unattended, though it is full of small movements: claws sorting through shell grit, ripples under a loading ramp, a rat crossing a road wide enough for six trucks. Then rain begins again, soft against the roofs, and fresh water briefly covers the salt marks before finding its way down to the sea.
