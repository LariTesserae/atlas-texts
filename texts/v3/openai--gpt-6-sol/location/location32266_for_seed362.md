The plain is low enough that nothing interrupts the horizon, though it is difficult to see very far. Leaves stand between you and every distant object. They are pale, thick, and nearly upright, packed in ranks like the fins of a great cooling apparatus. Their upper edges catch the light and shine white; beneath them the ground is cool and greenish and crowded with stems.

There is no soil. Underfoot, old stems have been pressed into hard courses, drilled through with channels and faced here and there with ceramic tile. New roots enter the holes prepared for them. Where a root grows too broad, a tile lifts, is carried a little way by the pressure, and settles into another socket. The surface has been doing this for so long that its ridges are rounded and its unused fittings have filled with fine dust. From a height, the whole continent would resemble a single, immensely worn floor.

The plants are dry. Cut one and it gives up a thread of amber resin, not a drop. Their leaves take in light and airborne mineral dust; deep in their ribs, a slow electrical charge draws salts from one chamber to the next. In the brightest hours the leaf ranks turn by a few degrees. They do it together over such an extent that a band of silver travels across the plain. The air remains cool, even under the fierce white glare.

Factories stand among the leaves, though at first they look like denser groves. Their towers are braided trunks braced with dark metal collars. Wide rollers flatten the season’s thick growth into panels, and articulated arms set those panels into the expanding floor. Elsewhere, narrow machines comb dust from the leaf edges and feed it into root beds. There are miles of this work: rows being raised, old rows taken apart, corridors opened for the passage of loaded frames. Most of it is nearly silent. The rollers move slowly; the frames run on soft tracks. If you stop walking, you may hear a light tick as a collar cools, then the faint, continuous rasp of leaves turning past one another.

No rain has ever worn these surfaces. Wind does the weathering, carrying grains so fine they polish the exposed metal and cloud the oldest glass. At intervals it brings a larger fall of dust, turning the brightness yellow. The leaves close their edges against it. Hours later they open, and the combing machines begin their patient rounds.

There are animals here, but few. A small six-legged thing sometimes crosses a lit aisle, feeling at the seams with its forefeet. It feeds on the soft insulation around old roots. You may follow it into the shade and come out beside a row you passed an hour ago, although you have not turned. Workers mark such aisles with paired black stakes. A single stake means the corridor is safe in one direction only.

Far out on the plain, the ranks rise and fall in broad shallow waves. Between one wave and the next is a strip of brightness where nothing casts a shadow. New panels are made there and carried outward; old leaves are brought back, ground down, and given to the roots. The strip seems close in the morning and very far away by afternoon. The loaded frames still reach it on schedule.
