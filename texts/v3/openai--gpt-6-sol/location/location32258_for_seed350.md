At first it looks like a flooded industrial plain. The water is warm and nearly black, and rows of young concrete ridges run away into it until the dimness takes them. Steel posts stand here and there, each carrying a transformer or a coil of pipe. There is no continuous factory, only these scattered stations, but something on an industrial scale is happening between them. Current moves through the plain in broad, forceful sheets. It presses the reeds flat in one direction, releases them, then drives them back the other way.

The ground is recent. Its ridges still have the sharp corners of poured material; shelves of pale silt end in clean breaks rather than worn slopes. Some of it was made this morning. A seam opens along the bottom, lifts a slab of concrete-colored stone a few feet, and closes beneath it. Roots are caught in the join. By the time the water clears, they have already spread over the new surface.

Vegetation fills nearly every space the structures leave. Thick green cords climb the posts and pass through broken instrument housings. Broad translucent leaves hang in overlapping layers, their veins lit faintly by the charge in the water. Fields of narrow stems grow from the silt, so dense that a change in current travels across them as a visible crease. Nothing grazes them. No fish pass between them, no small bodies stir the sediment. Leaves tear loose, drift into the dark, and keep growing from their torn edges.

The noise is difficult to place. Underwater, it arrives through the ribs and teeth: a continuous engine-roar with hard blows inside it, like heavy machinery striking from miles away and inches away at once. Close to a post, the roar divides into several rhythms. One is the cycling of a pump. Another follows the pulses of light in the leaves. A third continues after its source stops.

The stations have doors, inspection plates, warning paint. Most are too small to account for the force moving through the plain. One pump turns in an open frame with no pipe attached; each revolution draws a column of water downward, although there is no opening in the ground. Beside another station, a cable hangs from a gantry and ends in a clean-cut copper tip. The cable is taut, and the gantry strains toward whatever holds the other end.

Distance is unreliable here, though the plain is wide enough to make distance matter. A ridge glimpsed far across the reeds may be beside the next post when the current shifts. The post remains where it was; the reeds between have not moved. Sometimes a slab rises with a coating of old rust and a mature stand of roots, then settles back showing fresh stone. The plain seems to be forming and wearing out at the same time, in different orders from one place to the next.

There are intervals when the engines fall briefly out of step. In those moments the water becomes clear for hundreds of yards. The scattered stations appear among the green growth, and beyond them more new ridges lie under the warm dark water. Then the blows coincide, silt leaps from the bottom, and the whole plain changes shape before it can be seen again.
