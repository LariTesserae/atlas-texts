The place is no wider than a factory yard, though there is no clear point from which to see across it. Low shelves of black stone crowd together, their edges rounded by water and worn into shallow lips. Between them stand rusted iron fins, some only hand-high, set so close that a person must turn sideways to pass. Water beads on every surface. It gathers in the lips, spills from one to the next, and disappears through gravel that looks dry until a foot presses it.

A single brick pump house occupies one corner. Its roof has fallen inward, but beneath it a flywheel still turns a fraction of a revolution now and then. A pipe runs out from under the bricks and ends abruptly above a pool. Nothing comes from the pipe. Instead, water wells up through seams in the stone all around it, as if the pipe once belonged to a larger arrangement that has since learned to do without it. Bolts and lengths of rail lie among the rocks, nearly worn smooth. The metal is not buried; the ground has settled around each piece.

There are no reeds or leaves to soften the yard. A pale, slippery film grows where water meets iron, but elsewhere the surfaces are bare. The pools are crowded with life: translucent shrimps among the gravel, dark snails on the submerged bolts, long thin animals that retreat into holes when a shadow crosses them. Small crabs wait beneath the stone lips and catch what the slow overflow brings. Their movements are easy to miss until one stops walking; then the floor seems to be making tiny, separate decisions everywhere.

It is cool here even at midday. Light reaches the water as a dull reflection from the sky, and the brickwork stays dark. For a place with so much motion, it is quiet. Now and then a drop falls loudly into an empty iron socket, or the flywheel gives one low knock. The water continues rising and draining through its familiar routes. By the time a ripple has crossed one pool, a creature has found shelter in the next.
