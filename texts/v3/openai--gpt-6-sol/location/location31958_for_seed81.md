The city stands on a high shelf of dark stone, above a plain that is usually hidden by steam. Its streets are broad enough for traffic that no longer comes. Between blocks of windowless buildings lie vacant squares, loading yards, and long stretches of paving on which there is nothing at all—not even dust. Rain washes the dust away, and heat dries the stone before the next rain arrives.

Water is close everywhere but rarely stays in view. It seeps from retaining walls, gathers in the joints of bridges, and drips from the undersides of overhead pipes. In the deeper streets, narrow gutters run steadily toward cisterns cut into the rock. Their water is hot to the touch. Where a gutter crosses a patch of cooler stone, vapor lifts from it and hangs under the walkways, making the city seem to have a low, shifting ceiling beneath its real one.

The buildings have had time to weather into the plateau. Their lower courses are rounded by runoff; metal stairs have lost their sharp edges to rust. An old tram line follows the rim, then disappears into a tunnel whose mouth has been softened by mineral deposits. Nothing grows over it. There are stains of many colors on the masonry, but almost no green. A few pale films live in the wet seams, and small, hard-shelled animals sometimes emerge to feed there after dark. One may cross a whole district without seeing one move.

It is dim even at midday. The sky is a brown-gray brightness behind a high veil of vapor, and the sun shows through it only as a lighter circle. The air remains fiercely hot. Every few hours, cooling pumps start somewhere below the streets, drawing water through the buried works. A tremor passes along the railings; a chain of valves opens with distant knocks. Then the city is quiet again except for dripping and the thin hiss of water meeting hotter metal.

From the rim, its plan becomes clear: reservoirs, workshops, housing blocks, and service towers laid out across miles of rising ground. It was built for numbers its open spaces can no longer suggest. Yet the pumps still answer one another. When rain begins, shutters fold over the reservoir mouths; when it stops, the gutters empty and heat climbs back out of the stone. The city does not feel preserved. It feels occupied by its own cycles, carrying out the work for which it was made long after almost everyone has gone.
