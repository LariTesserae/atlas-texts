The path climbs by shallow steps, though from the far side of the valley it looks nearly level. That is the trick of this place: every street is part of a long ascent. Roofs rise behind roofs, then become balconies for the streets above them. In the bright air, the highest district is visible all afternoon, a pale rim of tanks and railings set against the sky.

There is no exposed earth on the climb. The steps are cast concrete, their edges rounded by generations of feet. Walls are brick or sheet metal, patched with newer pieces that have already begun to stain. Even the steep green slopes between neighborhoods are built: retaining grids packed with crushed ceramic, compost, and fibrous mats. Roots hold the filling together now. Ferns spill over drainage lips; broad-leaved vines have closed the gaps between factory windows; small trees stand in square steel collars and lean out above the drop. From a distance the whole mountain looks wooded. Up close, its trunks emerge from bolts and seams.

Water arrives irregularly. A short rain can make every level audible at once, dripping through gutters, striking roofs, running inside the walls. On clear days there is still moisture where warm air meets the cooler pipes: beads gather underneath them and fall into long planted troughs. The troughs stay lush while the open steps dry quickly. Sparrows and bright, quarrelsome birds work along the vines; lizards wait at the mouths of warm vents. Moths crowd the high windows at night, and bats follow them through the service courts.

The machinery is rarely silent. Behind the vegetation come the low strokes of pumps, the rougher sound of lifts taking a load, and, now and then, a whistle passed from one level to another. None of it is hurried. The lifts move at the same dependable pace as the water in the covered channels. A person walking uphill may hear one engine for half an hour before finally reaching the building that houses it.

At the upper rim, the view opens across several inhabited slopes. Walkways bridge the gaps between them, with rail lines laid down their centers and gardens pressed against their sides. Far below, the oldest decks are dark with weather and almost hidden by leaves. Their builders allowed for repairs, but probably not for so many seasons of roots, nests, rust, and patient replacement. The city has acquired the worn contours of a landscape without ceasing, anywhere, to be made.
