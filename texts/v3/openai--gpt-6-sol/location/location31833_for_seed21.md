The path ends at a rim of black stone no wider than a village square. Below it, clouds move through a deep valley; above it, the air is hot enough to make the distant ridgelines waver. Rain has passed recently, but the heat has not broken. Water stands in every fresh seam of the rock, and now and then a drop falls from an overhang into the cloud beneath.

The plateau seems newly made. Its edges are sharp, its loose stones have not yet settled into soil, and pale fractures run across it without the softening of moss. Yet in the fractures grow thick, glossy plants. Their roots grip bare stone. Some leaves are as broad as hands; others are fine and densely folded, holding beads of water long after the open rock has dried. There are only a few clumps, with long stretches of empty ground between them. In the heat, each one gives off its own faint, damp smell.

Near the middle stands a low shelter assembled from narrow ceramic ribs. There is room beneath it for one person to sit out a storm, though one side is open to the sky. Beside it, three slender posts rise from the rock. Their surfaces look worn until the light catches them: the marks are not scratches but tiny moving spaces, opening and closing in the material. The posts cast ordinary shadows. The spaces inside them cast none.

Water gathers at the feet of the posts without flowing downhill. It forms a shallow, clear pool over the highest part of the plateau. During the hottest hours, the pool slowly deepens. At evening it drains into the stone, leaving a ring of silt and small living things that have somehow arrived with it. A few translucent flies hatch there. A bird sometimes comes over the rim to take them, then disappears into the valley. For most of the day there is no animal sound at all.

The shelter’s floor bears the same silt rings, one inside another. A person who waits there can hear the plateau working: a little crack from sun-struck stone, the tick of a cooling post, a brief patter as water reaches a new height. Nothing hums. The posts do not visibly draw power from the sun or weather. Still, each afternoon, while the valley clouds drift past untouched, this small high place makes its pool and gives the plants another day.
