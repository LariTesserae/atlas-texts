The ridge is young enough that its stone still has edges. Pale slabs stand at angles along the crest, split cleanly from a darker rock beneath them, and the paths between are narrow, angular, and scarcely worn. Beyond the ridge, several more rise out of a broad upland basin. In the clear light you can see each fracture, each ledge crowded with green.

The growth is denser than the weather seems able to support. Tough little trees stand almost shoulder to shoulder with tall ferns and glossy shrubs; vines span the gaps, and moss packs every crack where dust has gathered. There is no stream in sight. Moisture comes at dawn, when the cold stone sweats beneath the leaves, and after the occasional brief rain. By noon the exposed paths are dry again, though drops remain under the canopy, held in the cups of leaves and on the undersides of pipes.

Those pipes appear at intervals along the ridge, entering low concrete housings half-hidden by roots. A few collector frames stand higher up, their fine metal mesh shining against the sky. They belong to a larger works somewhere beyond the basin: a cable as thick as a wrist runs from housing to housing before vanishing into the rock. Small lamps on the cabinets show that power is reaching them. Nothing can be heard working inside. Even close to a frame, there is only the faint click of a drop letting go.

In places the plants have grown around the machinery so completely that it is hard to tell which was there first. Roots follow the buried cable for long distances, then spread away from it in dense fans. The trees on either side lean toward the collectors, leaving bright, narrow aisles beneath the mesh. No one has cleared those aisles recently. An inspection stair ends halfway down a new break in the rock, its last few steps hanging over empty air.

The sun is strong here, but the air stays cool. You can look across the basin for miles: green packed into every hollow, white stone flashing between the crowns, a collector frame here and another far off. There are no birds over it. Once, deep in the shrubs, something small shifts a leaf and becomes still. After that, the ridge is quiet enough to hear water slowly filling a cup of stone.
