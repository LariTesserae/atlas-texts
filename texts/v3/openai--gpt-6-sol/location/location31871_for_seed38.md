At the top of the long ascent, the ground gives way without becoming a cliff. It softens underfoot. The high country is an immense, nearly level shelf of roots, peat, and living wood, with the old mountain somewhere beneath it. In a few places stone shows through: rounded humps worn smooth as river stones, though no river has run over them for an age. Water beads on their shaded sides and slips back into the green.

There is no open sky from most of the shelf. Trees grow close together, but not straight up. Their trunks spread into broad, low vaults; their branches meet, graft, and continue outward as if each tree were an unfinished part of a larger one. Ferns occupy the joints. Moss covers the upper surfaces and hangs from the undersides in long wet fringes. A traveler can pass beneath a root as thick as a house and emerge onto its back without quite noticing when the path began to climb.

The air is warm and dim. Rain arrives often, though it is difficult to tell when it starts: the canopy catches it, holds it, and releases it for hours afterward. Shallow pools form in the hollows between roots. They have dark, clear water and floors of fallen leaves so old that their veins remain while the rest has gone soft. Elsewhere the ground looks dry but yields a little water to every step. From the edge of the shelf, whole banks of mist can be seen forming below the vegetation and drifting out over the lower slopes. The plateau makes some of its own weather and keeps it close.

Small lives fill the quiet spaces. Pale insects shelter in the cups of flowers that never see direct sun. Frogs sit at eye level in the water held between branches. A long-tailed creature may cross far above, moving with such care that only a few drops fall to mark its passage. There are birds here, but they seldom call. Their wings make a brief, soft rush through the leaves, and then the water can be heard again: a drip into a pool, a slow trickle inside a hollow trunk. Even the larger animals leave little disturbance. Their paths are known by the places where the moss has been pressed flat and has grown back flatter.

Here and there stand low walls of stone, or what remains of them. Roots have lifted some blocks and clasped others in place. One circle of stones holds a pool; another surrounds a tree whose trunk is wider than the circle was built to contain. Nothing in these places suggests a recent hand. The stones are worn to the same patient shapes as the exposed bedrock, and seedlings grow in their seams.

The shelf extends beyond sight. Across its length the same slow work continues: wood thickens around wood, water finds a lower hollow, old leaves become ground. At its margins the living floor hangs out over the slopes in heavy green folds. Drops gather there, fall a great distance, and begin the streams of the country below.
