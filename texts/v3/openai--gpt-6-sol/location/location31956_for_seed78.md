At this height there is almost no soil. A mountain range has risen faster than earth can gather on it, leaving ridges with clean, sharp edges. From a distance the slopes look black. Up close, they are clothed in overlapping leaves: small, thick ones on the exposed crests, long pale ones hanging under the ledges, roots pressed into every crack. In places the roots have bound loose slabs so tightly that a ridge remains standing after the stone beneath it has split.

The range is immense, but it does not feel crowded. Between one vegetated ridge and the next lie gaps several miles wide, dropping into darkness. Clouds move through them below the level of the passes. There is room for whole weather systems to form among these peaks, and often nothing visible across a gap except the remote, wavering outline of another green-black wall.

Water comes in bursts. Rain needles the upper leaves for an hour, then a dry wind blows for days. Condensation collects under the broadest growth, where drops hang until their weight bends a leaf and spills them into a root cup below. Almost every creature knows the locations of these cups. The paths to them are worn into the living mat, though the mat slowly closes again.

It is cold enough that fallen rain sometimes freezes before it can drain. By morning the plants have lifted thin skins of ice from their surfaces; they shed them with little cracks as the wind rises. For most of the day the light is no stronger than deep twilight. The cloud ceiling hides the sun, and the steep slopes hide one another. On rare clear nights, snow on far ridges briefly shows how much country lies beyond the dark.

The place is loud. Leaves rub together in the wind with a sound like heavy cloth being dragged over stone. Root-bound slabs knock deep in the mountain as they settle. Small animals call from the foliage, answered by others too far away to see. There are grazers here, quick and sure-footed, that strip the tender growth from a ridge and move on before its defenders find them. Hunters follow their calls. Birds launch themselves across the wide gaps and vanish into cloud; sometimes one can hear wings beating long after it has disappeared.

A few narrow shelters stand near the reliable water cups. Their frames are branches bent while growing and lashed to stone with fiber. Flat rock blades, drying racks, and steps chipped into the newest exposed ledges show where people have worked. No path stays clear for long. A party returning after a season must cut its way back to a shelter it can already hear creaking in the wind.

Even from there, the nearest fire is only a point of warmth against the scale of the range. Beyond it the slopes keep shifting: frost loosens stone, roots catch it, rain feeds the roots, and the wind carries seeds across distances no walker could cross in a day.
