Behind one of the upper gates of the reservoir is a space no larger than a pantry. It is entirely underwater. The reservoir sits on a summit so high that its overflow falls for a long time before it strikes anything, but here, inside the gate, height is felt mostly as pressure: a steady weight against the ears and the joints of the walls.

There is no stone in the little chamber. Its floor is a shallow bowl of cast iron, worn smooth where generations of current have carried grit around its rim. Ceramic plates line the sides, their seams packed with the dark filings of old machinery. Even the silt is made from things the works have shed. In one corner, a bronze pipe enters the wall and emerges again a handspan away, as if the chamber were too small to contain its purpose.

Warm water comes through that pipe. Fernlike weeds grow thickly around its mouth, bright green at their tips and almost black down among their roots. Elsewhere the floor is bare. The clear spaces make the garden seem smaller and more vigorous: each frond has room to bow into the current, gather drifting particles, and rise again. Snails graze the plates. Little fish hold motionless under the leaves until a pulse from the pump sends them scattering. Transparent shrimp arrive afterward to pick over whatever the flow has uncovered.

Daylight reaches the chamber through a square inspection window. It gives the weeds ordinary green shadows and shows every slow turn of suspended dust. Beyond the glass is more water, then the pale outer edge of the reservoir; on clear days, a wavering strip of sky can be seen through the surface. At intervals a distant engine engages. The whole chamber knocks once, loudly enough to feel through the ribs, and the pipe pours warmer water across the bowl. Fronds flatten. Snails seal themselves to the plates. A minute later the flow eases, and all the small feeding begins again.

The gate has been running this cycle for longer than anyone here could remember. Its corners have rounded, its stamped numbers have become soft dents, and the groove cut by the current is deep enough to shelter a fish. Nothing in the chamber is still for very long, yet every movement follows a path worn ancient by repetition.
