The sea begins above the mountains. From the lower valleys you can see its underside: a dark, level blue held in a vast cup of roots. Up here, inside it, there is no shore in sight. Cool water reaches in every direction, clear enough for daylight to enter as a broad, even gray. Far below, the old peaks show through the water as softened green ridges. Their stone has worn into long, settled shapes; wherever it is exposed, a thin living skin has taken hold.

Most of the sea appears empty. Great distances separate the trunks that rise from the drowned slopes, and an animal crossing between them can remain in view for hours. Yet there is no bare ground and no ordinary surface. The floor is a continuous weave of root and leaf. Each distant trunk is a column of bundled stems, with fronds spread so widely above it that their edges meet those of other columns beyond sight. The open water lies inside a plant.

A steady roar comes through the stems. Water passes up their hollow cores under pressure, through chambers that narrow and widen with the grain of the wood. Some trunks give a low note; thinner ones whine. Along the roots, pale conductive tissue carries a persistent blue charge. The whole sea sounds like a factory at work, though nothing here has been assembled. No wall has a joint, no blade has been fitted to a shaft. The pipes grow, and the pressure that drives them comes from the living cup itself.

Fish hang among the columns, their fins held open in the fixed flow. Small grazers occupy the undersides of leaves, each at a little distance from the next. Larger, nearly transparent animals wait where a trunk’s current brings nourishment past their mouths. Their presence makes the water feel inhabited without filling it.

Nothing alters. The fish do not advance; the leaves do not shed; the charge neither flares nor fades. Even the loudness is constant. Looking across that immense, pale water, you may think a sound is approaching from a far column, then realize you have been hearing it all along.
