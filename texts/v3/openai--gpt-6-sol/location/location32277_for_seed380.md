Below the old street level is a district no larger than a few adjoining squares. Its roof is the underside of the city: brick vaults, the footings of demolished houses, iron beams whose ends have swollen with rust. Water comes through everywhere, but seldom in a stream. It gathers on bolt heads, travels along mortar seams, and falls from the same places so faithfully that shallow cups have worn into the paving beneath them.

The streets are narrow and nearly empty. A rail runs down one, disappears under a sealed factory door, and comes out again on the other side. Between the rails, the stone has been polished by wheels that have not passed for years. Pipes cross overhead at walking height, wrapped in old felt; a few remain warm enough to raise a faint mist. At the district’s center, a pumping engine turns behind a mesh guard. Its flywheel makes one unhurried revolution, pauses, and makes another. No one here remembers a time when it ran faster.

There are lamps, but they illuminate very little beyond their own wet glass. Farther away, shapes acquire their edges from the pale seams of calcite on the walls. Those seams have been growing over signs, doorframes, and the mouths of drains. A row of identical shopfronts is still legible beneath the deposits, though only one door opens. Inside are shelves of pipe fittings, arranged by size and lightly furred with mineral.

Almost nothing green grows here. A few translucent shoots rise where silt has collected in a cracked stair. Life is more visible in the spaces people left empty. Small white crabs occupy the holes in a retaining wall. Moths rest along the dry undersides of cables. In the channels beside the curb, dark fish hold still against a current too slight to see. There are so many of them that, when a lamp is carried past, the channel seems to change texture rather than move. None make much noise. Even the engine is heard chiefly through the soles of the feet.

At intervals, moisture condenses on the vacant tram shelter until its roof releases a single sheet of water. The crabs retreat before it falls. Perhaps they feel a change in the metal; perhaps the shelter has kept a timetable of its own. The sheet strikes the pavement, drains away, and leaves the district as it was.

Everything here is being altered, but slowly enough to look settled: iron becoming red stone, brick acquiring a second skin, the old street sinking by fractions into its foundations. The pump continues to turn. Water reaches its marked level, slips through a grate, and begins the long return.
