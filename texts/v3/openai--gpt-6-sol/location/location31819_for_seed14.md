Far below the reach of daylight, the seabed runs for miles as a low, crowded plain. Its ridges are scarcely higher than a body, worn down over ages until their edges have become long, blunt swells. Pebbles lie fitted against pebbles; larger stones are sunk almost flush in old clay. There is little bare ground. Even the shallow hollows are packed with grit, broken shells, and the fine remains of things that lived higher in the water.

The plain is old, but the water over it will not keep still. A cold, silt-heavy current presses along the bottom, splits around a rise, and meets itself on the other side. Smaller currents turn inside it, lifting pale sediment in brief, dense clouds. A ridge disappears, returns, disappears again. The stones do not travel far; they have been rounded and settled too long for that. It is the narrow spaces among them that change, filling and emptying as the current passes.

Almost nothing grows here. A few thin patches cling to the lee of stones where the moving grit cannot scrape them away. An occasional crab tests the bottom with its legs, then holds fast until the water eases. Most movement belongs to the sea itself. In the darkness, the turbulence has no roar: only a faint ticking of gravel against gravel, felt through the ground more readily than heard.
