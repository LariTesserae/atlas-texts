The city stands at the top of a mountain that has almost forgotten it was a mountain. Its summit is broad and low-shouldered, worn by ages of wind into shallow stone basins and long, rounded ribs. Beyond the last roofs, these ribs run into a dim distance, where other summits rise like islands above the cloud. There are no sharp peaks nearby. Even the cliffs have been rubbed smooth, though they still fall farther than the eye can comfortably follow.

Stone is everywhere, but little of it is bare. Dark moss thickens in the seams between paving blocks. Ferns crowd the stairwells that descend out of the wind, and small trees have rooted in old loading yards, their branches pressed close by the cold. The open ground is only damp; rain comes in brief, slanting visits and is gone. Under the streets, though, water gathers slowly. It beads on iron pipes, runs down the walls of covered passages, and fills cisterns cut into the oldest rock.

The town is built tightly around those cisterns. Narrow houses, workshops, and warehouses lean toward a few sheltered squares, with footbridges crossing above lanes scarcely wide enough for a cart. Roofs overlap like scales. Along the downhill edge stand the larger works: enclosed mills, pumping sheds, and rows of tanks whose curved sides are slick with condensation. Belts turn behind their windows. Somewhere a piston gives a soft, patient knock, then falls quiet beneath the hiss of steam escaping a valve.

Much of the work here concerns the passage of water. Gutters can be shifted before a storm; gates inside the rock send a new trickle toward a garden or a mill. When clouds blow up from below, the upper rails and rooftops become wet first. By morning the tanks are fuller, the moss has brightened, and someone is out clearing leaves from the drains. On dry days, the machinery keeps turning, drawing what it can from the cool interior of the mountain.

The place is seldom loud. Wind moves over the worn heights without finding many edges to strike, and the close streets break it into small drafts. One may hear a loose shutter, a distant wheel, drops falling into deep water. Birds are scarce at this altitude. Now and then one passes over the roofs and vanishes into the cloud, leaving the town to its many quieter motions: roots widening a crack, water finding the next basin, a mill taking up its slow knock again.
