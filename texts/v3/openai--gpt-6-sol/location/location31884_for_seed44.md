The district fits between a canal and a low railway embankment, no more than a few streets wide. From the embankment it looks almost entirely green. Roofs show through in patches—corrugated metal, the flat top of a pump house, a row of brick chimney pots—but the lanes are roofed in leaves. Vines have crossed from one building to the next on cables and drainpipes. Saplings stand in the silt caught behind curbs. Each vacant inch of wall holds fern roots or a dark seam of moss.

It is warm enough that wet brick gives off a smell even after the rain has stopped. Water remains close to the surface. It beads under the broad leaves of nursery stock stacked outside workshops, seeps from the canal wall, and gathers in the hollows of old paving stones. Beneath the green, the place is solidly made: brick warehouses, concrete loading steps, iron railings polished smooth where people pass. Decades of runoff have worn shallow courses through the steps, and the drains have been cleared so often that their edges are rounded. Nothing here feels newly abandoned. The plants are cut back from doors and electrical boxes, then allowed to fill the space between.

The pump house makes the loudest sound, a continuous motor hum with a heavier beat when its gates move. Somewhere nearby, a saw bites through timber; farther off, wagons knock together on the rail line. Water drips into these noises without ever quite being heard on its own. Workers call across the narrow yard rather than walking over. There are few animals in sight—sometimes a bird on a wire, briefly audible when a motor winds down.

By afternoon, ordinary daylight reaches the lanes in shifting pieces. The canal rises and falls a little, the pumps answer it, and damp marks climb or retreat along the brick. In time the roots thicken, a joint opens, someone packs it with mortar, and the leaves grow over the repair.
