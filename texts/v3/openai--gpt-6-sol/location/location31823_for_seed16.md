The street is no wider than a person’s shoulders. It runs between two rows of clay rooms whose roofs have settled toward each other until they nearly meet. Far above, a seam of daylight crosses the gap. At noon it lays a narrow stripe on the paving stones; for the rest of the day the little town is brown and dim.

It is very hot here. The walls hold the heat, and the floor gives it back through the soles of your feet. Dust has rounded every stair edge and filled the lower doorways. Handprints remain in the plaster beneath it, along with the shallow grooves of the tools that spread it. No one comes to maintain these rooms now. There are no insects under the stones, no birds overhead, no tracks besides those the wind makes and erases.

At the end of the street stands a thicket. Thick, dark leaves crowd a single courtyard, pressed against its walls and over the lintels. Their roots grip the dry brick; their stems are tough and glossy, with a faint resin smell when the heat is strongest. Nothing grows in the open street. From there the courtyard looks lavish, almost impossibly green, while the rooms around it remain bare.

The sound begins when the upper air warms. Wind enters through the roof seam and passes from room to room through holes left in the masonry. It whistles in one, booms in another, and sets loose shutters knocking hard against their frames. The leaves turn their pale undersides outward, then fold back as the gust passes. Pods strike the courtyard wall and spill seeds into the dust. By evening the wind has shifted, the street has fallen quiet, and a few of those seeds have lodged in the cracks between its stones.
