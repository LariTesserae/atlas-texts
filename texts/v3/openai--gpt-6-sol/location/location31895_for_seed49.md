The city begins well before anything that looks like a skyline. Across the low plain, the ground is paved in fitted stone and dark ceramic, worn smooth by centuries of rain and passage. Water stands in the joints. In some districts it reaches the ankles; in others it runs beneath grates so broad that entire streets seem to breathe above it. There are no fields between the settlements. One district gives way to the next by a change in paving, a new pitch to the roofs, a different rhythm of floodgates.

From a distance, the place might be mistaken for a coastline without a sea. Roofs overlap like shingles to the horizon. Elevated roads cross lower roads, and below those are market floors, pump galleries, sleeping chambers, service tunnels. Every level is occupied. Walls have been rubbed round at the corners by hands and freight; steps bow in the middle. Nothing stays clean for long in the warm, wet air, though nothing here is allowed to decay unchecked. Crews replace cracked surfaces while traffic streams around them.

Rain comes often, but much of the water has traveled farther than any cloud. It condenses on the undersides of transit decks and falls in steady lines. It seeps from the old embankments. At noon, sunlight reaches the open streets plainly enough to cast shadows, while the deep lanes keep their own dim, amber day. There is almost no green. The stone was laid over salt-heavy ground, and every exposed ledge is claimed for use: a rail, a pipe, a nesting shelf, a place to set down a crate. Where a wall stays damp, a thin pale film may gather, but it is scraped away before it can block a drain.

Animals thrive in the spaces people cannot quite seal. Small crabs patrol the gutters after each surge. Birds wheel between towers, snatching insects from the warm updrafts. Sleek, long-backed creatures slip through the flood channels and surface at night beside the food markets. Their calls are usually lost in the greater noise.

The noise is the first thing a visitor must learn to bear. Pumps pound underfoot. Freight platforms pass overhead with a grinding hum; locks open in long metallic shrieks; traders call across the water; rain rattles on acres of roof. When a storm approaches, warning tones travel outward district by district, until the whole plain appears to be sounding its depth. Conversation continues inside it, close and practical, with mouths near ears.

The city survives by moving faster than the water. Its foundations shift loads through buried structures whose workings few residents can explain. A crowded bridge may lift several inches between morning and evening. A row of buildings may lean together, then straighten after the flood passes. The changes are deliberate, but not always gentle: shutters slam, walkways buckle briefly into new alignments, and everyone waits for the painted crossing marks to settle before stepping out.

At the farthest visible edge, beyond miles of roofs, another bank of rain advances over another crowded quarter. The streets there will already be filling. Gates will close, channels will open, and people who have known those routes all their lives will take a different way home.
