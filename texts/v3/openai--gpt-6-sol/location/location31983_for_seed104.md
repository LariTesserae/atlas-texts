The plateau stands high above the seafloor, though there is no dry land anywhere near it. Its rim falls away into a blue so deep it looks solid. Above it, another great depth of water rises toward a surface that has not been seen in living memory. Here, height is measured by how far the bottom is below you, not by how close you are to air.

The stone underfoot is warm. It has been worn almost level over an age too long to picture, except where ancient channels cross it: broad, shallow hollows polished by currents that no longer run in the same direction. Fine sand collects in them, then lifts again in slow, soundless sheets. The plateau is so wide that its far side has different weather. A darkening passes across the distance as a cold current arrives; hours later, it reaches this shoreless place and bends the taller growth all at once.

Most of what grows here has no need to reach upward. Forests of translucent fronds spread close to the rock, overlapping like roof tiles. Thick, rust-colored stems grip the lips of the old channels. Pale fans stand between them, their edges continually folding and opening in the current. They grow densely enough to hide the stone for miles, but leave narrow lanes of bare ground where the water moves too quickly for anything to hold on. In the dimness, the smallest leaves give off a dull green light when touched. A passing current can make a whole hillside brighten, then go dark again.

There are few animals. Now and then, something small parts the fronds and is gone before its shape becomes clear. Far out over the drop, a single broad shadow travels without appearing to move its fins. Nothing follows it. Nothing flees. The plants keep bending, shedding, rooting, and flowering through long drifts of warm water.

Scattered across the plateau are low walls, or what remain of them. They are made from fitted slabs of the same stone beneath them, their joints softened by growth. Some form corners; others end halfway through a straight course. No road connects them. Inside one enclosure, the sand is always gathered against the eastern wall, even when the current runs west. The other walls behave as walls should.

That is not the only small refusal here. A channel may be crossed in a few strokes going one way and take half a day on the return. Fronds seen through a gap sometimes belong to a stand farther off than the gap could frame. At the plateau’s rim, the descending face can be watched for a long time without the eye finding a place where it begins. Yet a hand laid against it meets ordinary, warm stone.

The place is quiet enough for these changes to go unnoticed. Sand makes a faint hiss against rock. A stem under strain gives a soft knock when it slips free of its hold. Beyond that there is only the pressure of water and the slow motion of a landscape that has had ages to wear itself down, and is still being worn.
