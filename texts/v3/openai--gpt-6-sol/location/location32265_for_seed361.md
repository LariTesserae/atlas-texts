At the top of the mountain, where the ridge has worn down to a broad, almost level back, there is a hollow no wider than a room. Its floor is old stone: pale, fine-grained, polished by weather until even its cracks have rounded edges. Rain rarely lasts here. It passes in brief warm sheets, then drains away, leaving a little water in the cracks and a skin of condensation beneath the overhanging lip.

The plants keep to those damp places. Ferns spill from one deep seam; a vine crosses several feet of bare rock before finding the next. Small trees stand far apart, their roots exposed in smooth loops. Each has a full crown, but there is enough empty stone between them to see exactly where its shade ends. Up close, the hollow is green and close-smelling. From a step back, it is mostly bare.

The animals make it feel crowded. Bright beetles work under the wet leaves. Lizards wait on the sunlit rims of the cracks, darting at flies whenever a cloud moves over. Birds arrive in bursts, quarrel over the tree crowns, and leave without quite quieting the place. From somewhere below the ridge comes a deeper, regular call; each time it sounds, the insects fall silent for a breath and then start again. Wings, feet, calls, and the dry tapping of beaks make a noise far larger than the hollow.

Three slender posts stand among the stones. They are easy to miss, dark as the lichen around their bases, with no visible joints or fastenings. At intervals one gives off a low note that can be felt through a hand laid on the rock. Afterward, beads of water form along its length and run down into a shallow cut in the floor. The cut is ancient and nearly level with the surrounding stone, yet the water always reaches the fern seam. It may be drawn there; it may simply be following a slope too slight to see.

By afternoon the rock is hot enough to warm the air under the leaves. Moisture rises, gathers again in the shade, and brings the flies back. The birds return after them. Nothing stays for long except the trees, the posts, and the worn hollow itself, high above the valleys and noisy with the small lives passing through it.
