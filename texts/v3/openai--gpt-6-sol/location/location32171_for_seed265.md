The plain begins at the end of a paved road, though in the dark it is difficult to tell where the paving stops. Your boots find its edge by sound: a dry, hard tap gives way to the soft tack of new mud. Beyond that, the ground spreads nearly level for miles. Rainwater lies just under its skin. A heel pressed into the silt leaves a print that fills from below.

There are no stars here, and no visible horizon. The dark is complete enough that distance has to be learned by other means. Warm air touches the face in one place and leaves it cool in another. A low bank of gravel runs for perhaps half a mile before it gives way to shallow water; you can follow it by the small stones shifting underfoot. Farther out, something enormous makes a faint, regular tick. It sounds close for an hour before you reach it.

The gravel banks are recent. Their stones have clean corners, and the mud between them has not yet grown a crust. Here and there, narrow cuts have opened in the plain, no deeper than a handspan, each carrying a slow thread of water toward a basin nobody has mapped. On the higher strips grow scattered reeds with tough, widely spaced stems. Most of the ground is bare: silt, broken aggregate, sheets of warm water with a faint metallic smell.

Living things occupy the spaces the plants leave open. Small bodies push through the mud beside your feet. In the water, fish brush against one another so densely that their turns feel like a single movement against a submerged ankle. Birds stand throughout the shallows; their positions are given away by the light click of beaks and, occasionally, a feather drawn through air. None calls. At certain hours every creature stops at once. The plain remains crowded, but the only sound is water entering water.

The ticking comes from a pumping station: one concrete room on legs, a pipe descending into the basin, a motor housing warm to the touch. Other stations stand far apart across the plain. Their cables run out over the mud on porcelain feet. The motors turn slowly, moving water from one low place to another, but the operators long ago stopped claiming that they controlled where it went. A pump can run all night and return in the morning the exact volume it drew. Sometimes the wet silt beside a pipe rises into a new gravel bank, sharp-edged and dry, while an old bank sinks without a ripple.

The people who tend the stations work by touch. They count steps along the cables and keep their tools tied to their wrists. They say the distance between two stations changes, although the cables never tighten or slacken. A walk that takes forty minutes in one direction may take half that on the way back. Nothing lurches or shimmers; the plain alters so slowly that the body accepts each step. Only afterward, when someone compares the hours in two logbooks, does the missing ground become apparent.

By morning—if morning comes elsewhere—the gravel will still be sharp and the water will still be warm. New channels will have opened a finger’s width. Beneath the stations, the motors will continue their patient ticking. Somewhere in the dark, the birds will lift their feet one by one and settle them on ground that was not there when they arrived.
