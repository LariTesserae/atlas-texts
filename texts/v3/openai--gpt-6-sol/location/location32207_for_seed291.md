The city stands on a plain that has not quite finished becoming land. From the roofs, you can see its extent: rows of brick mills, freight yards, glasshouses, and narrow streets running outward until they lose their straightness among dark green thickets. There are no hills to give the view an edge. The ground is low and nearly level, but a street surveyed flat last winter may now have a shallow rise in it, lifting its paving stones one by one.

New ground appears most often at the margins of the rail yards. Gray clay presses up through the ballast, smooth and unmarked, still holding the shapes of stones beneath it. The workers call these patches births. They lay temporary track across them, wait to see which way the clay will settle, and only then drive piles. A few abandoned foundations sit a foot above their old sidewalks; elsewhere, doors have had their bottoms trimmed again and again. There is little evidence of long erosion here—few cut banks, few worn gullies. Even the river channels look recently pressed into the plain.

It is cold enough for breath to show in the morning. Rain is occasional, but damp gathers everywhere the machines make warmth. Condensation beads on the iron ribs of the glasshouses and falls into troughs. Ferns crowd those troughs, and thick moss seals the joints of warehouse walls. Between buildings grow dense stands of willow and low, glossy-leaved shrubs, their roots woven through rubble and cable. The city keeps cutting them back from switches and loading bays. By spring, the green has closed over the cuts.

Industry occupies nearly every available strip of firm ground. Belts move beneath covered ways; electric lines cross above the streets; carts wait nose to tail outside foundries. From a distance it ought to sound tremendous. Up close, much of the work happens in an odd quiet. The new clay swallows footsteps. Moss dulls the wheels of handcarts. Factory doors are kept shut against the cold, and their machinery reaches the street as a low vibration felt through a railing rather than heard. Now and then a coupling strikes, or a siren gives one brief note, and the noise travels startlingly far.

The quiet makes the changes easier to notice. A crack travels along a plaster wall while someone is looking at it. A stack of empty crates leans as the yard beneath it inches sideways. Surveyors work in pairs, calling measurements from marks painted on old chimneys, which move less than anything else. Birds gather on their poles and wires—starlings, winter thrushes, small pale birds that nest in the warm vents—and lift together whenever the ground gives its slow, deep shudder. Foxes and hares have learned the same warning, though they live among the busiest sheds.

People here build for adjustment rather than permanence. Pipes have spare bends. Bridges rest on broad shoes that can be drawn back into place. On the municipal map, some streets are printed with dashed borders, not because their destinations are uncertain, but because their sides are. By midday the plain lies under an ordinary, clear light, and from a rooftop it can look settled: smoke rising, green crowded around brick, tracks drawing long lines toward the horizon. Then a line of wagons stops for no visible reason. Far out in the yards, fresh pale earth has begun to rise between the rails, and the crews are already walking toward it with their tools.
