The highland is still rising. Along its outer margins, new stone shows in pale, unscored seams, as if the ground has not been exposed long enough to learn the shapes of gullies. Farther in, it is difficult to find stone at all. A continuous thickness of root, stem, leaf, and wet fiber lies over the young rock, deep enough to have its own slopes and hollows. There are no trunks with clear space between them. Growth fills the space a forest would leave empty.

It is cool beneath that cover and dim even at midday. Water hangs on every surface, gathers in the folded leaves, and moves through the living mass in thousands of narrow courses. Rain may have stopped days ago at one edge of the highland while it is still falling at another. Seen from above, long bands of cloud remain caught against ridges made largely of plants. Beneath them, water sometimes travels upward through translucent veins, against a slope that would turn any ordinary stream back. At intervals a whole hillside changes sheen as its leaves tilt together, guiding mist toward a dry region beyond sight. The coordination is exact across distances too great for a single living thing to sense by any familiar means.

The noise makes the breadth of the place harder to judge. Water drums on broad membranes overhead; taut fibers boom as the mass shifts; millions of small chambers click when they fill and empty. A deeper sound passes slowly underfoot, the sound of growth pressing against growth. There are no calls within it—no birds or insects, nothing moving independently through the foliage. Where a gap opens, pale new shoots enter it almost at once.

Near the margins, the rock keeps breaking upward and the plants keep taking hold. Inland, where the living layer is thickest, its weight makes the ground settle. On this vast, crowded height, a slope can change between one season of rain and the next without anything being washed away.
