The sea begins high above the surrounding world. From the air, it would look like a pale, restless cap laid across a range of new mountains. From within, there is no sense of a mountaintop at all: only an immensity of cool, clear water, lit far deeper than sunlight ought to reach, and the long upward climb of stone from a floor too distant to see.

The mountains are still forming. Their flanks are dark and sharp, broken along clean seams rather than worn into gullies. Here and there a ridge pushes a little higher while you watch. Its new surface is almost smooth, with no sediment settled on it yet. Sparse green threads cling in the shallows along these rises. They bend flat when a current passes, then stand up again. Away from the rock, the water contains almost nothing.

It is not quiet. A continuous roar travels through the sea, as if a storm were breaking on every shore at once. Currents meet in the open water with no visible obstacle between them. They fold into tall, slow spirals; a moment later, those spirals tear into fast, cold sheets. At intervals a pressure wave arrives from below, audible first as a deep note and then as a tremor in the water itself. The surface, far overhead, flashes silver in the ordinary daylight.

Nothing has been built here. There are no cables, walls, instruments, or ruins. Yet the water does things that water elsewhere cannot. It carries light around bends. It holds enormous differences of pressure across distances of only a few metres, then releases them without warning. In certain broad regions, its molecules align and pass a disturbance from one to another with such precision that the sea seems to be calculating where its next current will go. The mountains rise through it; the sea continually revises its answer.

For all that motion, the place feels empty. No animal cuts across the light, and nothing calls back through the roar. Between the sparse green threads and the newborn stone lie miles of bright, unoccupied water. When a ridge splits, the sound rolls outward for a long time. By the time it fades, the gap has filled, the currents have changed direction, and a new edge of mountain is beginning to show.
