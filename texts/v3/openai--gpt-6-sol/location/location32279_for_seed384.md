The district lies below the level of its own streets.

At first it looks like a flooded machine room: a narrow square enclosed by brick workshops, their upper windows lost in dark water, their lower doors opening directly onto silt. Pipes cross from wall to wall at every height. Cable bundles hang beneath them, heavy with mineral growth. There is barely room to swim between a stair rail and the flank of a boiler, though beyond both, the water continues upward into darkness. The whole place is no larger than a town market, but it has been built through many times over.

The cold is steady. It has worked into the iron and made the square seem orderly, preserving each thing where it came to rest. A cart lies on its side beside a loading step. Slates from a collapsed roof have settled in overlapping rows along the pavement. The bricks have softened at their edges; bolts are rounded, lettering worn shallow, the corners of buildings gently eaten away. Nothing looks recently broken. Even the wreckage has had time to find a position that holds.

Some of the works still run. Behind a grated arch, a flywheel turns too slowly to see turn. Its spokes appear in a slightly different place whenever you look back. Water passes through a series of low intake chambers, lifting fine sediment from one and laying it down in the next. Every few minutes a lamp inside the old tram shelter pales to blue, then goes out. No wires can be followed to it: they disappear into mortar, or under silt, or into another dense layer of pipes. The machinery offers no clear beginning or end.

It is almost silent. A loose pane taps once against its frame, then takes a long while to tap again. Close to a pipe, you can feel a tremor through your hand before you hear anything at all. Silt rises in little clouds around the gratings; a thread of colder water slips along the curb, carrying pale flakes of rust. These are the district’s weather. They change the view of a doorway, clear it again, then leave a new dusting on the threshold.

There is not much living here. Short weeds grow where old mortar holds a pocket of grit, their blades pressed flat by the current. A white growth feathers the underside of a stair. Once, something small and jointed retreats into the space between two bricks. Across the square, a fish hangs beneath a shop awning, so still it could be another scrap caught there. It moves only when the blue lamp comes on, turning toward it before the light fades.

A flight of steps climbs out of the square. It reaches a landing and goes no farther; the building above has worn away. From that landing, the district becomes easier to read. The roofs, pipes, rails, and retaining walls have enclosed a small body of water within the greater one. Currents enter through the old works and leave by routes too narrow to notice. They have been doing so for a long time. Below, the flywheel turns, the fish resumes its place under the awning, and a little silt settles onto the cart.
