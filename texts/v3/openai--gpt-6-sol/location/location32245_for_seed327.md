The place is only sixty or seventy paces across: a shallow hollow just below the crest of a mountain. Three sides are old stone, rounded and grooved by weather; the fourth ends at a clean drop into cloud. From the hollow, the distance to anything else is difficult to judge. A peak across the valley looks near enough to touch until a bird crosses the gap and dwindles to a speck.

Water comes through the rock rather than over it. It beads beneath overhangs, darkens seams, and gathers in a few narrow channels that run toward the edge. The ground beside them is springy with moss and short sedge. Elsewhere, bare slabs show through, broad enough to lie on and too smooth to hold a footprint. The plants are lush where they can take root—fern, cushion moss, small white flowers—but each green patch has stone around it. A person walking here crosses from wet growth to empty rock in a few steps.

It is cool even in full daylight. Clouds drift up from below, wetting one side of the hollow and leaving the other in sun. The change sets everything moving: drops begin to fall from the ledges, insects rise from the grass, and birds come after them. Swifts pass the open side at astonishing speed. Smaller birds pick along the seams, while calls from farther down the cliff answer them. At times a goat appears on the rim, stands listening, then descends by a route that seems to offer no footholds.

The hollow is seldom quiet. Wind blows hard across its mouth, and the channels pour into cracks near the edge with a sound much larger than their size. Loose water patters on stone. Bird cries cut through it; from below comes the shifting rush of a fall that cannot be seen from here. In a lull, the tiny scrape of a beetle in dry lichen becomes audible, but the lull rarely lasts.

Someone once set flat stones into a low windbreak beside the largest seep. A wooden scoop rests there, silvered and split at the handle. It is the only object that needs explaining. The hollow itself feels long settled: water has found its grooves, roots have found their cracks, and the ledge has held its shape through weather that still arrives with enough force to make everything living crouch.
