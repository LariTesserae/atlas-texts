The basin lies high among mountains, though from its floor there is no sight of land. Cold water fills it almost to the rim. Far above, a narrow, dim surface shivers beneath cloud; below, broad terraces descend into blue-black water. Every terrace is made. Pale blocks fit against dark ones in long, imperfect courses, and the slopes between them are paved with small overlapping plates. There is no exposed rock, no mud untouched by a wall or a floor.

The work is older than its makers. Edges have worn round, and whole flights of steps have been reduced to low, smooth ridges. In places the paving has parted and settled, revealing another course beneath it, laid in a different pattern. Nothing here resembles a machine. The blocks bear no tool marks, only the shallow pits and raised lips of things shaped slowly in water. Whatever assembled the basin did so piece by piece, as some animals build shells. Its descendants may still be here; there is no way to pick them out among the life in the joints.

For all that age, the water is rarely still. A current drops over the uppermost wall and races along the terraces, meeting slower water rising from the depths. Curtains of silt lift, fold, and vanish. Loose plates slide a few handbreadths, exposing clean undersides before settling elsewhere. In the open stretches, great slow-bodied animals hold themselves across the flow. Smaller ones gather in the shelter of each ridge: transparent swimmers, pale many-legged things, creatures no larger than the pits in a block. They feed on one another and on the thin, nearly invisible life suspended in the water. Nothing green grows here.

The turbulence can be seen more readily than heard. From a distance the basin seems almost silent. Close to a wall, there is a fine ticking as plates touch, then an occasional deep knock carried a long way through the stone. The animals scatter at each knock and return. The current goes on wearing down the old courses; in quiet joints, small bodies lay down new ones.
