The summit has no peak. It is a continent-wide table of pale stone, so high that the sky has gone dark blue even at noon. The surface is nearly level, but not smooth: aeons of grit have worn its harder minerals into low, branching ribs. Between them lie flats of fine, dry powder, packed so tightly that a footprint can remain for years. The nearest ridge may be a day’s walk away. There are no clouds to measure it against and almost nothing standing between.

The place is never still. Across the flats, sheets of powder lift and race in different directions, tracing winds too thin to feel. Far off, a stone rib breaks along its whole length; its sections separate, turn, and settle again. A crack can travel toward you faster than you can run, then stop a few paces short. These changes make almost no sound. In the scant air, even a large collapse reaches the ear as a faint tick, long after the eye has seen it happen.

Yet the ground looks impossibly old. Fresh edges do not stay fresh. A broken face whitens and loses its sharpness within days, as though the plateau has a settled shape to which every disturbance must return. The stone is banded with minerals from ages before the present sky. Whatever once cut valleys here has finished its work; now even the violent rearrangements leave the surface broadly flat.

Here and there stands a narrow black mast, no taller than a person. Most lean. Some have fallen and sunk halfway into the powder without leaving a hollow around them. At intervals, the horizon appears to bend between two of them, and a distant rib takes on a new position without crossing the space in between. The masts may be holding the plateau together, or they may be the last things unable to move with it.

Life keeps close to the stone. Dark, papery growth clings to the sheltered sides of the ribs, drawing warmth from a few hours of sun. Once in a long while, a small pale animal crosses an open flat, stopping whenever the powder begins to stir. Its tracks form a delicate line in the enormous empty ground. By evening the line is gone, though the flat has not changed in any way you can see.
