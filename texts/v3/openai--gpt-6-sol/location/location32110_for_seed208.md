The land is almost flat, but never quite level. Water has spent an age finding the slightest weakness in it: broad sheets lie across the dark stone, then narrow into channels no deeper than a hand. What were once hills have been worn down to long, low swells. Their bare backs emerge from the water at intervals, smooth enough to shine in the dim light. Nothing grows on them. There is no grass to show the direction of the wind, no root to split a seam.

The sound does that instead. Beneath the plain, water rushes through old hollows in the rock. Where a channel opens into one of them, it produces a continuous, enormous note; elsewhere the water knocks and rattles as though stones are being dragged along a floor. Rain passes in cold, slanting bands, adding a hiss that can be heard only when the deeper roaring briefly falters. Across a mile of open ground, the noises overlap until it is difficult to tell whether something has moved nearby.

Animals are everywhere, though they leave much of the plain looking empty. Pale, many-legged things shelter under the lips of submerged stone and come out when fresh water reaches them. Long-backed swimmers follow the channels, mouths pressed to the rock, grazing on films too thin to see unless the light catches them. At the pools, flocks of narrow-footed birds stand motionless against the current, then all strike at once. When the rain stops, their calls begin to carry through the hollows: sharp cries caught and repeated by the stone.

A few raised places hold signs of people. There are short walls fitted from fallen slabs, and lines of stones laid across channels to slow the water. Beside one wall sits a rack of lashed bone and driftwood, weighted with rocks to keep it from blowing away. The work is careful but small against the plain. Some of the oldest walls have worn down to single rows of rounded stones; water runs over them now without changing course.

By evening, mist forms close to the ground. The pools and the wet rock take on the same dark color, and the safe routes between them become hard to judge. The birds climb onto the bare swells. Underfoot, the stone keeps sounding.
