The city lies almost level with the plain. From the roof of its transit hall, you can see the next district several kilometres away: a scatter of narrow towers, long bridges low to the ground, and, between them, broad dark spaces filled with trees. It is an urban place, but it has left room around nearly everything it has built.

The ground looks new. Black banks rise beside the roads with edges too clean for a place that receives rain. Shallow channels cross the open lots, still bearing the fine parallel marks of whatever laid them down. Grass has taken hold in the cuts. Broad-leaved vines climb the tower faces, and small trees have rooted in deliberate gaps along the bridges, yet there is little fallen soil around them. Their roots run into seams that remain exactly wide enough.

It is warm beneath a low, luminous cloud deck. The light has the grey-green colour of a room with curtains drawn. Rain comes briefly, sometimes only over one district; more often moisture gathers without a shower. Water beads on leaves, on handrails, on the undersides of the bridges. It drops into narrow stone mouths at the base of each planting bed. Between those mouths, the paving stays faintly dusty.

A canal runs through the centre of the city, though for most of its length you can walk across it. There are pools in the deeper turns, warm and still enough to hold a reflection of the towers. In one, a small animal breaks the surface and disappears. Otherwise the living movement is chiefly vegetal: leaves turning slowly toward brighter parts of the sky, pale roots extending through the channel joints, seed cases opening after a damp night.

People here say the banks are young because they are. The city keeps its worn material elsewhere. Traffic, roots, rain and weather do their ordinary work, but the change is carried away through the paving and gathered at a few designated places. At the eastern edge, beyond the last inhabited tower, there is a basin full of rounded stone, soft brick dust and dark, mature soil. It smells of old gardens. Each morning its slopes are a little lower. Crews take the soil away for the trees and draw the basin’s new outline before anyone builds near it.

That work gives the city its peculiar quiet. You can hear a distant tram crossing a span, a drop of water finding the bottom of a drain, the dry click of a seed case. Under those sounds, if you stand on a newly made bank, there is a faint vibration passing through it toward the east. The street holds firm beneath your feet while, somewhere out beyond the towers, its years arrive.
