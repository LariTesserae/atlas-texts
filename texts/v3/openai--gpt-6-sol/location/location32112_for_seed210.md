The shelf ends on all four sides in a drop of cloud. It is no larger than a grove, high enough that the air stings the inside of the nose. Its ground is new black stone: sharp-lipped cracks, clean splinters, hollows not yet rounded by rain. Water stands in those hollows and beads along the edges of the rock. Nothing runs off the shelf. A drop can swell beneath an overhang until it is larger than a fist, then remain there, trembling.

There are only a few plants, but each occupies its space completely. Broad, dark leaves overlap above deep mats of moss. Pale stems rise separately from the stone, leaving open stretches of wet black ground between them. The moss is lush and cold to the touch; its green is almost the only color the dimness permits. Small animals crowd it—silver-backed beetles, snails with soft translucent shells, thin creatures that resemble newts until they climb a vertical face without moving their feet. Birds are harder to count. You can hear many of them calling from the leaves, though only one or two are ever visible at once.

Beneath the calls is a steady industrial noise. It has layers: a low engine thrum, the rapid tick of something feeding through teeth, a hiss that rises precisely whenever the thrum dips. There are no buildings, no wires, no discarded parts. Press an ear to the stone and the noise becomes louder. Watch a crack long enough and its two sides appear to mesh, like the edges of enormous gears turning just out of sight. They never shift against a finger laid across them.

The animals behave as if work is being done here. Beetles line up beside a pool, lift their front legs in sequence, and disperse. A bird drops a pale seed into a fissure; farther away, the same bird is already holding the seed above the same fissure. The water receives falling droplets with a succession of distinct plinks, yet its surface keeps the same set of rings. Even your breath returns as a little cloud in front of you, already there when you begin to exhale.

The sharp stone seems newly exposed, and the growth upon it seems long established. Neither impression gives way. The shelf remains cool and wet, the moss does not advance across the open ground, and the cracks never soften. At its edge, cloud hides whatever supports it. The sound continues at its exact pace, loud enough to fill the place but never loud enough to drown out a single bird.
