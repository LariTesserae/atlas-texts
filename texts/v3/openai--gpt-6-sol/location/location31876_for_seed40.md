From the upper road, the valley looks quarried, though nothing here was dug out. Its slopes are assembled from steep ranks of cast-white platforms, each edged with steel. Gantries span the gaps. Towers rise in close files, their bright faces nearly touching, until the far side of the valley becomes a fine, hard texture rather than a collection of buildings. There is no exposed earth. Even the ridgeline is a row of bolted sections, still sharp at the corners, with lifting lugs left in place.

The light is stronger than the cool air seems to warrant. It reaches the narrowest passages and lies on the undersides of bridges. A person descending through the works passes through bands of illumination that have no visible source, then beneath banks of electric lamps that cast no shadow at all. At a distance the whole valley appears still. Up close, indicator wheels turn in glass housings, belts creep behind grilles, and freight cradles advance along their rails so slowly that a watcher may mistake one for a fixed part of the track.

Rain comes occasionally, fine enough to leave the roofs merely darker. Most of the water is made by the place itself. Condensation beads on cold pipework and gathers under the lips of stairways. It travels through deliberate gutters and along accidental ones where two manufactured surfaces fail to agree. There are shallow basins full of clear water, and long dry troughs waiting for a flow that may return next month. Small plants occupy the damp joins: pale grass, a few rust-colored mosses, seedlings with roots spread flat against the sealant. None have much room to grow.

Animals have found more room. Birds nest in the hollow ends of beams and walk the ledges in busy, silent lines. Pale lizards bask on the illuminated walls. Under the grates, one can see the quick movement of many-legged creatures; in the drainage basins, translucent fish hold position beneath the drips. Their numbers are surprising in a place with so little greenery. They feed on one another, on residues from the works, and on the thin films that form wherever moisture stays.

The valley is quiet enough to hear a drop strike a metal plate far below. The engines are enclosed, and the belts do not slap. Even the birds seldom call. Sometimes a siren opens its mouth at the top of a tower and produces only a change in pressure, felt in the ears.

Survey marks suggest that the upper road is level, but walking it does not bear this out. A tower visible to the left of one stair may stand to its right at the bottom. Workers have painted arrows at these crossings, then painted over them and begun again. The freight rails are more dependable: a cradle enters a wall here and emerges somewhere else, eventually, carrying the same load. The people who maintain the route check the seals, grease the wheels, and record the time between departures and arrivals. They leave the crossings themselves alone.

By late afternoon the bright faces begin to cool. Water appears first on the newest bolts, then on the older spans, and the animals move toward the wet metal. Across the valley, the cradles continue their nearly imperceptible travel. The unfinished ridgeline keeps its sharp outline against the light.
