The plain begins just beyond the last rise of road. It is low enough that water shows everywhere without quite covering it: in the black seams between sedge tussocks, beneath the roots of young alders, in the shallow impressions left by yesterday’s rain. Farther out, narrow channels catch what little light there is and give the ground the appearance of having been cut into long, dark strips.

The land is new. Gravel still lies on top of the peat in places, carried there by a recent flood, and some banks end in clean, raw edges where the channels changed course overnight. Across the plain, reeds lean in different directions. They have been flattened by wind, stood up again, and bent by water moving the other way. Moss has already taken hold on the exposed stones. Willow shoots crowd the banks so thickly that, from a distance, the flood’s fresh cuts look fringed and green.

The cold comes through everything. Rain hangs from the underside of leaves and from the wire stays of the few structures on the plain. A pump house stands on short legs near the road; two more, smaller and barely visible, mark the route toward the sea. Their windows give off a dull amber light. Otherwise the night is made of silhouettes: alder crowns, reed beds, the distant straight line of the outer barrier.

It is difficult to hear a voice here. Wind drives through the reeds with a steady hiss, but under it is a much greater sound—the continuous, changing roar of water passing the barrier. At intervals a gate opens, and the roar drops into a heavy pulse that can be felt in the road. Rain strikes the pump-house roof. Somewhere close, a flock of small birds lifts suddenly from the mud, their wingbeats lost almost at once. Fox tracks and waterbird tracks cross on the higher patches; both are washed out before morning.

The barrier is too distant to look remarkable. Its visible parts are concrete and steel, dark against a darker sky. Most of its work happens below the channel beds, where a field holds the deepest tidal pressure a little longer than the walls alone could bear it. The gates can then release water gradually, though *gradually* on a night like this still means whole banks disappearing. The road is set back for that reason. Its markers show where people may safely walk, not where the land is expected to remain.

From the pump house, the plain extends several miles in broken bands of water and growth. Each gust reveals a different reach of it. Reeds part around a newly filled channel; a bank gives way with a sound too small to distinguish from the rest; the lights along the road tremble in their wet reflections. By morning the water will have withdrawn from some places and entered others. The willows will still be there, crowded along whatever edges are left.
