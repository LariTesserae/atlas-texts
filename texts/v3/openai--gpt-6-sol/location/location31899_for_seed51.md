The land is almost level, though it takes a long time to cross. A low ridge of clay may keep your feet dry for half an hour; on either side, dark water shows through sedge and the pale roots of reeds. Beyond that are more ridges, more pools, and a distant line of trees that never quite becomes a forest. Rain has worn the ridges down to gentle backs. Their shapes seem permanent, not because nothing changes here, but because water has had so long to find its preferred ways through them.

It is cool even when the rain stops. Moisture beads on the undersides of leaves and gathers in the pores of exposed stone. Some stones weep from seams well above the waterline. Their surfaces are soft with age, their hollows full of fine black silt, yet none looks ready to break. The whole plain has an old, settled appearance: channels shift by inches, banks give way grain by grain, and everything fallen has already become part of something beneath it.

There is little room between things close at hand. Rushes brush your sleeves. Moss thickens around the stems of small, silver-leaved shrubs. Films of algae lie one over another in the pools, clear enough that you can see the darker films below. Farther out, though, the country opens. You can hear water working through places you cannot see—a steady rushing and knocking from across the marsh, loud enough that the rare call of a bird reaches you only in its pauses.

At first the sound resembles a flood passing through a narrow channel. Then you notice that it is coming from several directions at once. Each shallow pool has a faint tone, and each tone alters as a ripple reaches its edge. When a larger sheet of water lifts under the wind, the whole plain answers. Some notes are too low to hear except as pressure in the jaw. Others make the reeds tremble while the water beneath them stays still.

The pools do not simply reflect the sky. In one, a cloud passes over a few breaths before it passes overhead. In another, the rain is still falling after your coat has begun to dry. These delays are small and regular. If you wait beside a pool, its reflections catch up; if you move on, a few of them seem to follow. No path divides the altered water from the ordinary kind. Their edges mingle in a single puddle.

Beneath the silt, threads finer than roots run through the clay and into the stone. They are living, or close enough to living to mend after a bank collapses. Along their length, droplets change weight, heat crosses cold water without warming it, and a ripple can arrive before the stem that made it has finished moving. There are no towers or buried machines. Whatever the threads do, they have grown through the plain for longer than the present channels have existed. The marsh wears them as it wears its moss.

Very few animals stay in view. A narrow-winged bird makes one circuit over the reeds and is gone. Something small leaves a wake under the weed, then no wake at all. But the vegetation is busy: seed heads opening in the damp, roots taking hold in yesterday’s silt, moss spreading over a place where water stood this morning. Toward evening the tones of the pools lower together. The weeping stones grow wet again, and across the broad, dim plain, rain begins at one edge and slowly reaches the other.
