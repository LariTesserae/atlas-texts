At first it looks like a range of ordinary high mountains after rain: pale ridges under a hard blue sky, a broad saddle between them, and water shining in every hollow. The air is cool enough to sting the throat. Nothing grows on the slopes. Their color comes from stone alone—chalk-white faces, seams of iron red, and banks of dark scree so finely broken that they look soft from a distance.

The saddle is wetter than its height should allow. Water beads on the undersides of ledges and runs uphill along hairline cracks before spilling from them in thin, cold sheets. There are no streams in the usual sense. Each pool drains into the rock at one end and is replenished at the other by a persistent dripping. Between the pools lie terraces of stone worn smooth over a very long time. Their edges are rounded, their channels deep, their loose pieces sorted by size. Whatever happens here has happened often enough to finish its work.

In the strong sun, every wet surface throws light onto another. The ground can be brighter than the sky. A traveler crossing the saddle sees the far ridge clearly, then loses it behind the reflection of a nearby puddle. Looking down, they may see the ridge continuing beneath their feet, complete with its old gullies and patches of snow. A thrown pebble breaks that image and reveals a few inches of water over solid rock.

Sound makes the distances still less trustworthy. Water falls continuously from the western cliffs, with a roar that fills the open valley. Close to the cliffs, though, the falls are almost silent. The loudness is out on the saddle, where loose stones rattle in their beds and a struck rock gives back several blows: the first from nearby, the others from places too distant to see. On clear mornings, the echoes arrive before the strike. The hands working here have learned to wait until the last one passes before striking again.

Their work remains along the terraces. There are low shelters of stacked slabs, roofed with heavier slabs and sealed at the joints with clay. Some have fallen inward; others stand dry despite water passing over their thresholds. Stone wedges are driven into cracks beside the pools. Shallow bowls, chipped from the pale rock, sit beneath particular drips. There are no paths through the saddle, only short flights of cut steps that end at bare faces or emerge from them several paces away. The steps are old. Their treads have been hollowed by use and softened by water, while the surrounding cliff is still sharp.

The water gives a reason to make such things. A bowl placed beneath one drip fills with ordinary cold water. Move it a handspan to the left and it fills with grit from a pool higher up the mountain. Farther left, drops land in the bowl before they leave the stone. The old wedges hold some of these cracks in place. Where a wedge has worked loose, the drip wanders across the face day by day, and the bowls below are abandoned.

By afternoon, wind lifts spray from the falls and carries it over the saddle. New rivulets appear; others stop. Scree shifts with a dry, heavy hiss beneath the louder water. No bird crosses the sky, and nothing stirs among the stones except what wind and water can move. Toward evening the light leaves the valley floor, but it remains caught for a while in the wet cliffs. From the far end of the saddle, the little shelters shine among the pools, and the mountain beyond them seems close enough to touch. It is best to keep walking toward a particular shelter. Anyone who walks toward the mountain alone may spend the remaining daylight approaching it without reaching its foot.
