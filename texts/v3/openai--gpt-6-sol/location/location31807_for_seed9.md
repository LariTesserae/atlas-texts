The garden occupies a single roof, though from inside it is hard to remember there is a building beneath it. Its walls are high enough to catch the warm air rising from the machinery below. Above them, clouds pass close to the handrails; beyond one gap, the lower slopes are only a blue suggestion. The floor is not earth but a fitted basin of dark ceramic plates, still sharp at the seams. Water gathers on them, finds the narrow channels left for it, and travels a few feet before disappearing into a grate.

Everything here was installed recently. Pale ribs arch between the walls to carry pipes and trellises. The concrete has the smooth, almost damp look of concrete not yet weathered, and the little banks of growing medium still hold the squared shape of their moulds. Yet the plants have begun to disregard those shapes. Ginger leaves press against valve housings. Ferns open in the shadow of an inspection hatch. Vines climb the trellises and, where they have run out of trellis, test the pipes.

Rain arrives often at this height, sometimes as a shower, sometimes as droplets that seem to form directly on every surface. A row of fine nozzles adds its own weather when the tanks are full enough. The roof changes quietly through the day: one channel fills and tips into the next; leaves bow under water and lift again; a pump starts far below, then stops. It is warm under the cloud, with a faint mineral heat coming through the plates. There is no streambed yet, no worn stone, only the first faint stain where water repeatedly takes the same turn.

Almost nothing moves among the leaves but the leaves themselves. Once in a while a small brown moth appears at a lamp housing, rests through the daylight, and is gone by evening. The place seems to have room for more life than it has acquired. For now it is a made little watershed, high above any natural one, learning where its water wants to go.
