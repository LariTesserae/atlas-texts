At the top of the mountain there is a nearly level shelf no wider than a small town. From its edge, the land falls away so steeply that the lower slopes disappear into haze. Yet the shelf itself has no peaks left on it. Ages of wind have worn the stone into low, smooth backs, with shallow hollows between them where dust has gathered.

The town occupies those hollows. Its houses are built close together from fitted slabs and sun-dried earth, their roofs weighted with loose stone. Alleys scarcely wide enough for two people pass between walls polished by hands and pack animals. Steps have been cut wherever the rock rises; after generations of wear, some are easier to find by habit than by sight. There are no gutters. Rain is rare enough that the old ones are used as paths.

Plants fill much of the space the builders left. Thick-leaved shrubs lean over doorways, and thorn trees spread across the roofs from pockets of soil held in place by little retaining walls. Vines root deep in cracks and hang in heavy curtains, making the town seem greener from within than it does from the rim. In the dimness under them, pale insects travel over bark and plaster. Lizards wait in the warmth of walls; small birds quarrel over seeds overhead. The larger animals—goats, mostly—keep to the outer paths, where they can crop leaves without stripping anyone’s garden.

Even at midday the alleys are dusky. Light reaches them through leaves and dust, or reflects off a facing wall. The air is warm and dry and smells of resin, flour, animal coats, and stone heated slowly through the morning.

Sound carries strangely here. A hand mill turning in one house can be heard three alleys away. So can a hammer setting a roof slab, the scrape of a broom, the bells on a passing goat. Beneath those sounds is the steady rasp of insects and, at the exposed rim, wind crossing the vast drop. Little happens quickly. A roof is mended one stone at a time; a thorn tree closes an abandoned doorway over many years. At the edge of town, the oldest steps end in bare, rounded rock. The wind has worn the path there almost smooth, but people still cross it each morning to look out and judge the day.
