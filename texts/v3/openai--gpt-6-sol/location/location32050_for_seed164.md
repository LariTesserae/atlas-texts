The plateau begins above the clouds and goes on past the horizon. Its surface is not a plain but a field of immense, shallow folds: dark stone rising in clean-edged ridges, dropping into basins wide enough to hold their own weather. The rock looks newly exposed. There is little gravel at the foot of the cliffs, no softened scree, almost no soil. Even where water runs over it, the corners remain sharp.

Water is everywhere, though seldom deep. It stands in the seams of the stone and gathers on the undersides of bridges before falling in steady threads. Fine rain hangs in some basins and leaves others clear. The cold makes every railing slick to the touch. Beneath the cloud ceiling, a few broad patches of daylight travel over the plateau without noticeably warming it.

The city occupies the folds rather than covering them. From a distance its districts look like lines ruled across an otherwise empty map: rows of pumping houses, gantries, tanks, and narrow streets, followed by many miles of bare stone. Up close, the works are enormous. Pipes enter the rock in bundles as thick as buildings. Service bridges cross gaps where their far ends disappear into mist. Windows are lit; switches are labeled; stairwells have been swept clean. No one comes to use them.

There are no birds, insects, or tracks. The vegetation makes that absence stranger. Moss grows in deep green banks around warm outflow pipes. Pale, jointed stems stand shoulder-high in sheltered cuttings, so close together that they hide the factory walls behind them. Their leaves carry beads of water that never seem quite ready to fall. Beyond the shelter of the works, plants thin abruptly, leaving the upland open and nearly empty again.

The sound reaches those empty places. It is a sustained industrial roar, lower than thunder and more even: turbines turning somewhere under the plateau, water forced through chambers, transformers humming along the streets. At the edges of the basins it reflects from cliff to cliff until its source is impossible to locate. There are no calls within it, no footsteps, no brief silences in which to listen harder.

The machinery may be keeping the place as it is. Water leaves the pumping houses clear and cold and returns to them just as clear. The young ridges do not seem to shed a stone; the green cuttings do not spread into the open. Here and there, fresh bolts shine on bridges whose foundations look centuries old. The plateau has all the equipment of a place built to meet an urgent change, and all the stillness of one in which that change has been indefinitely postponed.
