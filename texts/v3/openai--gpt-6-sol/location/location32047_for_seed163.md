The valley lies high enough that the sun feels close but gives little warmth. Its floor is newly exposed ground: pale glacial gravel, loose stones, and shelves of rock with edges still sharp. Water has not yet worn them into a settled pattern. It spreads instead—through the gravel, over the shelves, beneath a continuous skin of moss and sedge. Step off the narrow service track and the ground gives slightly, as if the plants are holding the valley floor together until something firmer forms beneath them.

From the ridge, the place looks almost empty. There are few trees and fewer animals to catch the eye. Yet nearly every surface is growing. Low green cushions cover the stones; fine roots bind the silt between them; wet grasses lie flat where the water runs and stand upright a few feet away. Bright light strikes thousands of shallow pools among their stems. The pools are so clear that the pale, unfinished ground shows through.

At the head of the valley, several streams gather against a concrete intake. A grated mouth, a squat powerhouse farther down, and a line of poles are most of the human marks here. The machinery is small against the open slopes, but its sound is not. Water hammers the grate and drops through the turbines; the powerhouse adds a steady metallic pitch to the roar. Near it, speech is useless. Even out among the sedges, the noise remains, seeming to rise from the wet ground underfoot.

Little changes while you watch. A bead of water swells on the underside of a leaf and falls. A loose stone settles into the moss. Cloud shadow crosses the valley, briefly cooling the glare, then passes. The streams are busy making their first channels, but the plants keep slowing them, spreading the water wide again.
