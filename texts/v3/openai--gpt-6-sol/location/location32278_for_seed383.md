The city lies beneath a plain of old limestone, where the roof has thinned into a maze of openings. From above, the openings might look like cracks. Below, they admit whole columns of daylight. Light strikes the pale floor and travels outward by reflection, under arches and along streets, until even the far walls of the cavern are visible: terraces, bridges, a distant line of houses, and then another district beyond them.

Everything has been worn round. The natural ledges have lost their edges; so have the oldest steps cut into them. A street can be recognized by its width and by the doorways beside it, but underfoot it feels like the bed of a dry river. Walls are made of fitted limestone, rubble packed with clay, and occasional dark slabs carried from deeper strata. There is little metal. The bridges rest on broad stone supports, and their repairs are plain to see: a new wedge driven beneath an ancient block, a fresh hand-cut stair joining two nearly vanished ones.

Few people live here now. Their houses are set far apart among empty courtyards and roofless workshops. Much of the city is given over to channels. They have low banks that can be lifted and rebuilt by hand, because water never follows the same route for long. On some mornings it beads under the vaults and drips steadily into the channels. On others the stone is dry before noon. After rain on the surface, a shallow current may run through three districts, filling old basins and leaving the rest untouched.

The animals know these changes before the people do. Pale, jointed creatures come out of seams in the paving; small fish appear in pools that seemed barren a day earlier. Along the damp margins, a faint rust-coloured microbial film grows on the stone. It supports minute grazers, which in turn draw larger hunters out of the cracks. There are many of them, though they are easy to overlook in so much open space. Nothing green grows here.

The place is almost quiet. A drop reaches a basin. Grit brushes against grit. Once in a while, a stone shutter is pulled across a doorway. Yet the city is seldom still. Air moves between the sun-warmed openings and the cool lower passages, carrying fine limestone dust in long, low streams. It clears one avenue while burying the next. Condensation spreads over a wall, then retreats and leaves a bright line of salt. Light shifts from court to court overhead, and the animals follow the wet edges across the paving.

By evening, someone may have to move a channel bank again. The work is small compared with the age of the city, but it is never finished.
