The first thing you notice is the light. You are far below the surface, but there is no need to wait for your eyes to adjust. The roof is a broad, thin vault of ice and pale stone, bright wherever daylight reaches it from above. Cracks run through it for miles. They show as sharp white lines overhead, and their light falls across the hollow without quite making shadows.

The floor is a long way down, though little of it is bare. Plants hold the chamber open. Great rooted stems rise from the ground and divide into flat, interlocking fans beneath the roof; other growth hangs from them in curtains that stop well short of the floor. Between these enormous supports are expanses of clear air. You can see a distant stand of stems as a fine dark comb, and beyond it another, fainter one. A place so thoroughly made of plants ought to feel crowded. Here, most of the space is the cold brightness between them.

Nothing has had long to settle into its shape. In some places the ice has recently split, leaving angular slabs caught among the upper branches. A broken stem shows a clean, wet interior against its dry, dark skin. From time to time a crack opens above with a report like an axe striking wood. A whole hanging curtain begins to sway; its motion travels from stand to stand across the hollow, until even the far comb is moving. Then loose leaves come down in a slow, disorderly storm. By the time they reach the floor, the air is nearly still again.

There is little running water. Cold beads gather on the underside of the roof and fall in widely spaced drips. Beneath each persistent leak, the plants grow close and soft: low cups, tangled roots, a bank of small pale leaves. Elsewhere they are tough and widely spaced, their surfaces dusted with old frost. An animal may have passed through a damp patch—there are shallow paired prints in the dark litter—but it is unlikely you will see it. Most sounds come from drops, shifting ice, and stems rubbing against one another high out of reach.

Someone has been here. Beside one of the drip pools, a few stones hold down the ends of a woven screen, now stiff with cold. A narrow ladder of lashed branches climbs partway up a living support and ends where a newer branch has grown across it. There is no road to either thing. The plants make passages and take them away again, and anyone crossing this bright hollow has to look up as often as ahead.
