The valley lies high in the mountains, though there is no air in it. Water fills it to the underside of an ice roof, and above that roof the peaks continue upward into weather no one down here can see. The old valley floor is a long way below: broad, dark, and worn smooth where currents have passed over it for generations.

Sound travels through the place almost too well. A fall of water enters somewhere beyond the western ridge and makes a continuous, low concussion. From time to time the ice overhead contracts with a report like a split tree. After each crack comes a finer noise, repeated along the stone walls as loosened grains slide into the depths. Nothing cries out or answers. There are no animals here.

Plants occupy the valley in separate, extravagant territories. At the foot of a cliff, black fronds grow taller than a house, their thin leaves combing dissolved minerals from the current. Across a wide stretch of bare gravel, round-leaved stems crowd a warm fissure so thickly that the rock beneath them cannot be seen. Elsewhere, roots alone emerge from the sediment, braided into pale columns; their leaves grow far above, pressed flat against the ice. Between these colonies lie long reaches of open water and empty stone.

The cold slows decay. Fallen leaves descend for days, turn once in a crosscurrent, and settle intact. The plants do not need daylight; their roots feed on what the mountain releases into the water. Where a spring brings up a new mineral, growth follows it. Where a seam runs out, a whole stand gradually darkens and lets go. That is how the patches move across the valley, though a person might return after a lifetime and find the same bare gravel between them.

On a shelf above the floor stand three little walls of stacked slate. Their doorways are too low for someone to enter without kneeling, and each encloses a stone bowl. Water passes freely through the joints. Silt has gathered in two bowls; in the third, a sprig of black vegetation has taken hold. Farther along the shelf, a path of fist-sized stones leads toward the drop-off and stops at its edge.

There is no steady light by which to follow it. Occasionally the ice takes on a dim grayness, enough to separate a cliff from the water before both disappear again. The plants remain dark even then. One must feel the current turn around them, and listen for the great fall at the valley’s far end.
