The ground is a continuous plant. It has no clear beginning underfoot: a dark, springy skin of interlocked roots and flattened stems, slick with water that rises when pressed and sinks back without making a footprint. Here and there, new ribs push through it in straight, sharp ridges. They have the pale, unweathered look of something only recently exposed. Nothing has had time to soften an edge, though little seems to move now.

The plain goes on beyond sight. Its great leaves overlap like roofs, low enough to make the air beneath them warm and close. Higher up, trunks have fused into long parallel walls; between them hang thousands of thick, motionless strands. Droplets gather along the strands and remain there, too small to fall. There are no channels or open pools. Water is everywhere, held in films, pores, and shallow hollows just under the living surface.

At first the dark feels complete. Then a dull green line appears far away, followed by another: light leaking through the seams of sealed seed chambers. In that faint light, the walls show regular bands, as though something had stamped them out in runs. Each band contains a row of woody chambers under tension, fed by narrow vessels from the wet ground. Their membranes separate salts and lay down dense sheets of new fibre. Across the plain, immense lengths of root keep a steady electrical charge. The place has the scale and repetition of industry, but no one built it. Its pipes, presses, and conduits are its anatomy.

There is almost no sound. A bead of water occasionally detaches in the dark and lands with a small, flat tick. Deep below that, if one stays still long enough, there is a pressure in the ears more than a hum. No insects answer it. No birds cross the canopy; no hidden creature disturbs the roots.

The vegetation seems to have filled every available space and then reached an exact balance. The young ridges remain sharp. The suspended drops do not grow noticeably heavier. Even the warm air holds still. Walking changes the surface for a moment, and then it closes behind the foot, leaving the vast plant plain as it was.
