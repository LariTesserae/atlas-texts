The ridge is high enough that daylight reaches it. Far below, the ocean falls away into blue-black depth; here, on the crown of a newly split seamount, you can see across a basin no wider than a room. Its walls are sharp black glass and pale, freshly exposed stone. There is no sand yet. Nothing has had time to wear down.

Cool water fills every notch. It is crowded with animals, though at first the basin looks like a jumble of loose metal: silver bodies packed flank to flank, dark shells wedged into fractures, transparent creatures suspended in the narrow spaces between. They part for you by inches and close again. No fronds or weed soften the rock. What appears from a distance to be rust-colored growth is a film of bacteria feeding where mineral-rich water seeps through the stone. Small grazers scrape it away; larger mouths wait for the grazers.

The whole ridge sounds as if it is working. Snapping animals strike their claws with reports that travel through your bones. Shells knock against glassy ledges. From deep in a crack comes a steady, low thump: water pressing through a natural valve in the rock, pausing, and pressing through again. At intervals the fish turn together, and their movement makes a sound like a heavy sheet being shaken. Nothing here was made, but the noises suggest an engine room—pumps, taps, a belt starting up—heard from inside its walls.

The seep gives off little heat. It releases iron and other dissolved minerals, enough to feed the bacterial film and, through it, this extraordinary press of animal life. Some of the larger creatures carry faint electrical pulses along their sides. When they pass close to the stone, the film darkens in branching lines, then slowly clears. In the ample daylight you can watch those lines appear without seeing anything move to draw them.

For all its noise, the basin changes patiently. A flake breaks from an edge perhaps once in a season. The thump deepens as a passage narrows with mineral deposits, then grows hollow again when pressure breaks them free. The animals keep their places until hunger, a pulse, or the shadow of a passing hunter shifts the entire crowd at once. Afterward the ridge looks much as it did before: bright water above it, the abyss below, and every small opening occupied.
