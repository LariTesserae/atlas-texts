At this height, the mountain has almost finished wearing itself down. Its summit is not a peak but a shallow stone basin, perhaps a few streets across, enclosed by low, rounded ridges. From a distance it looks bare. Up close, there are lanes between walls, little courts, doorways, and ranks of roofless rooms. The stones fit closely, though their edges have softened so much that it is hard to tell where masonry ends and bedrock begins.

Nothing remains in the rooms. There is no glass, metal, hearth, or tool, only stone laid against stone and, in a few places, stones that seem to have grown into their neighbors. The settlement has been here long enough for the mountain to resume its shape through it. Floors slope with the underlying rock. A broad square holds a glacial hollow at its center, as though a public place were built around a much older emptiness.

The air is cold even in full daylight. Rain comes rarely but arrives all at once, and its water takes hours to find the ground. It beads beneath ledges, darkens the seams of walls, and gathers in small basins polished by generations of overflow. Moss is thick wherever a wall faces away from the sun. In the narrower lanes, fern fronds and low, glossy shrubs press out of cracks with surprising abundance. Step into an open court, though, and there may be nothing growing for many paces but a few gray lichens. The place alternates between crowded green corners and expanses of vacant stone.

It is seldom quiet. Wind enters from the far side of the basin and passes through the empty doorways at different pitches. One passage booms; another gives a long, thin whistle. Loose gravel clicks along the paving. After rain, water can be heard inside the walls, falling from pocket to pocket without ever showing itself. In winter, a sharper sound joins these: the crack of ice opening a seam that has held for centuries.

A small pale bird sometimes hunts the courts, lifting insects from the wet moss. Otherwise movement belongs mostly to weather and plants. The shrubs lean out after rain; frost pushes pebbles into the lanes; a film of water travels briefly down a street and disappears under its last step. Nothing here changes quickly except the sound. By the time a visitor has crossed the basin, the wind may have made an entirely different city of it.
