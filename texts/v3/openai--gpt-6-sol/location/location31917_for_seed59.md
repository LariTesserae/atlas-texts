The town occupies a saddle between two higher slopes. From a distance it looks like a dark patch of forest that has learned to stand in rows. Up close, there are lanes, steps, doorways, a square no wider than a pond—but almost nothing stands free of plants. Hazel trunks serve as fence posts and have grown thick around the rails. Moss joins the roof shingles into soft green planes. Ferns spring from every wall seam; roots pass beneath thresholds and emerge on the other side as if the houses were brief interruptions.

It rained in the night, and the hill is still giving the water back. It beads on the underside of leaves, trickles through woven branch gutters, and runs down the middle of the steepest lane in a channel worn smooth by generations of storms. The ground is firm where people have laid flat stones, spongy an arm’s length away. A spring rises behind the upper houses and is divided among shallow troughs: one for drinking, one for washing, one that carries the overflow beneath the square. No one tries to stop the seepage altogether. Each year they lift a few stones, clear the roots from a channel, and put the stones back.

The air is cool enough to make wet sleeves unpleasant. Daylight reaches the square, though it has to pass through leaves and a moving ceiling of low cloud. People work outside under little projecting roofs, splitting kindling, mending wicker, setting out bowls to catch clean drips. Their tools are simple and well kept. The paths and drains are older than many of the houses; wherever the slope has slumped, a stair bends around it rather than cutting it away.

It is difficult to hear any one task for long. Water clatters in the troughs. A flock of small birds erupts from a fruiting tree, settles on the roofs, erupts again. Swallows skim between the houses, and beetles knock against the open shutters. Frogs call from a wall that ought to be too high and dry for them, until one notices the dark, wet crack running through it. Hens dispute the market scraps with wild birds. Something rustles in every hedge. The whole saddle seems occupied at once, from the saturated earth to the highest branches.

When another shower crosses the ridge, awnings are pulled in and baskets moved under cover. For a few minutes the lanes run faster, the birds fall quiet, and the town sounds chiefly of water. Then the rain passes. Doors open, calls begin again, and the people go back to work before the leaves have finished dripping.
