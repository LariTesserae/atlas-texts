Beneath the oldest part of the town is a court that has no earth in it. Its floor is fired brick; its walls are brick faced with lime; even the little mounds along its edges are heaps of broken pots and worn paving stones. Low arches divide it into bays, so that from the middle you can see only a few doorways ahead before the place folds out of sight. Someone built every surface here, though much of the work is too old to look deliberate anymore. Steps have shallow hollows where feet have landed for generations. Mortar has drawn back into the joints. The corners of the arches are rounded by shoulders, baskets, and passing loads.

Daylight comes through grilles in the street overhead. It falls broadly enough to make this an ordinary bright place at noon, but the light keeps the pattern of the grilles: bands across the floor, a shifting lattice on the walls. The air is warm and smells of dust, lime, old cooking smoke, and damp clay.

There is no steady stream. After rain, water ticks from the ceiling into a chain of shallow ceramic pans, then runs through narrow grooves to a covered cistern. For a day or two the court is full of drips and little spills. Later the pans dry, leaving pale tide marks and a fine crust that people scrape off with wooden blades. In the meantime, moisture persists where it can: beneath a jar, inside a crack, along the cool underside of a stair. A few tough green stems grow in those places. Most of what looks like foliage at a distance is hanging rope, stacked reed matting, or baskets waiting to be mended.

The court is crowded without being tall. Jars stand mouth to mouth along the walls. Shelves of pegged wood occupy the spaces above them. Earthen ovens, troughs, sleeping ledges, clay flues, and narrow passages all meet so closely that a person carrying two buckets must turn sideways to pass. There is scarcely a flat surface that has only one use. The cistern lid is a workbench when the water is low; the broadest step is where grain is sifted; a patch of floor under the eastern grille is kept clear because it is the best place to examine a chipped pot.

Noise gathers beneath the arches. Pestles knock in uneven rhythm. A stone wheel rasps, children call between bays, and rainwater, when it comes, makes every pan speak at a different pitch. Sparrows enter through the grilles and quarrel over fallen grain. Small lizards wait on the warm flues. Moths gather in the recesses by day; at dusk, bats take the route the sparrows used and leave it again before morning. Rats know the gaps behind the jars, and the court’s thin cats know which gaps have a second opening.

Nothing here stays quite where it was put, though almost nothing is replaced outright. A pan shifts to catch a new drip. A shelf is raised as the floor beneath it wears unevenly. A blocked groove sends rain across a threshold, and by the next afternoon somebody has cut it open with a chisel. The place holds its shape through these small corrections. From the street above it may seem like a buried room; inside, it is a whole warm neighborhood of brick and breath, wearing slowly into the form that suits it.
