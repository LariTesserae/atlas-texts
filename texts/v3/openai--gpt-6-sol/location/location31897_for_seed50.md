High on the mountain, inside a notch no wider than a room, there is a lake with no shore. Water fills the notch to its lip and pours away on every side. From within, the surface is a bright, shaking ceiling. Clouds pass over it; their shadows cross the stone floor a few seconds later, broken into loose pieces of light.

The walls are pale and sharply jointed. They look newly split, with no rounded edges, no beds of silt. On one side, a seam releases a constant drift of white grit. On the other, the rock is acquiring a thin clear skin: minerals settling out of the water grain by grain. The place is being made and taken apart at once. A ledge appears beneath the grit, holds for an hour, then vanishes in a burst of fragments.

There is almost nothing in the water. A few strands of weed cling to a crack where the current briefly slackens. Once in a while, a small dark fish is flung through the notch and disappears down one of its exits. Most of the space is open, cold, and violently moving.

The sound is in the stone as much as in the ears. Water strikes the outer face of the mountain, drives through hidden passages, and makes the whole pocket roar. Individual impacts can be felt: a deep knock underfoot, a rapid rattling in the wall, the high scrape of grit swept across rock. No voice would carry here.

Someone has been here nonetheless. Three flat stones are wedged into a sheltered cleft, one above another, and a length of twisted plant fibre is tied around the uppermost. It may once have held a marker or a tool. Now it streams straight out in the current, pointing toward a passage too narrow to enter.

The notch is near the mountain’s crest. In clear weather, a person at the surface could look over the rim and see lower peaks beyond it. Underwater, there is only this small bright chamber and the force passing through it. Even the empty places do not stay still.
