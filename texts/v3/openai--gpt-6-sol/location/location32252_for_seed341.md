The district fits inside a single bend of a drainage canal. From one end, you can see the other: a row of low brick pump houses, three empty lanes, and a square of dark water held in place by a concrete lip. Beyond the bend there are more roofs, but the steam makes them look distant. Nothing here rises very high. The ground is almost level, worn into shallow dishes where rain collects and stays warm.

The brick has gone soft at the edges. Mortar bulges from some joints and has vanished from others; old repairs show as pale rectangles among the blackened walls. Iron walkways cross the canal at intervals, their steps thinned to shallow curves by years of use. The pumps still run beneath them. Their strokes arrive through the soles of your feet before you hear them: a deep, uneven thud, followed by the hard chatter of a loose valve. Water slaps the square’s retaining wall. Somewhere inside a house, a belt shrieks, catches, and resumes its work.

It is hot even in the dimness. Light gets down through a brown ceiling of cloud and vapor, enough to bring out the green in the plants. They grow lavishly wherever they have found a hold, though there are broad gaps between them: a clump of reeds in an abandoned doorway, broad leaves lifting from a crack in the paving, vines hanging in heavy ropes from an overhead pipe. The cleared lanes remain oddly bare. Runoff sweeps through them each afternoon, carrying silt toward the canal and leaving a fresh dark line on every wall.

The place makes much of its own weather. Hot water comes out of the pump houses; cooler water seeps up through the paving. Where they meet, mist lifts and settles under the walkways. Drops gather on railings and fall in quick succession, sounding for a moment like someone walking there. Then the valve chatters again, and the drops begin falling in time with it. On still days they collect along the underside of the pipes, swelling until the next shudder sends them down.

There are no animals to mistake for movement. No calls come from the reeds, no small things scatter when the machinery starts. Leaves stir in the exhaust; canal weeds bend with the discharge; a screen of roots trembles where a pump draws water past it. Each motion has a source you can find if you wait long enough. In the square, the water repeatedly climbs the wall by a finger’s width, darkens the old tide mark, and slips back. The mark itself has been there for years.
