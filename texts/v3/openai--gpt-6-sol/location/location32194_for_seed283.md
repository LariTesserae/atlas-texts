At night the basin is almost flat. Its channels have had centuries to choose their courses, shifting only a little from season to season, until the ground between them has become a close-fitting arrangement of shallow hollows, low banks, and old silt ridges. In the dark, the ridges are felt more than seen: the soil firms underfoot, then gives way again to warm mud. Water occupies only the lowest places now. It lies in narrow black runs beneath the roots and in pools left by the last rain, while the exposed clay holds a damp sheen.

Nothing has been cleared. Reeds and broad-leaved shrubs crowd the channels; vines bind the shrubs together, and the spaces below them are filled with roots, fallen leaves, and new growth. The heat stays after sunset. Moisture gathers on the undersides of leaves and drops at long intervals, each drop distinct in the quiet.

There are animals in nearly every part of the basin. Small crabs work the soft banks. Fish remain in the deeper pockets, where wading birds stand so still that they seem part of the reeds. Bats pass overhead without showing themselves. Insects cover the leaves and the mud, though few are calling tonight. A sudden rustle travels through one thicket as something larger makes room for itself, then stops. The quiet is not emptiness; it is what so many creatures sound like when they are feeding or waiting.

Beyond the basin, too far away to mark its darkness with lights, engines run through the night. When the air moves from that direction, a faint mechanical hum arrives and fades again. Here there are no roads or walls, only the old channels slowly receiving silt, roots holding their banks, and crowded life following the water as it withdraws and returns.
