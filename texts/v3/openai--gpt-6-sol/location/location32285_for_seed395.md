The basin is so broad that its far edge is usually hidden by dust rather than distance. It lies low and nearly level, a pale floor of new mineral crust with scarcely a worn stone on it. In places the crust has lifted into shallow plates, their edges still sharp. Between them, dark soil is being pushed up from below. The land looks less like a desert that has endured for ages than one that has only recently become dry.

There is no water to see: no channel, pool, or damp margin. Yet the basin is crowded with life. Thorn trees stand close enough for their crowns to meet, and fleshy, ribbed plants fill the gaps beneath them. Roots follow old seams deep into the ground. Fallen leaves do not make a soft floor; they dry into curls, break underfoot, and gather against every stem. Even the open-looking stretches are packed with seedlings, burrow mouths, salt-tolerant grasses, and the low tracks of animals passing through.

Across this growth rises a city of earth. Termite towers cluster by the thousands, from knee-high cones to columns taller than the trees, with narrow ridges connecting one cluster to the next. Their surfaces are built, patched, and built over again. Some entrances face the morning sun; others remain in shade all day. The towers are the most conspicuous structures, but much of the settlement is below ground, where galleries run beneath the roots. From a distance the clusters join into long districts, then vanish into the dusty horizon.

The air is warm and bright. It is also unexpectedly quiet. A dry leaf ticks against a thorn. Fine grains slide down the fresh side of a tower. Lizards wait beside the paths until a procession of insects reaches them; small birds move through the branches without calling. By midday, workers seal exposed passages and retreat underground. Toward evening they reopen them, and new earth appears on the crust. The basin changes a little every day, though almost nothing in it seems to move quickly.
