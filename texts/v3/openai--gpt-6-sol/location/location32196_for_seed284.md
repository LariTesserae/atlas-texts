At the top of the mountain, the town fits inside a crack.

It is a new crack. The opposing walls still show matching teeth of pale stone, and the floor is strewn with chips too sharp to have been worn round. Walkways span the gap at several heights. Beneath them, cable drums, repair stalls, sleeping bunks, and freight cages occupy almost every ledge. From any one platform you can see a surprising number of doorways, but only a narrow strip of sky.

There is no spring here, no damp seam in the rock, and nothing green growing in the grit. The air is cold and dry enough to roughen the inside of the nose. In the dimness, dust gathers on bolts and window frames, then vanishes all at once when a gust finds its way down the crack. Most of the machinery is enclosed; you feel the engines through your boots more often than you hear them.

The place is crowded with animals. Small grey birds nest behind warm switch cabinets and wait for the freight cages, which bring grain and scraps up from below. Black beetles work through the spilled meal. Pale, long-legged hunters follow them into the narrow spaces between pipes. Every opened crate briefly becomes a busy little district. Nothing here could feed so many creatures on its own; the town has made a precarious habitat out of waste heat and lost cargo.

A red lamp comes on above the upper bridge. The platform empties without a shout. First a cable tightens, shifting its shadow across the wall. Then the freight cage rises past, close enough to make the bridge tremble. Birds lift from the cabinets in a single grey rush, circle once in the confined air, and settle where they can watch the unloading.

When the cage has passed, the town is quiet again. A few grains tick off a ledge. Somewhere deep in the new stone, a chip breaks free and falls farther than you can see.
