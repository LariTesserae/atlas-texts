From the upper lip of the place, you can see several valleys at once, though none has a natural floor. The slopes are courses of pale brick, cut stone, and hand-smoothed mortar, rising in tight steps toward the ridges. Each step carries a basin; each basin has a narrow overflow lip leading to the next. Between them stand walls, low partitions, capped shafts, and stacks of unused paving stones. There is scarcely a space wide enough to stand in without touching something made.

The ridge is high enough that clouds sometimes pass beneath it. On clear days the light is almost severe. It strikes thousands of shallow pools and returns from them in broken pieces, so that a distant slope looks at first like a bank of snow. Up close, the water is grey and perfectly transparent. You can count the thumb marks in the clay beneath it.

Rain is frequent here, but there are no streams. Every drop meets a roof, a gutter, a stair tread, or the sloping back of a wall. Water collects in cups worked into thresholds and runs through channels no wider than a finger. It darkens the mortar and beads on the cool faces of the stones. After rain, the place continues to drip for days, slowly emptying one level into another. Nothing grows in the joints. There are no insects stranded in the basins, no birds at the rims. The water carries only grit from the masonry.

It is hard to judge how recently this great work was finished. Its edges are sharp. Many bricks still bear the ridges of the molds that formed them, and the carved spillways have not yet been rounded by their own flow. Yet the whole region feels settled into a patient task. A basin fills; its surface reaches a notch; a thin sheet pours over. Far below, another basin answers it an hour later. No wheel turns and no hidden engine assists the descent. The builders needed only levels, plumb lines, hands, and an exact knowledge of how water hesitates.

The sound is smaller than the view. A drop strikes a covered cistern with a hollow tick. Somewhere behind a wall, another falls too softly to locate. Wind passes over the high rims without much to catch it. Across the bright, crowded slopes, change proceeds by the width of a wet line climbing a stone.
