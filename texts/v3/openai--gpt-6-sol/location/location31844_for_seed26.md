The floor falls away in shallow steps, each one cast with a lip sharp enough to catch a boot. From the upper service walk, the basin looks almost like a valley: a long grey hollow with pools in its low places, narrow channels branching across it, and scattered dark tufts that might, at a distance, be reeds. It takes a while to see the seams. Every slope is a poured panel. Every channel has the same deliberate width.

There is no bare earth here. The grit underfoot is ground furnace slag, spread thinly over concrete that is still pale where the wheels have not crossed it. Bolts stand proud of new retaining walls. At the far end, perhaps a kilometre away, a line of cranes rests beneath the ceiling with its hooks raised clear of the basin. The place is not finished, though water has already begun to occupy it.

It drips from the roof in separate, persistent places. Most of it falls too far to see; its arrival is heard as a scattered ticking against sheet metal and as soft impacts in the pools. Condensation hangs on the undersides of ducts and gathers along cable trays before it drops. Near the floor the air is colder. Water beads on handrails, and thin ice forms on the sheltered sides of joints, while the open channels remain black and moving.

The reeds are real, after all, though few of them have taken hold. They grow in pockets of wet slag where a channel has overflowed, their stems almost colourless under the low amber lamps. A little moss has appeared in the stamped numbers on one wall. Nothing covers a broad surface yet. Across most of the basin there is room to see each plant on its own.

The pumps begin without warning. A deep blow passes through the floor, followed by a hard, continuous roar. Across the basin the channel surfaces draw tight and then race toward the intake towers. Water slides off the shallow steps, exposing clean rectangular stains; small pools tremble but do not quite empty. When the pumps ease, water seeps back out of joints and drips from above, and the ticking becomes audible again. This happens often enough that the slag has sorted itself into little ridges along the wet edges.

Something alive moves only after the engines stop. Small pale creatures come out from under the grates and cross the damp concrete in quick, sideways runs. One disappears into a drain before its shape is clear. High overhead, a single bird calls from among the cables. The sound is thin and startling in a chamber large enough to send it back several times.

Past the last visible lamps, the floor and the roof continue together into darkness. From here it is possible to mistake the basin for an old place, especially between pump cycles, when water is finding its own paths. Then a warning light turns on beside an intake, and the whole wet valley shudders as the machines start again.
