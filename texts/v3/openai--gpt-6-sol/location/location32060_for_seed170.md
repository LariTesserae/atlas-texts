From the rim, the plateau looks almost unoccupied. It runs for miles in broad, worn shelves of red stone and pale salt, each shelf ending in a slope of loose black grains. Heat lifts from it without wavering the view. There is no watercourse to account for the rounded gullies, no root to hold their edges. The gullies were worn by wind so long ago, and so steadily, that even their rubble has acquired smooth faces.

Nothing has been built here. Yet the plateau has the sound of a factory district heard from the next valley over: a deep reciprocating thud, a thinner metallic clatter, then a hiss that travels along the cliffs. The sounds overlap without quite finding a common rhythm.

The animals make them. In a trough far below, what first appears to be a line of fallen columns is a procession of long, plated bodies. Each has a narrow head pressed to the ground. They draw mineral dust through chambers in their flanks and exhaust it in hot, measured bursts. Smaller animals inhabit the gaps between their plates, tapping and scraping at deposits the larger ones cannot reach. Across the plateau, others work alone in pits or under overhangs. There is rarely more than one visible nearby, but their calls and labor carry from every direction. It is difficult to find a place out of hearing of them.

They eat stone, apparently, though eating is too simple a word for what they do. The stone they leave behind is changed: some patches become brittle as fired clay; others ring underfoot. Their slow passages have given the plateau its terraces and its strangely even inclines. An individual may spend a season crossing a slope. The marks of many crossings lie together, so old that wind has polished them into the bedrock.

At noon, the distance begins to misbehave. A pale ridge seen across a mile of empty ground can sometimes be heard being scraped from beneath your feet. Shadows on the western wall belong, for a few minutes, to animals still moving along the eastern one. The beasts do not react. They keep their heads down and continue their work, and eventually the shadows settle where the light says they should be.

By late afternoon the salt is too bright to look at directly. The air smells of hot iron and opened stone. One of the great plated animals shifts its weight, and a tremor travels through the ground at walking pace. Long after it passes, the smaller ones begin to answer: hundreds of faint, precise knocks sounding across the empty shelves, each from a different distance, until darkness makes the plateau seem larger than it did by day.
