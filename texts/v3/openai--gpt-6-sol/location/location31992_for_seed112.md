The ground is alive, though at first it looks like stone.

Across the high plain, low silver plants have grown together into a continuous, hard surface: a rind of overlapping leaves, roots, and old fiber, worn smooth where the wind has worked on it for centuries. It gives slightly underfoot. In places it has buckled into ridges taller than mountains. Elsewhere it stretches level to the horizon, pale and bright beneath a sky with almost nothing in it.

Very little rises from this living floor. The great plants stand far apart, each with miles of open country around it. Their trunks are broad, fluted columns of dry tissue, and their crowns spread into arrays of narrow blades that turn edge-on to the sun. From a distance they seem solitary. From above, their roots and the silver ground-cover would be one unbroken organism—or so nearly one that there is no useful place to draw a boundary.

It is cool here despite the light. The air is thin and exceptionally dry; there are no streams, pools, or clouds promising rain. The plants keep their small stores of moisture deep inside wax-sealed chambers. What they take openly from the world is dust. Wind lifts mineral powder from exposed seams in the plain and drives it through the crowns. Leaf after leaf catches and sorts it. Hard veins flex like springs, knocking grains loose into funnels that descend through the trunks. Within, slow fibrous drums crush what the plants can use. The waste falls from vents at their bases in orderly cones.

There are no factories and no one has made a machine, but the plain has the sound of a working one. In a steady wind, the crowns clatter together over immense distances. Trunks thump as their inner drums turn. The ground-cover tightens and releases in long traveling snaps, loud enough to be felt through the soles of the feet. When a gust crosses the plateau, these sounds gather into a continuous roar. It can be hard to tell whether something nearby has broken or whether a ridge a hundred miles away has begun its daily shedding.

Animals live inside that noise. Small, dark grazers run along the sheltered sides of the ground-ridges, clipping fresh growth before it hardens. Pale-winged creatures ride the violent air around the crowns and pick seeds out of the dust funnels. Burrowers move beneath the silver skin, making brief, rising humps across an otherwise empty view. There are many of them, but the country is so large that a whole moving herd may look like a scatter of filings.

Change here is sudden, though the shape of the place is old. A crown can lose half its blades in an hour and begin growing replacements before they strike the ground. The living floor splits along a seam, folds one lip over the other, and slowly seals. Dust scours the ridges down; growth lifts them again. Nothing holds still, yet the same long slopes and open distances persist, made and remade by the same wind.
