The plateau lies a few hundred metres below the surface, high on the shoulder of a mountain that rises from the abyss. From here the water is bright enough to show colour: blue in the open distances, green against the pale stone. It is also cold. The light suggests an ordinary afternoon until it reaches your hands.

The ground looks newly broken. Long shelves of dark rock end in clean edges, and narrow cracks run between them without the soft fill that collects in older places. There is almost no silt. Beyond each ledge, enormous tracts of bare stone fall away, visible for a while through the water and then lost in blue. Nothing grows on them. Here and there a chalk-coloured film coats a crack, thin enough that the rock shows through.

The noise arrives before anything that might be making it. It is a continuous, deafening pressure in the water: a low grind carried through stone, with a finer metallic whine above it. At great intervals along the plateau stand pumping towers, small against the distances between them. Each is bolted over a shaft. Water enters the grilles, and a powerful current leaves somewhere below the ledge. The towers keep the same pitch day after day. Even far from one, you can feel it through your ribs.

Fish are plentiful, though the open water can look empty for hours. Silver schools hold in the current beside a tower, shifting together whenever one animal turns. Small white crustaceans crowd the grilles and the mineral-coated cracks; larger, slow-bodied hunters wait farther out where the stone drops away. Then there may be kilometres with nothing near you but water, sound, and the faint outline of the plateau below.

The current has worn bright spots around the bolts but has not yet rounded the natural edges of the rock. A loose plate on one tower trembles without coming free. The fish leave the grille to feed and return to it. Looking across the bare summit, it is hard to tell which parts have remained this way for years and which were exposed only recently.
