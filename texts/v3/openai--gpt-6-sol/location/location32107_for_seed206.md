At the edge of a warm floodplain, there is a space beneath the reed mat no wider than an outstretched arm. You find it by following the noise. From above, the reeds look still; below, their roots make a low ceiling over an open pocket of air. A little water lies in its shallowest groove. The rest of the floor is firm, dark root felt, worn smooth where water has passed across it for years.

Nearly everything here is plant, though surprisingly little crowds the open space. Pale roots descend at intervals like separate pillars. Thin green stems admit ordinary daylight through gaps overhead. Condensation gathers on their undersides and drops into the groove, where it travels a short distance before vanishing into the felt. There is no bare earth in sight, and nothing placed or built.

The noise is immense at this scale. Hollow reed bases draw water upward with a succession of clicks and soft knocks; swollen root chambers tighten and release with a sound like pumps working behind a wall. Fine plant fibers carry each vibration across the ceiling. Through it all comes the sharper labor of animals: the rasp of feeding larvae, beetles ticking against stems, small frogs calling from the water just beyond the pocket. A snail crossing the floor makes no sound you can distinguish, but leaves a bright track among the darker fibers.

Nothing seems to be starting or finishing. The drops fall in the same places. The groove keeps its shape. Water rises through the reeds, and more arrives through the wet ground to replace it. For all its engine-room clatter, the little hollow remains open and much as it has long been: warm, damp, and spacious enough for its creatures to pass one another without meeting.
