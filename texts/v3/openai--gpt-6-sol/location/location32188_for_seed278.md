From the rim, the plateau looks almost level. It takes hours of walking to see that the ground is a succession of shallow basins, each broad enough to have its own weather. Their edges have been worn down to low stone lips. Ancient ice and wind have taken everything loose; what remains is hard, dark rock polished at the crests and scored with channels that lead to occasional pockets of water. The channels look freshly cut in the dim light, though they have not changed course in ages.

It is very cold. Moisture gathers where it can: a silver skin on the shaded side of a stone, a thread of brine under a ledge, rain that crosses one basin and never reaches the next. There is little to drink and less to grow on. Low black lichen occupies some cracks; a few stiff plants stand beside the seeps, far apart from one another. Between them lie miles of bare mineral ground.

At first the animals seem equally scarce. One large shape moves along a far ridge. Another stands motionless below it. Then the eye learns the distances. Across every basin are more of them: solitary grazers, small groups, long, widely spaced lines making their way toward water. There may be thousands in view, with empty land between every pair. On this plateau, an overwhelming number of living things can still leave room for a person to feel alone.

The largest animals feed where mineral-rich moisture reaches the surface. Broad pads keep their weight off the fragile crust. Beneath their jaws, plates work slowly against one another, grinding stone and lichen together. Their bodies separate salts, store pressure, and send faint electrical pulses down wet tracks under the skin. Seen close, one is a whole industry of pumps and mills, patient and self-repairing. There are no buildings here, no discarded tools, no sign that anyone has ever tried to harness them.

For all that movement, the place is nearly silent. The animals’ feet settle softly. Thin air gives little reach to their breathing. Now and then a drop falls into a pool, or a ridge answers a step with a tremor felt through the soles rather than heard. The herds take days to cross a basin, and the water takes seasons to move from one hollow to another. Only the light changes quickly: cloud shadow passes over the old stone, and for a moment the scattered animals emerge from the gray as if the ground itself had begun, very slowly, to move.
