The basin is small enough to see across, though there is no clear way through it. Low ridges of worn concrete divide the ground into dozens of shallow compartments. Some hold rainwater; others hold a skin of gray silt that trembles when the water moves beneath it. Rusted pipes lie close together in the banks, their exposed curves polished smooth by years of overflow. Whatever this place once collected or drained, its channels have had a long time to find their own routes.

The air is cool and damp. Water beads on bolts, gathers under the lips of old housings, and runs down the sides of a squat brick pump shed. The shed has no door. Inside, a flywheel stands still among nettle stems and loose grit. Outside, the basin is busy without it. A thin sheet of water suddenly crosses a dry-looking patch; a few yards away, a pool sinks and leaves rings of foam hanging on the concrete. Silt slides into a crack, blocks it for a minute, then gives way. Nothing stays quite where it was, although every bank and rounded corner looks settled and old.

There is little growing here. Grass takes hold on the highest seams, and pale reeds stand in two pockets where mud has accumulated deep enough to keep them. Most surfaces belong to water, stone, iron, or the fine dark sediment caught between them. A small bird comes to drink from a raised channel, thinks better of it when the water rises, and flies off.

The basin makes less sound than its movement suggests. There is an occasional drip inside a pipe, the faint scrape of grit being drawn over grit, a soft slap when one compartment spills into another. Far below, perhaps, something is still opening and closing. Or the old works have simply left the ground with too many passages to behave like ordinary ground. In daylight you can watch a pool empty, cross to its edge, and find it filling again from a place you cannot see.
