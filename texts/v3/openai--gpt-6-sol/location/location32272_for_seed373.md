The plateau ends at a wall of open air. From its rim you can see the whole place: a shallow bowl of pale stone, scarcely wider than a village, held high above a bank of gray cloud. Nothing stands close to anything else. A few narrow towers rise from the rock, and between them grow a dozen trees with crowns so wide and thin they scarcely interrupt the view.

There is no stream, no damp soil, no dew in the cold mornings. The rock is dry all the way through, polished in long, shallow channels by centuries of windblown grit. The trees root in those channels. Their trunks are braided fibers the color of old silver; their leaves are broad, nearly transparent vanes that take what they need from sunlight and from the fine mineral dust passing through the air. Seen from below, each crown is lush with overlapping green, though a person could look through it to the sky.

The wind never holds one direction. It drops suddenly into the bowl, races around its rim, and rises again in twisting columns. The trees fold, open, and fold once more. Their leaves snap like thousands of small sails. Farther out, the towers answer with a lower sound—a continuous, deafening note that can be felt through the soles of your feet. Talking is possible only in the brief intervals when the note shifts pitch.

The towers are old, but none is quite ruined. Stone has worn away around their bases, exposing smooth dark shafts without seams or fastenings. At intervals a shaft turns a fraction of a degree. The air above it bends, and a passing gust breaks into several streams. No one here remembers whether the towers were made to calm the wind or to gather it. They have done both for so long that the bowl has taken its present shape around them.

Life crowds the empty space between the few things rooted here. Small six-winged animals pour through the channels of air, vanish over the rim, then return in dense, glittering bands. They graze the leaf surfaces without landing. Larger creatures, light-boned and dark against the cloud, pursue them with abrupt turns that ought to tear their wings. In the cracks underfoot, pale colonies retreat from every footfall and emerge again as soon as the vibration passes. Their calls, wingbeats, and dry rattling bodies are usually lost beneath the towers and the leaves.

A narrow path crosses the bowl. It has no paving, only a line of low markers half worn into the stone. At its far end stands a shelter with three walls and no roof, its inside crowded with scratches made by people waiting out a change in the air. From there, the place can look almost still: green crowns spread wide, animals circling, the weathered rim enclosing them all. Then a tower turns. The crowns draw tight, the flocks scatter, and grit sweeps across the path hard enough to erase fresh tracks.
