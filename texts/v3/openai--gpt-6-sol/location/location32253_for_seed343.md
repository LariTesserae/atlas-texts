At this height, the land has almost finished becoming level. The plateau runs to a pale horizon without a peak to interrupt it; its shallow hollows and rounded shoulders are what remain of mountains worn down over an age too long to picture. Far off, the ground falls away into broad, ancient valleys. From here their sides look smooth, though each one takes days to cross.

Almost none of that old stone is bare. A close, continuous cover of plants lies over it: tough grasses, low woody mats, fleshy leaves pressed flat against the ground. Roots hold a thin skin of soil in places where rain would otherwise take it. The country feels open because so little rises above knee height. You can see a long way across it, over an apparently empty green surface, before noticing how many things are moving there.

The sun is warm, but the air is thin, and a hand pushed into the vegetation finds coolness left by the night. Water arrives briefly—in passing rain, in mist driven up from the valleys, in beads that gather beneath leaves before dawn. It lingers in root hollows and shallow pans, then disappears. Plants grow crowded around each damp place; between them they grow slowly, holding their ground rather than spreading.

The noise begins before sunrise. Insects hidden in the mat make a hard, continuous rasp, joined by wings beating in such numbers that their swarms blur the distance. Small birds skim just above the grass, turning together after them. Burrowing animals whistle from below; others part the leaves in quick, visible lines. At the water pans, everything converges, and the calls become loud enough to drown speech. A few steps away the plateau still looks deserted.

Here and there, someone has raised a low stone windbreak or propped a shelter on poles cut in the valleys below. Paths appear as narrow seams through the plants and are soon difficult to follow. There is little else to build with, and little reason to build high: every afternoon the wind crosses the open ground without meeting a wall. It bends the grass, shakes rain from the leaves, and carries the living roar over the edge of the plateau.
