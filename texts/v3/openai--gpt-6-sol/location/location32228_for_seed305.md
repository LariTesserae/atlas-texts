From a distance the plateau looks almost level: a pale, sunstruck plain suspended above the clouds. Up close, it has no level ground at all. New stone stands in narrow blades and broken steps, as if the mountains rose too recently for their edges to soften. The air is so cold that each breath catches in the throat. Sunlight pours over everything without warmth enough to loosen the ice in the shaded cracks.

There is very little open stone. Low, thick-leaved plants fill the fissures and spread over the ridges in overlapping mats, dark green on top and woolly white beneath. Their roots keep a little heat in the rock. Wherever two mats meet, moisture collects: a line of droplets, a pocket of black water, sometimes a whole sheltered hollow slick with condensation. Beyond those hollows the plateau is dry enough to draw the moisture from your lips. Rain arrives rarely, as a fine, bright curtain that can be seen crossing the upland for hours before it reaches you.

The apparent vegetation moves. Some of the dark patches are herds of six-legged grazers standing shoulder to shoulder, their backs furred with the same pale moss that grows on the stone. Among them pass smaller animals in such numbers that they seem at first like dust blown across the plants. The ridges have their own traffic: long lines of shell-backed creatures taking turns at the wet cracks, birds landing almost on one another’s feet, translucent things opening in the sun and folding shut when a shadow passes. There is barely room for an animal to die without another settling beside it.

The sound carries farther than anything else. A deep, even vibration comes through the soles of the feet; above it run clicks, rattles, and a high electrical whine. In the narrowest gorges, plants hold their leaves against one another, and the wind makes them chatter like banks of small machines. Animals feed on the leaves, polish their surfaces as they pass, and expose them to the light again. Under the mats, roots and burrowing bodies squeeze water slowly from one crevice to the next. The plateau has the noise and throughput of an immense works, but nothing here has been assembled, and no part of it can be stopped without another living thing pressing into the gap.

For all that noise, little changes in a day. A herd advances the width of its own body. Water gathers on a leaf, falls, and begins gathering again. Far below, clouds form and disappear against the mountain’s sheer new face. Up here the sun remains fiercely bright, and the crowded plain keeps its cold, patient motion.
