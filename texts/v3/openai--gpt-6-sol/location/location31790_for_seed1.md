At a distance the plain looks smooth, a low country of pewter and black water extending to a dim, unbroken horizon. Closer, there is almost no bare ground. The surface is crowded with shallow basins, each fitted so tightly against its neighbors that the rims meet in long seams. They are stone, worn down to the thickness of lips. Water trembles in them without wind.

The rain here falls slowly and in great quantity. It gathers on the basins, runs over their edges, and sinks through the seams. Far below, something turns. The sound rises through the plain in overlapping pitches: a deep revolving thud, a finer metallic chatter, then the hiss of pressure escaping through thousands of narrow cracks. No one set a machine here. The strata have folded and ground against one another for so long that they have acquired the motions of one. Where a seam opens, you can see slick disks of shale revolving under the water, their edges worn rounder than any tool could cut them.

It is bitterly cold. Spray freezes on the basin rims, thaws when a pulse of warmer water comes up from below, and freezes again. Nothing green takes hold. The only colors are the gray of old stone, the bluish skins of new ice, and a dull red that appears at depth when the disks turn fast enough. Mist hangs close to the ground, so the far reaches of the plain seem to float a little above the near ones.

There are animals everywhere. Pale, jointed things cling inside the basins in layers, feeding on what the water carries up. Their shells knock together when the stone begins to shake. Long-bodied hunters move through the seams; now and then one breaks the surface to seize a cluster of the pale animals, and the whole basin rattles with their attempt to hold on. Dark, heavy creatures drift through the flooded channels between larger fields of stone. They have no visible eyes. They pause where the noise is loudest and open their mouths toward it.

At intervals the great turning underneath falters. The plain goes quiet enough for every drip to be heard. This is when the animals retreat beneath the rims, though no predator appears. A moment later the sound resumes—but not always in the same order. A splash may be heard before the water rises. Ice sometimes cracks across a basin that has not yet frozen. From a low ridge you can watch a pulse travel outward through the stone and see it arrive first at the distant basins, then at the ones beside your feet.

The place is very old. All its high ground has been reduced to those low rims; all its loose material has been taken into the water or pressed into the seams. Yet it is never still. Across miles of crowded basins, the rain keeps falling, the animals keep shifting from one narrow shelter to another, and the buried stone keeps turning, as if the plain has nearly finished wearing itself away and cannot remember how to stop.
