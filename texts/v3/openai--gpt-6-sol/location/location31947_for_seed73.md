The mountain has no summit that can be seen from the valley. Its upper walls rise through successive bands of cloud, and each time the cloud thins, another wall stands behind it, sunlit and impossibly far above. Down here, well above the height where trees ought to fail, the air is hot. It tastes of wet stone and flowers. Breathing takes a little attention.

Water comes out of the cliffs in thousands of narrow places. It does not gush. It gathers on the faces of dark, weathered rock, beads along old fractures, and drops into shallow runs crowded with roots. The runs cross broad shelves of soil, vanish under mats of fern, and appear again at the shelf’s edge. From across the valley the falls look like pale stitching. Close by, a single drop can hang long enough to reflect a passing bird before it lets go.

Trees grow wherever the mountain has held still long enough for their roots to bind its loose skin. Their trunks lean outward over the open air, carrying thick crowns of small, shining leaves. Between them stand reeds taller than a person, orchids with waxy throats, and low shrubs that give off a peppery smell when brushed. Nothing quite closes the view. Through gaps in the growth, one can see several neighboring shelves, each with its own little drainage and its own wavering line of canopy, until heat and distance turn them blue.

The valley is loud without seeming hurried. Water ticks and patters everywhere. Insects sustain a hard, even note through the hottest hours; frogs answer from pockets of mud that are invisible until they speak. Far below, a fall strikes a hollow in the mountain with a sound like someone steadily turning a huge wheel. The animals have made use of every ledge. Bright lizards flatten themselves against the warm rock, while long-winged creatures circle the updrafts and pluck insects out of the air. At dusk, pale grazing animals come carefully out of the trees to lick mineral salts from the weeping cliffs.

There are a few signs of building. A stair cut into one shelf climbs straight into a grove and stops at the roots of a mature tree. Across the valley, a squat shelter of fitted stone holds the sun on its roof long after the surrounding slopes have gone dim. On some exposed rock faces are rows of small, clear studs, too regular to be fossils. Most are buried in lichen. When mist passes over them, their depths show brief points of blue.

The oldest trees stand around patches where the ground remains moist even after weeks without rain. Beneath those patches lie thin sheets of a material that looks like mica until a broken edge is touched: it yields, then closes over the mark. The sheets draw water from the air and release it slowly into the soil. There are similar seams deeper in the cliff, beyond any visible reach. No one working with stone now could say where they end.

They have changed the mountain gently. A spring may move a finger’s width in a lifetime. A root following it may take another lifetime to shift a stone. The shelves are old enough to have found a balance between those movements, the water they receive, and the weight of everything growing on them.

Near evening, the cliffs give back the day’s heat. Mist forms in the open spaces between shelves and rises past the trees. For a while the far side of the valley disappears, though its birds can still be heard calling. Then the air clears around one dark wall, and the wall begins to shine with fresh water.
