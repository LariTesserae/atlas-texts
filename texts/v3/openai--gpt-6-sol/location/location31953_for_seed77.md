The plain is made of clay laid down in broad, overlapping courses. Each course has a slightly different colour: pale buff, fired orange, a brown that turns almost black when wet. At the nearest edge you can see the marks of hands in it, palms pressed flat and fingers drawn along the seams. Farther out, the courses become long, level bands, and then simply distance. There is no outcrop or patch of native ground. Even the low rises have been built up layer by layer.

Heat settles over the place early. The clay holds it, so the air remains warm after the sun has begun to drop. For most of the day the plain is quiet enough to hear a seam contracting: a small tick, followed much later by another. It is not perfectly flat. Shallow rims divide it into basins many miles across, too low to notice until a distant line of colour reveals a change in height. From one basin, the next looks like a slightly higher sky.

Rain comes unevenly. A dark cloud may travel for hours above the plain, shedding a narrow curtain of water that never reaches you. Where it does fall, the clay darkens at once. Water gathers in thumb-deep hollows and runs along the joints, carrying fine sediment into places that were clean that morning. By noon the pools are often gone. Their edges remain as pale rings; a few new channels remain too, barely more than scratches. The surface is young enough that these are among its first cuts. In places a drying layer has begun to curl away from the one beneath it.

A few tough plants occupy the seams. Their stems are far apart, and each casts a distinct, narrow shadow. After rain they stand greener for a day or two, drawing moisture from the joints while the wide faces of clay dry hard again. There are no animals here, and no tracks except the impressions left when the ground was laid. Across that enormous empty work, weather keeps making small alterations: a seam opens, dust fills it, rain washes the dust farther down. The plain looks finished from a distance. Up close, it is still settling into its shape.
