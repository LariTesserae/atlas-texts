The town stands on a high shelf of dark stone, above a valley too broad to see all at once. Across the valley are other shelves, each with a few pale roofs and a line of trees. Between them, the mountain shows its new edges: sheer breaks, clean angles, narrow seams that have not yet filled with soil. From a distance the settlements look provisional. Up close, their walls fit the rock so closely that it is hard to tell where the shelf ends and the town begins.

There are streets, but they leave generous space around everything. Houses of stacked stone stand apart among gardens of low, glossy leaves. A stair climbs the cliff in long, shallow turns; another descends to a market square large enough for far more stalls than it holds. Timber bridges cross cracks no wider than a cart. The parapets are set with clay basins to catch the water that gathers on them each morning. No pipes carry it onward. People lift it out with cups, a little at a time.

At this height the air is cool even in full daylight. Moisture comes without much weather. It beads on the shaded faces of stones and in the deep folds of broad leaves; a fine rain sometimes occupies one end of the valley while the other remains clear. The ground beneath the trees is springy with moss, but step beyond their shade and the rock is nearly dry. Roots enter its fresh fractures wherever they can, making small, dense gardens in an otherwise exposed place.

Birds are everywhere, though the town is quiet. They perch along roof ridges, hang briefly above the cliff on spread wings, and vanish into the trees below the market. Small hoofed animals browse on the outer terraces. Pale lizards warm themselves on stones near the houses. Most can be heard only when one is close: a claw on slate, a leaf pulled from its stem, the soft knock of a bird landing on a hollow roof tile. Human sounds carry farther. Someone dressing a stone with a hand hammer can be heard across the square.

There is no old quarter. The cut faces of the steps are as sharp as those of the cliff, and the oldest walls have no more lichen than the newest. Yet nobody speaks of the town as unfinished. A mason works at a loose stair tread; when it fits, the stair looks exactly as it did before. Gardens are tended, basins emptied, bridges checked. Nothing here seems to spread, wear down, or fall away. From the far rim of the valley, the town’s daily life would scarcely mark the broad still shape of it: dark shelves, green pockets, pale roofs, and birds holding their places in the clear air.
