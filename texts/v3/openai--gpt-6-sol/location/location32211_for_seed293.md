The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few streets. From the rim it looks almost empty: six tall trunks standing far apart, a sheet of dull green between them, and a few narrow roofs hovering just above the leaves. Only on the way down does the street become visible. It is a parting in the vegetation, kept open by the roots on either side. After rain, warm water runs along its center, carrying petals, shed insect wings, and the tiny silver fish that have somehow found their way into every channel.

There is little stone left here. Centuries of flooding have worn the basin floor nearly level, and what remains of the old foundations lies under a deep, springy mat of roots. Newer buildings are grown into that mat rather than set upon it. Their walls are broad leaves held edge-on, their rooms airy spaces between stems, their thresholds raised just enough to clear the water. Each house has space around it. Across the open ground, neighbors can see a door fold shut or a roof turn toward the light.

The oldest thing in the town may be the street itself. Its course has survived the loss of several districts because the roots remember it. They also remember weight. A loaded cart can pass over ground that would sink under an uninvited foot; a bridge of woven stems will hold until its crossing is complete, then unwind and let the stream through. No one here can quite explain how a root knows that a journey is over. The town’s instruments can describe the movement in extraordinary detail, but they cannot produce the same judgment.

It is not a quiet place. Rainwater knocks through hollow stalks. Frogs call from the house gutters, and flocks of long-legged birds argue over the mud exposed when a channel shifts. Beneath them all runs a busy, irregular creaking: branches drawn into bridges, balconies opening for the afternoon, one street narrowing while another clears. The basin may have spent ages being worn flat, but its living surface changes by the hour. Residents leave room in their plans for this. They set out for a shop and arrive by a different path, or wait while a roof lowers its leaves to empty a sudden weight of water.

Toward evening the water near the surface gives back its heat. Mist gathers among the widely spaced trunks, not thick enough to hide the opposite side of town. Through it, the houses show as separate warm shapes, with birds settling in their walls and small animals moving along the paths before those paths close for the night.
