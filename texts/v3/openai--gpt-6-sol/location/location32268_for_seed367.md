The passage is only wide enough for one person to turn sideways. It runs between two rows of houses cut into the crest of a high, worn ridge, then ends in a pocket courtyard barely larger than a sleeping mat. From the open country the village is hard to see. Its roofs lie almost level with the ground, and its walls are made from the same pale stone as the ridge. Wind has rounded the ridge for so long that there are few sharp edges left except those the inhabitants have made with their tools.

The courtyard is dim even in the warm part of the day. Thick-leaved vines cross it overhead on cords of twisted fibre; below them, thorn shrubs and fleshy, jointed plants crowd clay pots, wall cracks, and the narrow shelf above a doorway. Nothing here looks wet. The soil is powder, the pots are sealed around their stems with pebbles, and the few leaves that fall become brittle before they reach the ground. Still, green presses into every usable gap. Roots have spent years finding their way through the old stone.

There is little room for people because so much else lives here. Small birds nest under the roof slabs and erupt into the passage whenever someone lifts a grain basket. Lizards wait flat against the warm wall. Beetles work beneath the pots; ants maintain a dark, moving road along the mortar. Bees crowd the pale flowers overhead, and a hunting bird’s shadow sends them all into a sudden change of pitch and direction. The noise fills the little court: wings, calls, the scrape of claws on stone, a hand mill turning inside one of the houses.

By evening the court has changed hands several times. People spread seeds to dry, gather them again before the birds can take too many, mend a roof edge with packed earth, and brush ant traffic away from the threshold. The stone beneath all this activity is immensely old and almost smooth. Each day leaves some fresh mark upon it, though none seems likely to last as long as the shallow hollow in the centre, worn there by feet.
