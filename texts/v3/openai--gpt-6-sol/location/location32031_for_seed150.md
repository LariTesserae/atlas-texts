The town occupies a notch just below the ridgeline. From above, it looks like a dark patch of forest caught in a crack in the mountain; inside, it has streets. Narrow passages run between walls of interlaced stems, turn beneath low arches, and end in little courts where the sky shows through. A few of the walls rise three or four storeys, with ledges and hollows at regular heights. Nothing here has been cut to shape. The stems have grown toward one another and joined, and new growth keeps closing the gaps.

The mountain under them is young. Black stone breaks into hard angles rather than worn slopes, and in the few places where the plants have not covered it, pale fractures still show. Roots fill those fractures so completely that the stone seems to hold up the town and be held together by it. There is scarcely any open ground. You walk on springy mats of roots, over fallen leaves that have not had room to lie flat, past trunks pressed bark to bark. Even the courts are crowded with shoots.

It is cold enough that breath hangs in the passages. Ice films the north-facing walls, while water beads on the others and runs down their furrows. A spring somewhere above feeds the place by seepage: dampness appears at every level, gathers in the cupped bases of leaves, and drips from arch to arch before disappearing through the root floor. There is no stream to follow. After rain, every wall gives off water.

The leaves are small and thick, almost black where they overlap. Their undersides are silver, and a gust briefly turns whole passages pale. Higher up, exposed crowns wear rime; below them the air is dim and wet, with the smell of cold sap and old leaf mould. Fine roots hang across door-like openings. If they are parted, they settle back slowly.

In the quiet you can hear a drop strike a leaf, then another much farther down. Stems creak when the wind loads the crowns. Once in a while a small bird moves through the upper tangle, visible only as a shiver that travels along the branches. There are few other creatures to see. Seeds arrive on the wind and lodge wherever a seam has opened, so the town is always thickening, narrowing its own streets, raising another wall over a passage that was clear the season before.

At the outermost arch the growth ends abruptly. Beyond it, sharp rock falls away into cloud. Behind it, the passages continue under their crowded roofs, with water moving through them and the plants growing almost too slowly to notice.
