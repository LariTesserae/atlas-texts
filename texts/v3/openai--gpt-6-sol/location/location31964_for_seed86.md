The ground opens without much warning. A narrow break in the limestone leads down into a chamber broad enough that its far wall disappears into gray. Other chambers continue beyond it, joined by low passages and long, shallow slopes. This is not a deep place: roots hang through cracks in the ceiling, and a few slits admit the color of the day, though never enough light to show the whole floor at once.

The rock has the clean edges of a recent break. Angular blocks lie where they fell, with little soil between them; pale fracture faces stand out against older, darkened stone. Moisture gathers selectively. A seam in the ceiling beads with water, and beneath it a line of small pools crosses an otherwise dry basin. Elsewhere the floor is cool dust and loose chips. Moss survives beside the brighter openings, with an occasional fern rooted in a pocket of sediment. Between those patches there is mostly stone.

From the entrance, the chamber can seem empty. Then the sound resolves. Small claws rasp over the walls. Crickets call from deep among the fallen blocks. High overhead, bats pack the dark ceiling so closely that its surface appears rough; their thin cries and the rustle of shifting wings join into a continuous, surprisingly loud sound. In the pools, pale insects gather on floating specks. Droppings from the roost sustain more insects on the dry ground, and roots and washed-in leaves feed the life that stays near the cracks. There are far more animals here than plants, and most of them are noticed first by hearing them.

A person could cross these chambers with a flaked stone edge and a burning branch, but there is no worked step, wall, or hearth to suggest anyone has stayed. The land gives little sign of changing now. Drops fall into the same basins; the loose blocks rest at the same angles. Even the movement of the animals has a steadiness to it: a vast, busy occupation of a place that, from one visit to the next, would look much the same.
