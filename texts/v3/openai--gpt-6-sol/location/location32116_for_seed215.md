The city stands on a plateau that has not finished rising. Its streets run for miles across dark, nearly level stone, then stop at survey fences where the ground is still breaking through. Beyond them lie fresh ridges, pale at their crests, and shallow basins that filled with rain before they had acquired names. There are few gullies. Water spreads in thin sheets, finds a crack, and disappears. Hours later it beads on the undersides of overhangs or seeps out of a hillside somewhere far below.

From the western tram line, the plateau looks empty enough to walk across in a day. It is not. What appears to be one distant row of buildings is another district, forty miles away, its towers made small by the breadth of stone between them. The city has grown in islands along a network of covered routes. Between the islands are long reaches of open ground, marked by drainage stakes, animal tracks, and the occasional low shed with its door facing away from the wind.

It is cold even under a clear sky. Rain falls often, though seldom hard: a fine, slanting rain that collects on railings and lashes without seeming to touch the road. At night the water skins over with ice. By noon it has usually thawed again. Moss holds to the warm seams around foundations; elsewhere, vegetation is limited to scattered grass and a few tough shrubs in pockets of old soil.

Animals make better use of the space. Small white birds nest along the upper edges of the transit viaducts and lift all at once when a train passes, briefly drawing its shape in the air. Herds of thick-coated grazers cross the undeveloped tracts, pausing at the grates where water comes up from beneath the stone. Foxes follow them at a distance. In the city’s quiet hours, you can see them moving through the gaps between lit windows.

Most construction here is set on adjustable footings. Each morning, the buildings are measured against fixed points deep below the plateau; over the years, whole blocks have risen a little while their entrances remain level with the streets. New survey marks appear beyond the last occupied district whenever a ridge steadies enough to build on. The marks are easy to mistake for rubbish until you notice their straight lines running toward the horizon.

There is remarkably little noise for a place so large. The enclosed trains make a soft rush and are gone. Rain ticks against the tram shelters. On still days, sound carries from far away: a flock calling over a basin, work beginning on a new foundation, a sheet of rock settling with a single dry crack. Then the distance closes again, and all that remains nearby is dripping water and the movement of birds along the roofs.
