The ledge is no wider than a small field, but it has the exposure of a summit. Beyond its outer lip, the mountain falls away into white distance. Behind it rises a wall of pale, newly broken stone, its edges still keen, its cracks too clean to have gathered much soil. At midday the sun reaches every part of the ledge. The light is fierce enough to make the rock seem warm, though a hand laid against it quickly goes numb.

Most of the ground is bare. Then, wherever grit has lodged in a seam, there are sudden thick patches of green: tight cushions of moss, leaves pressed flat against the stone, little thickets of flowering stems no taller than a thumb. They grow around the mouths of cracks and beneath overhangs where frost lingers. A strip of snow survives at the foot of the wall. On clear mornings it shrinks by a finger’s breadth, and the melt runs silently under the plants before disappearing into a split in the ledge. By evening the wet seams have glazed over again.

There is a dry-stone windbreak near the wall, scarcely high enough to shelter a seated person. Its stones match the fresh rubble around it, except that their upper faces have been rubbed smooth by hands. Beside it lies a flat slab with a shallow hollow worn in one end. Nothing else has been brought here. From the windbreak, the ledge looks nearly empty: a few green islands, a few upright stones, an immense amount of air.

It takes time to notice how many creatures use those islands. Small dark birds arrive from below and pick among the flowers, then vanish over the edge without seeming to fall. Beetles shelter under leaves; pale moths sit with their wings closed against the rock. Now and then a larger bird passes overhead, and everything close to the ground holds still. Even the busiest moments are quiet. Wingbeats disappear in the open air, and the meltwater makes no audible stream.

The place changes fastest at its margins. A night frost can push a chip of stone out of the wall; the next sunny day can free it to skitter across the ledge. Flowers open while their roots remain cold. Clouds cross far below, briefly filling the drop with a level white surface, then break apart and leave the height visible again. The green survives not because this is a gentle place, but because it is ready for each short hour of water and light.
