The chamber ends forty paces from its entrance, but in the dark it is difficult to tell where the walls begin. A narrow inspection lamp glows above a steel door, too weak to reach the far side. Its light catches the nearest things in pieces: a pipe hanger, a wet leaf, the bright edge of a crack in the floor. Beyond them, the air is black and hot.

The floor was poured in one slab. It has split recently, lifting into sharp lips that still show clean aggregate. Warm air rises through the break with a mineral smell. Along one wall stand two old condenser cabinets, separated by a wide stretch of bare concrete. Most of their gauges are dark; one needle holds steady against its stop. A cold supply pipe passes overhead, and at intervals a drop gathers beneath it and falls. There is no puddle directly below. Water finds the fracture and disappears.

Greenery crowds the few places it can use. Broad leaves overlap beneath the lamp, their upper surfaces dusty and their edges heavy with condensation. Farther in, vines have climbed a rusted ladder to reach a seam that sweats after rain aboveground. Their roots hang down in a thick curtain, while the rest of the wall remains bare. Between these growths lies open floor, crossed only by a cable and the pale track where a machine was once dragged away. Nothing grazes the leaves or nests among them. Even close up, the roots are still.

The room is nearly silent. A drop strikes concrete; much later, a relay clicks inside a cabinet. Heat gathers under the low ceiling, then eases when the machinery somewhere beyond the wall starts drawing air. That change takes long enough to notice only by standing still. In the cooler hour, more droplets form, and the plants at the seam darken with water. By the time the pipe dries again, the fresh edges of the floor have made no visible movement, though each week a little more root finds its way into them.
