The ridge is high enough that clouds arrive from below. They climb its dark flanks and spill into a broad, shallow basin, where the air is hot despite its thinness. Rain has just passed. Every leaf holds a bright rim of water, and the stone sweats where no rain can reach it. Beyond the basin, other green ridges rise through drifting cloud; between them, waterfalls show briefly as white threads before vanishing into foliage.

The ground is old, not smooth. Channels worn into the volcanic rock have acquired their own beds of moss, roots, and black silt. Warm springs feed some of them; others carry cold rainwater off the heights. Where the two meet, vapor lifts in wavering sheets. Ferns crowd the banks, and trees grow close together in the wetter hollows, their roots clasping rock that has been exposed for centuries. On the shoulders above them, the vegetation thins to tough, glossy shrubs and grasses bent by winds that rarely reach the basin floor.

Heat comes from underneath as well as overhead. Deep in the ridge, pressure drives water through cracks and sends it back up mineral-laden and nearly boiling. The whole slope works on that cycle: intake in the rain-soaked stone, heat below, steam at the vents, runoff finding its way down again. At one spring, pale deposits have built up in a series of lips, each spilling into the next. It has the steady, prodigious output of a great engine, though there is no machine here and nothing anyone has made.

Most of the time the place is quiet enough to hear separate drops fall from the canopy. Then a vent draws breath with a low rush, or a pocket of water knocks inside the rock. Insects rasp for a minute and stop. Small birds hunt among the wet leaves; frogs wait at the edges of the warm channels, while larger unseen animals leave pressed tracks in the soft ground. By afternoon, fresh cloud may close the distant view entirely. The basin remains hot and green within it, its streams continuing downhill long after they disappear.
