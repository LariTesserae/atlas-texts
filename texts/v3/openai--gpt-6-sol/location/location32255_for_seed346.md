The district occupies a shallow hollow no wider than a few streets. From its edge, it looks crowded but ordinary: roofs pressed close, utility conduits crossing above the lanes, windows set so near opposite windows that a person could almost pass a cup between them. The ground is flat, and the sky is a pale winter gray. There is enough daylight to read by. No lamps are needed until evening.

Inside, the distances are less dependable. A lane that seems to end at the next junction can take twenty minutes to walk. Another lets you reach its far end before your footsteps have finished sounding behind you. The buildings do not move, as far as anyone can see. Residents mark routes by things they can count instead: seven drain covers to the clinic, four cold door handles to the bakery. These counts usually hold for months.

The district is dry underfoot except where water collects in particular places. Rain falls occasionally, fine and brief, and leaves dark seams in the concrete. Condensation beads on handrails that run beneath the streets. At the center of the hollow, a narrow channel carries water only some mornings; on others its bed is packed with a new, soft mineral, blue-gray as wet clay. Crews lift this material out in square slabs and use it to repair walls. It hardens well, though it sometimes acquires a window after being laid.

Most of the streets are built over older streets, with stairs descending from pavement into pavement. Pipes, balconies, cables, and covered walkways fill nearly every gap. Even the open square is occupied by a machine: a ring of tall ceramic vanes around an empty center. It draws almost no power. Once every few years, one vane turns a fraction of a degree, and for a day the addresses on the east side are one number lower. The postal office keeps the old labels in drawers.

Here, weathering is a young process. Corners have begun to round, but rubble is uncommon. Frost opens hairline cracks in fresh masonry, and the cracks fill slowly with grains that appear to have come from the stone itself. Along the channel, a few shallow grooves are forming where runoff returns each spring. Surveyors photograph them after every rain. They want to know which way the water will eventually teach the ground to slope.

There are few plants. Pale grass grows in the joints of a retaining wall; a single row of thin trees stands behind the transit stop, its branches still bare long after winter should have ended. Birds are scarce. Now and then one lands on a roof and calls once. More often the sound people hear is a pipe ticking as it cools, or a shoe on a stair above them where no stair is visible.

People speak quietly here, not from fear but because voices carry badly. A shout may be heard in the next room and nowhere in the hall between. The district has maps, schedules, repair crews, a school. Children learn the usual rules first: stop at crossings, dress for the cold, keep clear of the channel when rain is due. Later they learn the local ones. If a door opens onto a room you have not entered, close it gently. If you find fresh stone in a wall you know well, leave it until the workers arrive. And when taking someone home, walk with them all the way to their door; the shortest street is not always short for both of you.
