At this height there are no peaks left. The mountains have been worn down to a broad pavement of pale stone, higher than any cloud that reaches it. Far off, the pavement breaks into shallow shelves and rises again, so that the horizon resembles a succession of empty floors. Nothing here has the sharpness of a young cliff. Every edge is rounded, every exposed face rubbed smooth by ages of grit. Yet the ground is crowded: pebbles packed into seams, old shells turned to stone, dark little mounds pressed close together as far as one can see.

Most of the mounds are animals.

They stand knee to shoulder across the plateau: low, six-legged grazers with hides the color of dust; narrow birds resting upright on their folded wings; creatures like tortoises without shells, their long backs whitened by mineral crust. There are thousands in the nearest basin alone. More occupy the shelves beyond it. They do not flee an approaching footstep. One may turn its eye. A row of heads may be facing a different direction when you look back. Otherwise their stillness is so complete that the ancient stones appear restless beside them.

The air is cool and dry. It smells faintly of chalk and warm fur where the animals gather. Sound carries poorly. A loose pebble clicks against another, and the click ends there; even a call made into the open seems to stop a few paces from the mouth. Overhead there is ordinary daylight, neither dim nor especially fierce, though shadows sometimes disagree about where the sun is. Along one shelf every animal casts its shadow west. Along the next, shadows lie beneath their bodies like dark stains. A person crossing between them feels no change in light.

Almost nothing green grows here. In sheltered cracks are tufts of stiff black grass, each blade curled inward to spare what little moisture it holds. The grazers put their mouths to it, but the tufts do not appear cropped. Their nutrition may come from the fine mineral dust; their wide, soft lips continually sift the stone. No one has stayed long enough to watch one finish a mouthful. There is no rain to wait for, and no streambed down which it might run.

The few human marks are small: a ring of fitted stones on a distant rise, a cairn with a bone laid carefully on top, shallow cuts in the pavement made by a patient hand-tool. The cuts are used as routes. Follow one, and it will take you around a herd rather than through it. From above, though, some of those routes join on the wrong side of a shelf. Two cuts can cross without meeting; a cairn visible straight ahead can remain the same distance away for an hour. Travelers learn to keep a nearby animal in sight. Its position is more dependable than the land under it.

At dusk, if there is a dusk, a small sound passes through the basin: the dry contact of many teeth against stone. It never swells into a din. The animals are too numerous for the quiet to be natural, but the quiet holds. The plateau has held its shape for longer than anyone can reckon, and its herds seem to inhabit that duration as firmly as the rock. Somewhere among them, very slowly, a young one is growing.
