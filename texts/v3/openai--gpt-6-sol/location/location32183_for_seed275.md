The city lies beneath a roof of unbroken stone. From the floor, the roof looks pale and distant, its seams picked out by long banks of lamps. Their light is steady enough to feel like daylight, though there is no morning here. The air is cool, dry, and faintly bitter.

Nearly everything underfoot is alive. A tough, close-laid skin of roots covers the basin, rising here and there into broad streets, low retaining walls, and the foundations of buildings. Yet the place feels open. Blocks of tall, hollow stems stand far apart, with wide empty squares between them; beyond the last workshops, the rooted plain runs into the lit distance. New streets are still swelling out of it. Their edges are soft and blunt, not yet worn by wheels or feet.

There are no wells, drains, or damp places. The plants feed from salts in the rock and a thin mineral vapor drawn through their leaves. Along the industrial avenues, engines comb that vapor from the air and press it into growing beds. Belts turn slowly behind lattices of living wood. A mill cuts panels from stems bred to shed them, and carts carry the panels away before the cuts have fully closed. Nothing here has had much time to decay: even the oldest walls look newly exposed, their pale fibers only beginning to darken.

Small animals are everywhere once the eye adjusts. They rest in the joints of the lamp towers, cross the squares in quick, widely spaced dashes, and disappear into vents opened for them by the plants. Most make little noise. The ground takes the sound of footsteps, and the machinery works at a low, even hum. Occasionally a row of leaves turns all at once toward a fresh lamp, or a building gives a brief wooden knock as it grows against its braces. In the quiet that follows, the city seems less built than held in position while it becomes something else.
