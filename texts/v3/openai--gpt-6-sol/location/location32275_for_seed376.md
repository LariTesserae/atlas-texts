From the ridge, the valley appears to be covered in one continuous dark-green fabric. Up close, there is no ground beneath it. Roots cross roots; old trunks lie inside younger ones; mats of fine stems stretch over hollows deep enough to swallow the light. What passes for a path is a place where the growth has parted for the moment.

The valley sits high enough that breath shows in the afternoon. Frost survives in the shade of the largest leaves, which are thick and almost black on top, silver with insulating hairs underneath. Water is scarce in any one place but present everywhere in small amounts: beads caught in leaf cups, a slow seep along a root, rainwater stored behind a seam of bark. After a shower, the whole slope shines. By morning, most of that moisture has been drawn inward, and the surfaces are matte again.

Across the valley, broad columns of living wood rise at regular intervals. Their spacing suggests a plantation until you see the gaps, the fallen columns, the new ones forcing themselves up crookedly among them. No one planted them. The columns belong to a sprawling system that moves water and sugar through itself under pressure. At certain hours, pale sap runs through translucent veins as thick as a wrist. Chambers open and close; tough belts of vine travel steadily around living pulleys, carrying seed husks uphill. The work has the scale of industry, though there are no buildings and nothing here has been assembled by hand.

Almost nothing can be heard. The belts move without a squeak. A column several valleys away can split from top to bottom, its crown sliding into the canopy, and from this ridge the only sound is a soft brushing, like someone drawing a sleeve over paper. Closer by, a bead of water drops into a leaf cup. It is startlingly loud.

The motion is easier to see than to hear. One section of canopy lowers while another rises. Openings appear, admitting ordinary daylight to layers that have lived for years in green shade; within minutes, pale shoots turn toward it. Elsewhere, a mass of stems buckles and settles into an old depression. The valley’s shape is ancient—its rounded shoulders, its drainage lines, the deep sag at its center—yet its surface will not keep the same shape through a day.

There are few animals. A small white creature sometimes shows itself where two root systems pull apart, then vanishes before the gap closes. Most seeds go where the belts and the weather take them. Standing still, you begin to notice how much of the valley is occupied, and how carefully it uses every brief patch of light, every drop of water, every space left open by its own slow collapse.
