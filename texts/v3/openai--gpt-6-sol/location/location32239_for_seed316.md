The land is so flat that the weather has nowhere to go. Rain crosses it in broad, slow fronts, visible for hours before it arrives, and then remains in the air as warm mist. Underfoot lies an old pavement of stone worn nearly level with the sea. It is not smooth. Every inch is crowded with shallow cups, hairline channels, and pores that hold water; each cup has a rim softened by an age of overflow. There is scarcely a patch of bare, dry ground, though much of the plain stands above the water.

From a rise no higher than a person, the pavement reaches every horizon. Its colours change with the fronts passing over it: dull brown in the distance, black where rain is falling, briefly silver where some opening in the cloud admits light. Sparse clumps of reed grow along the larger seams. They look lost among so many places that might have held roots. There is no sheltering canopy, and nothing to soften the sound.

Water runs through the stone even after the rain stops. It ticks in the small cups and roars in the seams beneath them. Farther out, whole tracts begin to hum as pressure rises below the surface. The notes travel for miles, gathering other notes, until the plain gives off a continuous noise loud enough to be felt in the teeth. At times a seam opens with a report like a snapped cliff, but nothing rises or falls. Water wells up, carrying grit that has been ground fine beyond any useful measure, and settles again.

The pavement has worn down as far as it can. Its upheavals now are brief: a cup empties into one farther away; a dark, wet patch races against the wind; rain strikes stone in a place where the sky is clear. During the louder hours, distances become unreliable. A line of reeds a morning’s walk away may stand close enough to count, then be distant again when the humming ceases. The stones keep their shapes. Only the space between them seems to give way.

There are a few low walls, widely separated. Each is made of the same rounded stones and has been repaired so many times that no first course remains. They offer little protection from rain, but people once used them, or something like people did: the inner faces are worn where bodies might have leaned. Now water passes through them. Small pale creatures surface in the flooded seams when the noise abates, take what the water has brought, and disappear before the next front.

It is difficult to say when a storm is over here. The rain may have passed, but the channels continue filling from below, and beyond the horizon another part of the plain is already sounding.
