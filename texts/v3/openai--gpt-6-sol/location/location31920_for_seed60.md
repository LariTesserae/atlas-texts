At first it looks like a low, overgrown pumping yard. A brick wall encloses perhaps an acre of ground, though its far side is hard to pick out through the leaves. The yard is only a little higher than the adjoining marsh. Water stands in the old wheel ruts, beads beneath every stem, and darkens the brick almost to black. It is warm enough that the wall dries by noon, but never at its base.

There is no open floor. Tough green runners cross the paths shoulder-high; beneath them lie older runners, flattened into a springy mat. Broad leaves have grown through the railings of a short iron stair until the stair seems to have been cast around them. A doorway remains in the wall, its lintel sound, but the passage beyond has filled with upright stalks as closely spaced as reeds in a basket. To go anywhere here, you part foliage and let it close behind you. Even the shallow pools are occupied by fine floating roots and little round leaves that meet edge to edge.

Near the middle stands the surviving half of the pump house: two brick piers, a flywheel, and a row of pipes vanishing into the ground. Something still works. The flywheel advances a few teeth each hour, too slowly to watch, while pressure gathers in the pipes and escapes in a sharp metallic bark. It is not clear where the old engine ends. Thick, jointed stems enter its broken valves; pale roots swell inside the casings and relax when water moves through them. The pipes give the plants firm passages into wetter soil. The plants, in turn, keep the pipes packed and the wheel under strain. Neither arrangement could easily be removed without disturbing the other.

The place is loud. Drops strike the wheel housing, the pipe mouths, the hollow bricks. Leaves tap leaves in the faintest air. Every few minutes a valve calls over it all, and afterward water can be heard trickling back through the root mat. Yet almost nothing appears to change. A wheel tooth advances; a pool loses a finger’s width of water and regains it overnight. The wall has leaned to this angle for a long time, held there by roots thick enough to have taken its shape.

One small snail sometimes crosses the exposed rim of the flywheel. Otherwise there is little sign of animals. Flowers open among the pipes, shed their pollen into the damp air, and close. Seedlings take hold on the older leaves before those leaves have fallen. In the ordinary daylight, with no mist to hide it, the yard’s strangeness is plain: it is full beyond the point of passage, and still making room for more.
