At this height, the place is no more than a courtyard, though it takes a while to find its edges. Every surface is occupied. Low walls press against taller walls; steps have been fitted into the spaces between pillars; narrow shelves and blind doorways fill what might once have been gaps. The floor is made of small, close-set blocks, the roof of overlapping slabs. Even at the parapet there is no glimpse of earth beneath the structure—only more fitted stone descending into dimness.

Everything bears the marks of simple tools. Chisel strokes survive inside recesses, while the exposed corners have been worn round and smooth. The masonry is so old that its repairs are difficult to distinguish from its first construction: a plugged opening, a second lintel beneath a failing one, a stair narrowed by generations of added supports. Nothing grows in the seams. They hold only pale, dry grit. The stone is warm to the hand, and the air under the roof is warmer still.

The sound comes through the walls. Rows of clay throats have been set into them, each opening no wider than a fist. Air passes through continuously, making a dense, deafening chord—high notes close to the ear, a lower pressure felt in the ribs and soles of the feet. There are no bells, wheels, or people tending it. If the passages were shaped by hand, their makers found a way to make the height itself do the work. From one end of the courtyard to the other, the chord scarcely changes; a footstep vanishes into it.

Nothing here seems to be waiting for anything. No shadow travels across the floor. No loose scrap lifts in the current. The worn blocks, the crowded additions, and the open mouths in the walls remain exactly where they have settled. Only the air goes on passing through, loudly enough to make this small, still place feel immense.
