The district begins at a row of three brick workshops and ends, barely two streets later, in a field of pale gravel. There is no gate or sign to mark the boundary. The pavement simply gives way to stones too new to have settled flat. A few years ago this was the edge of a river; the channel has since shifted behind a low bank, leaving a narrow shelf on which the town has cautiously begun to build.

The buildings stand well apart. Between them are broad, unruly plots of nettle, dock, young willow and flowering weeds, all growing higher than the fences. From the street the place can look nearly empty, until a yard opens into view: stacked iron pipe, a crane with its hook lowered into the grass, a line of workers’ houses set back beneath vigorous climbing vines. Everything near the ground stays damp. Rain is infrequent, but the cool air leaves droplets on handrails and window frames each morning. Water collects for a while in the fresh gravel hollows, then disappears below them.

The workshops make enough noise for a much larger district. A press strikes somewhere out of sight, followed by the long rattle of loose panes. An electric motor holds a steady note under the blows. At intervals a cart crosses the bridge over the old drainage cut, and its wheels change pitch on every plank. Sound travels easily across the open plots. So do the calls of starlings, which gather on the power lines and answer the machinery with astonishing volume. Swallows skim the yards; beetles crowd the damp undersides of timber; in the evenings, small animals move through the weeds without needing to cross much bare ground.

In full daylight, the gravel shines almost white against the dark workshops. Its edges are still sharp, and rain has made only the beginnings of paths through it. People have laid boards where they intend to walk and left the rest alone. Each season the willows claim a little more of the shelf, while the river, now mostly hidden, quietly tests its new bank. Neither change is quick enough to interrupt the shift whistle.
