The plateau begins above the last reliable clouds. Its surface is not level but worn into long, shallow folds, with shelves of pale stone stepping down toward a valley too broad to see all at once. There are no sharp cliffs. Even the ravines have rounded shoulders and smooth floors, as though water spent an immense amount of time here, then gradually learned to come less often.

The rock is warm under the bright sky. In its hollows, however, the air is damp. Water gathers overnight on the undersides of ledges and at the mouths of small, dark holes. By noon most of it has vanished, leaving mineral rings and narrow trails of green. A few deeper basins hold water through the dry weeks. From a distance they look like chips of blue glass set far apart in the stone.

Plants grow where those brief supplies are dependable: low silver shrubs, tufts of stiff grass, a tree now and then with most of its roots exposed along a seam. There is plenty of room between them. The animals make that space feel occupied. Small hoofed creatures cross the open shelves in dispersed groups; lizards bask along the old runoff channels; pale birds wheel over the valley without seeming to beat their wings. At the basin edges, insects arrive in such numbers that the wet stone appears to darken. A long-legged hunter follows them, picking its steps around the water, while the hoofed animals wait farther off.

Almost none of this is loud. Wings make a dry flicker close by. Pebbles click beneath a passing foot. The plateau carries little wind at ground level, though high above it the clouds move quickly. When rain comes, it falls in separate columns across the valley. One shelf may shine with water while the next remains dusty and sunlit.

Scattered among the natural basins are structures that can be mistaken for more stone: low arches, single uprights, a few open rings laid into the ground. Their surfaces have weathered to the same color as the plateau, but their edges never quite chip. At first light, beads of water appear on their shaded sides even after a dry night. They run down fine grooves and disappear beneath the rock. Elsewhere, an arch holds a patch of cool shade that does not move with the sun. The animals know these places. They visit them without hesitation, then return to the older pools and paths.

No road connects the structures. Thin animal tracks branch toward them and away again, crossing the broader courses cut by ancient water. Across the valley, the marks are too scattered to make a pattern from the ground. The plateau keeps its wide, bare reaches between them: bright stone, small islands of life, and moisture passing quietly from one place to another.
