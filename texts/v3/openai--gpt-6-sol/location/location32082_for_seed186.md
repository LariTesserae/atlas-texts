At this height the mountain has stopped looking like a peak. It opens into a broad, nearly level basin of dark stone, with a distant rim of cliffs visible only when the clouds thin. The cliffs have been rounded and undercut for so long that even their fallen blocks seem settled. There are no paths across the basin, no walls or cut faces. From a distance it looks almost empty.

Up close, much of the ground is alive. Low evergreen growth lies tight against the rock: overlapping black-green leaves, thick moss, and pale stems that turn their narrow edges to the wind. It is lush without being tall. Nothing here can afford much height. Broad spaces of bare stone separate the living patches, and in the dark those gaps look deeper than they are.

Water arrives in brief shifts. Cloud catches on the rim and descends as mist; moisture beads on every leaf, runs into old cracks, then freezes there. A little later, warmth from far below reaches some of the cracks and sends the water back up as vapor. There is seldom enough to make a stream. In a few sheltered hollows it gathers into still pools with rims of ice, visited and emptied before the next mist comes.

The warmth also moves through the plants. Beneath certain moss beds are long, hollow roots that swell as vapor enters them, then slowly contract in the cold. Across the basin this happens in sequences: one bed lifting, another subsiding, leaves opening to release their collected drops into the stone. Seen in a rare wash of moonlight, the motion has the orderliness of machinery at work. There are no machines. The only light most nights comes from faint blue points under the leaves, appearing where the roots are most active.

Animals keep to that cover. Small grazers rasp at the moss from below, so that a patch may tremble while its surface remains unbroken. Winged things emerge to take them, and heavier hunters wait among the widely spaced stones. Tracks cross the frost by morning, often many tracks, though it is possible to stand here for an hour and see nothing move. The basin is busy at a scale the darkness conceals.

It is quieter than a place with so much life should be. Wind passes over the low growth without finding much to catch. Now and then a drop strikes ice, or a distant patch of leaves lifts with a soft, collective rustle. Underfoot, if one stands still, there is a slow pressure through the rock as vapor travels along its buried seams. The mountain feels immense around that small motion: cold, open, and patiently at work.
