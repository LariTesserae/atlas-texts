The city stands in a low basin where the ground never quite dries. Its streets are broad channels of dark stone, shallow enough to cross on foot, though there are nights when water rises through their paving and leaves every square under a warm, unbroken sheet. The buildings are close together along the main ways—courtyards, stairways, roofless halls, rows of narrow doors—but beyond them the basin opens into long stretches of bare mud. From the highest roofs, the city seems to have been set down in pieces, with generous darkness between its districts.

There are no lamps. What little light there is comes from the sky, a dull violet suffusion behind clouds too low to have a discernible shape. It shows the pale mineral lines on the walls and the wet edges of steps. Heat stays in the masonry after dark. Water condenses on the undersides of lintels and falls at intervals into empty rooms.

Nothing here appears recently made. Corners have softened; thresholds have worn into shallow bowls; whole staircases have settled until their lower steps vanish beneath the street. Yet the city is not being reclaimed by much growth. A few thin, jointed plants stand in the courtyards, their roots reaching the water just below the crust. There is no moss on the walls, no insects in the damp, no sound from the open country.

The place is active all the same. Pools fill without rain. A dark tide passes slowly along one avenue and returns by a parallel one, leaving both streets at the same level it found them. Walls sweat, dry, and sweat again. Sometimes a doorway is deeper in the morning than it was the night before: not wider or taller, but farther from the street. Its steps remain in place, stretching the walk between them so gradually that it is hard to say where the extra distance begins.

Across the central square stand several blocks of rooms with no surviving roofs. From one side they look near enough to touch. Walk toward them and a second square comes into view between you and the first, with its own worn paving and its own ring of vacant doors. Looking back, the street you crossed is still there. The basin has room for these additions. Its outskirts never seem crowded.

Almost everything happens quietly. A drop sounds beneath a lintel; far away, water slips over a stone lip. Then there is only the heat and the faint pressure of damp air. The city gives the impression of having lasted a very long time in this condition—not fixed, but settled into its changes.
