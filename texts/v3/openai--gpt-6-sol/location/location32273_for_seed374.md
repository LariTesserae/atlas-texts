The town sits on a low shelf of pale stone beside a shallow salt lagoon. The ground looks newly made: broad, blunt ridges rise from the flats without gullies or worn paths down their sides. Rain lies in small hollows; elsewhere moisture beads on the rock and gathers under walls. Even in the warm air, nothing quite dries. A ceiling of cloud keeps the streets in a gray light, though the lagoon remains visible far beyond the last houses.

There are many houses, packed close enough that a person can brace a hand against either wall of a lane. They are built from fitted stone and mud mortar, with roofs of flat slabs, hide, and woven cord. Shells have been pressed into thresholds to keep feet from slipping. Shallow channels lead seep-water toward shared cisterns. There are no gardens, no weeds in the joints, no driftwood stacked by the doors. Nets, fish bone, skins, and stone tools fill the places where another town might keep timber or straw.

The sound is almost too large for the place. Birds crowd every roof edge and bare outcrop, calling over one another without pause. In the lanes, people shout to be heard while they split shellfish, dress fish, and strike stone against stone. Beyond the walls, seals bellow from a spit at the lagoon’s mouth. The birds fly out to feed and return; the people go on working beneath them. From a distance, their separate noises join into one steady roar.

At the edge of town, steps cut into the rock end among wet stones and moored skin boats. Crabs occupy the cracks below them. The water is only knee-deep for a long way out, clear enough to show pale, unworn rock beneath its surface. Nothing grows on the shore. The settlement depends on what swims, crawls, or flies through it, and on the water that rises quietly through the stone.
