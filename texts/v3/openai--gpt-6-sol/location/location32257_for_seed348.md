The town occupies a shallow basin of worn stone. Its streets are broad enough for wagons, though there are no wagons, and its buildings stand well apart: a foundry, a row of low houses, a pumping hall, a few towers connected by sagging cables. Rain has softened every corner. Brick has lost its sharp edges; iron stairs are smooth in the middle of each tread. Nothing grows in the cracks. Between buildings there are only stone chips, dark grit, and shallow hollows that hold water for a day or two after rain.

In ordinary daylight, the town appears nearly empty from a distance. Up close, almost every surface is occupied. Thousands of pale, plated animals cling beneath the eaves, each fitted precisely against its neighbors. Long-legged ones stand in the gutters with their feet in the condensation, while smaller, blunt-headed creatures pack the mouths of drainpipes. They leave the middle of the streets open. You can walk for a hundred paces without having to step around one, accompanied all the way by their attention.

The pumping hall is running. Its flywheel turns behind high windows, and warm pipes carry water from the basin’s buried seams to troughs set along the walls. The animals drink there, and scrape mineral deposits from the brick with flat teeth. In return, their droppings collect in hard, pale banks that workers once shoveled into the foundry. There are no workers now, but the wheel turns, the troughs fill and overflow, and the banks remain worn down to much the same height. The town has been using what the animals leave for so long that the older walls were built with ledges for them.

It is loud without sounding busy. The pumps beat at a fixed pace. Water ticks from the cable joints. Above that, the animals make a continuous noise: a dense, uneven rattling, as if every roof held a drawer of loose cutlery. Now and then one opens its plates and the sound swells along a whole street, then settles again. The air is cool and faintly metallic, with the damp smell of stone that never quite dries.

At the basin’s edge, the last houses face bare slopes furrowed by old runoff. Their doors have been rubbed hollow where passing shells brush the wood. From there you can look back across the separated roofs and the open streets and see how little shifts. A creature lowers its head to drink; another takes its place. The flywheel completes a turn. In the brief rain, water runs through channels already polished by centuries of it, and when the rain stops, the town sounds exactly as it did before.
