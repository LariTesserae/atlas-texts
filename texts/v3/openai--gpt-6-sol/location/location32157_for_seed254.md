Below the street is a room large enough to have streets of its own. Brick piers divide it into narrow aisles, and a shallow canal runs between them, almost level with the paving. The bricks are dark with age; their edges have softened where water has traveled over them for years. Here and there, iron pipes disappear into the walls of an older culvert. The whole chamber is warm, as though the ground has been holding a summer afternoon long after the city above lost it.

Plants occupy nearly every surface. Ferns spring from the mortar joints in such numbers that the piers appear to be made of fronds. Roots hang through cracks in the vault, gather into ropes, and descend into the canal. Along the aisles, old planting troughs have overflowed their rims; their soil is held in place by a close mat of stems, and new stems have crossed into the next trough. There is still a way through, but it narrows at shoulder height and brushes anyone who takes it with wet leaves. Nothing here grows in open ground. It grows in brick, in silt, in pipe brackets, in the debris caught against a stair.

A few grates admit a grey light from above. Below them the leaves are broad and crowded, one layer turned over another to face the openings. Elsewhere, low electric lamps glow behind cloudy glass, lighting short lengths of aisle and leaving the vault dim. Water beads on the unlit leaves and drops from their tips. The canal barely moves; its surface is marked chiefly by those drops.

The noise comes from the far end, where a pumping engine works behind a screen of climbing stems. Its steady thud fills the room, joined by the rush through a valve and the occasional sharp knock of a pipe cooling or shifting in its clamp. These sounds make the chamber seem busy, though nothing moves quickly. A wheel turns at the same pace; a root thickens around a rail; water finds the same worn course through the paving. No animals call or stir in the leaves. The pumps keep drawing water beneath the city, and the plants keep taking what the pumps leave behind.
