The high country begins without a sharp edge. For hundreds of miles the ground rises in long, worn steps, each one so broad it seems level until you look back and see the last step lying blue in the distance. There are no young peaks here. The summits have been pared into tables and shallow domes; their cliffs have slumped into slopes of fitted rubble. In the brightest hours, every face of stone shows its age: pale ribs where wind has stripped it clean, dark hollows where fine grit has spent centuries turning in place.

Nothing flows across the ground. The narrow channels between the ridges were cut long ago, and now they hold drifts of powder soft enough to record a falling seed. Air moves through them instead, cool even under the fierce light. Sometimes it carries a veil of mineral dust over an entire range, briefly erasing the farther slopes. When the veil passes, the horizon returns, much farther away than it seemed possible for anything to be.

The vegetation grows in widely separated provinces. From a distance, one looks like a green stain on an empty plateau; nearer, it becomes a forest of low, immense crowns. Their stems grip cracks in the bedrock, and their branching leaves are made of fine, stiff threads that comb the air for dust. Sunlight feeds them. The minerals they need arrive on the wind and accumulate at their roots in little pale mounds. Where a crown has stood for ages, the mound may be taller than the plant itself.

Within these scattered forests it is lush. Green threads overlap until the stone disappears beneath them. New growth gleams silver; old growth fades to a soft black and breaks apart, making a thin, fertile crust that the wind cannot easily lift. Outside their shelter, there may be a day’s walk over bare rock before the next living patch. The space between is not dead, exactly. Small, dark colonies occupy the shaded sides of stones, and after a strong dustfall they slowly spread across the fresh coating.

Almost nothing calls. A thread-crown makes a faint dry hiss when the wind passes through it. Loose stone ticks as the sun warms it, then again when evening draws the heat away. Rare pale animals travel between the green provinces, carrying seeds caught in their coats. They are most often seen from far off, briefly crossing a sunlit slope and vanishing against the next ridge.

Changes here are easy to miss while they happen. A dust veil settles. A root widens a seam. A slab, loosened through many seasons of heat and cold, finally slips and comes to rest. The land is active on an enormous, quiet scale, wearing itself down and giving its scattered forests somewhere new to take hold.
