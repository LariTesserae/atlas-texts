The stair ends before the sound begins to make sense. At first it is only a continuous roar, as though rain were falling on a roof too large to see. Then individual noises separate from it: water striking stone, stone knocking against stone, a deep metallic note that swells and disappears. There is no roof in the ordinary sense. Forty metres overhead, the ceiling of the chamber shines an even, warm white, bright enough to cast short shadows beneath the ledges.

The floor slopes toward a channel cut through pale limestone. Most of the channel is dry when you arrive. Its bottom is smooth and dark, with small pockets of standing water. Then water comes round the bend in a low, fast wall. It fills the channel, leaps against the far bank, and throws spray halfway across the chamber. A few minutes later it drains away again. The edges of the flood are so regular that the shelf beside it can be walked without fear, though the water itself looks wild.

Everything solid has been here a long time. The limestone is worn into broad, comfortable curves; darker bands in the walls show where older waterlines held steady for years. A fallen block has been polished on its upper face and left rough underneath. Along the shelf stand three narrow structures, each no taller than a person. They might once have been markers. Their surfaces are pitted, and the water has rounded their corners, but a fine line of light still travels through each one when the channel fills.

Far below the surface, the chamber is warm and damp. Moisture gathers on the walls between floods and runs down in threads. It beads on the sparse life that has found a hold here: a tuft of grasslike leaves high in a crack, a patch of dark green growth where a drip lands reliably. There are bare stretches between them. Once, something small moves across the lit stone and slips into a hole before the next surge arrives.

The brightness comes from within the ceiling rock. It has no visible lamps or panels, although in one place a seam runs across it as straight as a drawn line. The old machinery may be beyond that seam, or farther away, working through the stone. Whatever sends the water through is out of sight too. The three standing pieces answer each surge with their passing light, and occasionally the channel gives a heavy, resonant thud that can be felt through the shelf.

It is easy to mistake the place for a system in trouble. Water bursts around corners; spray hangs in the bright air; loose grit skitters at your feet. But on every return the flood follows the same worn path. The chamber has had time to make a shape around its violence. When the water recedes, the roar falls back to a hiss, and for a brief interval you can hear drops falling from the ceiling one by one.
