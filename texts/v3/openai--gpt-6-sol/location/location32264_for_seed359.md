The grove lies a little below the height of its surrounding rim, in a shallow basin perhaps thirty paces across. You can see most of it from the entrance, though seeing it does not help much with crossing it. The floor is laid in overlapping plates of dark fired earth. Their edges are still sharp enough to catch a hem, and here and there the pale marks of a maker’s rubbing stone show where an edge was made safe. There is no soil beneath the plates. Through a narrow gap you can see another course of plates, and beneath that the close-woven cords that hold the basin up.

It is warm here. Light falls broadly through a roof of stretched fibre, neither dim nor glaring. Nothing drips from it. Nothing has ever washed dust into the corners; the fine powder there is shed from the grove itself.

Every growing thing has been made. The thick stems nearest the entrance were built from stacked rings of horn and ceramic, bound so closely that their seams look like growth marks. Higher up, they divide into a crowded canopy of thin greenish blades. Each blade is rubbed smooth on one face and rough on the other. At night it gives back a little of the day’s heat; by morning it has drawn mineral dust against its rough side, which the stem slowly takes in. New blades appear as small, blunt pieces pushed out between old bindings. No one comes to attach them. The makers know how to start a stem, but not how to stop it growing.

There are too many stems for the space allotted to them. Their crowns press against one another and against the roof. Shorter growths occupy the gaps: fans of black fibre, jointed pale shoots, low mats of bristles that turn, over the course of a day, toward whichever patch of floor remains warmest. A narrow aisle was left through the middle when the grove was built. It now runs out halfway across.

Small creatures live in the density. Several flat-backed animals cling beneath each broad blade, scraping at the dust caught there. A six-legged one, no larger than a thumb, carries loose fibres to a shelter in the floor cords. Larger creatures with soft, folded ears stand motionless among the stems until a shadow crosses them; then they put their heads down and nudge at the bristle mats. Though the place is full of bodies, it is mostly quiet. Feet settle on fibre. A blade may make a dry tick as it grows past its binding. From under the floor comes an occasional patient rasp.

The basin is new. Some lashings still have their ends hanging free, and the outer rim is raw where pieces were broken to fit. Yet the grove has already made its own difficulties. A stem too near the entrance has pushed one plate up at an angle. The animals have worn a passage under it. No part looks weathered, but almost every part has been altered.

At the far side, the aisle seems to continue behind a hanging screen of leaves. Walk toward it and you reach the rim instead. The continuation remains visible to your left, between two stems that are touching. A small animal can pass through that apparent gap; a hand cannot. The makers leave such places alone. Cutting the stems apart has only ever produced a wider view and less room to stand.

By late afternoon the roof cords cast straight shadows across the floor. The shadows reach into the false passages as if those passages had floors of their own. The animals gather at their edges, taking warmth from both sides. When the light fades, they withdraw into the crowded, touchable part of the grove, and the leaves give off their stored heat in silence.
