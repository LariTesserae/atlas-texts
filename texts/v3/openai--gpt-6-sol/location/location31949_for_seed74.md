The town fits inside a shallow bend of land, between two low banks worn smooth by a stream that no longer runs for most of the year. From the road, it looks almost empty. There is a broad yard, a few upright forms, a chimney no taller than a mature tree. Only on entering does it become clear that the upright forms are buildings: immense, close-planted stems trained into walls, their roots knitted over old brick foundations. Doors have been cut where trunks failed to join. Window glass sits in wooden frames that continue to grow around it.

The yard is kept open. Pale gravel shows through a scatter of tough leaves, and the spaces between the buildings are wide enough for carts to turn. Yet nearly every surface is alive. The long roof of the works is a low canopy of interlaced branches; vines make its gutter, carrying rainfall into covered cisterns. Moss closes cracks in the loading platform, dies back during dry weeks, and turns green again after a night of condensation. The cool air smells of bark, damp iron, and the faint sourness of stored rainwater.

The works is running, though it seldom sounds busy. Inside, a motor turns slowly beneath the floor. Its motion passes through shafts to presses and rollers that strip fibre from harvested stems. A belt gives a soft slap once each revolution. Every so often a valve releases a breath of steam into the leaves overhead, and drops fall onto the gravel as if a small, private shower has begun. Fibre dries on racks in the open yard; bundled stems wait beside a narrow rail set into the ground. No one hurries. A worker crosses from the press house to the cistern with a wrench, and for a while that is the only movement not made by plants.

The place has been maintained so long that repair and growth are hard to tell apart. Old channels in the banks guide brief storms toward the roots rather than the road. Stones along their edges have settled below the soil; the roots hold their shape. When the cisterns fill, water is allowed through in stages, and the whole town changes over several days—leaves lifting, new shoots reaching across gaps that must later be trimmed clear. In dry weather the motors still turn, but slower, and the cut stems are left standing longer.

There are few animals. Once, a small bird calls from within the roof, where it cannot be seen. Its note is sharp enough to make the belt and the dripping water seem quieter. Beyond the last loading rack, the open yard returns to grass and gravel, then to the low bank. From there the town is again only a handful of trees, with a thin line of steam leaving one of them.
