The place is no larger than a walled garden, though the wall is a natural rim of dark stone and the garden stands high above anything visible. Beyond the rim, the land disappears into dim, moving dust. Inside it, the ground is warm underfoot: old, pale slabs fitted together by ages of abrasion, their edges softened but their surfaces continually swept clean.

There is no water here. The plants rise far apart from one another, with broad empty floors of stone between them. Each is lush in itself—a crowded crown of thick green-black blades, folded fronds, and soft, branching growth—but none casts seed close to its parent. Their roots grip fissures that breathe a faint mineral heat. When the wind changes, the crowns open. The undersides of the blades are silver, and for a moment the whole enclosure brightens as if someone has turned over a handful of mirrors.

The wind changes constantly. It drops through the rim in heavy bursts, circles the floor, then climbs out again carrying grit and the dry husks of insects. Fronds snap against one another. Loose scales in the stone rattle. Somewhere near the far wall, a colony of small animals answers every gust with a chorus of hard clicks; higher in the plants, larger creatures give drawn-out, sawing cries. Even when none are visible, the place sounds occupied to capacity.

At the sheltered end of the enclosure stands a low structure made from stacked, uncut slabs. Its roof has fallen in, leaving a few stone ribs and a patch of shade. Shallow grooves run from it to the nearest plants. They do not carry anything now. Set into one groove are worn wooden paddles, each with a different notch along its edge. In strong wind the paddles lift and knock against the stone, adding an uneven beat to the noise.

The animals use those moments. Small ones race across the open floor while the wind covers the sound of their feet. Others hang motionless beneath the fronds until a blade unfurls, then crowd onto its exposed underside to scrape away the fine powder caught there. The plants hold fast through all of it. They bend, close, and open again, feeding in the warm breath rising from the cracks and in what the air leaves on their leaves.

Nothing stays still for long except the stone. Even that is slowly changing: the rim sheds a little grit with every gust, and the grit polishes the floor it lands on. The enclosure has endured this commotion long enough to be shaped by it. Its quietest intervals last only a few breaths, just enough time to hear a single creature moving inside a folded crown.
