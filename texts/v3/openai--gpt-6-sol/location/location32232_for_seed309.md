The town occupies a hollow beneath an old grove, though *hollow* suggests more empty space than there is. Roots make the ceiling, walls, streets, and foundations. They have grown around one another for so long that no seam marks where one tree ends and another begins. Between their broad, polished ridges lies a single open court, perhaps sixty paces across. Most of the town can be seen from its center: three workshops, a row of narrow dwellings, and the dew works set into a root as thick as a house.

It is warm down here, and almost dark. Lamps behind green glass mark the workshop doors; elsewhere, light leaks through cracks overhead or comes from pale fungi kept in shallow trays. The air smells of bruised leaves, hot metal, and the dry sweetness of old wood. Damp gathers on a handrail or the underside of a pipe, but the ground is seldom wet. Water arrives in drops, chiefly at night, when the upper soil cools. Copper sheets spread against the ceiling catch the condensation. A pump lifts what they collect into a tank no taller than a person. By morning, people are queuing beneath its taps.

The machinery makes a steady racket. A belt drive passes through the workshops, turning saws, presses, and a loom that twists root fiber into cable. Its flywheel rests in a notch worn deep into living wood. Generations of workers have planed the notch smooth without widening it; the root grows a little each year, and each year the bearings must be moved. Nothing here is quite fixed, but the adjustments are so familiar that their measurements are cut into the doorposts.

The louder sound comes from the animals. Moths strike the lamps like thrown seeds. Beetles crowd the warm casings of the engines. Small pale things race along the overhead roots, pursuing one another with sharp cries. At dusk, thousands of wingbeats gather in the court until speech has to be made close to the ear. The inhabitants leave the center clear then. The bats need room to hunt, and their hunting keeps the insects out of the water tank.

Even the bare-looking streets are alive. Their smooth brown surface is root skin, toughened by feet and freight carts. Old routes have sunk into shallow grooves; unused ones slowly close. A rail laid too near a new shoot will be lifted in a season, so the town keeps its heavy equipment on the oldest roots and builds everything else to be taken apart.

There is a rule posted at the dew works: **Do not seal a crack until you know what uses it.** Air enters through cracks. So do rain, beetles, bats, and, once each spring, the fine searching roots that find the town changed. The people patch their roofs and guard their drinking water, but they leave many openings alone. On a loud evening, with the belt turning and wings knocking against every pane, it is hard to say which activity belongs to the town and which belongs to the grove above it.
