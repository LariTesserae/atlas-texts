The settlement occupies the flat top of a new black ridge, no wider than a market square. Its edges still have the clean, broken faces of stone split from stone; nothing has had time to round them. Beyond the rim, pale salt ground falls away into a warm haze.

Up close, the ridge is crowded. Narrow lanes pass between walls of living, ribbed growth, some barely shoulder-wide. Roofs meet overhead in lattices of stiff leaves. There are courtyards no larger than a bed, balconies with rootlike railings, and a central tower made of many trunks grown so tightly together that it looks carved from one piece. No one laid its courses. The walls swell toward gaps in the light, and their older surfaces harden to a dull amber crust.

There is no water here. In cracks in the rock, a slow breath of warm, mineral-laden gas rises from below. The rooted growths take what they need from it and from the sun. Their leaves are thick and almost dry to the touch, green above and silver where they turn toward the stone. When a leaf is torn, it seals at once with a bead of wax.

Small animals fill the place without making it noisy. Pale, six-footed grazers work along the walls, shaving the silver undersides of leaves. Round-bodied creatures sleep in the warm joints of the tower. A thin-winged flock crosses the courtyard from roof to roof, settling so lightly that only their shadows announce them. Most have soft feet; most move slowly. Now and then a hard seed drops and clicks against the rock, a startling sound in so much quiet.

The lanes are kept open by use. The grazers return to the same routes, eating back new shoots, and the walls grow more thickly wherever those routes do not pass. A house can close its own doorway in a few seasons. A new one may open when a trunk leans away from its neighbour. From the salt plain the whole settlement appears still, a dense green crown on a sharp black height. Inside it, the city is changing all the time, too slowly to catch in the act.
