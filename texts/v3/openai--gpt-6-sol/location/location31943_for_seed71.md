The plateau is so broad that its edge is a matter of weather rather than sight. From the city’s highest roof, one sees low green country running to a pale distance, with the occasional rise of stone worn almost level with it. Clouds form over one quarter of the plateau and dissolve over another. A day’s walk may take a traveler from frost into rain without any change in altitude.

The ground is very old. Its exposed stone has no sharp faces left, only wide, shallow hollows that hold a few inches of black water after rain. Between them lie beds of short, thick-leaved plants. They are lush when seen close up: leaves overlap leaves, roots bind the thin soil, and every stem is bright with cold moisture. But the beds are separated by long reaches of bare, silver-grained rock. Nothing grazes them. Nothing flies overhead. There is no insect sound beneath the leaves.

The city occupies one of these reaches. It is large enough to have districts, canals, public stairs, and squares where several roads meet, yet much of it is open ground. Its buildings are made of fitted slabs, clay brick, and timber darkened by centuries of condensation. Some stand three stories high; many are only walls around roofless courts. Gutters lead from roof to cistern, from cistern to planting bed, from planting bed back beneath the street. The joints of this system were made for hands to repair: stone plugs, wooden gates, simple hinges. No hands live here now.

Water arrives unevenly. A rainstorm will cross half a neighborhood, leaving the other half white with frost. In the courts where water collects, moss rises around the drains and broad leaves lean across the paving. Elsewhere the plants keep their moisture under waxy skins and wait. They turn slowly through the season, opening and closing patches of green across the city, while the streets remain mostly empty stone.

At first, the unusual thing seems to be the silence. Frost loosens from a roof without a sound. Rain on the upper terraces can be seen from far away, but its patter seldom carries. Then a person notices that the upper terraces are not always uppermost.

The city changes its arrangement without moving its materials. A lane walked at dawn may return at evening to the same square by a different side. Across a courtyard, one doorway can show the next room and another can show a stretch of plateau many miles away. Both doors are set firmly in the same wall. Rain respects these openings more faithfully than people once did: a gutter may pour into a distant district, and a cistern there will fill under a clear sky.

The plants have made use of this. Roots disappear into narrow cracks and emerge wet in places that have had no rain for months. Vines cross thresholds and continue along walls that cannot, by ordinary measure, be near each other. This gives the city a scattered garden’s appearance. Here a courtyard is crowded with leaves; around the corner, acres of paving hold only a little lichen. The pattern shifts as doors and distances change, but never quickly enough to watch.

Beyond the city, the same uncertainty belongs to the land. A line of old, flattened hills sometimes takes the whole afternoon to cross. On other days the last hill is close enough to reach before the sun has noticeably moved. The rock underfoot remains continuous. Its shallow pools, seams of soil, and weathered surface offer no sign that anything has happened.

At night the cold settles first in the empty streets. Condensation gathers on the unused gates and runs into the drains. Somewhere far across the plateau a storm is at work; here, only a cistern fills.
