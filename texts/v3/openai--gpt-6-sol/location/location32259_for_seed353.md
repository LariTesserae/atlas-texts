The plateau is high enough that the air should be thin and cold, but heat rises through it from below. It comes up through the floor in long, uneven bands, making the dark seem to ripple. Nothing here gives off enough light to show the whole landscape at once. Now and then a row of dull red seams opens in the distance, and for a few seconds it reveals terraces, towers, and the immense low roof that covers them. Then the seams close, and the place goes back to being an arrangement of warmth, edges, and depth.

There is no bare ground. What looks underfoot like worn black stone is a dense mat of roots, compressed over ages until it has the hardness of pavement. Its surface has been grooved for carts and polished by their passage. Walls rise out of it in fitted courses: thick pale stems grown around molds, then left to dry and seal. Above them, canopies join across the streets so completely that the city has made its own night. They were planted to shade the workings beneath. They have long since grown past that purpose.

Almost everything is a plant, though little of it resembles a garden. A tower may be a single trunk trained into six hollow chimneys. The chimneys draw hot air from the plateau’s buried seams and drive slow wheels made of leaf-ribs and ceramic teeth. Broad, leathery fronds turn their dark faces toward the faint furnace glow; others fold tight against it, preserving what little moisture they hold. Roots wrap the axles, grip the tracks, and form the bins into which the towers sort their hard, dry fruit. The fruit is milled for oil, fiber, and fuel. No one tends the machinery now. The machinery tends the plants, and the plants keep making more machinery.

Water exists here mostly as a guarded substance inside living tissue. There are no streams, no tanks open to the air. A broken stem closes over at once, showing a brief wet shine before a skin forms. Dust lies thick in the unused corners, fine as flour and bitter with salt. At the outer edge of the plateau, ancient erosion has lowered the planted terraces to a succession of broad, shallow steps. From there, rows of chimneys can be seen continuing across the dark basin, their mouths faintly warm and occasionally red.

For a place so crowded, it is nearly quiet. Heat moves through the hollow stems with a low breath. Somewhere a wheel advances one tooth. Dry leaves rub together overhead. The larger changes happen without warning and with surprisingly little noise: a wall swells across an alley and closes it; a line of roots lifts a track from its bed; a chimney splits lengthwise to release a new one inside it. Whole blocks shift their work from one vent to another as the heat below wanders. By the time a slow cart comes around again, the route it followed may have been grown shut.

The oldest parts are not ruins set apart from the active ones. Their edges have been worn round by centuries of use, buried, uncovered, and put to use again. A doorway too low to enter still admits warm air. An obsolete conveyor, swallowed almost to its rim, guides roots toward a furnace. Deep in the terraces are chambers whose purposes have been lost, but their walls remain alive and continue to open and close on a patient schedule.

Nothing flies through the dim spaces under the canopy. There are no calls, no tracks but those made by the carts. Yet the plateau is never still. Its streets narrow and widen; its roof thickens; its foundations grip the old, dry height. In the darkness it is difficult to tell whether the city is enduring a long disturbance or has simply learned to live by rearranging itself.
