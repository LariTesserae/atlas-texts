The path ends on a high shelf of stone, no wider than a small grove. Its edges fall away steeply, but the shelf itself has been worn almost level. Rain has had so long to work here that there are no sharp corners left: only shallow bowls, smooth lips, and cracks filled with warm water. You find the ground by its give. A close mat of leaves and roots covers most of the stone, while the few larger trunks stand far apart. Between them is room to walk with both arms out.

There is no light to wait for. Even after hours, the eye learns nothing. The plants are known by touch and by the smells released when they are brushed—pepper, wet bark, a sweetness like bruised fruit. Broad leaves collect condensation and let it fall in heavy drops. Water is never deep, but it is everywhere near the surface. It seeps from the rock, gathers around a footstep, and withdraws so slowly that the impression remains after the foot is gone.

The shelf sounds much more crowded than it feels. Small animals call from the roots, from the cracks, from the steep air beyond the rim. Some answer one another; others keep up a single rough note for hours. Droplets strike the hollow stone with a force that makes each one audible over the calls. Now and then something large enough to part the leaves passes close by without touching you. Nothing here seems surprised by a stranger. The animals locate movement by sound, and the roots are sensitive enough that a careless step sends a faint shiver through the ground.

Three low posts mark the only built place. They are smooth and warm, sunk deep into the old stone. Water beads on them but never runs down. Beneath the shelf, whatever connects them still works: pressure from the saturated rock is taken up and returned gently, holding the thin soil in place through years of rain. There is no motor to hear and no opening to inspect. You can feel its operation only when you put a hand against a post and notice that the pulse under it is slower than your own.

People once used the posts to find their way across. They still serve, though nobody seems to tend them. Between their widely spaced warmths, the water falls, the creatures call, and the roots go on binding this small, ancient height together.
