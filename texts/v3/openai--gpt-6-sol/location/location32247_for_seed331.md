The ground here has almost finished wearing away. It is a broad, shallow dish of pale stone, no larger than a village green, with its rim only a handspan above the surrounding marsh. Rain and countless seasons of shallow water have taken every edge off it. Even the cracks are smooth. In bright weather the stone looks dry from a distance, but close up it is dark in the pores, and water gathers wherever a fingertip might rest.

There are only a few trees. Their roots travel sideways over the rock before finding the soft ground at its rim. Between them grow reeds, low flowering vines, and islands of grass separated by open stone and thin, warm water. The space feels spare until you stand still.

Then the animals come into view. Fish occupy pools too shallow to cover their backs. Transparent shrimp move through the film of water around your feet. Beetles cross the stone in glinting files; pale moths cling beneath the leaves, and dragonflies hold themselves motionless over the grass. Small frogs sit at intervals along the basin’s cracks. Above them, wading birds wait with their bills pointed down, so numerous that their shadows keep passing over the same patches of stone. Nearly every surface is being searched, grazed, hunted, or used as shelter.

For all that life, the place is quiet. A bill taps rock. Wings brush a reed. Once in a while there is the delicate sound of water entering a pool, though no stream runs into it.

Three short columns stand around the basin. From far away they could be remnants of an old landing or markers for a path that has vanished into the marsh. Up close, their surfaces have no grain, mortar, or seam. In the heat of the afternoon, water climbs them as a clear skin. It reaches the blunt tops, hangs there for a moment, and returns to the ground as a fine rain. Nothing pumps beneath the columns; the stone around their bases has lain undisturbed long enough for roots to grow through it.

The falling water moves slowly. Birds step in and out of it without startling, and insects assemble in the bright air beneath it. By evening the little pools have joined across the basin floor. By morning they will have retreated into separate hollows again. The trees keep their distance from the columns, but the marsh does not: seeds collect at their bases, hatchlings shelter there, and every dry day brings more creatures over the rim to wait for water.
