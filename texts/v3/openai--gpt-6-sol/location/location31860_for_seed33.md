At this height the plain has no horizon in the usual sense. It ends in a row of parapets, then begins again much farther away, tilted a few degrees against the sky. Between these repetitions lies the city.

Its streets are narrow cuts through pale stone. They meet in squares, pass beneath arches, climb stairs worn shallow at their centers. There are terraces large enough to hold crowds and doorways too low for anyone to enter upright. No joints or tool marks show in the walls. The same fine layers of rock run through a façade, across an empty window, and into the cliff behind it. Seen from above, the city might be an exposed bed of stone; from within, it offers routes and intersections so insistently that it is difficult not to use them.

Nothing grows here. Dust lies in the sheltered thresholds, but the open squares are swept clean by air too thin to feel against the skin. The stone keeps the cold of the long dim hours. Above it, the sky is a muted violet-grey, bright enough to show the next street and too faint to reveal where the city ends.

The place is active, though almost silent. At intervals a flight of steps lengthens by one riser. A distant arcade draws nearer while the square in front of it remains the same size. Hairline cracks cross a wall over the course of a day; by evening they have rounded edges, as if they have been exposed for centuries. Elsewhere an old cornice sheds a grain of stone, and the grain falls so slowly that it can be watched from one side of a courtyard to the other. The small accumulations of grit at the foot of each wall are the only reliable record of this movement.

There are inhabitants, but few. Once in a while a low, jointed animal emerges from a gap in the masonry and crosses an open street. Its shell has the colour and dull sheen of the rock. It pauses at intersections, feeling along the ground with delicate forelimbs, then chooses an arch and disappears. Their tracks are scarce, and some lead to doors that stand several storeys above the street. Nothing here offers an obvious meal. In the colder hours the animals gather where the stone gives off a little heat, though a wall that was warm yesterday may not be warm again for years.

The wind comes regularly across the plateau. It carries dust through the upper openings and wears the corners of the buildings into soft, settled curves. Almost no sound accompanies it. From a roof one can see whole districts separated by vacant expanses of stone, each district distinct in its towers and courts. The distances between them change. A traveller can wait for a far-off neighbourhood to draw close enough to reach by dusk, then find on returning that the road home has become a long one.

For all that shifting, the city has an old, patient appearance. Its walls have been weathered for longer than its streets seem able to remain in place. There are countless sheltered rooms, broad approaches, and spaces made for looking out across the height. Most of the time, they hold only cold air.
