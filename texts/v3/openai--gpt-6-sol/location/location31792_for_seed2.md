The floor comes into view a long time before you reach it: a pale grid beneath miles of clear, cold water. Its seams run straight to the limit of sight. Some panels are still clean enough to reflect the overhead lamps; others carry a thin grey film, already thickening around bolts and drain slots. There is no rock here. The slopes, shelves, and sudden deep channels were assembled, and their edges are still sharp.

Light arrives from towers spaced so far apart that each seems solitary. Between them the water is open and nearly empty, with a faint blue cast and slow-moving flecks of silt. A swimmer can travel for hours above an unbroken plain and see little but the next tower growing brighter. Then a service trench appears below, crowded with fish. They hold their places in the current from an intake, darting out to catch what the screens have stopped. Larger fish work the dimmer water beyond them.

The machinery is loud even at a distance. Pumps send a steady shudder through the floor; gate motors start with a hard, carrying whine. In places, the sound changes before the current does. Fish turn into it, and a few moments later the water begins to pull toward an opening hundreds of metres wide. Warning lights come on along its rim. Silt lifts from the panels in a broad sheet.

There are small gardens of weed on sheltered joints, but the moving water strips most surfaces bare. Crews in work craft cut away what remains when it reaches a hinge or sensor. They are still fitting parts of the basin: newly welded walls descend beside older ones, and empty mounting frames wait in the light. Far below, a gate closes. The vibration passes underfoot, through the metal plain and out beyond the next pool of lamps.
