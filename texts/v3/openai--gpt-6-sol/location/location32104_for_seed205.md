From the rim, the plateau looks bare. Its distances are too large for the eye to understand the low green covering them, and the taller growth stands miles apart: dark crowns on pale stems, each with an apron of shade around its base. The horizon has the clean, wavering edge of a hot plain. Only after walking down onto it do you realize that there is no ground in the usual sense.

Underfoot is a firm weave of roots, stems, and old leaves pressed together through centuries of growth. It gives a little with each step. In places it has risen into long ridges; elsewhere it sags over hollows where dead layers have collapsed and new growth has knitted across the gap. A few of the hollows still hold water from the last rain. Most are dry, their bottoms dark and cool enough to draw a crowd of small animals.

Rain comes rarely and hard. Water runs across the living surface before much can soak in, gathering in the deep seams and spilling off the plateau in brief falls. The plants keep what they can. At dawn, condensation beads on every upright fiber. By noon the beads are gone, and the heat has pulled a sharp resin smell out of the mat. Even the great trees look economical up close: their crowns are broad but thin, turning narrow leaf-edges toward the sun. Each trunk throws shade over a basin of softer plants, fallen husks, insects, and the animals that hunt them.

Between those basins lie immense open reaches. Nothing there grows much higher than a knee. You can see a tree all morning without seeming to approach it, though the land beneath you is crowded down to the width of a fingertip. Beetles work in the root weave. Quick, flat creatures vanish into slits that close behind them. Pale birds pace over the mat, turning back folds with their beaks. Farther off, herds move through the low growth in dark, uneven bands; when they pass, the whole surface shivers after them.

The noise is hardest to judge from a distance. It sounds at first like water, then like many kinds of dry things rubbing together. Wings rasp. Animals call from the tree basins, and others answer from beyond sight. The hollow trunks carry some of those calls so that a small creature close by can seem enormous and a herd miles away can seem to be crossing just behind you. When the wind rises, the leaves along an entire ridge rattle in succession. The sound travels across the plateau long after the gust itself has passed.

There are marks of people, though they are easy to miss. A footpath follows one of the firmer root ridges. Here and there a flat stone has been set into the weave to keep a crossing open, or a screen of bound stalks stands beneath a tree, repaired many times with fresh fiber. Beside some screens are shallow clay bowls for catching the night's moisture. No one clears much space. Cut growth returns quickly, and the strongest paths are the ones the plateau was making already.

By late afternoon, heat haze lifts the distant trees clear of their trunks. The open miles between them seem empty again. Then a flock rises from the mat, followed by another and another, each so far away that its beating wings cannot be heard separately. Their shadows run over the ridges toward you. Beneath your feet, something hears them coming and goes still.
