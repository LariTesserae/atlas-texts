At this altitude the sky is dark blue even at noon, but the stone underfoot is warm enough to soften the tar in the street joints. The city occupies a shelf on a mountain that is still rising. Beyond its outer workshops, the ground has not yet acquired gullies or scree: it stands in fresh, blunt folds, with seams of pale rock exposed as cleanly as the inside of a broken cup.

Buildings crowd the shelf from edge to edge. Foundries, compressor houses, dormitories and narrow covered markets lean against one another, their upper floors joined by bridges. There is little room for a horizon at street level. Pipes cross overhead in dense tiers; cables run beneath them, sagging between porcelain insulators. Where a lane opens unexpectedly, it gives a view across miles of high ridges, all similarly bare, and of another district fixed to a distant slope like a bank of dark machinery.

The heat comes from below. Furnace chimneys contribute to it, but even the oldest walls are warm, and in the basements workers leave a hand’s width of air between stored goods and the bedrock. Expansion joints are part of every building. Once in a great while a stair gains a shallow step it did not have before, or a doorway ceases to be square. Survey marks are painted on corners so that people can tell which part has moved.

Water is treated with more surprise than fire. Clouds occasionally catch on the heights and let go a brief, heavy rain. For an hour the roofs ring, cistern mouths stand open, and runoff turns the unpaved margins black. By morning most of it has gone into tanks or into the hot stone. In the cooler spaces behind the compressor houses, condensation gathers steadily on metal plates and drips into small covered troughs. It is not enough to green anything. There are no weeds in the cracks, no roots testing the foundations. The few living things one sees are ash-coloured birds that arrive singly from lower country, rest on a roof rail, and leave before dusk.

For a city of engines, it is remarkably quiet. Most work happens behind thick doors, and the heavy machines turn slowly to keep the shelf from trembling. In the streets there is the tick of cooling pipework, the scrape of a handcart, a voice carried oddly far through the thin air. Sometimes, from somewhere underneath the whole district, comes one low metallic note. Nobody stops for it. The mountain is making room again.
