The city stands on a high limestone tableland so broad that its far districts disappear into their own weather. From the western rim, the roofs look like a second terrain laid over the first: long ranks of brick, pale concrete, settling towers, and raised roads following the old contours of the rock. Rain has worn shallow channels through both city and plateau. Some streets are stepped because the stone beneath them has slowly given way; some canals were once streets. Nothing looks recently broken. Everything has been repaired around its change of shape.

Water is seldom deep here, but it is everywhere. It beads under warm railings, runs down the seams of retaining walls, and gathers in the hollows of loading yards. The reservoirs are narrow and numerous, linked by aqueducts that cross entire neighborhoods. On clear mornings the sun reaches the lower streets; by afternoon, vapor rising from the works often makes a low ceiling over them. Beyond the last buildings, rain moves visibly across the bare stone in wide, unhurried bands.

There are no trees to soften the edges and almost no green at all. The exposed ground is chalk, dark flint, and rust-colored deposits left by the water. Yet the city is crowded with animals. Birds occupy every cornice and bridge brace, their calls passing from district to district ahead of the light. Small pale crabs work the drain mouths. Moths gather so thickly around the factory lamps that their wings give the light a trembling edge. In the canals, broad-backed fish wait beneath outfalls, while animals too small to see cloud the warm water.

The food that supports them comes largely from below. Deep pumps bring up mineral-rich water, and the city's oldest industry grows edible microbial cultures in covered, heated basins. Some are harvested and dried in great quantities. Some escape into the drains and reservoirs, where larvae and filter-feeders take them. The birds take those, and larger birds take the birds. People have tried screens, sealed conduits, and smooth walls; the animals have found room in each successive design. A pumping station without nests is usually a new one.

The machinery runs steadily rather than fast. Belts turn behind open windows. A freight engine sounds its horn from somewhere in the haze, and the note takes a long time to leave the streets. Under it are the smaller sounds that never quite stop: wings, water dripping from stone, the clatter of shells in a drain, work crews calling across the canals. The plateau, the buildings, and the animals have had centuries to wear one another into place. Even after heavy rain, when water shines on every surface, the city seems less transformed than briefly shown more clearly.
