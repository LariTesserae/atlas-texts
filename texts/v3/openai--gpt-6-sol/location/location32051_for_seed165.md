The valley lies above the last ordinary clouds. Its floor is a long, shallow basin of dark stone, open for miles between ridges worn into low, blunt teeth. From here, even the neighboring summits look used up: their cliffs have shed their sharp edges, and pale fans of rubble rest at their feet. Nothing suggests a recent fall. The stones have the settled look of things that found their places a very long time ago.

Cold moisture gathers where the rock folds inward. There are black beads of it beneath overhangs and narrow, silver-bottomed pools in cracks, but the broad floor is dry. A few wiry plants grow beside the pools, each with room enough around it to cast a separate shadow. Farther upslope, the only marks on the ground are rings of stones, low and widely spaced. They might once have sheltered someone from the wind. There is no trace of a road between them.

It is dim without seeming to be evening. The sky is a uniform gray, and the whole basin is loud. A continuous sound comes out of the ridges—something between a struck bell and a great sheet of rock being dragged slowly across another. It does not swell or fade. At the pools it is no softer; inside a stone ring it seems to come from below. A small pale animal, sometimes visible among the scree, lifts its head at other sounds but never at this one.

The far end of the valley can be seen clearly. A white notch in the ridge, a pool beneath it, and three standing stones are distinct even from miles away. Yet a person walking toward the notch finds the pool passing on one side, then on the other, without having crossed its trickle of water. Looking back does not restore the route. The rings of stones remain where they were, though their openings face a different direction from each new stretch of ground. People who have passed through the basin learn to choose a nearby rock and walk to that, then choose another. Distance is unreliable here; touch is not.

Nothing much changes while they travel. The beads beneath the overhangs do not fall. The loud note holds. Only the thin living things make their small allowances to time: a stem bent under frost, the pale animal gone when one looks again.
