The summit is so broad that it takes a while to understand it is a summit. The ground runs away in long, shallow swells, and beyond them there is no higher country—only distant ranges showing their tops through a low bank of cloud. Once, this may have been a landscape of sharp peaks. What remains is an old stone table, worn almost level, its exposed surfaces smooth enough to hold the sun’s warmth long after the air has changed.

The warmth is real, though it never stays still. Wind crosses the open ground in violent sheets. It presses the vegetation flat, lifts grit from bare patches, and then drops everything so abruptly that beads of condensation appear on the leaves. Rain comes in brief, slanting passages. By the time it has passed, much of the rock is dry again. Water lasts in narrow seams and shallow basins, where roots have gathered soil over centuries.

Those rooted places are startlingly green. Thick cushions of small leaves crowd the cracks; vines run between them, gripping stone rather than climbing anything tall. A whole slope may be lush at ankle height and nearly empty above it. Across the wider plateau, the patches lie far apart, each a little sheltered world with its own dark soil, insects, and smell of wet stems. There are no trees to soften the distances.

The noise makes distance difficult to judge. The wind strikes the edges of the plateau and pours through passages worn beneath it, producing a continuous, enormous tone. Where a narrow opening faces another, the two notes beat against each other until the rock seems to shudder. Loose leaves rattle without pause. On the rare quiet seconds, water can be heard ticking into a pool. Then the sound rises again and swallows it.

A few low shelters stand near the deeper basins: slabs leaned together, their joints packed with moss and clay. Paths between them are marked less by wear than by stones set upright at intervals, each one small enough to have been carried by hand. In one shelter, a shallow bowl has been carved into the floor beneath a drip. Nothing suggests a settlement. Someone comes here, or once did, knowing exactly where a little water will remain.

Small animals keep close to the green seams. You may glimpse one crossing open rock, belly low, before it vanishes under the leaves. Larger shapes are usually shadows cast by cloud. Even the shelters can seem to move when the light changes quickly, appearing and disappearing among the shallow rises.

The stone itself changes least. Its grain has been exposed for so long that the pale bands stand proud of the dark ones, and every loose piece that could be carried away has already gone. The weather is fierce enough to fill the whole plateau with motion, yet after it passes, the same old surfaces remain: warm under the hand, damp in their hollows, holding their few green places against the wind.
