The shelf is scarcely wider than a grove. It lies high on a warm mountainside, with an open drop on one side and a low wall of weathered stone on the other. Nothing here has been built. The stone has rounded shoulders and shallow hollows that fit the place so well they are easy to mistake for deliberate work, until you follow one curve into another and find no beginning to either.

After rain, water remains everywhere without gathering into a stream. It beads along the underside of ledges, darkens the seams in the rock, and rises cold through the soil when a foot presses down. The air is warm enough to make those little springs feel surprising. Moss grows thick in the hollows, and broad, glossy leaves spill out of a few deep cracks. Between them are generous reaches of bare stone. From the far edge, the shelf looks lush and almost continuous; standing on it, you must cross open spaces to get from one green patch to the next.

A low rushing sound fills those spaces. At first it seems to come from below the cliff, but it stays just as near when you walk toward the wall. Water is passing through hidden channels in the rock. Now and then a drop strikes an unseen pool with a sharp, hollow note. The notes do not keep time. They come often enough that, after a while, you begin to wait for one. Wind moves through the leaves and is almost lost beneath them.

There are few signs of animals. A pale moth rests under a leaf; something small has bitten a neat arc from its edge. No path crosses the moss. A person could take loose stone from the shelf and chip a useful edge without having to move more than a few steps, but nothing has been left here to show that anyone has.

Near the wall, one seam stays wet even through the driest part of the day. The plants beside it grow outward by such small degrees that their oldest leaves have dried in place against the stone. Water slips past those leaves into the dark. The hollow sound comes again, close behind the wall, and then the steady rushing covers it.
