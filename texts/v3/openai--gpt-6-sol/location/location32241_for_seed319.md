The city stands on a broad shelf above the valley, though from its streets the height is easy to forget. The ground falls away beyond the outer mills in a long succession of stone steps, each one carrying a shallow sheet of water. On clear days you can see the next ridge through the gaps between buildings. After rain, the valley disappears under a low white haze, while the streets up here remain warm and bright.

There are few trees. A handful grow in the old market squares, their roots confined by paving that has risen and cracked around them. Moss keeps to the seams of retaining walls; tough grass grows in the silt caught behind drain grilles. Most of the green is below street level, where water has had time to gather. From the bridges, the city looks almost bare: roofs, chimneys, pale cut rock, and the long dark lines of channels.

Water comes through the rock even when it has not rained for weeks. It beads on the faces of buildings, runs down stairwells, and enters the covered gutters with a sound like someone pouring grain. The oldest houses were built with narrow spaces behind their walls to let this seepage pass. Newer ones copied the practice. The builders did not try to make the shelf dry; they gave the water routes through it, and put wheels and pumps where those routes met.

This is why the city is so loud. Beneath nearly every district, water turns something. The large wheels drive the works at the eastern edge, where presses strike metal and belts slap against their housings. Smaller engines haul stone, lift freight, and keep the drains clear. Their separate noises merge into a continuous roar that fills the open squares as completely as it fills the workshops. Even in the upper streets, where no machinery is visible, the sound comes up through the paving. A dropped coin can be seen before it is heard.

People speak close to one another here. At crossings they use broad movements of the arm; at the factories, lamps signal a shift change. The bells on the tramcars are pitched low enough to be felt in the chest. Once, the city tried quieter wheels, but those wore badly in the damp and were replaced. There are still quiet rooms, though: thick-walled places for sleep, hearings, and music. Their doors are heavy, and people pause after entering them.

The shelf itself has changed little in a very long time. Its edges are rounded, its runnels deep but smooth, its loose stones long since carried into the valley. The city has taken on the same worn shape. Steps hollow at their centers. Iron rails shine where hands pass over them. In some lanes, generations of paving have been laid over older paving until doorways sit below the street. Repairs are constant, but rarely urgent. Water finds a route; a mason follows it.

At dusk, a few pale birds appear over the settling ponds. They cross the city without landing, visible for a moment against the smoke from the works. Lights come on floor by floor. The valley stays bright a little longer, and then its far side goes indistinct. By night the workers have gone home, the trams run less often, and the sound beneath the city is still immense. It has no clear beginning or end. In the morning, people open their doors into it as they always have.
