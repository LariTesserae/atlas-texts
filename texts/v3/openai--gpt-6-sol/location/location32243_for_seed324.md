The place is a narrow service recess near the top of a pumping station, just large enough for one person to turn around in. Its floor is ribbed steel, tipped slightly toward a drain. The walls are new concrete, still pale at their sharp corners, with dark seams where rain has found the formwork joints. Nothing underfoot is earth. Even the ledge beyond the rail is poured and bolted into place.

It is high enough that cloud sometimes enters through the open side of the recess. In those moments the opposite wall vanishes, though it is only a few paces away. Water beads on the handrail and runs down the backs of the electrical cabinets. A thin sheet crosses the floor, catches briefly at each raised rib, then disappears through the drain. In one sheltered seam, a few blades of grass have taken hold in collected grit. They lie flat whenever the pumps start.

The pumps are running now. Their sound is not a single roar but several forces too close together to separate: a deep motor note, the hammering of water in a pipe, a loose panel chattering against its frame. Speech would be useless here. Every few minutes a valve changes position; the rail shivers, the pitch drops, and water begins striking somewhere lower in the works with a heavier rhythm. Then the pumps climb back to speed.

A caged lamp makes a small yellow patch on the wet floor. Outside it, the concrete looks nearly black. There is a view through the rail, but little to see—fog, another tier of steelwork, and now and then a distant warning light. No birds cross the opening. The only movement is water, cloud, machinery, and the grass bending down and rising again.
