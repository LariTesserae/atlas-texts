At ground level, the plain is almost invisible. It lies beneath a warm thickness of roots, stems, and leaves, a few feet above a dark, compacted earth that is exposed only where something has recently torn away. There is no stone to speak of. The sharp edges here are botanical: a broken rib of wood still pale with sap, a new wall of tightly packed shoots, the clean lip of a hollow where a whole stand of plants has lifted itself out of the ground.

The place has the extent and close order of a city. Broad corridors run between living towers, then divide into narrower passages roofed by interlocking crowns. At their junctions, roots have swollen into terraces and knots of upright growth make enclosed courts. Nothing has been assembled or cut to a plan. Each passage is the space left by plants pressing against other plants, and its shape lasts only as long as they do. Far off, above the lower canopy, a row of tall green spires marks another district; by afternoon they may have leaned together and hidden it.

The air is warm, but not particularly wet. Rain comes in brief, hard visits. Most days the available water is held deep in the crowded root mat or appears as condensation inside shaded folds. Leaves turn their pale undersides toward any passing dampness. In the open corridors they feel dry and slightly hot; within the courts, droplets gather under the roofs and fall one at a time onto the floor of living moss.

The noise makes the scale difficult to judge. Stems under tension creak and release with reports like splitting timber. Whole ranks of leaves strike together in fast, irregular waves. Beneath that is a continuous coarse roar from roots shifting against roots, so loud that the small, separate sounds of dripping vanish in it. There are no wings, calls, or footsteps. Nothing moves through the greenery except other greenery, wind, and the occasional fall of a collapsing crown.

Movement is not confined to growth. A corridor visible straight ahead can be reached by turning twice, while walking toward it leads back into the court just left. This is most noticeable after a disturbance: a tower bends, its roots wrench up fresh soil, and the space beyond it changes before the tower falls. Nearby plants continue in the new arrangement without a seam. Sometimes an opening remains visible between two trunks even when their bark touches. Leaves pass into it and do not come out.

The plants keep growing into these errors. Shoots emerge from impossible openings with the same tender color as shoots in ordinary light. A court closes, another opens, and the roar rolls across the low plain. By evening the exposed earth has already sprouted a thin green cover, though its broken edge remains sharp beneath.
