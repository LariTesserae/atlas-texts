The shelf is no wider than a small courtyard. Beyond it, cloud hides the drop, though the cries of birds come up from far below. Underfoot, dark stone is still being made. It lies in broad, slightly swollen plates with seams as fine as hair. There are no worn steps, no channels, no gravel softened by travel. Even the loose fragments have clean, sharp faces.

It is warm here, high above the colder slopes. Moisture gathers constantly on the new rock. Drops bead along the seams, join into shallow pools, and spill over the edge in thin ropes. Water is plentiful but has had almost no time to shape the place. When a pool empties, it leaves a pale mineral ring; when rain passes, the shelf shines for hours afterward. Cloud keeps the light low enough that the wet surfaces reflect mostly moving gray.

A few tough grass fans grow where dust has caught beneath three-legged instruments. The instruments stand well apart, each with a dull metal body and three points held a finger’s width above the ground. Between them are short frames that might be shelters if they had roofs. No one is tending them now. Their lights pulse through the mist, and, in answer, a seam in the floor opens by a fraction. Stone rises into it from below, hot enough to steam where water touches it. The gap closes without crushing the beetles crossing it. They lift their feet, wait through the movement, and go on.

There are far more animals than plants. Birds crowd the edge and launch themselves into the updrafts. Small pale creatures hunt among the instrument legs, taking the flies drawn to the warm pools; the flies, in turn, gather so thickly at times that a puddle appears to have a dark skin. Something larger calls from inside the cloud and receives several sharp answers from below. The shelf is seldom quiet. Wings beat against the air, water ticks from ledge to ledge, and every few minutes a deep note sounds under the stone. A chorus of cries follows it, then gradually settles.

After each note, the shelf is a little wider. The newest part has no grass, no mineral rings, not even a footprint. Birds land on it before the steam has cleared, testing the surface with quick, cautious steps. Behind them, water begins to collect in the first shallow hollow.
