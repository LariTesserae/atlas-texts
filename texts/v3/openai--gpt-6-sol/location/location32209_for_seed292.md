The lake lies higher than most weather. From its shore, the surrounding mountains do not look sharp so much as worn down to their bones: long gray shoulders of stone, broad saddles, slopes rounded by ice that vanished thousands of years ago. Water fills the shallow country between them. On a clear day you can see one pale ridge after another across it; on a windy day the far shore disappears behind spray.

Most of the landscape is below the surface. Sunlight reaches a long way through the cold water, enough to show beds of pondweed and water-milfoil bending in the current. Their stems grow thick around old stream channels and among cobbles polished smooth as eggs. Fish hold in the lee of boulders, then dart out to take insects washed from the shore. Farther down, the plants thin and the lake floor becomes a succession of low stone shelves, each one worn almost level before it was drowned. Even there, silt is seldom still. It lifts in pale clouds whenever a current turns.

The place is loudest at the surface. Wind drives waves over one another until they break against the rock in short, hard bursts. Gravel rattles in the shallows. Gulls call from the far bank, difficult to locate through the rush of water. At one end of the lake, where the valley narrows, a concrete intake stands alone beneath the cliffs. Its screens shudder with weeds; beyond them pumps and turbines make a steady low sound that can be felt through the water before it can be heard. A service track and a few small buildings are the only other marks of the works.

The machinery draws from the lake, rivers pour into it, and storms push whole masses of water from one shore to the other. After heavy rain, a brown plume travels out from each river mouth, carrying branches and stones into water that was clear the day before. Reeds are torn loose and gather against the intake; fish move into the new currents; waves strip a beach down to bedrock and pile its gravel elsewhere. The mountains hardly seem to change at all. Their age is in every smooth surface underfoot, while above them the water is busy remaking its shores.
