The place is no larger than a room, though it has no roof. Daylight falls into a square of open air between old concrete walls. Most of the floor is bare: a shallow, level sheet of water over smooth aggregate, with enough space around it to make the room feel strangely vacant. At the edges, greenery crowds the joints—ferns rooted in maintenance troughs, moss on the wet brick, long stems leaning out from behind a pipe. Nothing grows from earth here. Even the dark material in the troughs was carried in.

The concrete has lost its corners. Decades of overflow have rounded every lip and worn pale tracks down the walls; iron rails have rusted thin where hands once touched them. It is a made place worn into the softness of a riverbed. Warm air holds close to the water. Drops gather beneath the pipework and fall without making a sound anyone can hear.

Behind one wall, pumps run at a fixed speed. Their roar fills the little court so completely that the leaves seem to tremble in silence. Water enters through a low grille, crosses the floor too slowly to notice, and leaves through another. The level never appears to rise or fall. A single small bird sometimes visits the upper rail, opens its beak, and departs unheard. Mostly there is the empty middle, the green perimeter, and the steady work of keeping water exactly where it is.
