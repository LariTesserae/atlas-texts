At first it looks like a plain after rain. Water stands in long, shallow sheets, and the dark ground runs level to a low horizon. Then the straight lines become apparent: seams at regular intervals, rows of iron caps, a curb so worn it rises only a finger’s width above the water. There is no soil beneath the puddles. The plain is a floor, built in sections too large to take in from where you stand.

It has been here a very long time. The concrete has lost its corners and much of its aggregate. Rails lie flush with it, polished by water into dull parallel strokes. Farther out, the remains of loading bays and walls have been reduced to low steps; from a distance they look like changes in the land rather than buildings. The tallest things are scattered pump housings, squat and windowless, with pipes entering one side and leaving the other. Nothing here has collapsed dramatically. It has worn down in place.

A cold mist gathers a few feet above the floor. Rain has stopped, but water keeps arriving. It beads on cable sheaths, seeps up through expansion joints, and spills steadily from holes in the sides of the old housings. Along one channel it moves fast enough to show a silver edge; across most of the plain it barely moves at all. Sparse grass grows in a few cracks where grit has collected. Its blades lie flat under the weight of droplets. There are no birds above it, no insects rising when you step close.

The noise makes the emptiness difficult to judge. Somewhere beneath the floor, enormous machinery runs without pause. Its low, hard roar comes through your feet as well as your ears. At intervals a valve opens with a report that carries across the plain, followed by a rush of water through hidden pipework. Other valves answer at different distances. The mist shakes on a nearby railing. Between those bursts, smaller sounds become audible: drips from a gantry, a loose plate tapping, water drawing air down a drain.

A few lamps still work. They mark distant intersections with weak amber pools, too far apart to light the way between them. In their glow, the floor can be seen sloping toward a broad channel that crosses the plain. The slope is almost imperceptible. Over years, anything loose makes its way there: grit, flakes of rust, fragments from the housings. At the channel’s edge, grates catch the larger pieces while the finer material goes down with the water.

The pumps have kept running as the structures above them have thinned and settled. They draw water from under the plain and send it across the surface to be collected again. Each passage carries away a little more of what was built. The floor is nearly flat now, and the water has become very good at finding the few remaining differences.
