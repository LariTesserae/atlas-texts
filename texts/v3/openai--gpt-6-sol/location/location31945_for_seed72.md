The street ends at a wall that has not yet become a wall. Its lower half is cut rock, pale and rough where the boring machine left it; above that, fresh concrete holds the marks of timber forms. Water beads on both surfaces. It gathers along the seam, drops into a narrow gutter, and travels past shuttered workshops toward a sump at the far end of the block.

This is a small district beneath a much larger city. There is room for one lane, two rows of buildings, a pumping station, and a square scarcely wider than a loading bay. Pipes cross overhead so low that a tall person ducks beneath them. The air is hot from the buried machinery, though most of the engines are idle. A single pump starts every few minutes. Its first stroke shakes the railings; after that, its beat joins the steadier hiss of steam escaping a valve somewhere out of sight.

Light comes from workshop windows and a few lamps behind wire cages. It reaches the wet pavement but leaves the upper parts of the buildings obscure. The place can seem empty until something moves. Moths crowd the lamps. Cockroaches part around a boot and close together again behind it. In the gutter, tiny fish hold themselves against the current from the pump; rats wait where the gutter passes under a door. Bats occupy the unfinished recesses above the street. Their dry calls run across the ceiling almost continuously, audible even through the machinery.

There is little for anything to grow on. A line of moss follows one leaking pipe, and a fern has rooted in a crack beside the sump. The animals live instead on what the district brings down: grain spilled from sacks, scraps from kitchens, insects drawn to heat and water. At night, when fewer carts pass, they take up more of the street.

Nothing here has had time to wear smooth. The rock still shows fresh cuts, and the square's newest curb has a sharp edge under its film of condensation. Yet the water has already found a route through the district. Each drop takes its place in the gutter; each pump stroke sends it onward. Above, traffic crosses the city. Below, the street is being made slowly, by workers who are nearly finished and by water that has only begun.
