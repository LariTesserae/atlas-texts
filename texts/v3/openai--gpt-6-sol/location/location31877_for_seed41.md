The hollow lies beneath a shelf of warm limestone. From above, it looks like a crack half-hidden by grass; below, it opens into a chamber no wider than a small grove. The floor slopes toward a shallow basin, and the walls have been worn into rounded ledges and pockets. Nothing here has a sharp edge for long. Even the few stones set one upon another beside the basin have softened under mineral crust and root.

Rain reaches the hollow in short-lived streams. It slips down the entrance and through seams in the ceiling, darkening the rock, filling prints and little hollows, then disappearing again. Most days there is no standing water beyond a few shaded cups in the stone. The air keeps what the ground loses: heat and a close, leafy dampness. Drops gather on the undersides of roots and fall long after the rain has stopped.

Plants occupy nearly every place that will hold them. Ferns crowd the lower ledges; vines descend from the opening and turn sideways along the wall; pale shoots rise through drifts of old leaves where a little soil has collected. Near the entrance the foliage is thick and green. Deeper in, it thins into moss, roots, and the broad leaves of plants angled toward the faint light from above. Through that opening, for a brief part of the day, the sun finds one patch of floor. Small animals move into its warmth before it passes on.

At first the chamber seems still. Then a leaf shifts without a breeze. Tiny lizards hunt among the stones, beetles work through fallen fruit, and pale insects shelter in the cracks. A frog waits near the basin even when it is dry. The place is full of movement kept close to the ground: feeding, hiding, shedding, beginning again. Its sounds rarely carry beyond the rim—a drop striking stone, a soft scramble in the leaves, the brief brush of wings against the roof.
