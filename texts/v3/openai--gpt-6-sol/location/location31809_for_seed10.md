The plateau has almost no skyline. Its ridges have been worn down to long, low swells, and beyond them are more swells, fading into a light too weak to tell morning from evening. Water sits everywhere without gathering into a lake. It shines in the seams of the ground, beads on every stem, and lifts as a thin vapor from hollows that have no apparent warmth. Now and then a gust drives rain across the plateau. In the cold, the drops sting.

From a distance the country looks bare. The plants grow close to the ground, leaving great reaches of gray-green open space. Up close, there is no ground beneath them. The surface is a dense mat of roots and old leaves, compressed over ages into something as firm as peat and as springy as a living branch. The few exposed stones are rounded and small. Water has had too long with them.

At wide intervals, a taller growth rises out of the mat: a single pale stalk, sometimes twenty body-lengths high, carrying a narrow crown of black leaves. Each crown turns slowly against the wind. Far below, the roots transmit the motion through chambers full of water, and the plateau answers with a deep, uneven thudding. Other sounds join it—the rattle of seed cases, a high electrical whine between wet leaf tips, the rush of water through passages underfoot. Across the empty reaches, the separate pulses almost find a common rhythm. Then one stalk shudders, the rhythm breaks, and its crown begins turning the other way.

There are animals everywhere, though the eye keeps finding space instead of bodies. Small white grazers move inside the root mat; their progress shows as a traveling tremor. Birds with broad, soaked wings wait along the stalks for the tremors to reach open water. In the hollows, long dark creatures surface to breathe and vanish into depths that ought not to fit beneath a handspan of water. Their calls carry a great distance. A call heard ahead may be answered from behind, by the same animal still visible ahead.

The older parts of the plateau are the least dependable. Rain falls there into pools that have not yet appeared. A root torn free by a grazer may leave its shape in place for hours, a clear hollow held upright in the air while water runs through it. When the wind changes, whole bands of vegetation lean toward a ridge that is no longer there. The animals pause at these changes, then go on feeding. They know the places where the surface will hold, and the places where it will only seem to.

As the dimness deepens, the scattered stalks stand farther apart than they did before. Their turning can still be heard across the intervening ground, along with wings beating and water forcing its way through the roots. Nothing on the plateau keeps its shape for very long except its low, ancient outline. Even that disappears each time the mist rises.
