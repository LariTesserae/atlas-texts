The plain begins at the level of your feet and continues, almost without rising, beyond the distance at which its details ought to disappear. It is warm under a clear, ordinary-looking sky. The ground is black and pale gold in abrupt patches: fresh mineral plates, fine dust, seams with edges too sharp to have spent a day in wind. Nothing here has the softened look of age.

Growth crowds every seam. Broad, translucent fronds overlap in tiers; dark filaments climb through them and spread into canopies no higher than a person’s head. From a distance they make the plain look richly green. Up close, their colour comes from buried layers that catch the sun at different angles. The fronds are dry and supple, without sap, dew, or any scent of soil. They take light into patterned mineral veins and pass charge from leaf to leaf where their edges touch. A whole thicket is one continuous, quiet exchange. There is no water to seek beneath it. The fine dust goes down as far as anything has opened it.

Between the thickets lie wide bare stretches, so the eye can travel across miles of low country before another crowded stand interrupts it. Some stands appear to be behind the horizon and in front of it at once. Walking toward one does not resolve the mistake. Its nearest leaves become distinct while its far edge remains at the same impossible distance. Elsewhere, a narrow crack in the ground shows a bright slice of sky rather than darkness. These faults are not violent. They hold their shapes as faithfully as the mineral seams.

The place has no roads, walls, or discarded things. Yet the intricate regularity of a fallen leaf hints at knowledge beyond any visible maker: its veins divide, reunite, and divide again at scales too small to see, maintaining the same exchange even where one part of the leaf seems to occupy two positions. Whatever arranged this world did not leave machinery in it. The arrangement is the machinery.

A small animal is sometimes visible under the fronds, flat-bodied and the colour of dust. It feeds by resting against a vein. It makes no call, and when it leaves, the leaves do not sway. Mostly there is only a faint ticking from the mineral plates, too intermittent to become a rhythm, and the dry touch of foliage against itself.

Everything suggests a beginning—the clean breaks, the sharp margins, the abundance pressing into every available gap. Nothing suggests what happened after. A loose grain poised on the lip of a crack does not fall. The warmth does not fade toward evening. The thickets remain lush without visibly growing. The plain keeps the freshness of a place just made, though no one standing in it can find a moment at which it becomes older.
