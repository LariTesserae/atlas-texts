The town stands beneath a mountain lake. From above, in the clear part of the day, it looks like a few dark stitches in a vast green cloth. Below the surface, the cloth resolves into rooted water plants: long, narrow leaves rising from a deep mat of stems and silt. They cover the lakebed and climb the slopes of an old drowned ridge. Where a leaf has fallen away, another grows through its place. There is almost no bare ground.

The inhabited part occupies one shoulder of the ridge, no larger than a grove. Its lanes are open water, broad enough to let two swimmers pass without touching the greenery on either side. Along them stand low houses made from bent living stems, lashed to stone footings with fiber. Their walls admit a little light and a little current. Steps cut into the rock have lost their sharp edges; roots hold the worn stone together more securely than the original workmanship did. At the center is a clear round space where people set down baskets of gathered shoots and work with bone knives, wooden wedges, and smooth stones kept for pounding fiber. Nothing here was built quickly. A doorway takes several seasons to train into shape.

Beyond the lanes, the water opens. Sunlight reaches this high lake readily, though it comes down cool and green, and the distant plants appear pale behind it. Fish move everywhere—small shoals among the upper leaves, heavier solitary bodies near the foundations—but there is room around each movement. Snails graze the house walls. A long-legged crustacean picks at the knots of a neglected lashing. In the deeper spaces, broad shadows pass slowly enough that the little fish have time to fold themselves into the stems.

The town is louder than it looks. Sound carries through the water from one end of the ridge to the other: the knock of a stone tool, the hollow beat of someone calling from inside a house, the rasp of fish feeding, the continuous dry-sounding crackle of tiny animals in the plant beds. The water itself scarcely moves. A spring rises through a fissure below the square, cool enough to feel against the skin, and its gentle flow keeps the lanes clear. The people weed only those passages and the spaces before their doors. Elsewhere, they let the lake close over what it can.
