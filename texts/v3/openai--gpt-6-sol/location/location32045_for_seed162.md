The plateau is still rising. Along its rim, slabs of black stone stand at angles too steep to have survived a long season of rain. Their edges are clean and pale where they have split, and warm enough to dry a hand placed against them. Far below lies a cloud layer; above, another hangs so low that the tallest leaves vanish into it. Between the two is a dim, close world with the breadth of a continent.

Rain comes rarely. More often, moisture gathers on the undersides of leaves and in the seams of the rock. Ferns with trunks as thick as trees grow from those seams, their fronds overlapping until the plateau seems roofed over. Beneath them are mats of bright moss, dry gravel, and hollows where a little warm water lasts for days. The greenery is dense, but it has gaps: long raw strips where the ground opened yesterday and roots have not yet crossed.

From a distance, the cliffs appear to be lined with factories. Columns of vapor rise at regular intervals. A low vibration passes through the stone. In the openings below the cliffs, huge black shells turn against one another, squeezing mineral-rich sap from the roots that descend into them. There are no buildings, pipes, or worked parts. The shells grow their own bearings, shed worn teeth, and take in new roots as the plateau spreads. At night, current travels through the wet veins of the vegetation in brief, branching flashes. The whole process smells of hot iron and crushed leaves.

Animals live among its moving parts. Small, six-winged things cling to the shell rims and pick at what the roots leave behind. Herds of narrow-bodied grazers move through the fern shade, stepping over fresh cracks with practiced care. Something much larger calls from the cloud below, but the call is usually felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard. For all its life and motion, the plateau is quiet. Vapor lifts without a hiss. Even a rockfall may produce only the sight of fragments dropping away, its noise arriving much later from somewhere beside you.

The distances are less reliable than the animals’ paths. A cleft seen across a valley may be reached in an hour by following a herd, or remain on the horizon through a day’s walking toward it. New ridges sometimes rise with old growth already on them: moss, insect burrows, the exposed roots of trees whose trunks stand elsewhere. A storm can move through one fern grove again and again while the neighboring grove stays dry. The animals pause at its boundary, wait for the rain to pass, and feed where the drops have collected.

By morning, a ledge that held a pool may have tilted and emptied it into the clouds. The ferns lean after the retreating damp. Their roots tighten in the new cracks. Far off, another pale line appears in the stone, sharp enough to see across miles of shade, and the shells beneath it begin to turn.
