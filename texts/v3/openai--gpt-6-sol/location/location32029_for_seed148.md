The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few streets. From its rim, the first thing visible is an empty square: a broad, black space between low rows of buildings, with no carts, people, or trees to break it. Only when the eye adjusts does the square reveal itself as leaf. It is one immense leaf, stretched flat from wall to wall, its veins making the faint ridges of roads.

Everything here grows. The walls are packed stems with brick-shaped joints; roofs are overlapping scales that close against rain. Narrow chimneys rise from the workshops, though they have no masonry in them. Their skins are greenish and cool, and each bears a ring of old soot where a furnace mouth once opened. The machinery remains: presses, belts, flywheels, rows of small iron rollers. Roots have threaded the bearings without stopping them. Every few minutes a wheel turns a quarter revolution, slowly enough to watch, and somewhere under the square a belt takes up the motion.

It is cool at ground level, with the smell of wet bark and metal filings. Rain comes briefly and without much force. It gathers in the cups of roof scales and in hollows at the street corners; within an hour most of it has been drawn down. Between rains, beads of condensation stand on the iron rollers. Water is scarce enough that each drop acquires a little procession. Moths with folded, leaflike wings arrive first, then pale beetles, then the long-legged animals that eat the beetles. Small bodies are everywhere once one knows where to look: under the lips of doors, along the vein-roads, hanging motionless beneath the presses. A hunting thing the size of a cat crosses the square by slipping from one leaf vein into another. Nothing announces itself. Even the abundant wings make little sound.

There are no worn paths. New ones swell up overnight as the great leaf divides its veins; yesterday’s crossings remain beside them, still green, leading into walls. At the basin’s edge, the ground has scarcely begun to be ground. Soft ridges lift beneath the root mat, and fine grit appears in their folds, clean and sharp as if freshly made. After rain, a few grains travel downhill. By morning the ridges have risen again.

The town is darkest where its windows should help. A workshop window looks into the square from the outside and into a dense, sunlit thicket from within; through its open frame a moth can be seen moving on either side at once. The chimneys cast shadows before they turn. On some nights, a roof closes against rain that has not yet begun, and its drops are found in the cups by morning. These departures cause no visible alarm. The beetles drink, the rollers turn, and the wide leaf holds the town apart.
