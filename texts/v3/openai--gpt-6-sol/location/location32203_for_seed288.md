Beneath the plain is a hollow broad enough to have neighborhoods. Its roof has thinned with age, opening in long, irregular cracks through which a gray daylight falls. The floor is nearly level. Whatever ridges and rubble once interrupted it have been worn down to low shoulders of stone, and the old streets run between them without steps or sharp corners. From a rise at the edge, you can see rows of walls continuing into the dimness, with broad empty spaces between districts.

It is a dry city. There are no channels, wells, or marks of a former waterline. Its buildings are made of fitted stone, their doorways still recognizable, though many roofs have settled onto the floors beneath them. No lamps or mechanisms remain. Dust has softened the carved edges until a threshold feels much like the natural rock beside it. In places it is hard to tell whether a passage was made by people or merely kept open by them.

Green growth gathers wherever a roof crack gives it light. Tough-leaved shrubs stand in the courtyards; pale vines thread the masonry, and low mats of plants spread over floors that hold a little coolness. They grow slowly and far apart, leaving whole streets bare. Their leaves are thick and narrow, their roots deep in the seams. Nothing here looks freshly watered. Even the green has a dusty surface.

The sound is the first thing a visitor cannot account for. Wind crossing the cracks overhead sets the hollow sounding: a deep, sustained roar from one quarter, a thin whistle from another, and a constant clatter where dry seed cases knock against stone. Under it is the noise of animals. Small birds nest high in the broken roofs, insects rasp in the vines, and pale lizards scatter through the loose joints of the walls. At times their calls gather into a din so loud that footsteps disappear inside it. Then a shift in the air moves the roar to another district, while the streets themselves remain still.

Nothing changes quickly here. A wall may lean for centuries before it falls. A root may spend as long finding its way through a single course of stone. The city has become part of the hollow’s terrain, and the hollow, slowly, has taken on the shape of the city.
