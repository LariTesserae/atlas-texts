The valley lies so high that the nearer ridges seem level with the sky. At noon it is fiercely hot. Light strikes the pale stone from above and rebounds from below, finding every patch of exposed skin. Yet in the shade of a boulder the air is cool, and before dawn a skin of ice sometimes forms on the water caught in old drill holes.

From the pass, the valley looks open. On the ground it is difficult to find a straight way through. Weathered fins of rock stand shoulder to shoulder across the floor, with rounded heaps of scree packed between them. They have been here long enough to lose their sharpest edges. Dark seams cross the stone at irregular intervals; where a seam opens, it may hold a spoonful of water or a narrow strip of damp grit. There is no river to follow, only the signs of one: a polished chute, silt tucked beneath an overhang, reeds no taller than a hand at the foot of a cliff.

A few machines occupy this crowded country. An electric haul line runs from the far ridge to a sorting shed, its cables almost invisible until a loaded bucket passes against the sky. Small pumps, each under a metal awning, draw from cracks deep in the rock. Their pipes take long routes around the fins and vanish into gravel. The shed and the pumps are far apart, with no town between them; from most places only one human-made thing can be seen at a time. The motors run slowly in the thin air. A bucket crossing the valley makes a soft click at a pulley, then the quiet closes behind it.

There are more animals than the scant growth first suggests. Lizard-like creatures crowd the warm faces of the rocks and slip out of sight at a footstep. Small birds work the cracks for insects. Higher up, heavy-footed grazers pick their way across ledges where a person would want both hands free, feeding on dry cushions rooted in the stone. Every pocket of moisture has its visitors. At a seep beside the sorting shed, tracks lie so thick over one another that the mud has no clean surface. The animals come at different hours; they seldom need to meet.

By late afternoon, heat rising from the valley begins to pull air over the passes. Condensation beads on the shaded pipework, and clouds assemble above the ridges without reaching the floor. Occasionally rain does come: brief, hard drops that wake the smell of mineral dust and send water flashing along the old chutes. For an hour the valley is full of movement—creatures drinking, grit shifting, pumps taking in what the rock will yield. Then the stone dries from the top down. The dark seams remain dark a little longer, and the birds keep returning to them after everything else appears dry.
