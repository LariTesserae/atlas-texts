The town fits inside a bend of the bank. From the far side of the stream it looks like a single low, gray object, too tightly fitted to show streets: roof against roof, wall against wall, with a few pale strips of sky caught between them. Up close, those strips become passages just wide enough for one person carrying a basket sideways.

The bank is new. Last season’s flood laid down a shelf of blue silt and broken shale, and the stream has already begun cutting its edge away. Its little cliffs are crisp, without grass or weathering to soften them. Water stands in footprints. It beads on the dark stone and gathers beneath doors, though the rain stopped yesterday. The air is cool enough that every clay jar sweats.

Houses are made from what the bank provides: shale set on edge, fistfuls of gravel pressed into mud walls, roofs of bundled rushes carried from the thin marsh upstream. There is scarcely a straight wall. Each one braces another, and where two lean together someone has fitted a shelf, a sleeping recess, a covered place for drying fish. The town has no open square. Its widest space is the landing, where three people can stand without touching.

A shallow gutter runs through every passage. The gutters meet under the landing and empty into the stream through a row of stone mouths. They must be cleared each morning. Silt settles in them overnight, and if a mouth clogs, water finds a doorway instead. The people here say that a house is finished only while someone is tending it. Fresh clay is always being thumbed into a seam; roof bundles are lifted and retied; a step that stood firm at dawn sinks a finger’s width by evening.

For all this work, the place is quiet. Water slips through the gutters rather than rushing. Clay takes the weight of footsteps. Now and then there is the click of a hand tool against shale, the scrape of a basket along a wall, a brief call from the landing asking for room to pass. Beyond the houses, one long-legged bird works the stream margin. Rushes grow in scattered knots where the bank has held long enough for roots to take. Most of the new ground is bare.

The town is packed so closely because only this bend remains above the ordinary rise of the stream. From a roof, you can see how little separates it from the wet flats: a narrow lip of stone, a few steps down, then shining mud. In the afternoon light the walls show every shade of dampness. By morning some of those marks will have moved, and someone will know where to press the clay.
