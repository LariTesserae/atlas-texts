The place is a maintenance recess near the top of a manufactured spire. You reach it by opening a narrow door in a wall of machinery; beyond the threshold is a floor of ribbed metal, perhaps six paces long, ending at a waist-high guardrail. There is no exposed stone anywhere. Even the apparent cliff beyond the rail is a stack of fitted panels, descending out of sight through bright, cold air.

Everything here has an edge. Bolt heads are clean, the stamped numbers on the floor have not worn smooth, and the seams between panels are fine enough to catch a fingernail but not dust. The place looks newly assembled, though nothing about it suggests that anyone has worked here recently. A white work lamp burns over the door. Daylight reaches the rail from above, leaving two crisp shadows of every upright part.

It is bitterly cold. Touching the rail through a glove draws the warmth from your fingers. The air is too dry to make frost, and the shallow channels cut into the floor have never carried liquid. They lead instead to screened slots where fine mineral powder can be taken away by suction. Each slot is spotless.

From the outer wall comes a noise so great that, at first, it seems to have no source. It is the combined roar of engines housed throughout the spire, transmitted into this little recess through beams and fasteners. The guardrail trembles against your palm. You cannot hear your boots on the floor, or your own voice, although the engines’ note does not rise, fall, or pulse. After a while you can distinguish several tones inside it: a deep pressure in the chest, a thin metallic whine, and a steady rush like grit poured without end.

There are four patches of growth along the inner seam. Each is a small fan of black, stiff fronds, spaced well apart from the others. Their roots grip the heads of electrical contacts rather than the floor. They take what they need from the current leaking through the wall and from trace metals in the air; they have no use for water. Under the lamp their upper edges show a dull copper color. They do not bend in the vibration. Nothing visits them, and there are no tracks, nests, or other signs of animal life.

A plate beside the door gives instructions for replacing a bearing somewhere far below. Its diagram includes this recess only as a narrow rectangle marked **CLEARANCE**. Standing inside it, you understand why it was left empty. The engines require a little space around their casing, the door requires room to swing, and a person requires somewhere to put their feet. The fronds have occupied only the seam that neither machine nor person uses.

Far below the guardrail, the panels continue down into the glare. The spire is running, and has been running for a very long time. Here at its height, the sound remains exactly the same.
