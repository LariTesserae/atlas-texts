The town occupies one high saddle, no wider than a few streets, between two faces of newly broken black stone. Beyond its last wall the ground falls steeply into cloud. From the middle of the square you can see every roof, though not always at once: fern fronds lean over the lanes, and pale curtains of lichen hang from the lintels. The light is the colour of ash left in a hearth.

The stone is younger than the town. A ridge rose through its older foundations within living memory, splitting paving slabs and leaving narrow edges that have not yet softened underfoot. The inhabitants have built around the breaks rather than over them. Walls of hand-set rock step up and down across the saddle; little plank bridges cross fissures too narrow to descend into. Doorways stand at several heights along the same lane. There is an urban order to it—houses, terraces, store sheds, a square with a cistern—but the order has had to bend.

For most of the day the square is dry. The cistern holds water only after rain, and its bottom is often visible through a few dark inches. Yet the town grows thickly. Moss pads the north faces of walls. Ferns fill abandoned stairwells. Vines run from roof to roof on cords the residents have left for them, making green ceilings over stretches of empty street. At dusk, cold air begins to rise from the cracks. It beads on the undersides of leaves and along the bottoms of the bridges. Water drips steadily for an hour or two, collected in bowls set beneath the best places. By morning the stone looks dry again.

The animals know the hour more precisely than the people do. Small, flat lizards emerge first and place themselves beneath the bridges with their mouths open to the drops. Pale moths crowd the vine ceilings. Birds descend from the cloud and walk rather than fly among the roofs, picking insects out of the wet moss. There are so many bodies that a wall can appear to shift while you look at it. In the square, however, there is room to cross without touching anything. The creatures keep to edges, ledges, undersides, and the old routes worn into the town by generations of feet and claws.

Almost none of them call. You hear a wing brush a leaf, a claw tick once against stone, the thin fall of water into a clay bowl. Far below, the cloud occasionally lets through a low sound like a cart being dragged across gravel. The townspeople say it is the ridge still rising. They do not build across a crack that has begun to widen, and they leave the small plank bridges loose enough to lift away.

Near the highest house, a fresh seam has opened through an old garden bed. Its cut is clean; roots hang from one side, and the other side shows dark stone that has never seen daylight before this week. Already a bright moss is taking hold in the sheltered depth. At dusk, when the cold breath starts, insects gather there in such numbers that the opening seems to have a soft lining. A hunting bird waits above them on the garden wall. Below the bird, someone has placed a new bowl.
