Behind the freight shed, where the concrete apron meets its corrugated wall, there is a gap no wider than a hand. From above it looks packed with grit. Kneel, and the grit resolves into a crowded little country: sharp chips of new concrete, glass from a broken gauge, washers standing on edge, and ridges of rust-colored sand pressed against the wall. The crack opened this spring when a delivery truck struck the apron. Its edges are still pale and angular. Nothing here has had time to become smooth.

The ground is low, nearly level with the river beyond the sheds, though the river never reaches it. Moisture arrives by smaller routes. A pipe sweats through the warm afternoon; after rain, water travels along a bolt thread and falls, one drop at a time, into a pool held inside an old bearing. By morning the pool may be gone. A few blades of grass have found the deeper seams, but most of the green is a thin film on the shaded side of the wall.

There seems to be no empty surface. Tiny pale spiders keep to the concrete face. Woodlice shelter beneath a curled label, and flies with metallic backs visit the bearing when it fills. Ants pass through them all, carrying crumbs from the workers’ lunch and flecks of orange corrosion. Once, a narrow-tailed lizard wedged itself into the gap and remained there for an hour, hunting without room to turn around. When it left, everything that had hidden from it came out again.

The shed’s cooling machinery gives the place its weather. At irregular intervals a fan starts behind the wall. Its first vibration shakes dust from a seam; then a warm breath moves through the crack, drying one side while the pipe continues to wet the other. Loose iron filings gather around the washers in delicate black arcs. The ants cross those arcs without difficulty, but their tracks bend with them, so that for a few hours the traffic appears to follow lines drawn by something buried below.

In ordinary daylight the gap is bright enough to see into, and quiet enough to hear a drop strike the bearing. Forklifts work on the other side of the wall. Down here their engines arrive chiefly as tremors, briefly setting the glass, the water, and hundreds of small legs in motion.
