The bottom is not quite flat, though from a distance it appears to be. Long, low ridges cross it in parallel, with broad basins between them. Each ridge is a wall of fitted stone worn down until its edges can scarcely be distinguished from the silt caught in its joints. Beneath the silt are more walls, paved channels, steps too shallow now to deserve the name. The construction continues past sight in every direction. There is no exposed rock and no shore: only water above, and this old made floor below.

It is cold here. A little daylight comes through the water, enough to show the nearer ridges as darker bands, but it seldom reaches their bases. Kelp rises from beds of packed stone and clay into the dimness overhead. Its upper leaves gather what light there is; below them, long stems hang almost motionless among thick growths of moss and red weed. The planting has spread across the joins of the works without hiding their arrangement. From above, perhaps, the basins would still look like basins.

Nothing swims through the leaves. There are no shells fastened to the masonry, no tracks in the soft places. A frond occasionally touches another with a faint, dry-seeming scrape carried strangely far by the water. Otherwise the place is quiet. The current is weak enough that a loosened grain may settle close to where it fell.

Whoever laid the stones worked without great machines. Individual blocks show the uneven marks of tools, and the beds are edged with stakes and woven barriers that have been replaced, or grown through, by roots. It must have taken many hands and a very long time. It has had longer still to wear down. The highest walls have slumped to the same gentle height; fallen courses fill the channels they once bordered. Yet the pattern holds, immense and low under the dark water, with its green canopy drawing slowly upward from a floor that is still, everywhere, built.
