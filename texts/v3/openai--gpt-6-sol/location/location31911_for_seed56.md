The hollow is no larger than a grove, though it is difficult to find its edges. Roots descend from a ceiling lost behind leaves; other roots rise from the floor, and between them hang soft, dark sheets of living tissue. There is almost no bare ground. Where a boot might land, pale moss gives way to a mat of fine roots, then to another mat still forming beneath it. The place is below the surface, but it feels less like a cave than the inside of something that has not finished growing.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a pool. It beads along leaf ribs, gathers at the tips of hanging roots, and runs in narrow seams under the moss. A clear drop can remain suspended for minutes, swelling slowly while other drops pass through it. The air smells of wet bark and cold stone, though the stone is hard to find. Now and then a dark, raw patch shows through the plants: new mineral, smooth and unmarked, as if the walls have only lately arrived.

The cold is steady. Thin ice forms on the outer edges of leaves but leaves the veins beneath them supple. Small animals shelter in those veins. Silver things with too many jointed feet move through the water seams; white, soft-bodied grazers travel in crowded lines across the undersides of fronds. Deeper in the green, something larger shifts its weight, and a whole curtain of leaves shivers without opening enough to show it. None of these creatures is quiet. There is a constant loud rasp of feeding, the ticking of ice under tension, and a low, many-throated pulse from somewhere in the root beds. Each sound continues so regularly that it takes time to hear it as a sound at all.

Dim light passes from plant to plant. It begins as a blue point in one translucent stem, goes out there, and appears in the next. Sometimes the points move against the direction of the water. A droplet touched by that light may freeze in its middle while its skin remains liquid; a root may withdraw before anything brushes it. No wires or worked surfaces can be seen. The chamber seems to perform its most intricate operations in leaves, in water films, in the tiny spaces between adjacent living cells.

It is a young place. The floor still rises by imperceptible increments, lifting fallen leaves that have not yet decayed. Roots press into soft rock, then ease away, leaving hollows too fresh to have collected silt. The slow changes are hard to catch, but the inhabitants appear to know them: grazers abandon a frond hours before it folds; the hidden animal moves whenever a new seam of water opens. For all its crowded life, the hollow has room to become something else. It keeps growing inward, making a little more shelter out of the cold.
