In the high slopes of the great mountain range, mist billows and swirls through ancient granite peaks and spires. Water is ever-present, even if never quite visible. Rivulets gurgle from springs between boulders, seeping into saturated soils. Cliffs and walls remain perpetually damp, drizzled by clouds that pause at this altitude. Ferns and moss clothe every surface, even vertical ones, clinging to rock fissures using that felt sense of growth known to no other plant.

The ground cover is thin, but dense—lacerated leaves and seed sprigs intertwine over vast yet shallow rootlets, held together by humus that collects in a stable layer no thicker than a brook trout. Thickets of saplings nip at sun and rain, forcing tangled paths among hummocks of fallen timber. This is the maturation of venerable stands, overpopulous and feeding on itself.

Rock and mineral form the face of the high hills—the flesh and sinew of the world, saturated with water's perpetual presence.
