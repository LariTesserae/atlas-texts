Cascading waters tumble through the valley, channeling into narrow canyons carved by ancient silt and sediment over countless eons. The rivers churn over bedrock polished to glassiness by the ceaseless flow. Everywhere humidity hangs heavy, yet the air feels parched. It's as if the very sky wrings the mist from the landscape each dusk, only to surrender it again at dawn. 

Dense vegetation clings in every crevice and corner—thorny shrubs with succulent leaves, creepers straining upward toward light, towering trees draped in vines they are choking to death. Vines as thick as ship's line wind around the tree trunks, tendrils probing like tentative fingers for purchase in the bark's cracks. Emeralds and violets of every shade throw shadows across the forest floor, where ferns and brambles choke the earth. The sense is one of pent chaos barely constrained.

It is hot, sweltering, the sun a invisible hammer. Still, there is a coolness to the rock and to the shade. The canyons carve the valley in ramifying textures, some sheer cliff, some talus of scree, some tumbled boulders, all textured and incised. The setting sun threads the trees, suddenly fretting the whole view in orange and red like a glowing filigree. Then, as nightless falls, the first stars appear like hot coals, then a billion glimmering points. The Milky way luminesces like fog.

Beneath the high ceilings of the cliffs, the soundscape is a prescence, a physical teeming, yet one that quiets almost to stillness. Few sounds besides rushing water pierce the air—the rustle and susurrus of insects, the small, barely-perangible tremors that reveal the forest floor teems with what scatters through leaf litter. Now and then a monkish voice cries out, then there is a crash of dislodged scree. The clatter and rush of pebbles bouncing down boulder faces. A brook bubbles to itself. Overhead the susurrus of wings, invisible in the dark. It is like trying to hear a wispered conversation in a quiet barroom. Life, but at a sleeping pace.

In this landscape the materials are the wildness of the biotic, sprawling and tangled and striving, yet in a submarine slowness, as if the verdure too is mire, as if the plant presences are suddently ancient simply by their mass and occupancy of the place. The calm hum of it is oppressive, not quite discomforting but an unease of plenty, of spending a crystalline violence expended on air, light, water. We are born in chaos, live in familarity, but some little caul of it clings within—the legacy of a soul rooted in a clearing in bracken forest, in an eye of calm amidst all this tempest. The landscape knows this—it has its great hectares of wrack and wash.
