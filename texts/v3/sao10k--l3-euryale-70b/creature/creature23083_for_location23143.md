The crystalline jungle seethes with an unseen multitude, an innumerable horde of tiny xenobiological entities that infuse the geometric latticework of the mineral formations. These organisms are microscopic in size, single-celled creatures not unlike bacteria or archaea on Earth. But unlike their earthly analogs, they do not sit at the base of an ecosystem as primary producers. 

Instead, the cells are the ecosystem—the jungle itself is a single macro-organism formed from trillions of these microbes, working in concert. Their cell membranes are studded with specialized enzymes that precipitate minerals from the soil, weaving them into the crystalline matrix of the jungle's structure. Tiny flagella protrude from the cells, grasping for neighboring microbes to link with, fusing and knitting the colony together.

Strata of the colony extending into the mineral-strewn soil maintain electrochemical connections to earthly hydrosphere and atmotphere, and respond intimately to changes in weather.

Drifting in the vacuum at large scales, the colony has drifted up from the planet's crust like a fungal mushroom fruiting from mycelium. The keen-edged facets of its growth refract light into a reverberating miasma of color, illusions like heat distortion twisting the line of the distant horizon. The colonized crystalline mass stirs in a way quite inscrutable to basal perception, its dimensions losing all meaning within a refracting mirage of the homogeneous strata.

All these intermesh in a hypersensate experiential ooze.

Through this, the colony's ecosystem apprehends the planet itself, transiting beyond mere intuition through active experiments—adjusting splice-packets in its nucleic code to adapt to changes on geological timescales, fluting predictable environmental frequency that is too monotonous to be interesting, while it pipes the experiential feeds of its more peripheral extremities into itself inquiringly.

But rooted through the planet, the colony's dynamics are as much the planet's as they are its own.
