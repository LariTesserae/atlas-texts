The landscape is inhabited by a vast array of fungal and archaebacterial species that have evolved to thrive in this unique environment. The gigantic bracket fungi that sprawl across the limestone pavement are symbiotic species, the fleshy caps exuding pseudopodial hyphae that insinuate the fractured karst, siphoning minerals and sugars from the stone. The globules of slime they secrete are enzyme-laden, breaking down precious nutrients that are absorbed by the mycelial network. 

The archaea are equally ubiquitous, the effuvium-rich muck matting the cracks and fenestrae with sheets of colonial microbes. Metabolically promiscuous, they chip away at the stone as readily as the ancient roots of the shaggy lichens that plaster the cliffs.

The tree species have evolved stranger adaptations. Some, the waxy-tendril species, have open sinuses filled with jeweled exudates that ooze to the grasping fungal hyphae or even offer sugary secretions to some of the more dimly perceptive archaea and bacteria. Others carry their own symbiotes within the leaves - microbeads that almost seem to glare with mean sense. When pressed, these bead-exudates will sting and secrete paralyzing venom with which they subdue small prey insects that mistake them for eggsats. 

The trees themselves exude various aromatic compounds, some to attract pollinators, others to repel competition, and yet others that leech into the soil to influence the mycorelial networks. Some species of trees have exchanged more extensive mycorrhizal relationships for a more nuanced control of the archaea and demolithic chemotrophs that ease the trees' mineral deficiencies in exchange for flaovonal carbon-exudates.

Many of the organisms here have developed an extrasensory organosense - the lichen-glyphs and stained surfs on the cliffs are whorls of twitching, sentient fungal branching that respond to seismic and barometric cues to scrawl shifting designs on the surfaces. Their true function is to act as arboreal synapses, passing chemical and electromotive signals between the tree roots that they contact and bridging individual mycelial networks to achieve a distributed, continent-spanning intelligence.

The slow shiver of signal through the network will awaken motile archaeal swarms on the other side of the continent, which will burrow the electrical signal into the sulphate sediments there, triggering a reorganization of those microcolonies into gruntlers that will pulle the stone and shake the archive-sequent sigils to scar a record of this distant activity into shifts of compression and expression.

In this way, the continent itself acts a a sensory archive - the lichen-glyphs are a palimpsest of ceaseless impressions of seismic and electromotive cues, a landscape constantly rewritten by the ceaseless activity of the mycorelial myght beneath.
