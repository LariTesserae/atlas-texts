You would find yourself in the heart of the basin, standing on the yielding, moss-slick surface of the floor. You are not a visitor, not an explorer, not a scientist with a notebook. You are part of the census.

You are an arboreal amphibian.

Your skin is a dark, mottled grey-green, the color of wet stone and old leaves, slick with a perpetual film of condensation that never fully evaporates. It is not just skin; it is a lung, a sensor, a membrane so thin you can feel the pressure of the air change as a cloud of spores drifts past. You cling to the vertical flank of a plant-pillar, your toe-pads—broad, ribbed, and alive with microscopic capillary action—holding you fast against the pull of gravity and the slow exhalation of the plant’s own surface moisture.

From this perch, the world is a study in verticality and depth. Below you, the ground is a tapestry of liverworts and creeping succulents, a springy carpet that sighs and releases a puff of earthy gas when the heavy, slow-moving detritivores shift beneath it. Above, the canopy is a ceiling of waxy, overlapping leaves, filtering the outside light into a diffuse, green gloom that feels more like deep ocean than open air. The space between is not empty but woven—a lattice of hanging ferns, veils of trailing vines, and the thick, ancient stems that form the architecture of the basin.

Your existence is one of rhythm and resonance. The world is loud, but its noise is a single, layered drone. You feel it in your bones: the deep, subsonic throb of sap being forced through hollow stems the size of your own body, a slow hydraulic pulse that is the heartbeat of the entire system. Overlaid on this is the constant, percussive patter of condensation falling from the high leaves onto the mats below, a sound like a million tiny clocks ticking out of sync. And woven through it all is the papery static of the insect swarms—the billion tiny wings that fill the humid air, their shush a constant presence against your sensitive skin.

You are a predator, but your predation is a thread in the tapestry, not a tear in it. Your throat pulses, inflating with a resonant, hollow boom that echoes through the plant-pillars, a call that is both mate-attraction and territory-marking in a world where territory is not a patch of ground but a living column. Your eyes, large and black, drink in the minimal light, and your tongue is a ribbon of lightning—long, sticky, and precise—darting out to snatch a passing insect from the air. The insect is gone in a blur, its papery wings silenced, its body a brief, warm burst of energy that you will convert into the next pulse of your own slow life.

You are not alone, but you are not in a crowd. The other amphibians are echoes of yourself, clinging to other pillars, their calls overlapping with yours to create a chorus that is less a collection of voices and more a single, resonant frequency. The insects are the noise you hunt, the static you live within. The tiny crustaceans and isopods in the mat below are the unseen custodians of the world you drop waste into, the recyclers of your own brief existence.

To move is to become part of the architecture. You shift your weight, and the plant-pillar beneath you thrums with a slow, answering pulse of sap. You leap from one trunk to another, your suction-cup toes catching the waxy surface with a soft, wet *thock*, and for a moment you are suspended in the green twilight, surrounded by the hanging gardens and the veil of vines, a transient spark in a system that has already achieved its final form.

You are not a shepherd, and there is no flock. You are a cell in a vast, wet lung, a gear in a slow, biological machine. Your life is measured not in years but in the thickening of your skin, the resonance of your call, the number of insects you consume before you, too, are claimed by the enzymatic breakdown of the mat below. You are a resident of a place that is both cathedral and organism, and you are as much a part of its construction as the roots and the stems. You are the pulse, the hum, and the drip, forever clinging, forever breathing the warm, wet air of the basin.
