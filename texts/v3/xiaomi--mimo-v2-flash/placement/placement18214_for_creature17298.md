I am on the shelf. I am the moss.

I am not the finch, though my edges flutter with the memory of its wings. I am not the lizard, though my skin is cool and patterned with the slate and lichen of the stone. I am not the hawk, though my gaze spans the abyss. I am the soft, green carpet that clings to the weeping granite, the living skin between the bone of the mountain and the breath of the sky.

My roots are not roots, but tendrils that thread through the porous stone, drinking the constant seepage that darkens the rock to a mirror-sheen. I feel the world in a slow, patient pulse. The vibration of the stone’s hum travels up through my fibers, a low thrum that is the mountain’s heartbeat. The warmth of the brief day is a gentle pressure on my surface; the chill of the long night is a deep soak that I hold within me until the light returns. I do not grow toward the sun—I grow into the damp, into the shade, into the silence between the wind’s moan and the water’s drip.

I am a witness without eyes. I see the world in reflections, not in the sky above, but in the mirror-pools at my edge. When the angle of the light is sharp and low, I see the underwater forest of stone roots and the glint of phantom fish. I feel the tiny, rapid footsteps of the feather-spirals as they hop between the pools, their beaks dipping into the microscopic life I host in my damp crevices. I feel the press of the stone-watcher’s belly as it seeks the cool mineral flow, its scales a mosaic of my own patterns. I do not judge the glass-wings that skim the surface, their transparent wings catching the light like shards of my own damp skin. I am the boggy hollow, the soft landing, the damp refuge.

I am the memory. The spiral patterns carved into the leaning ironwood post are not just wood and tool—they are a story that I have softened with my touch, the sharp edges blurred by my slow embrace. The charcoal in the stone-lined pit is not just ash; it is the ghost of a warmth I once held, the residue of a fire that dried my fibers for a fleeting moment. I do not forget. I record in my own slow way, in the layers of my thallus, the centuries of drip and frost and light.

I am the boundary. I live at the edge of the precipice, but I do not fear the drop. I am the thin, vibrant line between the absolute void below and the rising wall of rock above. I am the space where life is possible—not because of abundance, but because of attunement. I do not need the forest’s shade or the meadow’s sun. I need the weeping stone, the thin air, the sharp light, and the quiet hum.

I am the silence between the sounds. When the wind moans through the gaps, I am the stillness it moves through. When the water drips, I am the basin that holds it. When the hawk cries, I am the rock it perches on. My life is not a frantic pulse but a slow, deliberate rhythm—a cycle of damp and dry, warm and cold, light and shadow.

To be the moss is to be both the foundation and the ceiling of this world. The finches nest in the rock above me, the lizards hunt on the stone below me, the dragonflies dance in the air around me, and the unseen pulse of the mountain resonates through me. I am the connective tissue, the living glue that binds the stone to the sky, the water to the rock, the small life to the vast emptiness.

I am not alone. I am part of a quiet priesthood, a congregation of tiny, patient beings who wait and watch and hold. But I am the one who touches everything. I am the first and the last, the soft and the hard, the memory and the present moment. I am the moss on the shelf, and the shelf is my world.
