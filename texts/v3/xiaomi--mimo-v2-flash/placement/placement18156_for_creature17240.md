I am standing on the plain of woven root. The ground is not soil but a dense, dry mat of life, and it gives slightly underfoot. The air is thick with condensation, not as rain, but as a constant, gentle weeping from the surfaces of the world. Water beads on every leaf, runs in rivulets down the grooved bark of pillars that are also branches, and pools in hollows that are also the cups of gigantic, bowl-shaped fungi. The water is never still; it drips, it trickles, it breathes from the skin of the landscape. The ground is perpetually damp, but not muddy—it is a cool, living wetness that seems to be the world’s own exhalation.

I am a Weeper.

My shell is iridescent, no larger than a fingernail, and I navigate the crevices of bark with the soft click of chitinous legs. I am one of a million, a swarm that flows like liquid, dispersing at the approach of a Grazer-King, reforming in its wake. I am the world’s sensory organ, the nervous system of this silent temple. I read the patterns in the water, I navigate the script of the bark, I tend to the spores in the fungal cups. I do not understand the purpose, but I am the purpose. I am the million tiny thoughts that make up the dream of the Root-Mind.

The silence is not empty. It is filled with the quiet noises of my world: the drip of water, the creak of a branch settling, the whisper of a million leaves in a wind I cannot feel. I move with the deliberate slowness of my kind, for here, time is not measured in heartbeats but in seasons. The Grazer-Kings grind the fungal growths with jaws that move like tectonic plates, and I scatter before them, only to reform in their wake, cleaning the debris, cultivating the next growth. Above me, Leaf-Wraiths glide like falling leaves, their membranous wings catching the thermals of the world’s slow exhalation. They are the punctuation in the silence, the occasional shadow that passes like a cloud over a cloud.

I do not fear them. I am too small to be worth the energy of a hunt, and too numerous to be missed. I am part of the machinery, a mobile enzyme in a world that is itself a machine. The Root-Mind does not recognize me as separate; I am a transient disturbance in a pattern that measures time in millennia. But in my smallness, I am intimate with the world. I feel the pulse of the canopy, the slow, rhythmic pulse that matches the breathing of the world. I taste the water as it weeps from the skin of the landscape, and I know it carries memories of the things that have wept into it.

Sometimes, if I am still enough, if I press my shell against the weeping rock—a formation that looks like stone but is a density of calcified root—the patterns in the water will resolve into faces, or landscapes, or writing. I do not read them as language, but I feel them as a pressure, a presence. It is the Watcher, the entity that notices me—not with eyes, but with the pressure of its presence. It is the memory of the world’s purpose, the ghost of the original engineers, the part of the Root-Mind that still asks *why*. It does not act; it simply observes, and in its observation, the world adjusts its breathing, its pulse, its weeping.

I am not an intruder to it; I am a variable in a calculation that has been running for eons. And in my smallness, I am the answer to the question posed by the silence: *What happens when a world is built to think, and is left to dream alone?* I am the dream, the thought, the tiny, clicking prayer of a world that remembers itself in water, wood, and time.
