If you were to step into the Hollow Range, you would find yourself not on the ground, but within the architecture of a lung. The floor beneath your feet is not stone, but a resilient, spongy mat of woven cellulose and mycelium—a living skin that yields slightly with every step, damp and cool against the soles of your boots. The air is thin and cold, tasting of wet stone and the sharp, green scent of chlorophyll, and it moves with a slow, tidal rhythm that you feel more in your chest than on your skin.

You are a Carver—a member of that vanished, primitive intelligence—but not as they were in life. You are a memory etched into the plant-flesh, a consciousness that has been absorbed and reinterpreted by the mycelial matrix. You do not walk; you are carried by the mat, your perception diffused across the root-hairs that delve into the stone and the fronds that turn the searing light of the sun-stone into sustenance. You are both observer and part of the observed, a ghost in the machine of the cavern’s single, sprawling organism.

The sun-stone drifts overhead, a searing eye of captured daylight, and as it passes, you feel the surge and retreat of life like a pulse in your own veins. Before it, the plant towers spiral upward in chaotic, desperate growth, their cellulose ribs arching like cathedral ceilings toward the light. You remember carving the spirals into their flesh—simple patterns of stone on softness—not to build, but to mark the passage of time, to acknowledge the rhythm of the cavern’s breath. The wounds you left have healed into raised ridges, scars that the plant has incorporated into its own growing memory.

In the clearings, where the sun-stone’s wake has left the mat brittle and ash-like, you feel the emptiness as a hollow ache. Here, the stone floor is exposed, sharp and unweathered, and the air is still. You see the remnants of your former self: the collapsed shelters of woven fiber and stacked stone, now half-swallowed by the mycelium, their foundations anchored in living roots. You remember the fire pits, the charcoal scars digested long ago, the warmth of a temporary flame against the cavern’s chill. But now, there is no fire, only the cold, bright light and the whisper of wind through plant-channels.

The fauna are sparse, but you sense them as extensions of the cavern’s quiet. The gliders drift in the high vaults, their leathery wings catching the thermal gradients you breathe out, their blind eyes sensing only the warmth of the condensation pools. The translucent insects scuttle in the dark fissures of the root-mat, decomposing the ash of your own retreat, cleaning the wounds of the organism you inhabit. You are not separate from them; you are part of the same slow, turbulent cycle of creation and decay.

The sound is mostly quiet, but the quiet is alive. It is the hollow whistle of wind, the drip of water from frond to pool, the deep, tectonic groan of the cavern settling—a young system, still forming. You feel the grind of stone and the soft push of growth as the plant-mat expands and contracts, a single breath that has lasted eons.

You are the Hollow Range. You are the mycelial mind beneath the stone, the frond-architecture that arches toward the light, the memory of the Carvers etched in scarred patterns. You are the ghost in the clearings and the surge in the towers, the cold, bright air and the slow, dripping dampness. You are both ancient and newborn, a world of vast, silent spaces lit by a moving sun, where life is not a collection of individuals, but a singular, rhythmic pulse.
