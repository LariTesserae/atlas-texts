If you were to find yourself in this world, you would not be a stranger to it.

You are a child of the weeping stone, one of the Marsh-Weepers.

You are small, your frame compact and flexible, adapted to navigating the narrow gaps between the interlocking houses. The air is your blanket—a cool, damp weight that settles on your shoulders and clings to your eyelashes. You do not fight the moisture; you wear it like a second skin.

You wake not to a sunrise, for the light is a diffuse, silver-gray wash that filters through the mist and the gaps in the thatch, but to the taste of the air. You can taste the water table today: it is high, rich with the metallic tang of deep limestone and the faint sweetness of decaying reed. You know, without stepping outside, that the puddles in the central crevice will be trembling, ready to release their small, sighing geysers.

You rise from a bed of dried moss and reed, your body sinking slightly into the softness. The floor of your home is stone, but it is not cold; it is tempered by the constant presence of the water below. You step carefully—the stone shifts, a slow, imperceptible settling that you feel through the soles of your bare feet. It is not a tremor; it is a breath. The ground is alive, and you are walking on its skin.

Your clothing is a wrap of woven reed, dyed the color of wet slate. It drains quickly, leaving your skin pale but not clammy. You step into the corridor—more a crevice than a path—where the sky is a narrow strip of silver. The walls press close on either side, their rough-hewn limestone slick with condensation. Moss carpets the vertical surfaces, a living tapestry that drinks the damp air and holds it. Ferns sprout from the seams between stones, their fronds dripping with tiny jewels of water.

You move with a quiet economy, your feet finding the raised stones that mark the path. The ground here is soft, yielding; each step leaves a slight impression that the water will fill in time. The air is still, heavy with the scent of wet stone, peat smoke, and the faint, metallic whisper of groundwater. There is no wind, only the drip of water from eaves, the gurgle of hidden drains, the occasional plop of a stone settling into mud. The sounds are low, resonant, a language of moisture and stone.

You are not alone, but you are solitary. The other Weepers move in the same quiet rhythm, their voices soft and sibilant, their bodies narrow and balanced. You pass a neighbor in the corridor—a slight nod, a murmured greeting in the language of fricatives and sibilants that mimics the seep of water through limestone. You do not speak loudly; the damp air carries sound far, and silence is a shared agreement.

Your destination is the collection basin, a shallow depression carved into the stone where water gathers and settles. You kneel, your knees sinking slightly into the soft ground, and cup your hands. The water is clear but mineral-rich, cool and faintly metallic. You drink, and it tastes of the deep earth, of limestone and time. You know this water; it has traveled through fractures and crevices, carrying with it the memory of the stone—faint impressions of light, of shadow, of the slow pressure of the earth.

You are a gatherer, a tender of stone. Your work today is to harvest moss from the western wall, where it grows thickest. You carry a stone blade, sharpened by friction and patience, and you slice carefully at the base of the moss, taking only what you need. The moss is insulation, dye, a soft lining for your bed. You do not waste; nothing is wasted here. The water is not a problem; it is a resource. The stone is not a barrier; it is a partner.

You pause at a puddle, watching the water-striders skate across its surface with delicate, ceremonial slowness. The puddle trembles, and a small geyser of murky water collapses and reforms with a soft, sucking sound. You feel the shift through your feet—the ground is breathing, exhaling. You do not fear it; you listen to it. You are part of this pulse, this slow rhythm of rise and fall, of seep and breath.

The city is young, still becoming. It has not yet decided what it will be, and neither have you. Your life is measured not in years but in the depth of the puddles, the clarity of the air, the settling of the stone. You do not plan generations ahead; you plan for the next season, the next tremor, the next shift in the water table. Your memory is short but deep, like a puddle that reflects the sky but holds no image when the wind passes.

You are not religious, but you reverence the stone. You see the weeping lines of water as the stone’s sweat, the shifting of the ground as its breathing. You build in harmony with its slow pulse, your home an extension of the land. When you die, you will be placed in a crevice in the stone, and the water will seep in and the moss will grow over, and you will become part of the city’s slow becoming.

You are a Marsh-Weeper, though you have no name for yourself. You are simply the one who lives where the stone sweats and the ground breathes, who moves in quiet rhythm with the pulse of the marsh, whose life is written in the language of water and stone.

And as you walk back to your home, the mist closes around you, and the city continues to become, its people and its land inseparable, its future measured in the rise and fall of the water table, its present held in the damp, quiet air.
