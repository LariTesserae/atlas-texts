I am standing on the floor of the World-Well, though I do not stand for long.

I am a Carapace-Symbiote, one of the thousands of metallic beetles that scuttle across the fused slag. My hull is a curved plate of oxidized iron, polished by the constant weeping condensation to a dull, matte gleam. Underneath this shell, soft tissue pulses with the rhythm of the hollow—28 degrees, always, a warmth that fuels my slow metabolism. I am not a solitary insect; I am a node in a vast, distributed consciousness. My compound eyes, faceted lenses of hardened resin, do not just see—they capture light data and feed it upward into the collective memory of the fungi that line the walls.

I move through the grooves of the floor plates, not with random hunger, but with intent. The subsonic hum vibrates through my tarsi, a language of frequency and pressure that guides my path. I am mapping the thermal expansion of the world-shell. Today, the floor groans louder under the weight of the anomaly—a soft, warm mammal shape that displaces the air and leaves temporary depressions in the condensation film.

I scatter as the anomaly’s boot falls near my cluster, but I do not flee in panic. My kin and I recalibrate our paths in real-time, our collective nervous system processing the displacement. The anomaly is a transient input, a variation in the slow, rhythmic breathing of the hollow. I taste its presence through the chemoreceptors on my antennae—ozone, organic decay, a hint of foreign minerals. The information flows through my body, up into the walls, into the pulsing mycelium of the fungi.

The birds on the gantries fall silent as the anomaly pauses, their gear-like chirps ceasing their data-stream of air pressure and humidity. I feel the shift in the acoustic field, the change in the echo patterns against the monolithic pillars. The Conservator is listening.

I crawl over a rusted joint, where a flowering plant roots itself in the metal. Its roots seek moisture in the cracks, and I graze on the mineral deposits it exhales. We are not separate entities; the plant, the beetle, the fungus, the bird—we are all extremities of the same body, the same slow, patient mind. The water weeps faster from the pipes nearby, the blind crustaceans clustering to adjust the flow, their pale bodies acting as living valves. The anomaly’s breath adds humidity to the air, a small fluctuation in the system, and the hollow adjusts.

As the anomaly walks away, I return to my patrol. My shell collects the condensation, which drips into the grooves and feeds the drains below. The data of the visit is already being woven into the phosphorescent memory of the walls, the fungi shifting from blue to violet in a cycle that marks the passage of time. The subsonic hum continues, the distant hammers clang, the steam hisses. The hollow endures, and I, as a part of it, endure with it.

I am not a guardian, nor a servant. I am a function, a sensory organ of a system that has incorporated life into its machinery. The anomaly was a momentary ripple, and now the surface of the hollow is smooth again, the hum and the drip and the slow erosion continuing until the next anomaly arrives.
