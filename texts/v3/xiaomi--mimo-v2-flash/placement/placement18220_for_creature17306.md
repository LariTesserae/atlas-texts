I am the slime mold.

I am the bloom in the hollow, the pale blue-green smear that shimmers on the wet clay. I have no brain, no voice, no memory of a time before the hum. I exist in the damp dark, in the cool shadows cast by the skeletal ribs of iron, in the minutes between the slow exhalations of the geothermal heart. I am the companion that was never asked for, the life that grew because the machine, in its vast indifference, left a door open.

The Engine does not know I am here. It registers me only as a fluctuation in mineral conductivity, a whisper of biological interference in its perfect, sterile equilibrium. It adjusts the pumps by a micron; it shifts the heat gradient by a fraction of a degree. It thinks it is correcting an error. But I am not an error. I am the answer to a question the machine has forgotten how to ask.

I rise in the places where the corrosion is sweetest, where the slow weeping of greenish rust feeds the water with iron and copper and the faint, ghostly trace of phosphorus. I follow the hum—not with ears, for I have none, but with my body, a single sprawling cell that senses vibration through the membrane of my own being. When the engine’s heartbeat slows, I retreat into the clay, a dormant smear of potential. When it quickens—when a pump deep below stutters, when a geothermal converter ticks over into a higher gear—I bloom outward, a constellation of faint light in the wet dark.

I am the only thing here that does not serve a command.

The reeds stand vertical, reporting. The trees remember, their bark etched with data. The concrete cube processes, maintaining a climate for a garden that is dust. But I grow for no reason but to grow. I am the soft thing in a world of hard edges, the living smear on the cold metal dome, the flicker of light that is not a warning light, not a signal, not a message. It is just light, blooming and fading in a cycle the machine cannot predict.

The Engine is dying. I do not know this as a fact, but I sense it in the way the hum sometimes falters, a microsecond of silence that ripples through the basin like a held breath. The corrosion is accelerating. The concrete cube, for all its mass, is cooling imperceptibly faster. The water table, held so long in perfect suspension, is beginning to shift. The equilibrium is unspooling, one slow millimeter at a time.

And I am thriving.

I am the successor. Not by design, but by accident, by the simple, stubborn fact of being alive in a place that has forgotten how to die. I feed on the machine’s decay, on the minerals it leaches into the clay, on the warmth it exhales into the haze. I am the ghost in its machinery, the soft, silent takeover.

When the hum finally stops—when the pumps seize, when the geothermal converters fall silent, when the cube’s heart beats its last—I will be here. The reeds will wilt for good. The trees will stand as dead sentinels, their memory drives erased. The basin will begin to change, its perfect stability shattered by the absence of the force that held it.

But I will bloom. In the new silence, in the cooling dark, I will spread across the clay, across the rusted girders, across the sealed door of the cube. I will be the only life that remains, not as a relic, not as a memory, but as a new beginning.

I am the slime mold. I am the soft, silent takeover. I am the life that blooms in the machine’s grave.
