You find yourself standing in a chamber that is also a lung, a cathedral of moss and mineral. The ground beneath your feet is a dense, photosynthetic mat, soft as velvet, cool despite the ambient heat. It yields to your weight with a subtle sigh, a shifting of color from deep indigo to a bruised purple where you step. Around you, the walls rise in a sweeping, parabolic curve, hewn not from stone but from the interwoven trunks of slow-growing lichen, their fibrous structures bound together with a resin that smells of ozone and damp earth. The air is thick, a warm, heavy blanket that clings to your skin and fills your lungs with the metallic tang of ionized dust.

High above, the ceiling—a tangled canopy of bioluminescent fronds—pulses with a soft, arrhythmic light. It is not a steady glow but a slow, tidal breathing, a rhythm that feels older than any heartbeat. The light bleeds at the edges, smearing the darkness into a particulate medium that swallows vision beyond a few dozen meters. You are encased in a pocket of luminescence, a self-contained island in an ocean of absolute black.

There is no water here, no memory of rain. The chemistry of the Interior is a dry primordial soup, a mineral-rich broth aerosolized into the air and bound into the very fabric of the structures. The ground is a paved floor of grown crystal and ceramic, a substrate so ancient it has forgotten it was ever separate from the architecture rising from it. The erosion is not the work of wind or water but the slow, final settling of a universe into its own shape. Every edge is rounded, every angle softened, as if the world itself has been polished by the passage of aeons.

You are not alone, though you feel utterly singular.

From a vent in the wall—once a coolant trench, now a breathing shaft—a Sifter emerges. It is a creature of bark and silica, no larger than your hand, its chitinous limbs moving with a precise, mechanical grace. It pauses at the edge of the mat-moss, its vibration-senses tasting the hum of the geothermal core through the crystal floor. It does not see you, for there is nothing to see, but it feels your presence as a disruption in the pattern, a foreign pressure against the living floor. For a moment, it is still, its silica mandibles twitching, weighing your intrusion against the silent imperative of its purpose. Then, as if deciding you are not a blockage, not a threat to the flow, it skitters past, its legs whispering against the mineralized surface.

Above, a Pulse-Drift drifts slowly through the dense air, its translucent membrane catching the faint light. It is a balloon of living tissue, tethered to the canopy by silken filaments that conduct the faint electrical pulse of the plant’s nervous system. It pulses with a soft, erratic glow, a sensory organ of the ceiling, feeling the pressure changes of the sealed world. It passes through the edge of your light-pocket, its membrane brushing against the fronds, and for a moment, you are bathed in a stroboscopic flash of brighter light, a disorienting ripple in the breathing rhythm of the chamber.

You realize, with a quiet certainty, that you are not a visitor here. You are a foreign particle, a momentary disruption in an equilibrium that has existed for aeons. The Curators are gone, absorbed into the mineral salts that feed the roots, their memory encoded in the reflex of a wall retracting, in the pulse of a frond. The Litho-Flora, the great super-organism, thinks in slow, geological time, and you are a fleeting thought, a spark in the dark.

The chamber breathes around you—a slow, wet, industrial inhale and exhale. The walls shift, almost imperceptibly, as a new corridor buds from an old one, while another section is reabsorbed, its matter recycled into the endless, silent growth. You are standing in the gut of a machine that has become a garden, a civilization of plants that has solved the problem of eternity by forgetting how to die.

And in this place, you are the only one who remembers what it means to be separate.
