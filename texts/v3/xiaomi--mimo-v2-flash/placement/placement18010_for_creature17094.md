In this world, you would find yourself in the **Vestibule of the Deep Moss**, a specific alcove within the lower labyrinth where the bioluminescent mycelia cluster thickest against a foundation stone that predates the current settlement.

You are a **Tender of the Drip**, one of the amphibious inhabitants described, but specifically attuned to the acoustic properties of the place.

You are crouched in a niche no higher than your shoulder, your broad, webbed fingers splayed against the cold, damp floor. The silence here is not merely an absence of sound but a presence you can feel against your skin; it is the baseline against which you measure the subtle shifts in the environment. You are waiting—not for an event, but for a change in the rhythm of seepage.

Your work is one of maintenance, but it is also one of listening. The city, though silent to the casual observer, is full of vibrations. The slow settling of stone into silt transmits a hum through the ground; the trickle of water through deep channels creates a resonance in the air that you feel in your chest. As a Tender, you ensure that these vibrations remain constant. You adjust the placement of a reed mat to change the drip pattern of a ceiling stalactite, or you pack a handful of damp moss into a crevice to muffle a vibration that has become too sharp, too insistent.

In your lap rests a tool—a smooth, heavy stone shaped by generations of hands to fit perfectly into the curve of your palm. It is not a weapon, nor a chisel, but a regulator. You use it to tap gently against the stone walls, listening to the pitch of the echo. A high, brittle ring indicates a dry spot, a crack in the equilibrium; a low, dull thud means the stone is fully saturated, settled into its place. Your task is to maintain the dull thud.

Around you, the bioluminescent fungi cast a faint, cold light, making your pale, iridescent skin gleam like the inside of an abalone shell. You do not look up at the bright, clear sky; your world is this twilight, this labyrinth of stone and water. The air smells of wet granite and the sweet, earthy decay of the moss. A small, blind amphibian skitters across your foot, its skin cool and slick, and you do not flinch. It is part of the dampness, part of the silence.

You shift your weight slowly, conserving energy. Your movements are deliberate, economical. You reach out and brush a webbed finger against a cluster of glowing caps, feeling the cool, moist surface of the fungus. It pulses faintly, a slow rhythm that matches the drip of water from a ledge three chambers away. You are part of this closed system, a living component of the architecture. Your silence is not emptiness; it is a language of touch, gesture, and resonance. You are the memory of the place, embodied and still, tending to the sustained moment of existence in the city frozen in becoming.
