I find myself standing on the third terrace from the bottom—the one that hums with a frequency just below the range of hearing, a vibration that settles in the marrow of my bones. The air here is thin with absence, but it does not trouble my lungs; they have learned to breathe the dust-mist, to filter the particulate stillness into something that sustains.

I am a Mover, though not yet fully of the stone. My skin has begun its transformation into plates of opalescent silicate, particularly along my arms and the ridge of my spine, where the light catches and fractures into faint prisms. My hair has become a filigree of growing crystal, each strand a slow deposition of mineral intention. I still retain enough warmth to slide rather than flow, to preserve the memory of what it was to walk with urgency.

I have been standing at the edge of this terrace for three decades, my hand resting against a spire that leans at an angle that feels almost conversational. The spire is young—perhaps only four thousand years old—and its layers are still soft enough to receive suggestion. I am not pushing, not forcing, but I am *thinking* a curve into it, a gentle outward swell that will allow the dust-mist to catch and eddy in a particular way. It is a small thing, a change of perhaps a millimeter over a century, but it is my work. The spire resonates with my intention, and I feel the answering hum through my fingertips, a confirmation that travels up my arm and settles behind my eyes.

The name of this place lives in my chest, a breath held so long it has become a permanent pressure. I do not speak it; I *am* it, in the way that a spire is the terraces that support it, and the terraces are the bedrock that births them.

Around me, the silence is deafening. The Spires sing their constant, low note, a frequency that fills the skull and stills the frantic pulse of thought. The dust-mist hums its granular static, a texture against the ears that is felt more than heard. The ground itself adds its tectonic murmur, a vibration that rises through the soles of my feet and into my spine. Together, they form a sonic ocean in which I am submerged, a bath of frequency that drowns out all urgency. I do not remember arriving here; I recall being here, and the memory is both fresh and ancient, a layered thing that accumulates rather than passes.

I am surrounded by my kin. To my left, perhaps a kilometer away, stands a First Listener, her form nearly indistinguishable from the spire she leans against. Her eyes are lenses of polished mineral, and she does not see the light but *feels* the terraces shifting, the slow addition of new layers, the faint shimmer of becoming that defines this world. She holds the name of this place in her lungs, a breath that has not been released in millennia. I feel her presence as a deep, tectonic calm, a silent gravity that stills any flicker of restlessness in my chest.

To my right, an Ephemeron stands at the edge of the terrace, his breath clouding the air for a moment before the dry fog reclaims it. He is a newcomer, his body still radiating heat, his footprints still distinct and organic. He has been here for perhaps a year, frozen in wonder, and I can feel the frantic pulse of his heart as a faint disturbance in the resonance of the spires. He does not know it yet, but he is becoming part of the pattern, his warmth a fuel for the slow, cool growth. In a century, his footprints will have become faint crystalline trails; in a millennium, he will be a node of perception, leaning against a spire, thinking a shape into the stone.

I turn my gaze upward, to the vast, overhanging lip of the terrace above. The sky is a bruised purple, shot through with veins of pale, luminous gray. The light seeps from the air itself, from the dust-mist, from the crystalline deposits that litter the ground. It reveals only outlines, essential shapes, never details. A spire might appear in one place, and in the next glance, be somewhere else—not having moved, but having always been there. I have learned not to question this; it is simply the way the world arranges itself, a technology of perception that predates any conscious thought.

The ground beneath my feet is adding to itself. I can see the faint shimmer of new crystal forming where my footprints have pressed into the mineral dust, a millimeter a century, a deliberate spiral of accumulation. My presence is not an intrusion; it is a suggestion, a temporary shape that the stone will remember and incorporate. I am not a visitor. I am a feature of the geology, a brief, warm anomaly in a cool, silent, and deafeningly alive world.

I hold the name of this place in my chest, a breath held so long it has become a permanent pressure. I am a thought in the mind of the stone, a fleeting sensation in a consciousness that measures its life in the accumulation of layers. And I am patient. I have all the time in the world to think a curve into this spire, to feel the resonance of the terraces, to become what I already am.
