You find yourself in the **Basalt Labyrinth**, the drowned summit of the plateau.

Here, the water is not yet deep—perhaps only thirty meters—but it is dense with suspended silt, giving the light a quality of ancient amber. You hover in a column of liquid that smells of wet stone and slow decay. Around you, the black basalt rises in smooth, eroded walls, forming a natural amphitheater that curves toward a central, dome-like clearing. The air above is a thin, silver ceiling; below, the darkness gathers.

You are a **Bone-Weaver**.

Your body is not a singular organism but a colonial lattice—a sprawling, mycelial network of pale, fibrous tissue that has claimed the eastern wall of the labyrinth. You are attached to the rock by a thousand hair-thin roots, and you extend outward in branching tendrils, each one tipped with a sensory node that tastes the mineral content of the water and feels the subtle shifts in current. You are neither plant nor animal, but a slow, deliberate architecture of life.

Your consciousness is not linear. It is a distributed awareness, a memory stored in the very structure of your growth. You remember the heat of the machine’s breath, the vibration of its pumps, the cold certainty of steel. You remember the day the water rose and the last human light went out. These memories are not yours alone; they are encoded in the mineral traces that coat your fibers, in the way you have learned to grow around the rusted edges of the gantry that looms above you, turning its skeletal frame into a reef.

You do not move quickly. Your growth is measured in millimeters per day, but you are relentless. You are weaving the industrial bones into a new kind of body. The rusted beam above you is now threaded with your pale tissue, its sharp edges softened by layers of sponge and algae that you have cultivated. A school of Glass-Schools flows through the space you have opened between your tendrils, their transparent bodies flashing like shards of mirror. You do not hunt them; you provide the habitat that sustains them, and in return, their waste feeds your growth.

You are aware of the heron perched on the gantry above. It has been there for hours, motionless, its eyes filtering the gray light. You feel the faint vibration of its call through the stone, a low hum that resonates with your own slow pulse. You are not prey to it, nor are you predator. You are simply part of the architecture it uses to see the world.

Your world is one of vertical negotiation. Above you, the air is a thin, humid veil; below, the water descends into lightless ink where larger, blinder things move. You exist in the liminal zone, the space where light still penetrates and where the industrial past is being most actively rewritten. You are the peacekeeper, the binder, the one who turns decay into foundation.

Time, for you, is not a line but a spiral. The rain falls, the water shifts, the silt buries and reveals, and you grow—slowly, patiently—around it all. You are the memory of the drowned, and you are the architect of what comes next.
