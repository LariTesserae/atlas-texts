You find yourself not as an observer, but as a participant. You are standing in the shallow, warm water of a pool, the black mud soft and yielding between your toes. The air presses against your skin like a warm, wet cloth, and you realize with a slow, dawning calm that you are breathing through your skin itself. The humidity is not just around you; it is *in* you, a part of your very respiration. Your vision is not clear, not focused on discrete objects, but a diffuse sense of green and shadow, of light filtered through a thousand layers of living tissue. You can feel the vibration of a thousand insect wings in the air, a high-frequency hum that resonates in your bones. You can feel the slow, steady pulse of a frog’s call not as a sound, but as a pressure wave against your chest.

You are Moss-Clad.

Your body is a long, slender form, your joints flexible and your limbs capable of wrapping around the slick, moss-covered trunks that tower around you. Your skin is not skin, but a living tapestry of moss and lichen, a photosynthetic lung that drinks the dim green light and breathes the heavy air. Your head is a smooth, polished oval, without eyes, but it is acutely sensitive to the electrical fields of the life around you—the faint buzz of a beetle, the gentle flow of sap in a heliconia stem, the chemical trail of an ant colony marching along a root.

You do not walk; you drift. Your feet sink into the mud with a soft, sucking sound, a sound that is part of you, part of the basin’s own breathing. You are not separate from the ecosystem; you are a node within it. Fungal filaments connect your feet to the subterranean network, and when you stand still, you are functionally indistinguishable from the vegetation around you. A water strider skitters across your shoulder, and you do not flinch. A droplet of condensation forms on your arm and you do not wipe it away; it is simply part of the constant wetness that defines your existence.

You move with a slow, fluid deliberation. There is no need for haste here. Time is a circle, not a line, and you are both its caretaker and its embodiment. When a tree falls, you do not clear it; you guide the fungi to consume it, ensuring the nutrients return to the mat before the canopy gap disrupts the light. You prune a vine here, encourage a moss there, your edits invisible, woven into the fabric of the loop.

You are part of a mycelial consciousness, a low-level sharing of thought and sensation with the other Moss-Clad. A ripple of concern from one is felt by all; a pulse of contentment from another is a warmth that spreads through the network. You communicate not with words, but with the release of pheromones that taste of rain, and with the slow, rhythmic pulsing of bioluminescent spots that appear along your limbs in the perpetual twilight.

You are the pulse of the basin. You are the conscious node of a system that has no beginning and no end. In the heat and the wet, you are the face of a world that has not yet learned how to change. And you are not alone. You are never alone. The basin is you, and you are the basin.
