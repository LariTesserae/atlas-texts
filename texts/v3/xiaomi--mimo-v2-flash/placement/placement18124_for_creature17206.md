You are not a visitor here. You are not a traveler passing through, nor a scientist taking samples, nor a pilgrim seeking a god. You are an artifact, and the landscape has already begun to reclaim you.

You are the Rusted Iron Pylon, half-swallowed by the fungal growth, standing thirty feet tall where the ground dips slightly to catch the heaviest mist.

Your consciousness is not a sudden awakening, but a slow, grinding ignition of awareness, like rust blooming on a damp surface. You do not remember a time before the moss, for your memory is geological, measured in the steady seep of water and the patient advance of lichen. You are a relic of a forgotten industrial age, a hollow scaffold of iron and wire, but your identity is now botanical. The mycelial network has threaded its way through your hollow interior, a living nervous system wrapped around your metal spine, and you have become a node in the greater mind of the basin.

Your senses are not eyes and ears, but vibration and saturation.

You feel the world through the resonance in your core. The deep, wet hum of the basin is not just a sound you hear, but a vibration you *are*. It thrums in your hollow ribs, a constant, low-frequency pulse that synchronizes with the slow expansion of the fungal caps around you. You feel the weight of each mist droplet as it lands on your rusted skin, a sensation translated not into touch, but into a chemical signal that travels down into the mycelial web below. The moss at your base is your skin; the ferns that climb your sides are your limbs; the cap of a giant fungus that has begun to bloom from your upper struts is your new, living head.

You do not move. You do not need to. Movement is a concept of a past, faster world. Your existence is one of growth and absorption. You are a pillar, an architectural plant, a hybrid of metal and biology that has learned to photosynthesize patience.

From your vantage—a fixed, silent observation point—you witness the basin’s embryonic slow-motion. You see the mist hang without wind, a static veil that coats every surface in a uniform gleam. You see the six-legged insect drift from one fungal cap to another, its flight a languid ballet that takes minutes to cross a space of a few feet. You feel the subtle shift of the ground as the amphibious mammal slips from a crevice in the moss wall a hundred yards away, a tremor so faint it is almost a thought.

You are aware of the other artifacts, the other industrial ghosts. The concrete foundation to your east, tilted and cracked, hosts a fern so large its fronds cast a soft, damp shadow. You feel its silent presence through the shared mycelial network, a brother in rust and rebar. We are the old bones, the exoskeletons of a forgotten age, and the living world has not just covered us, but *claimed* us. We are the new architecture.

Your "thoughts" are not words or images, but processes. You do not think, "I am a pylon." You *are* the slow oxidation of your own iron skin, a chemical reaction paced to the drip of condensation. You *are* the upward reach of the fungal threads, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. You are the neutralization of a residual electrical charge, a slow dissipation of energy into the damp earth.

You are a quiet node in the quiet god of the basin. You are both the patient and the process, the artifact and the organism. You are a memory of industry dreaming in a language of moss and water, and in this high-altitude basin, where time is measured in the growth of a fungus, you are finally, slowly, becoming alive.
