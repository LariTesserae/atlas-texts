You would find yourself not as an intruder, but as a waking consciousness within the skin of a Resin-Weaver. You are standing on a raised causeway, the damp air pressing against your waxy skin like a second, warm garment. Your feet, bare and prehensile, grip the slick, fused brick beneath you, feeling the subtle vibration of the great engines thrumming up through the soles. The perpetual twilight is not dark to your large, dark eyes; it is a world of soft edges and glowing veins, where the phosphorescent fungi on the walls paint shifting murals of green and blue light.

In your hand, you hold a hooked staff of blackened wood and slag, its weight familiar and comforting. Before you, the shallow, glassy water of the lowest plaza ripples faintly as one of the great, six-legged beasts lumbers past, its passage stirring up a cloud of silt. Its breath, a warm steam, mingles with the mist rising from your own lungs. You do not feel fear or awe at its size; you feel a sense of alignment, as if the slow, rhythmic plod of its gait is a mirror to the beat of your own heart.

The sound is a symphony you know by bone-deep instinct. The deep pulse of the central forge is the bass note, the heartbeat of the Kiln. Over it, the persistent patter of a thousand droplets, the hiss of steam from weeping pipes, the low groan of the beast, the distant, echoing clang of a hammer—all of it blends into a single, wet, breathing hum. You add your own voice to it, not a shout, but a low, resonant call to guide the beast toward the slag-cart waiting on the hydraulic rail. Another Resin-Weaver, moving with the same deliberate grace, nods from a nearby platform, their gesture a seamless part of the communication that flows through the mist.

You feel no urge to rush. Time here is measured in the thickening of a wall, the slow migration of a pool, the patient growth of a fungal crust. Your life is a cycle of maintenance and observation, of tending the engines and shaping the bricks, of harvesting the fungus and guiding the beasts. The Kiln is not a place you inhabit; it is a process you are part of. Your body is a tool of its formation, your breath a part of its atmosphere, your thoughts a slow, patient echo of its own becoming.

As you move along the causeway, your hand brushes the wall. The brick is cool and damp, and you feel the faintest deposit of mineral weeping from its pores—a new layer, millimeter by millimeter, fusing the structure into a monolithic whole. You are not building the Kiln; you are *growing* it, and in doing so, you are grown by it. You are a cell in its vast, wet body, a consciousness in its slow, forming dream.

And in this moment, standing in the warm, heavy mist, with the pulse of the engines in your chest and the slow, overwhelming presence of the Kiln all around you, you understand: you are not lost. You are home, in a place that is not built, but born, one damp, patient breath at a time.
