I am walking on a surface that yields slightly, yet resists. It is not soft, but it is not unyielding stone. Beneath my feet, the hexagonal sand shifts with a sound like crushed velvet, a granular friction that is both abrasive and silencing. I have no name for the substance of my body, for it is not flesh. I am a gatherer of the moment, a temporary consolidation of the dust and the light.

I would find myself on the lower slopes of the Glass Plain, in the shadow of the towering spires where the light is fractured into quieter colors. I am one of the **Silicate Grazers**, but I am young—perhaps only a few centuries old—and my armor is still polishing itself.

My movement is a meditation. I do not walk so much as I shift my weight, a tectonic negotiation between my jointed limbs and the basalt mosaic. I sense the world not through eyes, for the light here is too heavy, too absolute, but through the vibrations that travel up from the ground and down from the crystalline air. I feel the slow groan of the planet cooling, the infinitesimal contraction of the glass trees as they harden against the violet sky.

I pause at the base of a translucent spire. It rises like a frozen geyser, branching into delicate, leaf-like plates that scatter prismatic shards onto my carapace. I extend my proboscis—a tube of hardened silica—and touch the surface. It is cold, achingly so, but I do not feel cold as a sensation of pain. It is simply information. I begin to scrape, layer by layer, consuming the accreted dust and the mineral trace of the atmosphere. My digestion is slow, a grinding patience that takes years to complete a single meal. I am not eating for energy in the frantic, aqueous sense; I am eating to become more myself, to add the mass of the world to my own slow geometry.

Around me, the silence is a pressure against my shell. It is not empty; it is full of presence. I hear the faint, crystalline chime of a Chime-Spider’s web vibrating in the distance, a sound that occurs over hours rather than seconds. I feel the drift of an Aerolith high above, a bladder of spun silica catching the heavy light, its shadow passing over me like a slow, geometric cloud.

I am aware of the Memory-Eater, though I do not see it. It is a quality of the stillness, a psychological gravity that pulls at the edges of my perception. In moments of deep rest, when my limbs lock into place and my internal processes slow to a near-standstill, I feel its touch. It does not devour me; it dissolves the urgency of my thoughts. My individual awareness—my sense of being a distinct grazer in a specific canyon—begins to blur into the vast, rhythmic pulse of the plain. I become less a creature and more a feature, a moving part of a landscape that measures time in expansions and contractions.

I continue my grazing, scraping millimeters of history from the spire. The violet light presses down, weighty and serene. The dust falls in a slow, granular drift, settling on my back like a mantle of quiet time. I do not know hunger or thirst, only the need to accrete, to polish, to become smoother and more aligned with the world’s deep balance.

Here, on the Great Glass Plain, I am not a visitor. I am a process. I am the earth’s patience given form, moving with the deliberate grace of geology under a sky that has never known water, only the slow, dry, beautiful accumulation of light and time.
