I am a **Moss-Gnawer**.

I wake not with the sun, for the sun does not strike this bowl, but with the shift in pressure as the cloud-ceiling presses lower against the mountain’s shoulder. I uncurl from a ball of damp slate skin and prehensile tail, my small, webbed feet finding purchase on the slick root of a Vein-Fern. The air is thick, tasting of wet iron and the slow decay of forgotten metal. It is a taste I know as well as my own name, though I have no name.

I live in the cracks, in the spaces between the rust and the root. My world is one of verticality and depth—down into the saturated soil where the Chitin-Worms hum their slow, grinding songs, and up into the tangled canopy where the Glass-Winged Midges refract the green-tinged light into fleeting rainbows. I am a creature of the in-between, a tenant of the Sump’s perpetual damp.

My day begins with a drink. I slide from my perch into the nearest channel, the water warm against my skin, oxygen-rich from the seepage below. I absorb it through my pores, a slow exchange that leaves me feeling heavier, more rooted. The water here is alive with the faint charge of the old machinery, a metallic hum that vibrates through my bones. I taste it, I breathe it, I become it.

Then, I feed. I emerge at dusk, when the light fades to indigo, and I graze on the aggressive mosses that carpet the basin floor. They are tough, mineral-laced, and stubborn—perfect for my teeth, which are flat and grinding. I move carefully, my tail curling around slick roots for stability, my large, pale eyes scanning the shadows for the mottled bulk of the Sump-Lurker. I do not see it often, but I feel it—a stillness in the drip-pattern, a shift in the air’s humidity as it passes.

I am not alone, though I am solitary. The Midges chime around the rusted gears, their wings a soft vibration that blends with the percussion of drips. The Chitin-Worms beneath me sing their grinding song, their phosphorescent trails glowing faintly in the dark soil. The Vein-Ferns sway in the slow breath of the earth, their leaves heavy with condensation. We are all here, all part of the Sump’s slow, wet cycle.

I am small, I am quiet, I am persistent. I do not fight the Sump; I collaborate with it. I am the patient pulse beneath its perpetual sigh, the living proof that even in a place forgotten by time, life finds a way to breathe, to feed, and to endure.

I am a Moss-Gnawer, and this is my home.
