You would find yourself standing on a high, wind-scoured shelf of shale, the horizon a soft, indefinite smudge where the pale grey earth dissolves into an equally pale grey sky. The air is thin and bitingly cold, carrying the scent of damp stone and the faint, earthy smoke of peat. Beneath your feet, the rock is a mosaic of sharp edges and smooth, worn expanses, its deep cold seeping upward through the soles of your boots. You are not lost; the concept feels foreign here. The scale is too vast for direction to hold meaning. Instead, you are simply *present*, a small, warm point in an immense, cold system.

You are one of the Hollow’s people. You are not a king, a warrior, or a prophet. You are a Steward of the Seep.

Your body is compact, insulated by layers of woven fiber and the thick, shaggy hide of a great-mammal, its heat-retaining properties a second skin against the conductive cold. Your face is pale, not from a lack of sun, but from a life lived in its silver-grey reflection, and your eyes are narrowed, accustomed to reading the subtle language of frost and stone. You move with a deliberate, economical grace, each step tested before your weight settles, your senses attuned to the slightest tremor in the ground or shift in the wind’s sigh.

Your dwelling is not a building so much as a niche carved into the folded land, its doorway low and its window a mere slit facing the prevailing wind. Inside, the air is still and marginally warmer, smelling of stone, fire-stone, and the slow, steady burn of rendered fat in a shallow clay lamp. Your tools lie on a shelf of fitted shale: a bone needle threaded with sinew, a stone blade needing resharpening, a woven basket for gathering the jewel-toned lichens that are your primary source of color and, in a lean season, nutrition.

Your life is measured in the flow of water. You are a Steward of the Seep, a keeper of one of the slow, syrupy trickles that descend from the high collection basins into the inhabited fissures. Your days are spent in quiet observation, mapping the path of a single drop over weeks, clearing minute blockages of frost or grit from the stone conduits, ensuring the precious liquid reaches the community’s central cistern. It is work that requires no haste, only patience and a deep, intuitive understanding of the land’s hydrology. You speak little, but you listen constantly—to the wind, to the stone, to the subtle changes in the frost patterns that signal a coming thaw or a deepening freeze.

You are an artist of stillness. In the long twilight, you might join others in a dwelling to create music not of melody, but of resonance: the soft, rhythmic tap of stone on stone, the low hum produced by rubbing a prepared hide over a resonant rock face. Your stories are not of grand heroes, but of the slow, patient unfolding of a single frost-crystal’s life, or the journey of a water-drop from basin to fissure. You understand that time is not a river but a glacier, and you move with it, your consciousness a quiet, observant part of the Hollow’s vast, cold, beautiful system.
