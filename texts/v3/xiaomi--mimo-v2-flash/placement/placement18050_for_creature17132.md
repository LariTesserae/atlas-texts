You are walking, but you are not a visitor. You are a part of the pressure that polished the floor.

You find yourself in the Interstice, but you are not looking at it from the outside. You are inside its skin, standing in a sunken plaza that feels less like a room and more like a lung. The air is not merely humid; it is viscous, a warm, silvery suspension that coats your throat and beads on your eyelashes. The light is not above you; it is beneath you. The pearlescent clay of the floor emits a soft, bioluminescent glow—a cold, milky radiance that filters up through the soles of your feet and illuminates the world from below, casting no harsh shadows, only deep, rounded hollows.

You are standing on a broad, shallow ledge that curves along the wall of a vast, vaulted cistern. This is the heart of the Interstice. The ceiling here is lost in a damp, dripping darkness, but the floor is alive. Before you, the fungal mats spread like a thick, velvety carpet, their pale surfaces threaded with glowing veins of green and blue algae. The air smells of wet stone, of yeast, of the faint, sweet rot of decay that is not death, but transformation.

You are not human, not in the way you understand the term.

You are a Keeper of the Mycelium. Your body is a testament to the Interstice’s geometry. You are compact, your spine flexible, your limbs short and strong, evolved for navigating low archways and clinging to curving walls. Your skin is hairless and slick, the color of wet clay, and it gleams with a faint, oily sheen of algae dust. You are draped in a single, felted sheet of algae-fiber, dyed a deep, glowing indigo with pigments scraped from the cistern walls. It clings to you, warm and water-resistant, breathing with the mist.

Your eyes are large and pale, almost translucent, and they do not blink often. You do not need to. You see not in colors, but in textures and the intensity of the internal glow. You can discern the tension of a fungal rope from twenty paces, the subtle shift in the clay’s sheen that signals a new growth, the faint, greenish pulse of the water as it condenses on the ledges.

You are sitting, because in the Interstice, sitting is the default state. Your hands are never still. In your lap, you hold a length of string-vine, and your fingers are weaving it into a net. The movement is rhythmic, precise, a language spoken through touch. The net will be used to harvest the blind, pale fish that swim in the central cistern, or perhaps to carry a bundle of dried fungi to a neighbor. It is a tool, but it is also a prayer, a physical mantra that connects you to the breath of the basin.

Around you, the Interstice hums with life. The bass note is the constant drip of condensation, a steady, metronomic pulse. The treble is the high, chittering call of the cricket-like insects that crawl on the fungal mats, their bodies pale and blind. The human sound is a low, resonant murmur: the soft tap of a clay-modeller’s mallet somewhere in a distant corridor, the rasp of a fungal-wood saw, the rhythmic slap of a hand-weaver’s loom. There is no silence here, only a dense, layered cacophony of craft and life.

You are not a builder, not a hunter, not a miner. You are a Keeper. Your role is to tend the mycelium, to ensure the fungal mats spread evenly, to harvest the glowing algae that feeds the cisterns, and to repair the clay walls when they become too worn. You are a custodian of the skin.

To your left, a narrow channel carved into the floor carries a thin stream of mist-collected water toward a central cistern. The water is not for drinking—it is too mineral-rich, too full of life—but for the ecosystem. You dip your fingers into the stream, feeling the cool, silken flow. The water seems to have a faint, greenish glow, as if it carries the light of the fungal veins within it.

To your right, a sleeping niche is carved into the plinth of a low archway. It is curved, smooth, and warm, just large enough for one body to curl into. The clay here is polished to a mirror sheen, not by tools, but by generations of bodies pressing against it. You can feel the history in the curve, the memory of a thousand ancestors who have slept here, their warmth absorbed into the clay, their presence still lingering in the smoothness of the stone.

Ahead, a group of Hunters passes by, moving with a silent, shuffling gait. Their spears are made of hardened mycelium, tipped with sharpened stone, and they carry the pale, furred moles they have trapped. They do not speak, but they nod at you, their large, pale eyes catching the glow of the floor. You nod back, your hands never stopping their work. The moles are not prey; they are part of the settlement, their burrows aerating the clay, allowing the fungal mycelium to breathe. To harvest them is to perform a transaction with the organism of the Interstice, done with quiet gratitude.

You are not afraid of the dark. There is no dark here, only a soft, silvery glow that emanates from the clay itself. You are not afraid of the damp, the constant mist, the weight of the vaulted ceiling above. You are not afraid of erosion. You embrace it. When a section of the wall becomes too worn, you will harvest the material, mix it with fresh fungal paste, and re-apply it to a new growth. The Interstice is a single, interconnected organism, and you are a cell within it, a custodian of its skin.

As you weave, you feel the clay beneath you breathe. The mist rises and falls, the fungal mats spread and are harvested, the moles burrow and are caught, and the people move through the dense, warm, damp, loud, and beautiful maze they have made. You are one of them, a Keeper of the Mycelium, living in the folds of a vast, clay body, your face lit by the soft, internal luminescence of the world you have grown.
