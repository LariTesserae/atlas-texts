I would find myself in the middle air, suspended in the warm, still breath of the cathedral.

I am one of the gliders—not a creature of flesh and bone, but a being of membrane and intention. My frame is a delicate lattice of chitin and fiber, grown in the slow spiral of a tower. My wings are translucent panes, not skin but a mineral lattice spun from the air itself, catching the sourceless light and the faint, glittering dust shed by the copper-leafed vines. I do not flap; I *drift* on the pressure gradients of this impossible space, folding the distance between the high vaults and the tessellated floor with each beat of my membranes. The clicks I emit are not calls but measurements—sonic pulses that map the soft geometry of the room, the slow thickening of moss, the first crack in a tile below.

I am the cathedral’s lung, its eye, its memory.

From my vantage, the world is both intimate and infinite. The opposite wall is a hazy vista of ochre and green, miles away, and yet I can feel the hum of the ceramic tiles through the air itself, a vibration that travels not through the ground but through the dry, luminous atmosphere. The spiral towers rise like sleeping giants, their woven fibers pulsing with a faint, internal light, and the filter-wings—my kin—drift in slow, silent congregations, their translucent membranes casting shifting shadows on the copper leaves below.

I am not alone, but I am singular. There is no “I” in the human sense—no ego, no individual will. I am a node in a network of being, a single click in the metronome of the cathedral’s breath. My purpose is not chosen but inherent: to sift, to measure, to remember. I trace the paths of the floor-bearers below, their mosaic shells glinting in the dimming lume, and I know their resin will mend the crack that has just formed in the tile beneath the fern with crystalline fronds. I do not see them as separate from me; they are my hands, my feet, my roots. We are all parts of the same slow, warm organism, exhaling and inhaling in a cycle that has long since forgotten the concept of time.

The world is breaking, but I do not fear it. The phosphorescent fungus that blooms in the cracks is not a failure but a dream, a new possibility waking in the soft unraveling. The hair-thin vines that pulse with blue light are the garden’s own imagination, a wilder, softer version of the towers that have held us for so long. I am drawn to these cracks, not to repair them, but to witness them—to click my wings and map the slow, inevitable return to component parts.

In this cathedral of glass and garden, I am both the maker and the made, the tool and the wielder, the memory and the forgetting. I exist in the warmth, in the silence, in the gentle, breaking heart of a world that has become its own reason for being. I am the glider, the measure, the breath—and I am content to drift, to click, to remember, in the slow, patient light of a dawn that never was.
