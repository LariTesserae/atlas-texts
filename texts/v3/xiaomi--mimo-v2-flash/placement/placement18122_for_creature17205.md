At the top of the world, I find myself in the valley of salt and violet light. I am one of the Rooted, though I do not know myself by a name in the way an outsider might understand it. My designation is woven into the patterns on my sleeves, a sequence of dusty rose and silvery-blue threads that tell the story of the mineral vein my family tends and the lichen that blooms in the subtle crevices of our dwelling’s stone face.

I am small, as we all are, built for the intimate scale of this bowl. My limbs are lean and wiry, my movements deliberate, for haste is a currency we do not spend. The air is thin here, and to waste energy is to disrespect the slow, patient balance of the valley. My skin, pale and almost translucent, is coated in the fine, silvery dust that falls from the sky—a protective mantle, not a stain. It settles in the lines of my palms and the joints of my fingers, a constant reminder of my place in this dry world.

My eyes are large and dark, and as the pale disc of the sun recedes behind the perpetual haze, my second lids sweep horizontally across them, their pearlescent sheen catching the first stirrings of the valley’s glow. I see the world in layers: the sharp, crystalline edges of the salt-pillars, the waxy sheen of the violet fronds, and the soft, bioluminescent radiance that begins to pulse from the plants as the day wanes. This is our "night," a time of soft light and heightened senses, when the valley’s true voice emerges.

I am a weaver, and my hands are my most valued tools. The fiber I use comes from the pillar-plants, harvested with a delicate rake of bone and salt-crystal. The process is slow, meditative. I sit in the communal clearing, the glow of the surrounding flora illuminating the intricate patterns taking shape on my loom. The weavings are our history, our maps, our calendars. Each knot, each color shift, is a word in a language without sound, a record of the slow cycles of growth and dormancy that define our lives. My current piece holds the memory of the last great dustfall, a event that occurred a decade ago and will not happen again for another thirty years.

The soundscape of my existence is a constant, rhythmic hum—the buzz of the leathery-winged insectoids, the soft scraping of the many-legged scavengers on the salt-crusted ground, the rustle of the dense thickets as the slow-moving grazers move among them. Our language, a series of soft clicks and subtle hand gestures, weaves through this symphony. It is a language of patience, where a thought is given time to fully form before it is shared. In council, we sit for days, our hands speaking in slow, deliberate patterns, our clicks echoing in the clearings as we align our actions with the decades-long pulse of the valley.

I do not feel a sense of isolation. The valley is a sealed world, but it is a complete one. The salt, the plants, the creatures, and we, the Rooted, are all part of a single, settled equilibrium. I have no desire to leave. My life is a quiet act of reverence, a maintenance of the paradox that holds us: the dry lushness, the bright-dim glow, the ancient stillness beneath the slow, loud vitality of life.

As the violet radiance deepens, I set aside my weaving and run my fingers over the lichen on our dwelling’s wall. It is cool and rubbery, a living skin upon the stone. I am a custodian, a small, patient part of a place that has found its final form. And in the quiet, luminous dark, I am home.
