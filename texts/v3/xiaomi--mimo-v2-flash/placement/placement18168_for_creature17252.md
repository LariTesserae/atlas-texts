I am sitting on the edge of the flat roof, knees drawn up to my chest, a shallow bowl of stone in my lap. The sun has just dipped below the western rim of the basin, and the light is fading from ochre to gray. My hands are stained with the reddish clay I’ve been mixing for the evening’s repair work; it’s cool against my skin, a familiar grit beneath the fingernails.

I am small—wiry, like the rest of them—and my skin carries the same palette as the walls around me: pale brown, weathered smooth, with the faintest flush high on my cheekbones from the cool air. I wear a tunic of grass-fiber, soft from wear and mended at the shoulder where the seam had frayed last season. A smooth river-stone hangs from a thong around my neck, a gift from the Dewcatcher when I was old enough to scrape the roofs myself. It rests warm against my sternum, a weight that reminds me of the basin’s constancy.

My name is Lowroof. It is not a name I chose—it is the name that fits, as it fits all of us. When I was young, I slept on the lowest roof in the hamlet, the one nearest the spring, where the condensation gathers thickest in the morning. Now I sleep in the same place, but the name has stayed, as names do here. It is a description, not a title. It is enough.

The air is thin but not biting; it carries the scent of stone cooling after the day’s brief warmth, and faintly, the sharpness of the herbs that grow in the cracks between the walls below. I can hear the wind moving through the alleys, a soft hiss that is the basin’s constant breath. Somewhere to my left, a wren calls once, a quiet chirp that is swallowed almost immediately by the quiet. The spring bubbles at the northern edge, the sound of it barely audible but always present, like the heartbeat of the place.

I have been scraping dew from the roof for an hour, my flat stone moving in slow arcs across the tar-sealed surface. The dew is already forming on the shaded side of the wall below; by morning, it will bead on the stone and collect in the cracks. I will gather it before the sun clears the rim, as I do every day. The water from the spring is for drinking, for mixing clay, for the herbs. The dew is for softening the thatch of the grass-mats we use to seal the windows in the colder nights, for moistening the seeds we plant in the cracks, for the rare bath when the basin feels too dry. Every drop is accounted for.

I pause, setting the bowl aside, and look out over the basin. The twenty structures are huddled in the stone bowl, their flat roofs level with my own. From here, the settlement looks like a patch of the earth itself—no grandeur, no ambition, just the practical shapes of survival. The walls are low, the alleys narrow, the edges where stone meets clay blurred by generations of foot traffic. The twisted trees at the outer edges are stunted, their roots crawling shallow over the rock, but they are alive. They hold on.

I do not think about the world beyond the basin. There is no road out, only footpaths that wander up the slopes and disappear into the low scrub. The people who might live out there—if they exist at all—are not a concern. The basin is complete; it has everything it needs. The people are complete; they have everything they need. There is no want, only the quiet rhythm of maintenance.

Tonight, after the last of the dew is scraped and the clay is mixed for tomorrow’s repairs, I will sit by the spring with the others. We will drink the cold water and speak in short syllables about the state of the roofs, the growth of the herbs, the timing of the next rain. The words will hang in the air like condensation, and then we will move to our sleeping places—some on roofs, some in alcoves carved into the stone—and let the darkness fill the basin.

The stars will be sharp and close, but there will be no moon. The darkness will be complete, and the quiet will deepen. I will sleep wrapped in my grass-fiber blanket, listening to the wind, feeling the stone beneath me, and knowing that tomorrow will be the same as today.

This is my place. This is who I am. I am Lowroof, a mender, a keeper of the cracks, a part of the basin’s equilibrium. I do not want to leave. I do not need to. The stone is settled, and so am I.
