I am a **Stone-Shaper**, and this is my world.

My name, if you could call it that, is a vibration felt through the soles of the feet—a low hum that speaks of granite and patience. I am standing on the eastern ledge of the First Terrace, watching the morning seep begin. The "sun" here is a pale diffusion, a faint silver wash that filters down through the twisting chimneys of the upper world, barely enough to define the edges of the slate slabs before me. To my eyes, which drink the dark, it is bright enough.

I am four feet tall, my skin the color of wet river clay, cool to the touch even as the air around me remains perpetually damp. My pale eyes, lacking pupils, do not squint; they expand, taking in the subtle shifts of luminescence from the fungal gardens clinging to the cliff face below. I am dressed in layers of woven reed-fiber, dyed the deep green of moss, the fabric heavy with moisture but breathable, a second skin against the chill that seeps not from the air, but from the stone itself.

My hands—long-fingered, calloused at the tips but sensitive enough to feel the grain of a rock—are resting on the ledge beside me. I am not merely looking; I am listening.

The Hollows are never silent. To the untrained ear, it is a quiet place, a velvet void filled only by the metronomic *drip-drip-drip* of water falling into hidden cisterns. But to a Stone-Shaper, the Hollows are a chorus. I can hear the high, brittle chatter of quartz veins settling in the ceiling above; the wet, sucking sigh of peat expanding in the crevices; the distant, rhythmic scrape of a neighbor carving a new dwelling niche three levels down.

I am assessing a fracture line in the granite slab before me. It is a clean break, sharp and angular, typical of the raw geometry of this place. Erosion here is in its infancy—a creative force, not a destructive one. The edges of the slate are still keen, holding the memory of the deep earth. My task today is not to break the stone, but to coax it.

In the surface world, they dig with violence—shovels and picks, explosives and fire. Here, we use the patience of water. I reach into the pouch at my belt and withdraw a wedge of dried ironwood. It is small, no larger than my thumb. I dip it into the clay bottle hanging from my neck, saturating it with water drawn from the deep cisterns. With a delicate touch, I insert the wedge into the hairline fracture. Over the next few days, the wood will expand, gently prying the stone apart with a force that is slow, inevitable, and silent. It is the way of the Silt-Kin: we do not conquer the stone; we invite it to open.

Below me, the Hollows spread out in layers of terraced habitation. From this vantage, I see only the topology of life. Dwellings are not built but excavated, doorways low and arched, following the natural fissures of the rock. The streets are the gaps between leaning monoliths, a labyrinth of passages where the air is thick and still. Ropes of braided reed fiber stretch across chasms, connecting levels, swaying slightly in the draft that whispers through the upper arches.

I see the glow of Hearth-Four, where the Fungus-Tenders are tending to the luminous moss beds. The light is soft, pulsating, a living luminescence that provides the only true "daylight" in the lower tiers. Near the water channels, I spot the slight, shuffling movement of a Water-Singer, their hands pressed to the stone, listening for the subterranean flow that will guide our next expansion.

There is no waste here. Every niche is occupied, every ledge utilized. The infrastructure is primitive but ingenious: clay pipes channel seepage into cisterns, polished stone reflectors bounce the dim light into darker corners, and bone awls hang from every belt. The technology of the Silt-Kin is one of twine, wood, and observation.

I shift my weight, my wide, splayed feet gripping the slick stone. The dampness is not a discomfort; it is a condition of existence. It is in my lungs, in my skin, in the very taste of the air—wet rock and cold earth.

As I turn back to my work, a vibration travels up through the soles of my feet. It is subtle, a low hum from the deep galleries where the elders sit in council. It is not a call for alarm, but a steady pulse—the rhythm of consensus, the murmur of the community deciding the pace of the next harvest or the carving of a new terrace.

I close my pale eyes for a moment, expanding them to catch the faint reflection of a polished slate shard I have set in the wall beside me. It catches a glint of the distant surface light, flashing a brief signal to the Shaper on the opposite ledge. A response flickers back: *All is well. The stone is quiet. The water flows.*

I return to the fracture line, running my fingers along the sharp edge. The Hollows are still learning their final shape, and I, a Silt-Kin Stone-Shaper, am here to listen, to guide, and to wait.

The stone will open. In time.
