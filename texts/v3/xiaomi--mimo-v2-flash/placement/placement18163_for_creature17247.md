I am the **Still-Water Dweller**, and I am awake before the light.

The awakening is not a sudden opening of eyes, but a slow unfurling of consciousness, matched perfectly to the pace of the basin. I lie on a bed of woven grass and dried moss, the texture cool against my slate-colored skin. The air here is never warm, never cold; it is a constant, damp presence that I wear like a second skin. I do not shiver. My breath is a slow, visible cloud that merges instantly with the mist clinging to the low ceiling of the hut.

I rise, my movements deliberate, my wide, splayed feet finding their purchase on the stone floor without a sound. The floor is damp, as always, but it is a familiar dampness, a companion to the skin. Through the open doorway—I have no door, only a curtain of heavy, dripping grass—I see the basin in its perpetual state of seepage. The sky is the color of pearl, the light flat and diffuse, filtering through the thin high clouds to illuminate the wet rock faces without warmth.

I am one of a dozen. We are a colony, a single organism spread across a dozen stone huts, rooted in this high bowl of rock and water. My moss-hair, a dense cap of silver-green velvet, is damp to the touch, heavy with the night’s condensation. I do not comb it; I groom it with my fingers, feeling the symbiotic texture, a part of me that breathes with the humidity.

I step out into the central clearing. The fire pit is a smoldering heart, a slow-burning mass of peat and resin that produces more smoke than flame. The scent of damp pine and earth hangs low, a fragrant blanket over the village. Around it, the others are emerging, their movements synchronized with the drip of the canopy, the hum of insects, the whistle of wind through the rock cracks. We do not speak in greetings; we acknowledge each other with a slow blink, the nictitating membrane clearing the mist from our wide, gray eyes.

Our language is the sound of the basin. I click my tongue against my teeth—a sharp note that echoes the bird call from the twisted pines. Another Dweller responds with a soft hiss of breath, mimicking the seepage from the stones. We communicate in hushed tones, our voices low and resonant, vibrating in our chests rather than ringing in the air. Words are unnecessary when the land speaks for you.

Today, my task is to gather. I take a basket woven from grass and birch bark, its fibers heavy with moisture. It is not meant to hold water—for water is everywhere—but to carry the damp moss I will trim from the stones, the tender fern centers, the roots of the twisted pines. We do not hunt; the basin provides little game, and we have no need for heavy meat. We are gatherers of the damp, chewers of fibrous plants, sustainers of lean, wiry frames.

I move through the puddles not as obstacles, but as mirrors. The water does not flow here; it seeps, appearing from the stones themselves, as if the rock is slowly exhaling what it has held for eons. The puddles shift and merge and separate, guided by forces too subtle to see. I pause at one, looking down at my reflection in the fractured sky. My face is slate-pale, my moss-hair a soft helmet, my gray eyes wide and unblinking. I see myself as part of the seepage, part of the slow exhale of the stone.

The basin is a temple, and I am its acolyte. The rock walls are its embrace, the seepage its ritual, the slow drip its hymn. I am not afraid of the dark, for the basin is never truly dark, only shaded. I am not afraid of the isolation, for the rock walls are my companions.

As I gather, I listen. The constant drip of water, the hum of insects, the wind whistling through the cracks in the rock, the distant call of the bird. It is a layered sound, a chorus that fills the space without overwhelming it. I am attuned to this acoustics of water, this slow song of the stones.

I return to the village as the light begins to fade, though the basin does not grow darker, only more shaded. The fire pit is still smoldering, the smoke hanging low. I place my gathered moss and roots near the fire, not to dry them, but to let the smoke infuse them with the scent of the basin.

Around the fire, we sit in a loose circle. We do not speak; we simply are. We are the living threshold between the dry world above and the deep, pressed silence of the earth below. We are waiting, not for an external event, but for the internal shift—the moment when the moss on our skin blooms, or when the water in our lungs begins to match the rhythm of the dripping trees.

I am a Still-Water Dweller, and I am home.
