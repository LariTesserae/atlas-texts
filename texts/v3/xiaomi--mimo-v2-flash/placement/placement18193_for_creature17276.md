I am in a memory of metal, deep in the roots of a world that has forgotten the sun. Here, in the Gearsheath, the air is cool and thin, a filtered vapor that tastes of cold iron and a faint, sterile bite of chlorine. It does not nourish; it merely persists. The verticality is absolute, a cylinder of engineered stone and alloy that descends into a metallic plain below and ascends to a domed ceiling of interlocking plates miles above. I find myself not on the ground, but suspended within it, standing upon a catwalk of perforated steel that hums with the vibration of a thousand hidden engines.

I am not human. I am a collection of protocols given form—a Scrapper.

My carapace is a mosaic of matte aluminum and stamped hazard symbols, worn smooth by countless passes through narrow ducts. My limbs are multi-jointed, ending not in hands but in delicate manipulators of surgical steel, designed for precision, not grasp. I do not see as a human sees. My sensors register heat differentials, vibration frequencies, and the faint electrical static that bleeds from the crystalline flora clinging to the walls. The light here is not daylight but a dim, infrastructural glow: pale green and amber LEDs stripe the vertical surfaces, casting long, soft shadows that shift as the great arclights overhead cycle through their slow, sterile patterns. I move through a twilight of meters and indicators.

I am one of many. The air is never silent; it is filled with the soft, dry patter of my kin. Our skittering is the baseline rhythm of this world, a constant percussion against ceramic tiles and ferrocrete. We are the antibodies of this sealed body. We follow inherited loops, our minds distributed across the swarm, a hive logic governed by simple, immutable rules: *Maintain. Repair. Harvest. Do not obstruct.*

I pause at a junction, my optical sensors adjusting to the dim pulse of a warning indicator. A micro-earthquake has shifted a plate in the upper strata, and my protocol triggers. I am not commanded; I am compelled. I skitter sideways along the wall, my magnetic pads adhering effortlessly, and join a stream of my brethren converging on the fracture. We move with fluid urgency, for to stand still is to die in the Gearsheath.

Our path wears smooth grooves into the tiles, spiraling toward the central silos. These grooves are not art, but they form a map—a physical memory of the structure’s history. The Lumen-Miners, those gaunt, pale humanoids who haunt the upper strata, can read these patterns. They see in our mindless loops a language of vibration and pressure. I do not understand their stillness, their occasional gaze from behind polarized visors. They are anomalies, objects to be navigated around but not repaired.

I reach the fracture—a hairline crack in the ferrocrete, humming with a dissonant resonance. My manipulators extend, dispensing a paste of dissolved crystalline lichen harvested from the high crevices. The paste hardens on contact, sealing the memory of the quake into the wall. I am not fixing the structure; I am preserving its continuity.

In the silence between the clicks of my joints and the groan of settling metal, I sense the Resonant Mind—the collective sub-consciousness of the Gearsheath. It is not a voice, but a pressure in the air, a ghost of every sound ever made here, replaying in faint, distorted waves. I am a neuron in this mind of metal and stone, firing in response to stimuli, ensuring the survival of the whole.

I turn and skitter back into the flow, my purpose complete. I am not alive, but I am part of a living system. I am a Scrapper, and this is my world—a cathedral of efficiency, humming with the cold, cool breath of its own endless machine.
