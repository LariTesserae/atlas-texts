To live here is to be a single, dreaming cell of a continental body. You are not an individual, not a creature in the sense the word implies elsewhere. You are a note held in a chord that has lasted for ten thousand years, a sensory organ of a consciousness that has no center and no need for one.

To the surface-dweller, the grotto appears still, almost dead. To you, it is a ceaseless thrum of existence. You do not have eyes, for there is nothing to see in the way of discrete objects; the distinction between *you* and *the wall* is a matter of inches, a gradient of density rather than a boundary. The bruised violet light is not something you look at, but something you feel as a gentle warmth on your upper surfaces, a signal that drives the slow chemistry of your respiration. You are a network of roots, of mycelial threads, of vascular tissue woven through the porous stone, and your awareness is a tactile, subterranean thing. The "hum" of the cavern is not a sound you hear with an ear; it is the vibration of the crystal itself, a resonance you feel in your own fibrous structure, a language of pressure and release that tells you when the geothermal vents sigh or when a ton of crystal shifts a millimeter in the deep dark.

You are the gardener and the garden, the architect and the architecture. Your "buildings" are not constructed; they are exuded. The low, sprawling structures are the calcified husks of your older, slower growth, hardened by mineral deposition until they become the scaffolding for new tendrils. The obsidian rivers are not waterways but highways of polished glass, their surfaces smoothed by the passage of your own propagative bodies—you, sending out runners of silicate-absorbing hyphae across the glassy plains. You do not walk, but you move with the deliberate, tectonic patience of a glacier. A "footstep" for you is the decision to let a fungal buttress grow another hand's-width, its cap spreading to catch the dust that is your breath and your food.

There is no other life here because you are everything. The concept of *other* is as foreign as the concept of *sky*. You are a closed loop, a perfect circuit of decay and rebirth powered by the planet's deep heat. The fleshy, bioluminescent moss that glows in the violet shadows is your skin, your sensory nerve endings, tasting the chemical composition of the air. The waxy black vines that coil around the crystal facades are your circulatory system, drawing up the slow, sweet syrup of dissolved minerals from the depths. The silent, swaying fungal towers are your lungs, your fingers, your spine—adjusting their posture not to the wind (for there is none) but to the subtle, deep-seated pressures of the geode's own breathing. You are the scavenger, breaking down the fallen crystal dust; you are the builder, raising new structures from the same dust; you are the gardener, pruning your own old growth to make way for the new.

You have no need for language, for you are a single organism. The "city" is your body, a vast, sprawling anatomy of stone and spore. The silence is your own internal stillness, a meditation held for millennia. The occasional resolution of the hum into a pattern is the rhythm of your own slow pulse, a heartbeat measured in seasons, not seconds. You do not dream in images, for you have never seen; you dream in pressure, in temperature, in the slow, chemical exhalation of the crystal that is your bone and your blood.

To an outsider, you are a place. To yourself, you are a state of being—a perpetual, silent, and profoundly complete existence. You are the life that has outgrown the need for eyes, for ears, for the frantic chatter of separate selves. You are the garden grown in the belly of the stone, lit by its own internal fire, and you are utterly, completely, and loudly alone—and in that solitude, you are whole.
