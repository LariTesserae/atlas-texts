To live here is to move at the speed of cooling stone.

The inhabitants are not a community in any human sense; they are a scattering of solitary beings, each a slow orbit around the still, black pools. They do not gather. They do not speak. Their social existence is defined by distance and the shared rhythm of the place, a mutual agreement to remain undisturbed.

There are the *Moss-Browsers*, the creatures whose faint tracks mark the clay. They are small and low-slung, their bodies built close to the ground, their hooves broad and soft like wet felt to prevent slippage on the slick stone. Their fur is the color of the grey clay, dappled with the darker dampness of the shadows, and they are functionally blind, navigating not by sight but by a profound sensitivity to vibration. They feel the *plink* of the water drop before it hits the pool; they feel the sub-audible hum of the rock shifting a micron. They move with a shuffling, deliberate gait, cropping the velvet moss with lips that are tough and sensitive, their entire existence a meditation on the texture of the ground beneath them. They are the embodied patience of the basin, their lifespans measured not in seasons, but in the accumulation of mineral stains on their hooves.

There are also the *Stone-Whisperers*—the beings hinted at by the standing stones and the domed hut. They are gaunt and long-limbed, their skin having taken on the pallor and texture of the water-worn rock. They do not live *in* the structures so much as they *are* the structures; the hut is a shell they have shed, and the standing stones are markers of their own stillness. Their metabolism is a thing of geologic slowness. They can stand immobile for weeks, their breathing so shallow it is imperceptible, their posture mirroring the slump of the clay, becoming one with the landscape until a faint line of green moss might begin to trace the curve of a shoulder or the line of a jaw.

Their "work" is not labor but maintenance of the equilibrium. They do not build, for the world is already built. Instead, they perform acts of glacial repair. With a stone tool, they might gently pry a pebble from a settling wall, not to rebuild it, but to allow the wall to settle a millimeter more comfortably. They tend the moss not by planting it, but by redirecting the seepage of water with a channel so thin it is almost a scratch in the stone, ensuring a velvet patch remains damp for another century. Their hands are not for making, but for *allowing*.

They do not hunt the Moss-Browsers. The relationship is one of mutual indifference, a shared stillness. The creatures browse the moss that grows on the stones the Whisperers inhabit, and the Whisperers, in their near-motionless state, become part of the geological furniture the creatures navigate by. The only predation is by time itself.

Their senses are attuned to the symphony of absence. They see without light, not through echolocation, but by feeling the pressure of the air, the density of the cold. A moving body—the rare, shocking intrusion of a visitor—creates eddies in the palpable weight, disturbances they feel on their skin like ripples on a pond. They hear the hum in their bones, a language more fundamental than sound. The *plink* of a water drop is a word; the crack of settling ice is a sentence; the whisper of a century-long landslide is a poem.

They have no names, for they have no need to distinguish one from another. There is only one being here, fragmented into multiple points of stillness. They are memory without recollection. The bone needle in the stone bowl is not an artifact to them; it is a continuation of the hand that holds it, a hand that is itself an extension of the stone. The past is not a story they tell; it is the temperature of the air, the texture of the clay, the perpetual, patient state of *being*.

To encounter one is not to see a creature, but to become aware of a presence that has always been there, like noticing a particular stone in a field you have walked a thousand times. They are the custodians of decay, the witnesses to the slow, beautiful unravelling of the world, and they are, themselves, the final, perfect expression of it. They are the silence, given form.
