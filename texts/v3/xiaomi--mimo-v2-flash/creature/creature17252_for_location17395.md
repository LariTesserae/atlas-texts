In this basin of settled stone lives a people of similar make. They are not numerous—perhaps two hundred souls at most—and they are not a tribe in any loud sense of the word, nor a family in the blood-bound way that outsiders might expect. They are simply *the people of the basin*, a collective that has smoothed itself into the landscape so thoroughly that the distinction between the human and the mineral has begun to blur.

They are small in stature, lean and wiry, with skin weathered to the same tones as the stone that surrounds them: pale ochre, gray-brown, with the faint flush of high-altitude capillaries visible at the cheekbones. Their hands are thick-knuckled and scarred from a lifetime of mending—their fingers seem permanently coated in a fine dust of clay and ground stone. They move with a deliberate slowness, not from age, but from a kind of internal rhythm that matches the pace of the place: the drip of water, the creep of moss, the slow settling of dust.

Their clothing is woven from the fibers of the hardy grass that grows in the cracks, softened with the grease of small mammals and dyed with the rust of iron-rich clay. The garments are layered for the cool thinness of the air—tunics and wraps that hang loose, allowing the wind to pass through without stealing body heat. The colors are muted: browns, grays, the occasional streak of reddish clay where a patch has been freshly mended. There is no ornamentation, save perhaps a smooth stone worn on a thong around the neck, or a twist of dried herb tucked behind an ear.

They are a quiet people, but not a silent one. Their language is sparse, composed of short syllables that seem to hang in the air like condensation on stone. They speak softly, often in pairs, and their conversations are practical: the state of the spring, the mending of a roof, the timing of the dew harvest. They do not raise their voices; the basin carries sound well, and there is no need to shout when a whisper will do. Their names are simple—single words that describe a trait or a place: *Stonehand, Dewcatcher, Lowroof*. They answer to these names without question, as if the label and the person are the same thing.

They are a people of routine and ritual, though the rituals are so embedded in daily life that they are indistinguishable from work. Each morning, before the sun clears the basin rim, they gather at the spring to collect water in clay jars. The water is cold and clear, and they drink it slowly, as if savoring the moisture. Later, they move to the roofs, scraping dew from the tar-sealed surfaces with flat stones, collecting the precious liquid in shallow bowls. The dew is used for drinking, for softening clay, for moistening the few herbs that grow in the cracks.

Their food is sparse but sufficient: the grass seeds ground into a coarse flour, baked into flat cakes on heated stones; the occasional lizard or small bird, caught in hand-woven traps; the roots of the twisted trees, boiled into a thin soup. They harvest the herbs with care, taking only what grows in the cracks, leaving the rest to seed. They do not farm in any traditional sense—the basin does not allow it—but they tend the cracks as if they were gardens, coaxing the hardy plants to thrive in the narrow spaces.

They are a people of menders. Every structure in the basin is a testament to generations of repair: the clay patches over older layers, the stones reset in the walls, the roofs re-sealed with tar each season. They move through the settlement with an intimate knowledge of each crack and joint, as if the buildings are extensions of their own bodies. They do not build new; they maintain what is already here. There is no ambition to expand, no desire for grandeur. The basin is complete, and they are its caretakers.

They are a people of the dark. When night falls and the basin is filled with a darkness so complete it feels solid, they move with the same quiet purpose as in the light. They do not use torches or lamps—their eyes are adapted to the dimness, and the stars are bright enough to cast faint shadows. They sleep on flat roofs or in narrow alcoves carved into the stone, wrapped in their grass-fiber blankets, listening to the wind move through the alleys. The darkness is not feared; it is simply another state of being, as natural as the light.

They are a people of equilibrium. The seasons change, but only slightly—the cool thinness of the air persists, the spring level remains constant, the roofs are always repaired in the same way. They move through the same paths, mending the same walls, harvesting the same herbs. They do not speak of the past or the future; the present is enough. They do not seem to want anything more than what the basin provides: water, shelter, the company of stone and sky.

And yet, there is a sense that this is not a beginning, but an end—a people who have weathered the world and decided, quietly, to stop. They do not leave the basin; they do not seem to want to. They are stunted but alive, like the twisted trees that cling to the outer edges, their roots crawling over the rock in shallow networks, holding on rather than growing.

They are the people of the basin, and they are as settled as the stone itself.
