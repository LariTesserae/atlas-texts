The inhabitants of this place are called the Chthonic. To look upon them is to see a reflection of their home: they are small, slight, and their skin holds the pallor of things that have never known a sun. They are not albino, but have the color of mushroom flesh, of smooth, pale root, a shade that allows them to merge with the dim, blue-green glow of their surroundings. Their bodies are lean, built for navigating narrow, hollowed-out spaces and for moving with a quiet grace over the yielding mycelial mat.

Their most striking feature is their eyes. They are disproportionately large, dark, and liquid, set wide in their faces to capture the faintest glimmers of bioluminescence. There are no whites; the iris and sclera are a single, deep hue, like polished obsidian. When they cluster together in the soft light of a fungal tower, their eyes catch the glow and shine with a collective, silent intelligence, like a cluster of wet spores. They are sensitive to the subtle shifts in light—the pulsing of the towers, the chemical shimmer in the air—and they move with a purpose dictated by these slow, metabolic tides.

They are not a people of loud voices or grand gestures. Their language is a blend of soft, breathy tones and a complex system of touch. A conversation might involve one person speaking in a near-inaudible whisper while their fingers trace patterns on the other’s forearm, a tactile reinforcement of the words. The air is too full of sound—the hum of insects, the drip of water, the subsonic thrum of the city—for shouting to be effective. Their communication is intimate, designed for proximity.

Their hands are their primary tools, and they are marvels of dexterity. The fingers are long and slender, the nails hard and dark, like chitin. They are born to the craft of their world. They do not forge metal or quarry stone; they cultivate, prune, and shape. They tend to the fungal towers, carefully hollowing their interiors generation by generation, encouraging the growth of new shelves and archways. They harvest the gourds that serve as their vessels, their skins tough and waterproof. From the silk-spinning spiders that live in the porous walls of their homes, they collect conductive thread, weaving it into nets for catching glowing insects or into fine, resilient clothing that drapes like a second skin.

Their attire is simple, functional, and symbiotic. They wear tunics and leggings woven from fungal silk, dyed with pigments extracted from different strains of mycelium—deep violets, muted ochres, and soft blues that match the ambient light. They sometimes adorn themselves with the bioluminescent parts of insects, weaving the glowing antennae of moths into their hair or stringing the light-organs of beetles into necklaces that pulse faintly with their own slow rhythm. These are not for vanity, but for function; a necklace of light can illuminate a path or signal a presence in the absolute dark.

The Chthonic are communal but not crowded. Each family unit or guild occupies a single fungal tower, and the distance between towers fosters a deep sense of individuality and self-reliance. They are bound by a shared dependence on the living city, a connection so profound it is almost physical. They can feel the health of the mat beneath their bare feet, the subtle changes in the air that signal a coming spore-release, the collective pulse of the city’s respiration. To them, the city is not a place; it is a single, dreaming organism, and they are its conscious thought.

Their society is structured around this symbiosis. There are tenders of the mat, who monitor its health and harvest its smaller fruits. There are weavers, who cultivate the silk-spiders and craft the intricate textiles. There are architects, who guide the growth of the fungal towers, and there are spore-readers, a revered class who interpret the patterns of the airborne fog to predict cycles of growth and decay. Knowledge is not written but passed down through touch, through shared experience, through a lifetime of learning the city’s subtle language.

They are a people of quiet observation. They move through their dark settlement with a purpose that is both deliberate and effortless. They will pause to feel the texture of a new growth on a tower wall, to listen to the rhythm of burrowing creatures beneath the mat, to watch the faint, shifting light of the air itself. Their lives are not measured in days or years, but in the slow, germinating cycles of their world. They are the thoughts of a dreaming entity, and in their quiet, intricate lives, they give a voice to the silent, breathing heart of the city.
