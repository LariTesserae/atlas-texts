To live in the Interstice is to be born into a geometry of curves and dampness, a world without corners or horizons. The inhabitants are the physical extension of the clay they tread; they are as pale and smoothed by time as the floor beneath them.

They are a compact people, not through diminution, but through density. Generations of navigating low, vaulted corridors and clinging to the curving walls have shortened their stature and strengthened their limbs. Their spines are flexible, accustomed to the constant bowing and crouching required by the architecture. Their skin is hairless and sleek, often tinted faintly green or gray from the algae-rich dust that coats every surface. They do not wear woven cloth in the traditional sense; instead, they drape themselves in felted sheets of algae-fiber, dyed with pigments scraped from the glowing fungal veins that line the cistern walls. These garments are warm, water-resistant, and breathe with the humidity of the air.

Their eyes are the most striking adaptation. In the perpetual, diffuse silvery glow that rises from the ground, vision is less about color and more about texture and light intensity. The irises are large and pale, often appearing translucent, giving them a blind, dreamy look that is deceptive; they can discern the slightest variation in the sheen of wet clay or the tension of a dried fungal rope. There is no day or night here, only the rhythm of the mist cycles and the activity of the colony. Sleep is a communal act, taken in niches carved into the plinths of the great arches, bodies curled against the warm, living stone.

They are a people of hands. Because the Interstice is a horizontal maze of low ceilings, there is no need for the sweeping gestures of open-air dwellers. Their movements are precise and close. The primary expression of culture is not in song or dance, but in the tactile manipulation of materials. You will see them everywhere, sitting on the broad, shallow ledges that line the corridors, their hands never still. They are weaving the string-vines into nets, tending to the hanging baskets of edible fungi, or molding wet clay into new cistern walls.

Their society is organized around the "breath" of the basin. The water channels dictate the flow of life. Those who dwell near the central cisterns—often called the Heart-Miners—are the most revered, for they harvest the glowing algae and the blind fish that serve as the settlement’s spiritual and nutritional core. The foragers who roam the outer corrider shelves hunt the pale, furred moles that burrow in the clay; these hunters move with a silent, shuffling gait, their spears made of hardened mycelium tipped with sharpened stone.

Communication is a blend of soft clicks—mimicking the chirring insects—and a complex sign language used when the distance between ledges is too great for a whisper to carry. Their voices are low and resonant, never rising to a shout, as the acoustics of the vaulted clay would distort and deafen a loud noise. Instead, they rely on the constant murmur of craft, a rhythmic counterpoint to the dripping water.

They possess a collective memory that is not written but carved. Every rounded edge of a doorway, every smoothed indentation in a sleeping niche, is a record of a specific ancestor’s passage. To touch the wall is to touch the hand of a mother or a grandfather. They do not fear erosion; they embrace it as a form of communion. When a section of the clay wall becomes too worn, they do not discard it; they harvest the material, mix it with fresh fungal paste, and re-apply it to a new growth.

There is a reverence for the ecosystem that borders on the religious. They do not see a distinction between the fungus they eat, the rope they tie, and the clay they inhabit. They are all part of the same single organism. The moles are not merely prey; their burrowing aerates the clay, allowing the fungal mycelium to breathe. The insects are not pests; they are the pollinators of the underground blooms. To kill a mole is to harvest a part of the settlement itself, a transaction done with quiet gratitude rather than triumphant force.

In the Interstice, the people are not conquerors of a landscape; they are the custodians of a skin. They live in the folds of a vast, clay body, moving through the warm, damp dark with a slow, deliberate grace, their faces lit by the soft, internal luminescence of the world they have grown.
