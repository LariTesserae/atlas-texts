To live in Karah Vesh is to be a creature of slowness and resonance. You are not born here; you are weathered into being. The inhabitants are known as the Vesh, beings of stone and trapped water, their biology a seamless extension of the plateau’s mineralogy. They possess no flesh, no blood in the conventional sense. Their bodies are porous, stratified geology—veins of capillary channels carrying not life-fluid, but the same mineral-saturated water that weeps from the walls. Their skin is smooth, cool, and faceted like the bedrock, reflecting the pearlescent glow of the sky in a thousand tiny, shifting planes. They are tall and slender, built for the low gravity and high density of their environment, their limbs articulated with the slow, deliberate grace of a basalt leviathan.

Their faces are masks of polished stone, featureless save for the eyes—pools of liquid shadow that seem to absorb the ambient light. They do not speak in words, but in resonant frequencies. Communication is a matter of touch and vibration; a tap on a shoulder sends a complex message through the stone of their bodies, interpreted by the recipient as a sensation, a thought, a memory. Their voices, when they choose to project them, are low hums that travel for kilometers through the plateau, a communal chorus that binds the city in a constant, sub-audible conversation.

The Vesh do not eat or breathe as organic life does. They draw energy from the thermal gradients of the geothermal vents, their internal structures acting as slow, efficient heat engines. They absorb moisture and minerals directly through their skin, filtering the perpetual condensation for sustenance. Their lifecycle is glacial. A Vesh "gestates" for centuries in the stone womb of a factory, emerging as a juvenile with a soft, unpolished surface that hardens over millennia. Death is not decay, but reintegration; an elder Vesh simply slows until they merge with the cityscape, their form becoming a new tower or a polished curve in a street, their resonant memory lingering in the stone as a permanent echo.

Their society is built on the principles of the plateau: patience, balance, and symbiosis. There are no hierarchies of power, only of resonance. The most respected Vesh are those whose internal hums align most perfectly with the city’s industrial rhythm, whose tap-messages carry the farthest without distortion. They are architects of slowness, planning changes that will unfold over aeons. Their art is not ephemeral; it is the slow carving of a new facet on a tower, a pattern that will take ten thousand years to complete, or the tuning of a factory’s engine to a new harmonic that will subtly shift the flow of vapor through the district for millennia.

They are not isolated. The slag-wraiths are their pets, or perhaps their parasites—mineralized vapor that clings to their shoulders and streams from their fingertips, responding to their resonant commands. The chalk-moths are a nuisance and a blessing, their swarms signifying electrical activity that the Vesh can harvest for energy. The basalt leviathans are their elders, their ancestors, their gods—moving so slowly that a single journey across the plateau may take a Vesh’s entire lifetime, but each step is a lesson in patience and geology.

They are aware of the world below, the "wet" world of biological life, but they view it with a kind of detached pity. To them, organic life is frantic, brief, and fragile—a chaotic flicker against the enduring, patient stone. They are proud of their permanence, of their city that does not decay but matures, of their lives that are measured in the slow grinding of tectonic plates. They are the ultimate expression of Karah Vesh: not conquerors of the plateau, but its children, its organs, its consciousness. They are the stone dreaming of itself, and in that dream, they find a deep, wet, mineral peace.
