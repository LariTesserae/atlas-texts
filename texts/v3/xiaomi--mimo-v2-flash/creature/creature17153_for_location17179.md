Here is what lives in that place:

They are a people of angles and silence, their bodies shaped by the wind they are born into. They do not speak; to open one’s mouth to the gale is to invite the air to scour the throat raw. Instead, they communicate through the subtle cant of a shoulder, the tilt of a head, or the slow, deliberate blink of eyes that have adapted to the glare—slit-pupiled and filmed with a nictitating membrane that filters the ultraviolet glare of the high-altitude sun.

Their skin is the color of wet slate, mottled with patterns that mimic the quartz veins running through the granite. It is not soft flesh but a tough, chitinous hide, dry and cool to the touch, resistant to the constant abrasion of wind-blown grit. On their palms and soles, the skin is calloused into deep ridges, like the bark of ancient trees, giving them purchase on the slick rock faces and allowing them to move without sound across the crushing regolith.

They live not on the plateau, but within it. Their homes are the deep fissures and natural wind-shadows carved into the stone. They do not build; they excavate, following the ancient glacial striations and the softer seams of sedimentary rock that lie between the granite sheets. Their dwellings are narrow, vertical slots—just wide enough for a body to press through—spiraling down into the earth where the wind’s scream becomes a low, resonant hum and the perpetual dampness of the deep stone collects in cisterns carved with geometric precision.

Their sustenance is drawn from the fog. At dawn, they emerge from their crevices, standing immobile as the mineral-laden mist condenses on their skin. They absorb this moisture directly through their hide, a slow osmosis that sustains them. They supplement this with the hardy lichens that grow on the lee side of boulders—scraping the grey-green film with obsidian blades that never lose their edge, chipping the stone to expose the hidden growth.

They are solitary hunters, yet communal in their silence. The great raptors that circle above are their kin in a way—their eyes, too, are designed to catch the slightest movement from a mile away. They do not hunt the hyraxes or the ungulates; they observe them, tracking their migrations across the plateau as one tracks the passage of seasons in a place where time does not move. The hyrax warrens are sacred to them, not as prey, but as echo-chambers where the wind’s moan takes on a lower, more intimate frequency. They will sit at the entrance of these warrens for hours, listening to the stone sing through the earth.

Their tools are the stone itself. They do not carry them; they find them. A shard of quartz becomes a blade; a rounded pit carved by water becomes a bowl. They leave no cairns, no marks, because their presence is the mark—the slight polish on a rock face where a body has pressed against it for generations, the faint depression in the regolith where a foot has stepped a thousand times in the same path to the fog-catcher.

They are ageless, not because they do not die, but because death here is imperceptible. A body does not decay; it is scoured clean by the wind, the bones bleached white and embedded into the regolith, becoming part of the crunch underfoot. They do not mourn; they simply cease to note the presence of one who has moved from the open rock to the deeper stone.

To a visitor, they might appear as nothing more than a trick of the light—a silhouette against the glare, a shadow that moves only when the eye is not looking directly at it. They are the silence within the roar, the stillness within the eternal moment. They are the ones who have learned to breathe the thin air without lungs that burn, to hear the wind’s voice without flinching, and to exist in a world that has no memory of anything but itself.
