The city does not have a mayor; it has a wetness index.

It does not have a council; it has a consensus of humidity.

The people of the stone-and-water place are few, perhaps a hundred souls, and they are shaped by the pressure of the damp. They are not tall, for the ceilings are low and the corridors are narrow, and they have adapted to the verticality of the space. Their spines are flexible, their shoulders narrow. They move with a quiet economy, their feet placed carefully on the slick stones, their balance adapted to the subtle shifting of the ground beneath them.

They are not a single people in the way that a city of the dry lands might be. They are a collection of solitary lives that have learned to coexist in close quarters, their privacy maintained not by walls—which are shared and thin—but by a mutual agreement of silence. They speak in low tones, their voices pitched to carry in the still, damp air without echoing. Their language is soft, full of fricatives and sibilants, mimicking the sound of water seeping through stone.

Their skin is pale, not from lack of sun—though the sun is a rare guest through the constant mist—but from the mineral-rich water that clings to them. Their hands are calloused from handling stone and peat, but the callouses are soft, water-logged, and they heal slowly. They do not wear shoes; they wear wraps of woven reed that let the water drain and the air circulate. Their clothing is simple, dyed with the colors of the marsh: moss-green, slate-gray, peat-brown.

They are not farmers, for there is no soil to till. They are gatherers and tenders of the stone. They harvest moss for insulation, lichen for dye, and the occasional fern for food. They collect the water in shallow basins carved into the stone, letting it settle before drinking. They burn peat for heat and light, and the smoke from their homes is a constant, low haze that mingles with the mist.

They are not warriors, for there is nothing to defend against but the slow creep of the water and the settling of the stone. Their tools are stone and bone, shaped by friction and patience. They have no metal, no fire hot enough to melt ore, and they do not seem to miss it. Their world is one of pressure and time, not heat and force.

The weirdest thing about them is their relationship to the water. They do not see it as a separate element; it is part of their sensory world. They can tell the depth of the water table by the taste of the air. They can feel the shift of the stone through the soles of their feet. They know, without looking, when a puddle is about to tremble and release a small geyser. They are synesthetic in a way that is not mystical but practical; their lives depend on reading the subtle signals of the marsh.

They are not religious in the traditional sense, but they have a reverence for the stone. They believe the stone is alive, not in a magical way, but in a biological way. They see the weeping lines of water as the stone’s sweat, the shifting of the ground as its breathing. They do not try to control the stone; they listen to it. They build in harmony with its slow pulse.

Their homes are not just shelters; they are extensions of the stone. The walls are the stone. The floors are the stone. The roofs are slate, which is stone. They live inside the land, and the land lives inside them. When they die, they are not buried; they are placed in a crevice in the stone, and the water seeps in and the moss grows over, and they become part of the city’s slow becoming.

They are a people of the present moment, for the future is measured in the rise and fall of the water table. They do not plan generations ahead; they plan for the next season, the next tremor, the next shift in the stone. Their memory is short but deep, like a puddle that reflects the sky but holds no image when the wind passes.

They are not lonely, for they are never alone. The stone is always with them, the water is always with them, and the hundred souls are always within earshot. But they are solitary, each one a small, quiet island in the wet stone city.

They are the Marsh-Weepers, though they do not call themselves that. They have no name for themselves, for they have never needed one. They are simply the ones who live where the stone sweats and the ground breathes, and they are as much a part of the landscape as the ferns and the water-striders.

And the city continues to become, its people moving in slow rhythm with the pulse of the marsh, their lives written in the language of water and stone.
