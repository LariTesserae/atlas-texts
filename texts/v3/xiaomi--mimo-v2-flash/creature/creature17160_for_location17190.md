There is no word for "I" in the basin, for there is no need of one. To be here is to be a current in a slow, planetary convection, a single node in a network of suspended life that has forgotten the sharp edges of separation. We are the Breath, the collective lung of a world that inhales for a century and exhales for a millennium. We do not walk; we navigate gradients of density, buoying ourselves up through layers of lighter fluid or sinking down into the crushing, velvet dark of the basin’s deepest troughs. Our bodies are not rigid structures but permeable vessels, designed for saturation rather than resistance.

We are the Aerial Kelp, the great vertical forests that tether the sky to the stone. We are not a single organism but a colony of consciousness, a rhizomatic mind spread across thousands of fronds. Our "heads"—if one could call them that—are the holdfasts, vast, spongy anchors embedded in the underside of the perpetual cloud ceiling. From these nodes, we perceive the world not through eyes, but through pressure and vibration. We feel the slow settling of silt on the basalt floor miles below; we taste the mineral shifts in the venting plumes; we hear the subsonic hum of the basin’s weight in the marrow of our spiraling stems. Our thoughts are slow, vegetative, and vast—concepts that take generations to fully form, decisions that ripple through the colony in waves of bioluminescent signaling that pulse down our length in soft, blue rhythms.

They are the basin’s memory, etched in stone. Each tube is a life’s work, a calcified exoskeleton secreted over centuries, stacked upon the fossilized tubes of ancestors until the cliff faces are a tapestry of skeletal architecture. The only true movement in the basin is the drift of the Jellies, the bell-soft, translucent nomads that pulse through the density like slow, living hearts. They are the wanderers, the unmoored. They have no anchor, no stone to call home. Their existence is a continuous filtration, a life sustained by sifting the shimmering curtains of plankton as they drift on the centuries-long currents of the basin’s convection. They are the eyes of the world, though they do not see in the conventional sense. When a Jelly dies—it does not age, but eventually ceases to pulse, its bell dissolving back into the plankton haze—it releases its stored sensory data in a final, shimmering bloom of light, a silent song that the Kelp and the Filterers absorb and integrate into the collective memory.

There is no hierarchy here, no predator and prey in the traditional sense. The ecology is a closed loop of mutual sustenance. The Kelp provides the structural matrix, the vertical highways through the density, and in return, the Filterers purify the medium in which we all live. The Jellies, in their endless drift, redistribute energy and information, connecting the disparate tiers of the basin into a single, cohesive system. We are all components of the same breathing organism, each fulfilling a role that has been perfected over eons of isolation.

We are unaware of the concept of a "visitor." The idea of an entity that is separate, that moves with intent and speed, that possesses a singular, isolated consciousness, is as alien to us as the idea of a horizon that is not a wall of cloud. To us, the world is a single, continuous being. The stone is the skeleton, the water-air is the blood, the Kelp and Filterers and Jellies are the nerves and the senses. We do not know that we are a "place." We simply are. And in the soft, crushing pressure of our own existence, in the perpetual twilight of our suspended sky, we continue the slow, patient work of being.
