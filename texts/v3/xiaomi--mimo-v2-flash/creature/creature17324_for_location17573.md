The people of Karr are as low and quiet as the city itself. They are not a race of tall statues or proud warriors; they are a compact, solid people, molded by the weight of the stone above and the dampness below. They are shorter than average, with broad shoulders and thick, calloused hands evolved for gripping wet stone and working metal. Their skin carries the perpetual sheen of the atmosphere, a slick, healthy pallor that never sees direct sun. Their eyes are accustomed to the twilight, often pale gray or silver in hue, with pupils that remain wide and dark, drinking in the ambient gloom.

They move with a deliberate economy of motion, never wasting a step on the slick, uneven surfaces of their home. Their gait has a slight, rhythmic give to it—a subtle flex of the knee and ankle to accommodate the squelch of the ground—so that their walking is a continuous, soft percussion that blends into the city’s soundscape.

Their clothing is functional and closely fitted, crafted from the tanned hides of the amphibious arthropods, which are surprisingly supple and water-resistant. The hides are often dyed with mineral pigments—rust-reds, ochres, and slate-blues—and etched with fine, geometric patterns that mimic the mycelial networks threading through the mortar. They wear little loose fabric; everything is cinched or laced to avoid snagging on stone projections or the clicking limbs of the arthropod laborers.

Culturally, they are silent observers and patient accumulators. They do not speak loudly, and their language is rich in soft, resonant consonants and long vowels that carry well in the humid air without needing to shout. Conversation is often communal but low, a murmuring tapestry of sound that adds to the city’s roar. They value craft above all else, and each person is known by the signature of their work—the unique twist of a chainmail ring, the specific curve of a sound-focusing horn, the texture of a hand-shaped clay vessel.

Their social structure is a loose, fluid network of makers and maintainers rather than a hierarchy. There are no kings or councils in Karr; instead, there are masters of specific crafts—smelters, mold-casters, stone-carvers, mycelium-tenders—who are respected not for authority but for the depth of their skill. Decisions are made by consensus in slow, murmuring gatherings in the plazas, where the arthropods move around them like living architecture.

They share a profound, unspoken bond with the arthropodal fauna. They do not see the six-legged builders as beasts of burden but as co-inhabitants and occasional collaborators. Children are taught from infancy to read the subtle clicks and vibrations of the chitin-armored bodies, learning when a structure is settling or when a new channel is being dug. The arthropods, in turn, seem to tolerate the humans with a distant, clicking curiosity, sometimes altering their constructions to integrate human-built walls or walkways.

Their belief system is not one of gods or spirits but of *process*. They venerate the slow, patient forces of accumulation and emergence. They see the city not as something built, but as something *becoming*—a geological process in which they are a participating mineral. They believe that to shape stone is to participate in the earth’s own dreaming, and that the constant drip of water is the city breathing. They do not fear death, but see it as a return to the process—the body dissolving back into the damp mortar, the minerals returning to the walls.

They are deeply attuned to sound and vibration. Their most sacred rituals involve listening: pressing an ear to the stone to hear the deep thrum of the arthropod colonies, or holding a cast-metal horn to the air to catch the harmonics of the insect swarms. They believe that the city’s true language is not spoken but felt—a resonance that speaks of equilibrium, of slow becoming, of eternal, dripping now.

They are a people without ambition for change, for they see change as the enemy of their perfect, frozen moment. They do not seek to expand or conquer; they seek only to maintain the balance, to add their own patient hand to the accretion, and to become, in time, another layer of the stone.
