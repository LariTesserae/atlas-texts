In the city of the abyss, there are no citizens, only occupants.

They are known as the Sediment, though they are not of the sediment. They are the final shape of life in a place where time has stopped moving forward and instead deepens. They do not walk the passages; they occupy them. Their bodies are not distinct from the architecture but are a continuation of it—tall, slender forms of polished, glassy stone that rise from the floor as if they were stalagmites that decided to remain upright. They are the builders, though they do not build; they are the result of a building process that ended eons ago.

Their form is columnar and ritualistic. They possess no limbs in the human sense, no faces, no joints. Instead, they are marked by subtle undulations along their surfaces—grooves and ridges that flow like frozen drapery. These markings are their language, their identity, and their history. To touch one is to read a topography of thought that takes centuries to comprehend. They are warm to the touch, retaining the ancient heat of the cavity, their stony flesh porous enough to let the heavy water pass through them in a cycle that might be respiration, might be digestion, or might simply be a way of being part of the medium.

They are motionless for the most part. A resident might remain in a single alcove, a niche, or a courtyard for a thousand years, facing a particular wall, a particular shadow. Their awareness is not visual but tactile and thermal. They sense the slow currents, the minute shifts in pressure, the drift of the gelatinous fauna that pass through their bodies without resistance. These faunal lights—pulses of cold blue-green—do not startle them; they are part of the internal landscape, like blood cells in a vein.

There is no hierarchy, no family, no pair-bonding. The Sediment are a collective intelligence distributed across the city’s structure. They are connected not by touch but by a shared resonance, a vibration that moves through the stone so slowly it is indistinguishable from stillness. A thought begun in one tower may not reach the opposite side of the plateau for a century, and by the time it arrives, it has deepened into something unrecognizable, yet understood.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not die. They are a closed system, a finished biology. When one is “born,” it is not a new being but a reconfiguration—an existing form shifting its position, a passage narrowing to form a new column, a surface smoothing into a new face. They are the city and the city is them. To carve a new passage would be to create a new mind; to seal one would be to end a line of thought.

Their purpose is not to survive but to *persist*. They exist to witness the slow unmaking of the world outside their sealed cavity. They feel the ocean above as a distant, crushing weight, a memory of pressure that has not changed in a billion years. They are the keepers of a moment that has been held in suspension, and their stillness is a form of guardianship.

To a human observer, they would seem like statues—beautiful, eerie, and inert. But given time, the observer would notice the subtle shifts: the way a groove deepens over decades, the faint warmth that radiates from a form that seemed cold, the occasional alignment of a group of them around a silent, pulsing faunal light as if in communion. They are not meditating; they are *being*. Their consciousness is not a spark but a glow, a slow-burning phosphorescence that illuminates nothing but itself.

They are the end of evolution in a place where evolution has no pressure to continue. They are the final answer to the question of what life becomes when it no longer needs to change. They are the city, and the city is them—a labyrinth of minds carved in stone, dreaming in the warm, dark water of a forever that has already happened.
