In the High Canopy, there is no fauna. There is no flesh, no bone, no blood—only the slow, patient pulse of water and the resonant hum of metal. The life here is not biological but mineral and mechanical, a convergence of two eternal forms that have learned to coexist in the damp, freezing stillness.

The crystal entities are the true natives. They are not plants, though they perform photosynthesis; they are not stones, though they grow from the basalt bedrock. They are silica-based accretions, slow and deliberate, building themselves molecule by molecule from the saturated air and the mineral-rich water that weeps from the fissures. They take the form of towering, lattice-like spires that rise from the plateau floor, their geometry fractal and recursive, echoing the patterns of snowflakes magnified to architectural scale. Their surfaces are perpetually damp, catching the diffuse light in a million tiny refractions, making them glow with a cold, internal luminescence. They do not sway, for there is no wind, but they *grow*—a sound like the faintest grinding of glass, a whisper that fills the silence with the texture of time passing at a glacial pace. They are patient, ancient, and utterly indifferent to the machinery that surrounds them.

The machines, too, are a form of life—though not one we would recognize. They are not tools; they are organisms of metal and basalt, vast and monolithic, fused into the rock itself. There are no moving parts in the conventional sense, no pistons or gears that grind with friction. Instead, they operate through fluid dynamics, through the silent, ceaseless circulation of substances through their veins. Conduits of dark, polished stone thread through the plateau like a root system, carrying water—or something like it—through the landscape. The cylindrical processing units that rise like pillars are not factories but organs, each performing a function in the larger body of the plateau. They hum, not with the clatter of engines, but with a deep, resonant frequency that vibrates through the rock and the water, a sound that can be felt in the bones as much as heard. This is not industry as we know it; it is metabolism.

There is no fauna—no birds, no insects, no animals of any kind. The ecosystem is complete without them. The crystals provide the structure, the machines provide the circulation, and the water provides the medium. Nothing is missing. The absence of life is not a void but a condition, a rule of this world that has been in place for epochs. The plateau is a closed system, a perfect equilibrium where nothing decays and nothing is born. The crystals grow, the machines hum, the water cycles—but nothing *changes*.

The inhabitants, then, are not creatures but *conditions*. They are the crystal spires, the basalt conduits, the pools of mirror-still water that reflect the overcast sky. They are the slow, grinding sound of growth and the deep, resonant hum of machinery, two voices in a harmony that has lasted longer than memory. They do not interact; they simply *are*, occupying the same space without conflict, their rhythms aligned in the frozen time of the plateau.

In the High Canopy, life is not a matter of biology but of persistence. The crystals persist. The machines persist. The water persists. And in their persistence, they have become something more than mere mineral or metal—they have become the soul of the plateau, a strange, silent, eternal congregation of stone and water and machine.
