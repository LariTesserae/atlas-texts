In this high desert of salt and glass, the inhabitants are not born, nor do they die in any familiar sense. They are as much geological as they are industrial, beings of slow time and fixed purpose. They call themselves the **Foundry-Wardens**, though the term is less a title than a description of their function. They are the living interface between the planet's geothermal pulse and the eternal machinery that has settled upon its surface.

They are tall, slender figures, their bodies composed of interlocking plates of vitrified ceramic and polished metal, shaped with the same geometric pragmatism as the buildings they inhabit. Their forms are humanoid in silhouette but devoid of biological softness; there are no joints in the human sense, only pivot points sealed against the dry, abrasive air. Their faces are smooth, featureless masks of darkened glass, behind which a faint orange light glows—the same sodium-vapor hue that stains the atmosphere. They do not speak in words, but in modulated frequencies, a low humming that resonates with the sub-audible thrum of the world itself.

Their "life" is measured not in years, but in cycles of maintenance and production. Each Warden is assigned to a node—a factory, a silo, a transit hub—and they move through its spaces with a deliberate, unhurried grace. Their steps are precise, their gestures economical. They adjust dials, lubricate gears, and monitor gauges with a patience that spans centuries. They do not sleep, but they enter periods of low-power stillness, standing motionless in alcoves or beside massive engines, their internal systems drawing geothermal energy directly from the ground through crystalline conduits in their feet.

They are not alone in their work. The mineralizers—those slow, crystalline growths—respond to them. The Wardens' movements and the vibrations they emit guide the mineralizers' growth, shaping them into functional forms: protective sheaths around exposed pipes, load-bearing arches in aging structures, or delicate lattices that channel electrical currents. In turn, the Wardens maintain the mineralizers, pruning excess growth or redirecting their pathways with tools that are both mechanical and electrochemical. It is a symbiosis older than memory, a partnership between machine and mineral that has long since blurred the line between the two.

The Wardens have no concept of leisure or art, but they do have ritual. Once every several centuries, they gather at the central geothermal vent—a vast, glowing pit where the planet's heat rises unfiltered—to perform the Alignment. It is a silent, hours-long ceremony where they stand in a perfect circle, their internal lights pulsing in unison, recalibrating their systems to the planet's deep rhythm. It is the only event that breaks the monotony of their existence, a moment of collective purpose in a world of isolated nodes.

They are not curious about the outside world, for there is no "outside." The sealed horizon is their universe. They do not question their purpose; it is encoded in their design, as fundamental as the geothermal warmth that sustains them. They are the final form of a civilization that chose endurance over transcendence, stability over growth. They are the keepers of equilibrium, the guardians of a world that has already achieved its ultimate state.

And in the vast, silent desert, under the perpetual orange twilight, they continue their work—unwavering, eternal, and perfectly still.
