In this sealed and silent world, there are no animals, no insects, no birds—only the flora and the humming machinery. But the flora itself is the living thing, and it is not merely passive.

What lives here is a civilization of plants. They are not wild, but they are not tame. They are engineered, yes, but in the centuries since their creators abandoned the jar, they have become something more than the sum of their genetic code. They have learned to live with the machinery, to treat the industrial thrum as a kind of weather.

Each cluster is a distinct organism, a colony of spindly stalks that rise from a shared root system buried in the polymer soil. The stalks are not uniform; they have adapted to their specific coordinates in the jar. Some grow tall and thin, reaching for the filtered light with a desperate elegance. Others stay low and broad, their leaves spread like solar panels in a flat, geometric rosette. Their leaves are dark, almost black-green, and coated in a waxy film that traps heat and condenses the barest trace of moisture from the manufactured air. They do not drink water—they drink humidity, the ghost of a river in a world of salt.

They are not alone in their sentience, though they have no minds as we know them. They communicate through root systems that intertwine beneath the salt dust, sharing nutrients and chemical signals in a slow, deliberate language. When one cluster thrives, it sends a pulse of growth through the network; when one suffers from the mineral imbalance in the soil, the others adjust their uptake, compensating in a quiet, cellular solidarity. They are a single organism in a thousand bodies, a distributed intelligence that has learned to survive by cooperation.

Their reproduction is a marvel of adaptation. Without pollinators, they have turned to the machinery. Their flowers, if they can be called that, are tiny, metallic-looking buds that open only when the industrial lights cycle to a specific intensity. They release a fine, glittering pollen that floats in the static-charged air, caught by the subtle currents from the humidifiers and carried to the stigmas of neighboring clusters. The seeds are heavy, encased in a crystalline shell that can withstand the dry grind of the salt plain. When they fall, they do not scatter—they plant themselves, anchoring into the polymer soil with a chemical hook that mimics the action of a root. They wait for the nutrient pumps to cycle, for the ground to settle, for the next century to begin.

They are patient. They are older than the memory of their purpose. They have watched the salt dust shift and resettle, the gullies form and erode, the light bend in impossible ways. They know the machinery better than its creators ever did. They have learned to grow in sync with its rhythms, to curl their leaves when the oxygen scrubbers cycle up, to spread them when the nutrient pumps hum at a certain frequency. They are not just living in the jar—they are living *with* it, a symbiotic partnership between plant and machine.

They are lonely, but they do not know it. They have never known a world beyond the dome, never felt rain or wind or the touch of another living thing that is not of their own kind. Their universe is the salt plain, the glass walls, the humming dark beneath the floor. They grow for no one but themselves, a garden that is its own reason for being.

And in the quiet, in the heat, in the slow, mineral grind of the earth, they persist. A green and breathing thing in a jar that is not a world, waiting for a rain that will never come.
