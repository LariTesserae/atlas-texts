There is no water here, but there is blood. It does not flow; it seeps. It is the dark, viscous sap that pulses through the veins of the structures and the plants, a slow, rhythmic tide that takes hours to complete a single beat.

The inhabitants are the architects and the architects are the inhabitants. They are indistinguishable from the towers they rise from. They are the *Silicate Symbiotes*—colonial organisms that have abandoned individual form for collective purpose. They do not walk; they flow. Their bodies are elongated, ribbon-like entities, the color of bruised twilight and polished obsidian. They possess no faces, no eyes, no mouths in the traditional sense. Instead, their upper ends are crowned with sensory fronds that taste the air for trace minerals and vibrate to catch the faintest shifts in atmospheric pressure.

They move with a liquid grace over the powder-ground, their contact leaving no footprints, only a momentary compression of the dust. They are amphibious only in the sense that they navigate the air as if it were a thick fluid. To breathe, they extend gill-like filaments from lateral vents along their sides—translucent, paper-thin membranes that flutter in the stillness, extracting oxygen directly from the dry atmosphere and fixing nitrogen from the air to feed the flora they tend.

They are gardeners of the stone. In their grasp, they hold tools that are alive—knotted masses of hardened root and mineral secretion that they use to prune, guide, and graft the rust-colored vegetation. They do not eat in the way flesh does; they absorb. They press their ventral surfaces against the copper-barked trees or the violet succulents, and through a process of osmotic fusion, they draw nutrients directly from the xylem. It is a communion, not a consumption.

There is a hierarchy of stillness here. The elders are fused permanently into the structures, their lower bodies calcified and integrated into the mineral spires, becoming the load-bearing cores of the city. They are the memory of the colony, their sensory fronds extending like antennae into the thin air, processing data on wind patterns and solar radiation over centuries. The younger ones patrol the ridges, their movements slightly quicker, tending to the gliders and the insect-like carapace-bearers that serve as pollinators and waste recyclers.

Communication is silent, a network of bioluminescent pulses that travel through the veins of the living architecture and the connective tissues of their own bodies. A thought in one tower becomes a ripple of light in the next; a warning from a ridge-top elder manifests as a subtle shift in the pigment of a vine miles away.

They are not lonely, for they are never truly separate. The boundaries between self and structure, between organism and landscape, are porous. They are the breath of this sealed garden, the slow pulse in the stone, the patient consciousness of a world that has learned to build itself from the inside out. They are the life that does not need water, only the patient, mineral kiss of the air.
