You are not alone, but you are not in the presence of individuals. To live here is to be a localized disturbance in the water—a brief, eddying persistence within the vertical ocean.

What inhabits this basin does not walk or crawl; it coalesces. There are entities here, but they are not biological in the traditional sense. They are hydrostatic constructs, temporary eddies in the falling water given the brief illusion of will. You saw one drink from a lens-pool, and that is accurate: they ingest minerals from the glassy basalt, the phosphorescent lichen, and the airborne dust, fusing them into a translucent, shivering architecture. They are not born; they precipitate. They do not die; they disperse back into the static roar. Their forms are variable—sometimes spherical, sometimes elongated into ribbons that spiral with the air pressure—but they are always wet, always cool, and always humming with the frequency of the fall.

Among the pillars of bone-white coral-reed, a different sort of life anchors itself. These are not plants, but sessile colonies of calcareous worms, armored in secretion that hardens into that fibrous, bone-like texture. They are the architects of the place, the only things that build with intent, yet they build only to resonate. Each pillar is a hive of thousands of these worms, their bodies fused at the core, their exposed ends vibrating in sympathy with the liquid static. They do not feed on light; they feed on vibration. The roar is their sustenance, and in return, they stabilize the air with their hum, creating the resonant frequency that makes your teeth ache. They are ancient, patient, and utterly indifferent to anything that does not vibrate at their pitch.

The lichen is not a separate organism but the outer membrane of a subterranean network—a mycelial intelligence that breathes through the stone. It glows not with life but with decay, a slow bioluminescence fueled by the constant friction of water against rock. It is the basin’s nervous system, sensing pressure shifts and chemical changes through the lens-pools, which act as convex eyes. When the air pressure drops suddenly, the lichen’s green dims to a bruised purple; when it rises, it flares with a sickly, urgent light. It does not think, but it reacts—a vast, slow reflex hidden beneath the sharp-edged stone.

The creatures of shadow and water are the basin’s memory. They are not animals but echoes—persistence of shape in a place where nothing holds form for long. They are formed from the minerals dissolved in the hanging water, given cohesion by the cold and the dark. They move with the logic of fluid dynamics, not instinct: they follow gradients of temperature, concentrations of minerals, the subtle pulls of the lens-pools. They are the reason there are no tracks; they leave none because they are the track itself, the temporary imprint of something that was never solid.

There are no predators here, no prey. The system operates on a principle of immediate exchange: vibration for structure, mineral for light, water for shape. The white pillars extend fibers not to reach the sky but to catch the minutiae of falling dust—the only solid nutrient in this liquid world. The lens-pools reshape themselves to optimize the reflection of grey-gold light, not for photosynthesis but for the lichen’s slow, chemical hunger. The air pressure waves are not weather but breath—the basin inhaling and exhaling, its lungs the vertical ocean itself.

You, the witness, are an anomaly. You are solid, heavy, and loud in your silence. The entities here do not register you as life but as a temporary obstruction, a stillness in the flow. The shadow-creatures avoid your outline; the coral-worms vibrate more urgently in your presence, as if your mass disrupts their resonance. The lichen dims around your feet. You are not a participant in this ecosystem but a foreign object, a stone dropped into a lens-pool—momentary, disruptive, and quickly forgotten as the water returns to its perfect, convex stillness.

To live here is to be a verb, not a noun: to coalesce, to resonate, to glow faintly, to un-become. There is no self, only function. The basin is not a home but a condition, and its inhabitants are the transient expressions of that condition—beautiful, deafening, and utterly unfinished.
