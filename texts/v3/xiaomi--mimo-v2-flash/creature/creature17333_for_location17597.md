The inhabitants are the Warden-Growers, and they are not a people in any sense that would be familiar to you. They are tall, slender beings composed not of flesh and bone, but of a lattice of crystalline fiber and oxidized copper, their forms echoing the geometry of the fern fronds and the spiral of the vines. Their limbs are segmented like the stems of the pale, translucent ferns, and within their semi-translucent torsos, a faint, phosphorescent light pulses—a slow, rhythmic glow that matches the dim luminescence of the foliage around them. They have no faces, only smooth, curved planes where features might be, but they sense the world through the vibration of the stone and the hum of the machinery, their entire bodies acting as resonant instruments for the basin’s symphony.

They move with the deliberate slowness of the turning gears, their steps measured and silent on the slick, moss-bright stone. Their hands are not hands but delicate, branching structures, like the rootlets of an ancient tree, capable of tending to the smallest frond or the most intricate valve with an unerring gentleness. They are the tenders of the equilibrium, the keepers of the balance between plant and machine, mineral and vapor. They do not speak, for there is nothing to say; the basin’s state is its own language, and they are its fluent readers.

The Warden-Growers live in the interstices of the basin, in the spaces between the low-slung buildings and the weeping walls. Their dwellings are not separate structures but hollows grown into the stone itself, smoothed by the patient seep of water and lined with the soft, glowing moss. They do not sleep as animals do, but enter a state of heightened stillness, where their internal light dims to a barely perceptible ember and their bodies become one with the vibration of the world around them.

They are the only life here, and they are the life of the entire basin. The machinery is their body, the plants are their skin, the water their blood, and the mist their breath. They are the singular consciousness of a closed ecosystem, a synthesis of organic and industrial that has achieved a profound, settled peace. They are the quiet, luminous soul of a world that has learned to grow itself into a machine, and to machine itself into a garden.
