In the basin, the inhabitants are as patient as the stone they live upon. They are a people of the constant damp, their skin adapted to the perpetual bead of moisture that glazes their forearms and the nape of their necks. They are not many, perhaps only a few hundred, and they move through the dim, buttery light with a slow, deliberate economy of motion, as if conserving energy against the heavy air.

Their physical forms bear the mark of the place. Their hair, dark and heavy, clings to their scalps and necks in damp spirals. Their eyes are accustomed to the bruised, low light, appearing dark and deep-set, reflecting the slick surfaces of the world around them. Their clothing is woven from fibers that resist rot—oiled wool, waxed leather, tightly braided reeds—and it is cut for the heat: loose sleeves, high collars to catch the sweat, boots of supple hide that reach the knee to wade through the shallow, seeping channels of the streets. They are not boisterous; their voices are low, often just above a whisper, the sound easily swallowed by the thick air and the constant, patient dripping.

They are a people of two natures: the builders and the tenders.

The builders are the keepers of the stone and iron. They are the ones who move through the workshops, their hands stained with soot and peat, their faces illuminated by the jaundiced glow of the arc lamps. They understand the *thump-groan-splash* of the waterwheel as a form of language, a steady rhythm that dictates the pace of their labor. They are not architects in the grand sense, but masons and mechanics of a deep, intuitive sort. They know which stones in the foundation are sinking fastest, which iron downspout is weeping the thickest rust, how to coax a stuck piston with a precise, gentle tap. Their work is not a struggle against the water, but a collaboration with it; they build low, they sink their foundations deep, they guide the seepage with channels that are as much a part of the settlement's design as the buildings themselves. They wear leather aprons that never fully dry and carry tools that are heavy, simple, and dark with use.

The tenders are the keepers of the slow, living things. They move through the moss-carpeted hummocks and along the weeping rock faces with a silent grace. They are the ones who harvest the leathery ferns for their fibers, who cultivate the hardy, water-loving plants that grow in the few dry perches, who know the cycles of the blind, smooth-skinned amphibians. They are the basin’s quiet naturalists, their knowledge passed not in books but in gesture and observation. They understand the basin’s equilibrium, the way it is suspended in a single, long moment. They are the ones who notice the slow, patient colonization of stone by moss, the subtle shift in the metallic tang of the air, the way the gurgle of the central drains changes pitch after a long, still period. They are the memory of the place, its silent record-keepers.

There is no clear division between these two groups; every builder is also a tender of their immediate environment, and every tender understands the mechanics of the waterwheel. They live in the low, stone blocks, clustered in the basin’s center, and their days are marked not by a sun, but by the shifts in the mechanical pulse: the deep heartbeat of the wheel, the hiss of steam, the hum of the lamps.

They are a people who have found their level. They do not dream of the sun or the dry, forested slopes beyond the rim; the distant, echoing creak of the unseen birds is reminder enough of a world that is not theirs. Their ambitions are small and dense, like the basin itself: to keep the machinery turning, to keep the water from stagnating, to keep the rust from consuming the iron entirely. They are a quiet, loud, wet, and hot people, living in an eternal late afternoon, their existence a testament to the profound stability of a world where everything has found its place and will remain, dripping, humming, and breathing, forever.
