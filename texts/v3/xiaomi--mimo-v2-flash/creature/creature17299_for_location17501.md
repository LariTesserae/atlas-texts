Here is who lives in that place.

**The Damp-Born**

They call themselves the Substrates, though outsiders have long called them the Stone-Singers or the Weepers. They are a people carved from the same granite as the plateau, their bodies shaped by the humidity and the weight of the atmosphere. Their skin is pale and smooth, lacking hair almost entirely, not from evolution but from constant saturation; it is a skin designed to glisten, to breathe moisture, to never crack in the dry air that never comes. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the perpetual twilight of the mist, with a nictitating membrane that sweeps across the cornea to protect against airborne grit and condensation. To look at a Substrate is to see a face that seems perpetually wet, not with sweat, but with the exudation of the place itself.

They are a slow people, moving with the deliberate economy of tectonic plates. Their joints are hyper-flexible, allowing them to navigate the carved stone corridors and low-ceilinged workshops with a fluid grace that resembles flowing water more than walking. Their fingers are long and slightly webbed, not for swimming, but for gripping the slick, mossy surfaces of the quarry walls and the damp controls of their heavy machinery.

**The Geomancers of Industry**

The inhabitants are divided not by class but by function, each tied to a specific layer of the plateau’s reality.

*   **The Aquifers:** These are the mystics and the engineers. They do not merely plumb the water; they commune with it. They listen to the subterranean hum through metal rods driven into the stone, interpreting the pitch and vibration to predict shifts in the water table or the stability of the rock. They maintain the cisterns and the weeping walls, ensuring that the moisture remains a life-giving force rather than a flooding solvent. They are the keepers of the "intentions" of the water, believing that the mist carries memories from the deep earth.
*   **The Masons of Patience:** In a place where stone is both foundation and adversary, the masons are the true architects. They do not fight the erosion; they anticipate it. Their buildings are designed to settle, to sink gracefully into the basins over centuries. A Mason’s skill is measured not by the sharpness of their cut, but by how well their structure accommodates the slow creep of gravity and moisture. They carve the living rock with tools that vibrate at specific frequencies, coaxing the granite to split along its crystalline lines rather than shattering it.
*   **The Steam-Wardens:** The industrial heart of the city beats in the deep, stone-cut workshops where the "slow machines" churn. The Wardens tend to the massive, piston-driven engines that power the city. These machines are not efficient by modern standards; they are durable, built of brass and iron that resists the corrosive damp. The Wardens are covered in a fine layer of soot and condensed steam, their clothing heavy with oil and water. They move through the mist-shrouded factory floors like ghosts, their ears tuned to the rhythmic groaning of the machinery, which they claim is in harmony with the groaning of the stone itself.

**The Domestic Rhythm**

Life in the Substrate city is communal and vertical, though the verticality is inverted. The "ground" level is a roof; the true living spaces are within the stone, in chambers carved into the domes and basins. Here, the light is provided by bioluminescent fungi cultivated on the walls or by electric lamps that cast a warm, amber glow, refracted through the condensation on the stone surfaces.

They do not cook with fire, as open flames are dangerous in the gas-rich mists. Instead, they use geothermal heat tapped from fissures in the rock, slow-cooking stews of hardy tubers and cultivated fungi in sealed stone vessels. Their clothing is made from waxed fibers and treated leather, designed to shed water without absorbing it, though nothing stays dry for long.

**The Fauna and the Familiar**

The Substrates share their damp city with a symbiotic fauna. Large, blind snails with shells like opals traverse the ceilings of the caverns, grazing on the mineral-rich moss and cleaning the air of spores. The Substrates keep them as pets and utility animals, their slime trails used as a base for medicinal salves. In the deeper, warmer pools, small, eel-like amphibians are raised for food, their flesh translucent and rich in minerals leached from the granite.

**The Social Etiquette of Silence and Sound**

Because the ambient roar of the city is so constant—water, wind, and machine—the Substrates have developed a form of communication that minimizes auditory interference. They speak in low tones, their voices pitched to carry just beneath the hum of the engines. More often, they communicate through touch and gesture, their damp hands conveying complex information on the textures of stone or the vibrations of a machine.

They have a reverence for "The Dry," a mythical state that exists only in stories or in the highest, wind-swept points of the plateau where the mist thins for brief moments. To be "dry" is to be isolated from the collective consciousness of the stone, a state of existential loneliness. They take pride in their saturation; to be damp is to be connected to the lifeblood of the plateau.

**The Ancestral Memory**

The most peculiar aspect of the Substrates is their relationship with the past. Because the stone remembers, as the description suggests, the Substrates believe their ancestors are not buried, but integrated. When a Substrate dies, their body is placed in a shallow, water-filled basin in the deep rock. Over years, the mineral-rich water deposits a calcified shell around the body, eventually merging it with the granite. They do not see this as death, but as "settling"—becoming part of the structure that shelters the next generation. To walk the streets of the city is to walk upon the shoulders of those who came before, literally and physically.

They are a people without sharp edges, without the jagged urgency of the lowlands. They are the breath of the plateau made manifest, existing in the warm, wet equilibrium between the geological and the mechanical.
