In the Hollow, the people are as angular and spare as the shale from which they build their homes. They do not walk so much as they traverse, moving with a deliberate economy of motion born of a landscape that punishes haste. Their bodies are adapted to the cold: compact, insulated by layers of woven fiber and the dense, heat-retaining hides of the great, shaggy mammals that roam the higher plateaus. Their skin is often pale, not from a lack of sun, but from a life lived in the silver-grey light that reflects endlessly off the stone and frost. Their faces are wind-carved, their eyes narrowed against the glare of the weak sun and the constant, blowing dust.

They are a quiet people, their voices low and their words sparing, for sound carries far in the thin air and silence is a form of currency. Their language is one of nuance and implication, where a pause or a shift in posture carries as much meaning as a spoken phrase. They have no written script; their history is a tapestry of oral tradition, woven into memory and passed down through generations in the long, dark hours of the extended twilight.

Their technology is a testament to patience. Their tools are not forged but knapped from stone, their blades sharp but brittle, requiring constant resharpening. Their needles are bone, their thread sinew or the fine, tough fibers of a subterranean plant that grows only in the deepest, most protected fissures. They have mastered the art of conservation, of using every part of what they harvest. The bones of the mammals become tools and structural supports; the sinew becomes cordage; the thick hides become shelter and clothing; even the fat is rendered for fuel, burning with a slow, steady flame that provides more warmth than light.

Their society is a delicate, interdependent network, not a hierarchy. Leadership is situational, falling to the one with the most knowledge of a particular task—the one who can read the subtle signs of a coming frost-shift, or who knows the exact location of the deepest, most reliable water seep. There are no kings or chieftains, only stewards. Their most sacred law is the sharing of resources, for they understand that in a world of such stark scarcity, hoarding is a death sentence for the community.

They are a people of deep perception, their senses honed by the starkness of their environment. They can read the patterns in the frost, the subtle shifts in the wind's sigh, the nearly imperceptible tremor in the stone that signals a distant thaw. They live in a state of constant, quiet awareness, their minds as still and observant as the frozen pools that dot the landscape.

And yet, they are not merely survivors. They are artists of stillness, their culture a reflection of the world they inhabit. Their music is not of melody but of resonance: the soft, rhythmic tap of stone on stone, the low hum that can be produced by rubbing a carefully prepared hide over a resonant rock face, the complex patterns of silence and sound created by the careful placement of their bodies in a dwelling. Their stories are not of epic battles or grand heroes, but of the slow, patient unfolding of the world: the tale of a single frost-crystal forming over a season, the journey of a drop of water from a high basin to a deep fissure, the life cycle of an ice-worm.

They are a people who understand that time is not a river, but a glacier—immense, slow, and inexorable. They do not fight it; they move with it. They are the hollow's consciousness, its patient, observant mind, living in a state of profound, quiet communion with a world that is both severe and beautiful, and in their stillness, they find a depth of presence that a faster world might never know.
