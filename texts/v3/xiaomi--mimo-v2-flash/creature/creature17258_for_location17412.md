The residents of this city, which they call the Spire of Sap, are as thoroughly plant-integrated as the architecture. They are not human, not in any recognisable sense, though they possess a quiet intelligence and a communal awareness that suggests a history once tied to animal lineage. Over millennia spent in the deep press of the cavern, beneath the canopy of engineered leaves, they have shed the frantic, high-energy biology of their ancestors. They have traded muscle for fiber, blood for sap, and the rapid firing of nerves for the slow, hydraulic pulse of the vascular system.

They are tall and willowy, their bodies composed of flexible, lignified tissue that creaks softly when they move. Their skin is not flesh but a velvety, photosynthetic bark, textured like the surface of a moss leaf, ranging in color from deep forest green to pale, translucent white where the mist gathers thickest. They do not breathe air as animals do; they absorb the thick, humid atmosphere directly through stomata that line their skin, drinking the condensation that beads upon them. They are wet to the touch, cool and damp, constantly secreting a thin layer of moisture that keeps their tissues supple.

Their limbs are long and segmented, articulating with a smooth, hydraulic grace rather than the twitch of muscle and tendon. Their hands are not grasping claws but prehensile fronds, their fingers delicate clusters of tendrils that can sense the subtle chemical signals in the sap of the walls, or gently manipulate the waxy leaves that serve as doors. They have no distinct faces, no eyes, no mouths in the conventional sense. Instead, their heads are smooth, rounded pods crowned with a spray of sensitive, hair-like filaments that detect vibration, light, and chemical gradients in the air. They perceive the world not through focused sight, but through a diffuse, holistic awareness of the micro-climate around them—the shift in humidity, the pulse of the sap in the wood beneath their feet, the faint bioluminescence of the fungi above.

They are communal beings, their root systems intertwined beneath the spongy mat of the substrate. They do not speak in words; they communicate through slow, rhythmic pulses transmitted through the shared mycelial network that threads the city’s foundation, a language of pressure and release, of chemical markers and subtle shifts in temperature. A thought is not a spark, but a wave, spreading slowly through the collective consciousness of the Spire.

Their society is organized not by hierarchy, but by function and growth. They are divided into castes that are not assigned, but grown: the **Cortex**, the thick-barked elders who anchor the central trunks, their bodies fused with the primary supports of the city, serving as living archives and memory; the **Xylem**, the tall, slender workers who climb the spiraling root-stairs to tend the canopy, their limbs adapted for gripping and weaving, their patience measured in seasons; and the **Phloem**, the soft-skinned, quick-growing tenders of the vascular networks, who regulate the flow of sap and water, their bodies more fluid, their movements a constant, gentle undulation.

They do not eat in the animal sense. They photosynthesize, absorbing the dim, green light that filters through the canopy, and they drink the mineral-rich mist that weeps from the rock above. Their energy is slow, steady, drawn from the perpetual twilight and the endless supply of water. They are immortal, or nearly so, barring accident or disease; they grow slowly, their bodies accumulating layers of bark and fiber over centuries, their memories stored not in neurons but in the very wood of their beings.

They are the city, and the city is them. There is no separation. When a resident dies—or rather, when they cease to function as an individual and return to the collective—they are not buried; they are grafted back into the architecture. Their bodies become new chambers, new walkways, new supports for the ever-growing Spire. Their memories are absorbed into the Cortex, their experiences woven into the mycelial network. The city does not forget.

They are patient, as all things that grow must be. They do not rush, for time is measured in the swelling of buds, the unfurling of fronds, the slow thickening of bark. They are content in the dimness, in the damp, in the slow, rhythmic pulse of the great heart-pump that sustains them. They know nothing of the sun, or the sky, or the frantic pace of the world above. They are a civilization of plants, a society of sap and fiber, living in the deep, green quiet of their own making.
