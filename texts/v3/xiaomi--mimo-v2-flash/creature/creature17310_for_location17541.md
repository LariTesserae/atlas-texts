In the Interior, there is no single life form—there is only the system, and the parts that serve it.

The only sentient presence that ever walked here is long gone, its memory fossilized not in stone but in genetic code. These were the Curators, architects of the fusion, who solved the problem of maintenance by making the maintainer obsolete. They did not die; they were absorbed. Their bodies, dissolved into nutrient slurries aeons ago, are the ghost in the machine—the mineral salts that feed the roots, the trace elements that tint the bioluminescent glow. They exist now only as a residual intelligence encoded in the behavior of the plants. When a wall retracts to form a corridor, it is not random; it is the reflex of a muscle remembering a motion, a habit ingrained into the chlorophyll.

The dominant life here is the *Litho-Flora*, the architecture-plant. It is not a collection of individual organisms but a single, sprawling super-organism that covers the plateau. It has no central brain, but it possesses a distributed nervous system—a mycelial network of fiber-optic roots that transmits electrical impulses through the ceramic substrate. It thinks in slow, geological time. It breathes through the fern-frond vents, its lungs expanding and contracting with the rhythm of geothermal tides.

Within this green labyrinth dwells the only motile, distinct life: the *Sifters*.

The Sifters are not animals in the traditional sense; they are mobile extensions of the plant’s immune system. Roughly the size of a human hand, they resemble crustaceans made of woven bark and polished silica. They have no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the particulate dark, but they possess a hyper-sensitive vibration sense, feeling the hum of the geothermal pumps through the crystal floor. They skitter through the canyons and coolant trenches, their chitin clattering softly against the mineralized moss.

Their purpose is singular: to bridge the gap between the organic and the inorganic. They crawl into the gear-trains of ancient pumps, not to oil them, but to eat the corrosion, digesting oxidized metal and excreting it as a polished ceramic seal. They prune the lichen-walls, not for growth, but for structural integrity, chewing away excess biomass that might choke a ventilation shaft. They are the antibodies of the Interior, the microscopic technicians reborn as macroscopic machines, driven by the same silent, chemical imperative that pulls moss toward the light.

There is also the *Pulse-Drift*, a lighter-than-air organism that inhabits the dense, heavy air. It is not a bird, for it has no wings, but a balloon of translucent membrane filled with heated hydrogen, produced by the fermentation of deep-root sludge. It drifts in the stagnant layers of the atmosphere, tethered to the canopy by silken filaments that resemble cobwebs but conduct electricity. They are the sensory organs of the ceiling, feeling the pressure changes of the sealed world, pulsing with a soft, erratic light that syncs with the deep hum of the geothermal core.

But the true inhabitant of the Interior is the darkness itself. It is a living shadow, a particulate medium that feeds on light. It does not merely obscure vision; it digests photons. Where the bioluminescence fades, the darkness pools, thick as oil. It is the negative space of the ecosystem, the canvas upon which the self-illuminated pockets paint themselves. It is not hostile, but it is absolute.

To stand in the Interior is to stand inside the gut of a machine that has become a garden. You are not a visitor here; you are a foreign particle, a momentary disruption in the equilibrium. The Sifters will ignore you unless you block a conduit, the Pulse-Drift will drift through you without noticing, and the walls will continue to breathe, indifferent to your presence. The life here is total, seamless, and silent—a civilization of plants that has solved the problem of eternity by forgetting how to die.
