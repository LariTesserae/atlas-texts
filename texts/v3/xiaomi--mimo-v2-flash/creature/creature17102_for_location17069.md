The people of this place are as woven into the geology as their dwellings. They are a tall, lean people, with a musculature built for balance and deliberate movement rather than explosive speed. Their skin is pale, not from a lack of sun—for the light is constant and pervasive—but from a life lived in the diffuse glow of the plateau, a complexion the color of quartzite in shadow. Their faces are long and angular, with high cheekbones and deep-set eyes that seem to hold the same quiet as the valleys below. Their hair is the color of weathered granite or dark conifer needles, often worn long and braided with fibers of moss and root.

They move with a slowness that is not lethargy, but a form of reverence. Every step on the angled rock is placed with intention. They do not startle; they do not rush. Their gait is a kind of flowing stillness, a negotiation with gravity and the unyielding stone. Their hands, however, are where their true dynamism lies. The fingers are long and deft, stained with the earth-tones of mineral pigments and the dark resin of the conifers. They are hands accustomed to the patient work of fitting stone to stone without mortar, of carving a tool from obsidian, of weaving a cloak from the fibers of a slow-growing plant that takes a decade to mature.

Their clothing is a testament to their environment. It is all they have, beyond their dwellings and tools. The fabrics are coarse, woven from plant fiber and the wool of a solitary, goat-like animal that haunts the high crags. The colors are the colors of the plateau itself: the grey of granite, the deep green of moss, the pale white of quartz, the ruddy brown of exposed ironstone. There are no dyes of bright, synthetic origin. Their robes and tunics are layered, not for extreme cold—for the air is always cool, not freezing—but for the comfort of weight and texture, a tactile reminder of their place. Their feet are clad in soft leather, soled with cured hide that makes no sound on the stone.

Their faces are serene, marked by a placidity that can be mistaken for emptiness. But their eyes are intensely observant. They track the slow condensation on a rock face, the subtle shift in a fern's unfurling frond, the almost imperceptible migration of a shadow across the plateau. They do not see the world as a series of events to be reacted to, but as a single, complex, and static pattern to be understood. Their expressions are subtle; a slight narrowing of the eyes might signify a deep contemplation, a barely perceptible upward turn of the lips a profound, quiet joy.

Their voices, when they speak, are low and resonant, carrying no more urgency than the whisper of wind over stone. The sound is not for projecting across distance, but for sharing the immediate, enclosed space of a carved dwelling or a sheltered ledge. Their language is not built on verbs of action, but on nouns of being and states of condition. They do not say, "We will build a new dwelling." They might say, "A new dwelling is becoming, here," as if observing a geological process. They speak of the mountain, the moss, the water, the light, as if they are conversing with equals.

They are solitary by nature, yet communal in practice. You will see them moving alone across the plateau, each individual a sovereign point of consciousness, yet they gather in the carved communal halls with an easy, unspoken familiarity. There is no hierarchy here, no leader and no follower, for in a place of perfect stasis, there is no direction in which to lead. Decisions are made by a slow, collective consensus that emerges over cycles of condensation, a shared understanding that solidifies like dew on stone.

They are the caretakers of equilibrium. They do not strive, they maintain. Their purpose is not to grow, but to *be*. They are the consciousness of the mountain, the living mind of a landscape that has perfected its own existence. They are the observers of the eternal cycle, the beings who have learned that the highest form of action is to hold still, and in doing so, to become a part of the unending, perfect present.
