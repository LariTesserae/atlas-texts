Living here is the rhythm of water and the geometry of silence.

The inhabitants are not a civilization, but a congregation. They are the Crystalline Parish, a populace of living facets whose bodies are sculpted from the same porous, bone-white stone that forms the chamber walls. They do not breathe, for the air is too thick with mineral mist; instead, they filter the saturation through micro-pores in their skin, tasting the ozone and the metallic tang of the deep earth through their entire surface.

They are formless in their youth and rigid in their age. When a drop of water falls from the unseen ceiling and strikes the clay floor, a new life begins. Over centuries, the mineral-rich runoff collects around a grain of sand, layering calcium and silica into a nucleus. From this, a member of the Parish extrudes itself—slowly, imperceptibly—until it lifts from the mat of moss as a spindly, multi-legged construct of quartz and chitin, or a flat, disc-like skater riding the meniscus of a damp depression.

Their bodies are not uniform. Some are tall and needle-sharp, clicking across the cracked clay with a sound like settling dust. Others are low and rounded, their surfaces veined with rust-colored filaments that pulse with the same slow light that veins the walls. These filaments are their nervous system, a network of soft luminescence that communicates not with words, but with shifts in hue and intensity. A slow, rhythmic pulse of violet means contentment; a sharp, staccato flicker of rust-red is a warning of a change in the humidity.

They move in patterns that are not learned, but inherent. A disc-shaped entity will glide in a perfect circle around the central pool for a thousand years, its iridescent slime etching a faint groove into the clay that is slowly filled by the encroaching moss, only to be cleared again by the next pass. A spindly walker will trace the same labyrinthine path across the walls, its legs finding purchase in the same porous divots, its body absorbing the trace minerals left by its own previous passage. They are their own ancestors and their own descendants, recycling the same physical space into eternal motion.

They do not eat. They absorb. They are photovores and lithovores, drinking the flat, white glare from the walls and siphoning iron and silica from the damp stone. Their digestion is a geological process, taking centuries to break down a single molecule of rust into usable energy. Their excretion is the clear, glassy sap that hardens into nodules on the succulent rosettes—a waste product that is also a seed, a crystal that will one day crack and give birth to a new, smaller facet of the Parish.

The central pool is their altar, their womb, and their tomb. They do not drink from it, for its water is too cold, too dense, too void of the minerals they crave. Instead, they gather at its rim to bask in the absolute darkness it reflects. The starless dark is their sun; it is the source of the coolness that defines their existence. When a member of the Parish grows too large, too heavy with accumulated minerals, it will eventually cease its endless pattern and slide into the pool. There is no splash, only a slow submersion, a descent into the perfect stillness. Over the next millennium, its body will dissolve into the dark water, its geometric form surrendering to the amorphous, until it becomes part of the pool’s bottom, another layer of sediment in the heart of the dryness.

Their language is the tapestry of sounds that fills the chamber. The *tick* of the dripping water is the metronome of their time. The soft *click* of quartz legs on stone is their speech. The low, visual hum of their crystalline bodies is their collective thought. They do not speak of the past or the future, for there is only the stretched moment. They communicate in the present tense: a shift in the light, a change in the condensation, the arrival of a new drop.

The artifacts of the built world—the stone archway, the circular walls—are not their making. They are relics of a forgotten scale, a race that once tried to impose order on this saturated place. The Parish does not recognize them as architecture; to them, they are simply part of the topography, another niche in the interlocking puzzle. The archway is a shelter for the disc-skaters; the circular walls are channels that guide the seeping water into new, interesting patterns.

To be a member of the Crystalline Parish is to be a single, eternal note in a chord that never resolves. There is no hunger, no fear, no ambition—only the slow, imperceptible thickening of the self, the endless repetition of a pattern that is both movement and stillness. They are the embodiment of a moment frozen in amber, a living geology that has solved the puzzle of existence and will never change, never decay, never die, until the lid of this world is finally lifted and the outside air, warm and rushing, shatters them into dust.
