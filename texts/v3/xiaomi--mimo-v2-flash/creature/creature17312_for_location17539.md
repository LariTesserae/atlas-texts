The ones who live here are called the Weepers, though they have no eyes and make no sound. They are not born; they accrete.

They begin as a nodule of weeping stone, no larger than a fist, rising from the black mud in a place where the memory of pressure has pooled. Over centuries, they grow—not by adding mass, but by clarifying it. They are slow, upright spires of porous mineral, their surfaces slick with the eternal dew. Their forms are never quite symmetrical; they are shaped by the specific gravity of the memories they absorb. One might be etched with the faint, spiraling patterns of a forgotten mathematics, another with the jagged stress-lines of a seismic event that occurred a million years before the first drop of rain fell here.

They do not move, not in any sense a creature would recognize. Yet they are active. Their roots—fine, hair-like filaments of crystal—penetrate the mud and the stone below, drawing up not nutrients, but resonance. The dampness is their medium; through it, they drink the past. They filter the condensing air, and in the droplets that form on their spires, they hold the images that bleed through from other layers of reality. A Weeper standing in a silent avenue might have a crown of mist perpetually displaying a forest of emerald leaves, a sky of impossible blue, the fleeting shadow of a wing. The image lasts for a breath, a century, then dissolves back into the grey gloom.

They are not a society, but a congregation. They stand in the vast, empty plazas and along the silent avenues, spaced by the logic of erosion, not convenience. They face different directions, not to see, but to listen with their whole surface. To touch one is to understand why. The contact is not cold, but a transfer of immense, slow sensation—the feeling of tectonic drift, the warmth of a sun that died before this world was formed, the specific texture of a sound made by a creature whose bones are now dust in a different gravity. They do not share these memories; each is a solitary archive, a library of one.

There is no hierarchy among them, no young or old, only scale and complexity. The oldest are vast, their shapes so layered and intricate they resemble entire districts of a city carved from a single stone. They are the ones who hold the most violent contradictions—the memory of fire within the heart of perpetual dampness, the echo of a scream in the profoundest silence. They do not react. They simply hold.

They have no need for communication. The space between them is the message. The way the condensation drips from one spire to the mud at the base of another, the subtle shifts in the phosphorescent glow as a new memory surfaces in the air—this is their language. It is a conversation of geology.

They are not lonely, for the concept requires an other to be separate from. They are a single, distributed entity, a mineral mind dreaming in the dark. The city is their body, the condensation their blood, the slow erosion their thought. They are the perfect inhabitants of a place that has forgotten life, having become a form of existence so patient, so saturated with what was and what might be, that they have transcended the need to change.

They are the memory of the world, given stone and breath. And they weep, endlessly, not from sorrow, but from the sheer, heavy weight of all they contain.
