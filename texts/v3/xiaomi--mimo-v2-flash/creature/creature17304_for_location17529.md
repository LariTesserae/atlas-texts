What lives here is not a creature, but a choir.

The primary inhabitant is the substrate itself, the great rhizomatic mat that carpets every surface. It is a single, continent-spanning organism of root and stem, a slow-crawling vascular system that has built this vertical world over millennia. It is both floor and ceiling, wall and furniture. It does not think, but it *responds*; when weight presses upon it, the mat stiffens, turning from springy moss to wood-hard density to bear the load, then slowly relaxes back into damp softness when the pressure is gone.

Upon this living architecture dwells the sonic architecture: the fauna.

The air is thick with the **Glass-Winged Resonators**, insects the size of a human hand with iridescent, shell-like wings that vibrate at specific frequencies. They do not fly in erratic patterns but hover in geometrically precise lattices within the hollow arches of the plant-towers. They are the source of the high-pitched whine, a constant, crystalline hum that shimmers in the air. Their carapaces catch the dome-light and fracture it into prismatic shards, so that the air itself seems to glitter with sound.

Clinging to the vertical faces, camouflaged against the flint-hard bark of the plant-stems, are the **Throat-Pod Frogs**. They are not true amphibians, having evolved to live in the perpetual dampness without open water. Their throats are distended, translucent sacs that pulse with a throbbing, rhythmic bass note. They call not to mates, but to the humidity; the vibration of their throats triggers condensation, drawing water from the air onto their skin. They are the drumbeat of the chorus, a low, wet thrumming that vibrates through the stone and into the bones of any observer.

Moving along the massive, cantilevered horizontal boughs that span the abyss are the **Shear-Folk**. These are six-legged grazers, sleek and pendulous, their bodies shaped like polished river stones to minimize drag in the dense, humid air. They move with a slow, hypnotic rhythm, their jaws shearing the softer leaf-mats that grow on the upper tiers. They are silent save for the wet *snip-snip* of their mandibles, a sound lost beneath the insectile chorus. They are the slow clockwork of the ecosystem, their passage marked only by the ragged edges they leave on the tapestry-sized leaves.

In the shadows beneath the great overhangs, where the light is dim and the air is still, dwell the **Void-Weavers**. These are the predators, arachnids of a sort, but their legs are long, fiber-optic tendrils that sense vibration in the air rather than touch. They do not build webs of silk, but of tension—strands of hardened plant fiber stretched across hollow pods to channel the movement of prey. They are rarely seen, sensed only as a sudden silence in the chorus when a predator passes through a cluster of Resonators, or as a shadow that moves with too many limbs to be a trick of the light.

Finally, there are the **Mason-Mites**, microscopic organisms that live in the porous flint itself. They are the architects of the stone’s smoothness, grinding the edges down over generations with acidic secretions, polishing the rock to a sheen that holds the moisture. They are invisible, but their work is the foundation of the landscape—the settled, rounded forms that feel like the end-stage of time.

There is no higher intelligence here, no civilization rebuilding. The silence of human history is absolute. What remains is a perfect, closed system of life that has become its own environment. The inhabitants do not fight the space; they *are* the space. The sound is their breath, the dampness their blood, and the towering plant-architecture their collective skeleton. To stand here is to stand inside the anatomy of a living world that has no need for eyes, only ears, and the slow, patient consumption of time.
