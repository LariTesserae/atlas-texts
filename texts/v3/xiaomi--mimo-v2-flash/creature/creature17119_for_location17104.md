In this high basin, the people are as still and wet as the world they inhabit. They are not a tribe in the boisterous, sprawling sense, but a quiet congregation, few in number and slow in movement. They are built low to the ground, not out of fear, but to meet the weight of the sky; they move with a deliberate economy, knowing that haste only disturbs the silence and chills the damp air.

Their bodies are shaped by the plateau. They are not tall; generations of stooping under low eaves and winding through narrow stone passages have shortened them, broadened their shoulders, and given them a low center of gravity that navigates the slick, polished stones with instinctive grace. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, damp to the touch even when dry, for the humidity seeps into the pores and never truly leaves. They have no hair on their faces or bodies; the constant wetness would make it a burden, so they have grown smooth, like river stones.

Their most striking feature is their eyes. They are wide and dark, adapted to the perpetual twilight of the mist and the low reflection of the water. They do not squint against the sun, for the sun is a diffused, hazy presence, a pale coin behind the vapor. They see well in the gray, noticing the subtle shift of shadow on rock, the minute change in the color of a fern, the tremor of a water surface disturbed by a drop from above.

They are clad not in woven cloth, but in treated moss and cured reeds. The fibers are coarse but water-resistant, layered thickly over the shoulders and chest, cinched at the waist with braided sinew. Their feet are bare and tough, the soles thickened from a lifetime of walking on damp stone, feeling the texture of the ground as much as seeing it. Around their necks and wrists, they wear small, smooth tokens: a chip of obsidian, a coil of copper wire found in a dry crevice, a fossilized shell from a time when the basin was deeper.

They are a people of touch and sound. In the profound quiet, they have learned to speak without raising their voices; their language is low and resonant, carrying vibration through the air and the ground more than through volume. They communicate with their hands as much as with words, pointing not with a finger but with a chin or an elbow, keeping their hands free for work. Their touch is constant—a hand on a shoulder to guide, a palm on a wall to feel the seepage, the press of a stone into a channel bed.

Their lives are organized around the water. They are its guardians and its students. They know the rhythm of the springs not by a clock, but by the feeling in the air, the pressure in their ears, the taste of the mist. They do not fight the damp; they cultivate it. Their cisterns are not just for storage, but for ritual—a place to watch the reflection of the peaks in the still water, to see the sky in the earth.

They are not hunters, for there is nothing to hunt. They are not gatherers in the explosive sense, for the growth is slow and constant. They are *tenders*. They tend the moss beds, encouraging the softest varieties for their bedding. They guide the flow of water, not to conquer it, but to coax it into a dance that keeps their foundations sound. They harvest the slow-growing tubers that root in the wet soil, and they collect the rare, dry minerals from the highest, wind-scoured ledges above the basin.

Their tools are extensions of their hands. The hammer-stone fits the palm as if grown there; the wooden wedge is smoothed by use, not by design. They work in the quiet hours, when the wind is still and the drip of water is most clear. They do not rush. A wall built in a day is a wall that will fail; a channel carved over a season is one that will last generations.

They are a people of memory, but not of history in the linear sense. Their stories are not about great battles or migrations, but about the plateau itself: the time a spring shifted three feet to the east, the pattern of frost on a particular stone, the name of a moss that only blooms in a hundred years. They are the memory of the water, the stone, the wind.

They do not look outward. The sharp peaks are not a challenge to be climbed, but a frame, a boundary that defines their world. They rarely venture beyond the last wall, and when they do, it is with a sense of trespass, as if leaving a room. The basin is their body, and they are its quiet, beating heart. They do not seek to change it. They seek only to be in equilibrium with it, to be the part of the plateau that feels the water, that knows the silence, that holds the world in its gentle, wet grasp.
