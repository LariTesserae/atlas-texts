The people of the Mire-Built Plateau are known, simply, as the Weepers—not by themselves, for they have few names for things that are not directly observable, but by the rare outsiders who occasionally stumble into their domain. They are a small population, scattered across the vast tableland in clusters of three to twenty, living in the hollows and lee-sides of the great stone domes.

Physiologically, they have adapted to the perpetual dampness. Their skin is pale and smooth, lacking the dry ridges of those who live in arid lands; it is perpetually cool to the touch, and seems to absorb the ambient moisture rather than repel it. Their eyes are accustomed to the soft, even light of the high haze, and are often a watery gray or pale blue, with nictitating membranes that flicker reflexively to protect against constant condensation. Their hair—if they have it—grows thick and straight, often worn long to keep the neck and shoulders warm in the cool air, and it is frequently damp, steaming faintly when they enter the relative warmth of their carved stone dwellings.

They are a quiet people, their voices soft and low, for the acoustics of the plateau do not carry shouting. Their language is as much gesture as sound: a slow raising of the hand to indicate "yes," a tilt of the head to signify listening, a tap of finger on stone to call attention. Their words are few, and their sentences are long and patient, often trailing off as they consider the weight of what they mean to say. They speak of water as one speaks of a relative: with familiarity and respect. They have a dozen words for different kinds of dampness—the slickness of fresh seepage, the clinging wetness of moss, the cold breath of fog.

Their clothing is minimal and functional, woven from the fibers of tough grasses and the inner bark of rare, stunted trees that grow in the deepest hollows. The fabric is naturally water-resistant, treated with waxes pressed from local fungi, and is worn in loose layers that can be adjusted to the temperature. They go barefoot, their soles tough and calloused from a lifetime on stone, and they move with a deliberate, balanced gait that respects the slickness of the ground.

Their settlements are not built so much as they are grown into the landscape. A typical home is a chamber carved into the base of a basalt dome, its entrance a low, curved archway that directs the constant seepage around the threshold. Inside, the walls are smooth and cool, and the air is filled with the scent of wet stone and the faint, earthy aroma of stored fungi. They have no furniture in the conventional sense; they sit on stone benches that are part of the floor itself, or on woven mats of moss that cushion the hardness of the rock. Light comes from small, covered niches where bioluminescent fungi are cultivated—their glow soft and blue, providing illumination without heat.

Their economy is one of patience and barter. They trade in things that are slow-growing and enduring: mats of rare mosses with specific medicinal properties, carved stone tools that last generations, and small, carefully tended patches of edible fungus that take years to mature. They have no concept of wealth in the accumulation of objects; a person’s worth is measured by their ability to endure, to listen, and to contribute to the slow, steady balance of the plateau.

Socially, they are egalitarian and deeply interconnected. Decisions are made by consensus, often after hours of sitting in silence, listening to the drip of water and the whisper of grass. They have no leaders, only elders who are respected for their patience and their memory of the plateau’s long cycles. Children are raised collectively, taught from infancy to move with care, to listen to the subtle changes in the seepage, and to understand the language of stone and water.

They are not a spiritual people in the sense of worship or ritual, but they are profoundly reverent. They believe that the plateau is a single, living entity—stone and water and moss and all—and that their role is to be its quiet consciousness, to witness its slow endurance. They do not seek to change the landscape; they seek to become part of its equilibrium.

In the evening, when the light softens further and the haze thickens, they gather in the sheltered lee of a dome, sitting in a circle on the damp ground. They do not speak much. They listen to the plateau breathe, and in that listening, they find their place.
