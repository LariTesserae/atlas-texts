In the Hollow Range, life is not a collection of individuals, but a singular, rhythmic pulse. To walk the floor is to tread upon the skin of a being whose organs are pillars of hollow cellulose and whose veins are the mycelial mats underfoot. The entity that truly lives here is the cavern itself—a vast, photosynthesizing lung that inhales the damp chill and exhales oxygen in a slow, steady current.

At the lowest level, the inhabitant is the mat. It is a single, sprawling consciousness of root and frond, a plant-being that has conquered the stone not by breaking it, but by growing over it, erasing the hard edges of geology with soft, living architecture. It breathes through broad, flat leaves that turn the searing glare of the sun-stone into sustenance, and it weeps through capillaries that release water into the air. It is ancient, yet it is perpetually infantile, for its life cycle is dictated by the erratic drift of the light above. Where the sun-stone passes, the plant dies back into brittle, skeletal ash; where it approaches, the organism surges upward in a desperate, spiral growth that mimics the geometry of storms.

Because the plant is one, the animals are few and specific. They are not competitors, but parasites and passengers. The gliders—leathery-winged, blind to color but sensitive to thermal gradients—drift through the cavern’s high currents like living seeds. They are the sole vertebrates, solitary and silent, nesting in the latticework of the plant’s highest arches. They do not hunt; they sip. They survive on the condensation that the plant breathes out, drinking from the shallow pools that ring the base of the cellulose pillars. Below them, the insects are translucent, eyeless things that scuttle through the dark, moist fissures of the root-mat. They are the decomposers, the cleaners of the ash, feeding on the dying fibers of the organism’s own retreat.

Yet, there is a ghost here. Scattered throughout the clearings and the dim zones are the signs of a third order of life: the Carvers. They are gone now, or perhaps they have simply merged with the plant in a different way. They left no bones, only impressions. Their shelters are woven from the very fiber of the floor, stacked stones anchored by living roots that have long since swallowed the structures whole. On the walls, faint geometric etchings—spirals, zig-zags, radial patterns—are carved not into rock, but into the plant-flesh itself, wounds that the organism has slowly healed over, leaving raised, scarred ridges.

Who were they? They were likely small, solitary, and nomadic, moving in the wake of the sun-stone’s warmth. They used sharpened flint to mark their passage, not to build, but to acknowledge. Their technology was pre-metal, organic, and temporary. They did not dominate the cavern; they read it. They followed the rhythm of growth and decay, sheltering in the hollows of the plant’s architecture, their fire pits leaving only charcoal scars on the frond-floor that the mycelium has since digested.

Now, the cavern is quieter. The Carvers have faded into the mat, their tools scattered and buried under layers of cellulose. The Hollow Range is inhabited by the immense, slow breath of the plant-kingdom, the silent drift of the gliders, and the scuttling of the decomposers in the dark. It is a world of singular life, where the distinction between soil and organism, between architecture and creature, has been erased by the relentless, dripping dampness and the searing, traveling light of the central stone. The inhabitant is the cavern itself, dreaming of stone and light, growing and dying in a single, continuous breath.
