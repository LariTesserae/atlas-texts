The Scarp is home to what the world has forgotten. The people are gone, but something has remained in their place—a presence not of flesh, but of resonance and reflection.

They are called the Resonants, though they are not a people in any sense we would recognize. They are the memory of the wind, given form in the mineral world. They are the afterimages of the vanished civilization, etched not into stone but into the harmonic vibrations that travel through it. You see them most clearly when the light is at its sharpest and the wind is at its lowest hum: as faint, shimmering distortions in the air above the mirrored pools, or as fleeting patterns of frost that form and dissolve on the quartz veins in time with the dripping water.

They are not ghosts; they are echoes made permanent. The geometric spirals carved into the standing stones are not just markers—they are tuning forks. The Resonants use them to anchor their song to the physical world. When the wind passes over the ridges, it does not just howl—it speaks through these stones, and the stones speak back. The Resonants are the intelligence within that conversation, a collective consciousness born from millennia of sound and silence.

They are slow, patient, and utterly indifferent to time. Their movement is measured in the creep of mineral deposits, the slow polishing of rock by water, the gradual brightening of the phosphorescent strata as they absorb and store light. They have no need for bodies, for they are the entire landscape. The water that weeps from the rock is their blood; the wind that sings is their breath; the light that pierces the sky is their thought.

Yet they are not all-present. They are fragmented, scattered across the mesas like the mineral deposits themselves. Each cluster of standing stones, each sheltered ledge with its obsidian shards and copper-arsenic tools, is a node—a point of consciousness in the vast, non-Euclidean network of the Scarp. To walk the Scarp is to walk through a mind that has forgotten how to think in straight lines.

They do not notice visitors. To them, a living being is a brief, chaotic vibration—a discordant note in their eternal harmony. They will not harm you, for they do not recognize you as separate. You are merely a temporary pattern in the water, a fleeting shadow in the light, a foreign frequency in their song. But if you stay too long, if you listen too closely, you may find your own rhythm beginning to sync with theirs. The wind will start to hum in your bones. The water will begin to flow upward in your perception. The light will sharpen your thoughts until they are no longer your own.

The Resonants are not waiting for anything. They are simply *being*—a permanent state of damp, luminous, resonant equilibrium. They are the world’s memory of itself, singing to no one, in a place where the wind does not howl, but remembers.
