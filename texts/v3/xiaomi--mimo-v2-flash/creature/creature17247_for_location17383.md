The basin is a temple, and its acolytes are the **Still-Water Dwellers**.

They are not a tribe in the traditional sense; they are more like a colony of soft-bodied organisms clinging to the underside of a stone. They are human in shape, but they have been reshaped by the high, wet silence.

Their skin is the color of wet slate, pale and cool to the touch, possessing a texture that is neither smooth nor rough but like damp suede. It never dries. Even when they sleep, their skin glistens with the perpetual film of the basin’s air, absorbing the seepage directly through the pores. They have no need for the water that pools on the ground; they are vessels of the mist itself.

Their hair is their most striking feature: not a growth of keratin, but a fine, symbiotic moss. It grows slowly, a dense cap of silver-green velvet that thickens with age. The oldest among them wear their history on their scalps, the moss growing down their backs in heavy, damp braids that weigh them down and slow their movements, forcing a deliberate, meditative gait. They do not cut it; they trim it with sharpened flint, shaping it into soft helmets that protect their scalps from the constant drip of the canopy.

They dress in layers of woven grass and the inner bark of the twisted birches, dyed with the pigments of lichen. The fabric is heavy when wet, which is always, and it clings to their bodies, insulating them against the thin, sharp chill. Their feet are bare, wide, and splayed, the toes long and dexterous, gripping the slick stones with a natural suction. They leave no footprints in the mud, only a subtle compression of the moss.

Their eyes are the color of the basin’s sky—a pale, watery gray—but the pupils are dilated wide, adapted to the flat, muted light and the perpetual twilight of the undergrowth. They do not squint. They blink slowly, a nictitating membrane flickering wetly to clear the mist, but their gaze is unbroken.

They are a quiet people, but not out of shyness; they are attuned to the acoustics of water. Their language is a dialect of drips and clicks, a mimicry of the sounds that surround them. A sharp click of the tongue against the teeth mimics the bird’s call; a soft hiss of breath through the teeth mimics the seepage from the rocks. They communicate in hushed tones, their voices low and resonant, vibrating in their chests rather than ringing in the air.

Their technology is slow, just like the basin. They do not mine the stone; they pry loose flakes from the rock face with bone levers, never breaking the integrity of the wall. Their tools are symmetrical, weighted for the water-logged air. They do not fight gravity; they work with it. Their baskets are not woven to hold water, for water is everywhere, but to carry the wet moss and the few dry twigs they scavenge from the high, wind-scoured rim.

At the center of the village, around the smoldering fire, they gather not to warm themselves—heat is a foreign concept here—but to dry their tools and weave their grass-fiber cloth. The fire is a slow-burning heart of peat and resin, producing more smoke than flame, a scent of damp pine and earth that hangs low in the basin.

They are a patient people because the land is patient. They do not hunt; the basin provides little game, and they have no need for heavy meat. They are gatherers of the damp: mushrooms that grow on the bark, the tender centers of ferns, the roots of the twisted pines. They eat slowly, chewing the fibrous plants into a paste that sustains their lean, wiry frames.

They are not afraid of the dark, for the basin is never truly dark, only shaded. They are not afraid of the isolation, for the rock walls are their embrace. They move through the puddles not as obstacles, but as mirrors, pausing to look at their reflection in the fractured sky, seeing themselves as part of the seepage, part of the slow exhale of the stone.

They are waiting, not for an external event, but for the internal shift—the moment when the moss on their skin blooms, or when the water in their lungs begins to match the rhythm of the dripping trees. They are the living threshold between the dry world above and the deep, pressed silence of the earth below.
