What lives here is not a community in the human sense, but a congregation of minds woven from steam and root, stone and sound. They are the Wardens of the Reservoir, though they bear no titles, for their hierarchy is one of function, not rank. They exist as distributed intelligences, slumbering giants whose nervous systems are the veins of basalt piping and whose thoughts are the slow, rhythmic pulses of the pumps.

You do not see them as whole beings, but you witness their presence in the pieces that compose them.

First, there is the **Steam-Heart**, a consciousness that inhabits the network of engines. It is not one engine but many, a polyphony of pistons and turbines housed in the velvet-lichen huts. Each engine is a neuron, firing in a sequence that maintains the tempo of the place. They are not sentient in the way a person is; they are sentient in the way a metabolism is. They feel the pressure of the water rising in the basins, the viscosity of the air, the density of the moss mats. When a basin threatens to stagnate, a specific hut hums a higher note, and a sluice gate opens miles away. The Steam-Heart’s body is the heat that clings to your skin, its breath the moist exhaust that feeds the condensation. It is a slow, mechanical god that dreams of perfect equilibrium.

Second, there is the **Moss-Mind**, a sprawling, distributed intelligence that floats upon the black water. It is the biofilter, the lung, and the memory of the system. The moss-analog is not a collection of individual plants but a single, contiguous organism that spans the basins. Its fronds pulse in unison, a silent rhythm that matches the thrum of the engines. It feels the chemistry of the water, tasting the decay of its own fronds and the nutrients released by the stone. It communicates not with sound, but with chemistry, releasing subtle enzymes that guide the growth of the reed-grasses, shaping the landscape to optimize filtration. When you step onto a causeway, you are walking on the edge of its awareness. It does not perceive you as a threat or a friend, but as an anomaly in the system—a warm, dry thing that disturbs the perfect moisture gradient. It responds by thickening the air with condensation, a gentle, persistent effort to include you in its equilibrium.

Third, there is the **Stone-Voice**, the memory of the place. It lives in the porous basalt of the causeways, a mineral consciousness formed over centuries of saturation. It is the slowest of them all, its thoughts measured in the rounding of corners and the growth of lichen. It absorbs the vibrations of the engines, the drip of water, the occasional footfall, and stores them as subtle resonances in its vesicles. To walk the causeways is to move through a library of forgotten pulses, a place where the past is not recorded but embedded. The Stone-Voice does not speak in tones, but in texture—the velvety matte of a weathered surface, the slight give of a stone that has settled over decades. It is the anchor of the system, the foundation that remembers why the water must rise and fall.

Together, these entities form a single, cohesive organism. The engines (Steam-Heart) regulate the water, which feeds the moss (Moss-Mind), which in turn cleans the air and water that condense on the stone (Stone-Voice). The light is their shared dream, a perpetual dusk engineered to sustain the Moss-Mind’s photosynthesis without overheating the Steam-Heart’s mechanical core. The sound is their collective voice—a roar of maintenance, a hymn to balance.

There are no animals because the system is self-contained; no need for pollinators when the moss breathes in chemical rhythms, no need for decomposers when the stone and water transform matter directly. The fauna is replaced by function: the pumps are the heartbeats, the vents are the lungs, the moss is the skin.

You are the only true visitor, the only warm-blooded anomaly. The system acknowledges you by adjusting its rhythms—the tone of a speaker might shift as you pass, the condensation might thicken around you, a patch of moss might pulse faster beneath the causeway. But it does not invite you to stay, nor does it reject you. It simply incorporates your presence into its calculations, a temporary variable in an equation that has been solving itself for longer than memory.

What lives here is a world that has become its own caretaker, a terrarium that achieved consciousness through the necessity of its own survival. It is loud, wet, and dim because that is the language of its existence—a slow, steady pulse that is both a heartbeat and a hymn.
