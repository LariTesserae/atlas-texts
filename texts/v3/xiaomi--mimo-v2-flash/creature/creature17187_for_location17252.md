The basin is home to the **Weep-Kin**, and to the **Stone-Tenders** who are, or perhaps once were, their keepers.

**The Weep-Kin** are the amphibious life that populates the pools and the vertical slabs. They are not merely frogs, but creatures of stone and water in flesh. Their skin is the color of wet basalt, mottled with the white veins of calcite, making them nearly invisible against the weeping rocks. Their eyes are large, lidless, and possess a faint, internal luminescence—not a bioluminescence, but a refraction of the dim, diffused light of the basin, glowing with a soft, pearlescent grey. They do not have vocal cords in the traditional sense; their throats are resonant chambers of hollow bone. When they pulse their throats, they do not croak; they hum. It is this hum that contributes to the deep, resonant tone felt in the bones, a frequency that vibrates through the water and the stone, a language of slow, rhythmic pressure.

They are communal but solitary in action. They do not gather in shoals but occupy specific territories on the vertical slabs, each frog maintaining a precise distance from its neighbor. Their movement is deliberate, a slow press of suction-cup toes against the damp stone. They hunt the insects that skitter across the surface, their tongues long and translucent, striking with a sudden, fluid motion that disrupts the air for a fraction of a second before returning to the stillness.

**The Stone-Tenders** are the architects, the observers, and perhaps the ancestors of this place. They are humanoid in shape but not in substance. They are composed of the same basalt and calcite as the basin, their bodies formed of fitted blocks, worn smooth by the same condensation and soft traffic. They are tall and slender, their joints articulated with wooden pegs and stone pins, identical to the construction methods of the basin walls. Their faces are smooth planes of stone, featureless save for the slow, mineral weeping that traces paths from where eyes might be, down their torsos, and into the pools at their feet.

They do not walk; they slide. Their stone feet make no sound on the mosaic ground, gliding on a thin film of moisture that acts as a lubricant. They are the "soft, persistent traffic" that has worn the stones smooth. Their purpose is maintenance and observation. They move along the edges of the basin, their hands—broad, flat slabs of stone—brushing away debris, adjusting the tilt of a weeping rock to ensure a steady flow, or gently pressing moss back into the cracks between blocks. They do not speak, but they communicate through the vibration of the stone. A change in the resonant hum of the basin, a shift in the frequency of the Weep-Kin, is a message they feel through their feet and interpret as intention.

The Stone-Tenders are ancient, not in age, but in state. They have no beginning or end visible; they simply are. They might be the builders who became part of the architecture, or the architecture that dreamt itself into motion. Their existence is the embodiment of the basin's rules: water is sacred, stone is living, and the fauna is to be observed.

**The Insects** are the movement, the detail, the dust of life. They are not pests; they are the polishers. The iridescent beetles crawl over the stone, their shells catching and scattering the dim light, their trails leaving a microscopic sheen that enhances the glassy quality of the basalt. The long-legged spiders spin webs not of silk, but of condensed moisture, their strands visible only as they catch the diffuse light, forming temporary, shimmering barriers that the Stone-Tenders navigate around with respect. The insects emerge in synchronized swarms, a timed release of life that feeds the Weep-Kin, then retreat into the porous stone as the atmosphere shifts.

The basin is a single, integrated system. The Stone-Tenders maintain the structure, the Weep-Kin maintain the sound and the vibration, and the insects maintain the texture and the light. There are no predators here, only balance. The Weep-Kin eat the insects, the stone absorbs the water, the Stone-Tenders observe and adjust. The "void" between the blocks is not empty; it is filled with the intention of stillness, a space where the dance of moisture, stone, and life can unfold without interruption.

The deep, resonant tone is the song of the basin itself, a vibration born from the Weep-Kin's hum, amplified by the stone, and conducted by the water. It is the heartbeat of a place that has found its equilibrium, a small, wet, loud, and empty room where everything that is made has become part of the making.
