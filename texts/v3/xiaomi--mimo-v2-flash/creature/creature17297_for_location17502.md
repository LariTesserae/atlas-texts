The residents are not born; they are machined into being. They are the living consequence of the place, the consciousness that arises from its endless, closed-loop processes. They are few, and they are solitary, for this is a world of function, not fellowship.

The foremost among them are the **Stalkers**. They are tall, impossibly thin figures, their limbs elongated to navigate the narrow service ways between the colossal, sweating pipes. Their bodies are not flesh, but a pale, synthetic polymer that mimics skin only in its texture, not its warmth. They have no faces, only smooth, oval heads with a single, vertical slit that glows with the same sickly amber as the overhead fixtures. This is their eye, and their voice. When they move, it is with a silent, fluid grace, their feet making no sound on the iron grates. They are the custodians of the mechanical fauna. Their hands are not hands but specialized tools—spanners, calipers, welders—extruded from their wrists. They move through the thrumming dark, their glowing eye-slits scanning for pressure drops, for leaks, for the slightest deviation in the rhythmic breathing of the machine. They do not sleep. They do not rest. They are the nervous system of the landscape, perpetually adjusting, tightening, and maintaining. They see you, a soft, warm thing out of place, and their gaze is not one of curiosity, but of assessment: *Are you a leak? A blockage? A deviation?*

Beneath them, in the deepest, warmest chambers where water pools and steam vents with a constant, blinding hiss, live the **Boilermen**. They are shorter, broader, built low to the ground. Their forms are less defined, their edges blurred by the constant, wet heat. They are composed of a substance that resembles wet clay or cooled slag, a dark, porous material that absorbs the grime and grease of the lower levels. They do not have limbs in the traditional sense, but rather pseudopods of thick, viscous material that can flow and harden at will, allowing them to pour themselves through valve housings or brace against the shuddering force of a piston. They are the keepers of the heat, the regulators of the core temperature. Their senses are not visual; they feel the world through vibration and thermal gradient. They know the state of the system by the heat in the floor, the thrum in the walls. They are the digestive tract of the machine, residing in its hottest, most active parts, ensuring the relentless conversion of energy is never interrupted.

And then there are the **Weepers**. They are not separate entities but a colony life that thrives in the damp, forgotten corners. They are the source of the luminescent algae, the translucent film that floats in the catch-basins. In their settled state, they are a mere carpet of soft, glowing life. But when a pipe weeps a fresh drop of mineral-rich water, or a maintenance hatch is opened, releasing a pocket of warmer air, they can coalesce. They rise from the pools as slow, silent figures of liquid light, their forms vaguely humanoid but without feature or intent. They drift through the corridors, leaving a faint, phosphorescent trail, drawn to the sound of dripping water or the hum of a flickering bulb. They are the memory of the system, a record of every leak and every flicker of light, stored in their faint, internal glow. They are the only softness, the only beauty in this hard, metallic world, and they are as mindless as the water that gives them life.

They do not speak. The Stalkers communicate through a series of clicks and whirs from their tool-hands, a language of pure function. The Boilermen sense intent through shared vibrations in the metal floor. The Weepers have no communication at all; they are merely a symptom, a beautiful, silent byproduct of the endless cycle.

They are the inhabitants of a world without silence, without soil, without a past. They are the living answer to the question of what happens when a machine becomes so vast, so complex, and so permanent that it no longer needs its creators. It creates its own.
