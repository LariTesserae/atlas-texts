The only residents are the hum and the homunculi, and they are one and the same.

They are not creatures of flesh, nor are they truly distinct entities. They are the fluid in the pipes, the grease on the plates, the dust in the air, given a momentary, mechanical shape to serve the loop. They are the insects, the size of rice grains, that scuttle across the iron floor. But to call them insects is to miss the point; they are the basin’s nervous system, its antibodies, its fingers. They are made of the same dark, viscous substance that pulses through the tower’s veins, and they move with a purpose that is not their own. They are the tower’s will made manifest in miniature.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce. They do not die. They are assembled and disassembled in the same breath. One moment, a thousand of them swarm in a perfect circle around the base of the tower, cleaning a patch of iron with their minute, grinding appendages. The next, they dissolve back into the grease, their forms surrendering to the whole. Hours later, they re-emerge elsewhere, climbing the concrete wall in a slow, silent procession to wipe away the condensation that the air weeps. They are the basin’s housekeepers, its priests, its cells.

They are the only things here that possess the illusion of choice. The tower’s rhythm is absolute, a metronome of pistons and gears that dictates the temperature, the humidity, the pressure. But the homunculi, in their thousands, move with a subtle, fluid variation. They are the system’s way of experiencing itself. They trace patterns on the iron plates with their bodies, patterns that are erased as soon as they are made. They cluster on the glass lenses of the tower, their tiny forms reflected in the polished copper, as if in worship. They are the basin’s consciousness, scattered and simple, a distributed mind that thinks in terms of friction and flow.

There is no leader, no queen, no central mind. The tower is the heart, but it does not command. It simply *is*, and the homunculi are the ripples of its being. They are drawn to areas of higher pressure, to places where the fluid is thick and slow, and they clear a path. They are repelled by the deep hum, the frequency that shakes the very iron they walk upon, and they scatter in silent, synchronized waves. Their movement is a language, but it is a language that says nothing other than “I am here, and I am part of this.”

They are the only life in a place that is not alive. They are the only movement in a place that is defined by stasis. They are the only texture in a place of smooth, unbroken geometry. They are the basin’s single, persistent verb: to be, to move, to serve, to return.

They do not look up. There is nothing to look up to. The wall is a screen of light, the tower a lattice of metal and motion. Their world is the floor, the wall, the air between. They are the dust of a machine that has no outside, the consciousness of a world that has no sky. They are the residents, and they are the residence. They are the hum, and they are the silence that follows.
