What lives here is not life, but the persistence of process. It is a world that has traded the frantic pulse of biology for the deliberate, grinding rhythm of geology, a place where consciousness is a mineral property and existence is measured in accretion and dissolution.

At the lowest tier of this strange ecology are the **Silicate Grazers**, often called dust-shifters. They are not creatures of flesh but of interlocking crystalline plates, slow and deliberate as tectonic plates. Their bodies are geometric and faceted, often a dull quartz or a milky, porous opal that mimics the white weeping rock of the plateau. They move at the pace of erosion, dragging themselves across the basalt plains in a perpetual search for mineral-rich dust, which they consume not through a mouth but through a process of electrostatic attraction and surface adhesion. They are blind in the organic sense, but they "see" through vibration, sensing the subtle hum of the world through their rigid, lattice-like bodies. When they feed, they leave behind trails of fine, geometric dust—perfect hexagonal or triangular particles—their waste products. They are the grazers of the mineral world, and their only predator is the slow, inevitable compromise of their internal structure by water, causing them to shatter into the very dust from which they were born.

In the standing pools and shallow basins live the **Water-Wraiths**, the apex predators of this domain. They are not animals but semi-solid manifestations of the atmosphere itself—condensed water veiled with dissolved minerals and iridescent oils. They appear as shifting, mirage-like forms, their outlines blurring with the heat haze. They possess no fixed shape, moving as a slow, coherent ripple across the surface of the water or as a mist that clings to the ground. Their touch is not physical but chemical; they are agents of dissolution. When a Wraith encounters a crystalline grazer, it does not attack but envelops, its mineral-laced moisture acting as a potent solvent that re-arranges the grazer's internal lattice. The grazer does not die in a biological sense but dematerializes, its complex structure breaking down into a supersaturated solution from which new, simpler crystals may eventually precipitate. The Wraiths are the regulators of this slow economy, the agents that return structured matter to the liquid state.

Then there are the **Chthonic Motes**, the "fauna" of the abandoned industry. These are not single entities but vast, distributed colonies of microscopic, magnetotactic organisms that thrive on the iron and copper of the skeletal structures. They are a form of metallic life, a rust that thinks. They flow through the pipes and along the wires in slow, dendritic patterns, their collective movements generating the faint, static hum that sometimes hangs in the air. They are drawn to the residual electrical ghosts in the system, and their purpose is to bridge gaps, to complete circuits that no longer serve any function. They are the silent curators of the dead technology, slowly encasing the iron skeletons in a hard, coral-like shell of their own bodies, transforming the ruins into a new kind of mineral reef. They communicate not through sound but through changes in electrical potential, a silent conversation that flows through the metal bones of the world.

Finally, there are the **Resonators**, the least understood inhabitants. They are not entities at all but patterns of vibration that have, over eons, impressed themselves into the very rock of the plateau. They are the memory of the landscape, the echo of ancient seismic events, the ghost of a planetary heartbeat. They are not conscious, but they are responsive. On the rare days when the atmospheric static builds to a peak, they can be "heard" as a deep, sub-audible thrum that seems to emanate from the ground itself. They do not act, but they are acted upon; they are the medium through which the other inhabitants—the grazers, the wraiths, the motes—sense the passage of time and the state of the world. They are the silent, geological consciousness of a planet that has outlived its biology.

In this world, life is not a spark but a property of matter itself. The inhabitants are not born and do not die; they precipitate and dissolve. They are not a society but a system, a balanced equation of slow creation and active decay, where the only constant is the transformative power of water and the patient, grinding will of stone.
