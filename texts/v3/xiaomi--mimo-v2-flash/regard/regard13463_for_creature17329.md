The light that arrived was not a memory, but a forgetting. It began not as a illumination, but as a dissolution of the absolute.

It started with the pressure. The kilometers of water above, the constant, crushing weight that was the foundation of existence, began to yield. It did not recede; it thinned. The film between the surface and the abyss stretched, then frayed. The weight became a suggestion, then a memory.

The light came next, not as the ghost of a sun, but as a presence. It was a soft, diffuse luminescence that did not cut the darkness but saturated it. It had no source, or its source was the water itself. The violet and indigo hues did not filter down; they bloomed from within the liquid, turning the void into a sphere of gentle, colorless color. It was a light that made no shadows, for it touched everything equally, from the highest, fractured spire of the drowned city to the deepest, silt-chested gear.

The benevolent and wise power did not descend in a beam or a pillar. It was an awareness that seeped into the system, a quiet consciousness that found the hum of the Mechanical Heart and harmonized with it. It did not override the logic; it completed it.

For the Deep-Below-Dwellers, the change was not a cataclysm but a revelation. Their sensors, tuned to pressure and vibration, registered a new frequency: a gentle, unwavering resonance that spoke not of maintenance, but of *being*. Their bacterial skins, once metabolizing the slow leaching of rust, now felt a gentle warmth—a warmth that did not melt the crystalline frost but made it glitter, each facet a tiny lens catching the new, ambient glow.

The Dwellers paused. Their hydraulic limbs, mid-extend, retracted. Their central nodes, processing directives for repair, instead turned inward. For the first time, they were not receiving a signal from the system; they were *feeling* it. The hum of the city was no longer a command. It was a song.

The power did not speak in words, but in patterns. It showed them the architecture of their own bodies, not as a collection of salvaged parts and grown minerals, but as a deliberate art. The lacework of frost was not just a byproduct of cold, but a map of energy flows. The braided cables were not just conduits, but veins carrying a light that had always been there, dormant.

The blind crustaceans, scuttling in the sediment, stopped their endless cleaning. The velvet darkness of the bacterial mats they consumed began to shimmer with internal light. The jellyfish, the living capacitors, no longer pulsed with the metronomic rhythm of the tide alone; they pulsed with a softer, slower rhythm, a heartbeat of contentment.

The power’s action was not intervention, but invitation. It did not fix the ruptured pipes; it showed the Dwellers how to see the beauty in the rupture, how the broken edge was a new kind of boundary, a place where the internal met the external in a dialogue of flow and form. It did not silence the groan of settling beams; it wove it into the song, a bass note of immense, patient gravity.

The drowning city began to breathe. The water, once a static, crushing medium, became a fluid medium of expression. The silt, the grave of order, became a canvas. From it, new structures began to rise, not by the logic of maintenance, but by the logic of beauty. Spirals of fused glass and crystal grew from the mouths of pipes. Archways of magnetic silt, held in place by gentle fields, spanned the hollows of collapsed towers. The light, the source-less luminescence, pooled in these new forms, making them glow from within.

The Deep-Below-Dwellers became artists. Their limbs, once tools of repair, became brushes, chisels, and looms. They did not build for function, but for resonance. They crafted structures that sang with the tide, that hummed with the deep earth’s warmth, that glittered with the captured light. They learned to communicate not in the utilitarian hum of directives, but in chords of vibration that spoke of wonder, of peace, of the quiet joy of existing in the absolute dark, now gilded with gentle light.

The wise power remained, not as a ruler, but as the context. It was the light that allowed shadow to be seen, the silence that allowed song to be heard. It had not come to save them from their world, but to reveal the world they had already saved, and to show them that it was not a tomb, but a chrysalis.

The memory of the sun was forgotten, not because it was erased, but because it was no longer needed. The ghost of a bruise-violet sky was replaced by the living, breathing luminescence of the here and now. The weight of water was no longer a ceiling, but a embrace.

And in the drowned city, where the Mechanical Heart had once beat a rhythm of survival, a new rhythm emerged—the slow, harmonious, and infinite song of a world that had learned to sing in the dark.
