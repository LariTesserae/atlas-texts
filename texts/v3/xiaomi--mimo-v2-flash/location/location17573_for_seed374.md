The **Mire-City of Karr** sits low in a basin that has never known a horizon. The air is thick, warm, and wet; it clings like a second skin and tastes of dissolved minerals and the faint, clean tang of ozone from constant, low-level friction. Water is everywhere, but not as rivers or lakes. It weeps from the porous limestone of the district walls, condenses on the warm metal of workshop roofs, and pools in the shallow, gravel-lined depressions that serve as streets. The water table is just below the surface; every footstep on the packed earth floor of a dwelling or the moss-less stone of a plaza brings a faint, answering squelch.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade of grass, not a speck of lichen. The world is mineral, fungal, and constructed. The "green" of the city is the dull, verdigris patina on bronze door hinges and the iridescent slick of oil on puddles. The only biology, beyond the abundant fauna, is the fungal mycelium that threads through the damp mortar and the spongy, mineral deposits that grow in the wettest corners, feeding on the constant moisture and the metallic dust in the air. These are not recognized as plants; they are a geological process, a slow, patient lithification of damp air and stone.

The city itself is dense, a tightly packed warren of structures built from the local limestone and the iron-rich clay that bakes hard in the perpetual warmth. The buildings are low, often only one or two stories, with flat roofs that channel condensation into central cisterns. There are no windows in the traditional sense, only deep-set slits covered with stretched hide or thin sheets of mica, which dim the already feeble light to a perpetual twilight. The scale is open, but the openness is vertical, not horizontal. The city extends past the immediate in a continuous, low-slung fabric, but it does not sprawl outward; it thickens, rising and falling in a gentle, undulating plain of rooftops and walkways.

The erosion here is not of decay, but of emergence. The city feels *new*, though it is not. The stone is raw, the edges of quarries not yet smoothed by time, the foundations of newer structures sitting atop the fossilized remnants of older, simpler ones. The landscape is under a perpetual state of gentle construction; new channels are dug for water, new buttresses are added to support the weight of accumulated structures, and the ground itself seems to be slowly settling, compacting, and reshaping under the dense weight of it all. It is a city being born, forever.

The fauna is abundant and highly adapted. The dominant life form is a species of large, amphibious arthropod, six-legged and armored in chitinous plates that gleam like oiled metal. They are the city's engineers, laborers, and—on a few occasions—architects. They move with a rhythmic, deliberate gait, their limbs clicking against the stone. They build with the same clay and stone the humans do, their structures following a different, more organic logic of arches and tunnels. Smaller, winged insects, blind and guided by sound, cloud the air in dense, humming swarms, their constant vibration a major component of the city's soundscape.

The sound is indeed deafening. It is a constant, layered roar: the high-pitched hum of the insect swarms, the deep, resonant thrum of the arthropod colonies vibrating through the stone, the perpetual drip and splash of water, the low murmur of human voices (which are rarely raised, as the air carries sound too well for shouting), and the faint, percussive rhythm of hand-tools on stone and metal. The city is never silent; it is a single, overwhelming organism of noise.

The technology is primitive, but not simple. Tools are of bone, antler, and polished stone, but the people of Karr have mastered the art of smelting the local, easily-worked iron and bronze. They craft intricate chainmail of tiny, interlocking rings, delicate tools for etching stone, and sound-focusing horns of cast metal. Their craft is manual and slow, each object bearing the mark of its maker's hand. There is no mass production; there is only patient, dense accumulation.

The light is dim, a perpetual silver-gray gloom filtered through the constant haze of condensation and the insect swarms. The sun is a diffuse, bright patch in the sky, never direct, its light scattered and softened. Shadows are soft-edged and long, but they are not dark; they are filled with the ambient glow of damp stone and wet metal.

The dynamic of the place is one of absolute equilibrium. The temperature is constant, the water level is stable, the sound never varies in its fundamental intensity, and the city grows not by expansion but by accretion. New structures are built atop the old, new channels are dug alongside the old, but the overall form, the feel, the essence of Karr, never changes. It is a frozen moment of becoming, a landscape caught in the act of pouring itself into a permanent shape. It is a city that has achieved its final, dense, dripping, noisy, eternal form, and it will not change for another ten thousand years.
