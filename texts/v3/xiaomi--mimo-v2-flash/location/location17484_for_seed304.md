It is a city of stone and water, built on a marsh that has forgotten its own name.

The land is low, barely rising above the level of the water table. The ground is not solid earth but a lattice of limestone and soft shale, fractured by recent tectonic shifts—hence the *young* erosion. The rock is sharp, its edges unweathered, splintered into knife-edges and sudden crevices. Water seeps from every fracture, not in streams, but in weeping lines, as if the stone itself is exuding a slow, perpetual sweat. The air is cool, never warming beyond a mild damp chill, and condensation clings to every surface. Puddles are everywhere, but they are not still; they tremble with the pressure of groundwater rising from below, and in some places, the ground breathes—small geysers of murky water that collapse and reform with a soft, sucking sound.

The city is packed. Buildings—what you would call buildings—are not separated by streets, but interlock like puzzle pieces, their walls pressed directly against one another. The scale is small: a single district, perhaps a quarter-mile across, with no clear boundaries. It is a hamlet that has consumed itself, growing inward rather than outward. The architecture is primitive—no metal tools, no refined joins. Stones are fitted by hand, rough-hewn and slotted together with a sort of dry-masonry precision, but the joints are tight. Roofs are slate or thatch, but the thatch is old and brittle, and the slate is often broken, letting water seep into the rooms below.

The materials are what the land provides: limestone for the walls, slate for the roofs, peat for fuel, and reeds for thatch. Wood is scarce; the few trees that grow are stunted, water-logged, their roots tangled in the soft substrate. They are not a resource; they are part of the drainage system, their roots knitting the ground together where stone cannot.

The atmosphere is dense with moisture and quiet. There is no wind, or almost none. The air is still, heavy, and scented with wet stone, peat, and the faint, metallic tang of groundwater. The sound is low: the drip of water from eaves, the gurgle of hidden drains, the occasional plop of a stone settling into mud. Birds are rare—perhaps a few solitary wrens that nest in the cracks of stone walls—but their calls are thin and distant. Insects are not numerous, but they are persistent: gnats hover in the damp air, and water-striders skate across the puddles with a delicate, almost ceremonial slowness.

The ecology is sparse but intense. Mosses and lichens cling to every vertical surface, forming a living skin over the stone. Ferns sprout from the seams between walls, their fronds dripping with condensation. There is no grass, only patches of hardy, wiry sedge that grow in the mud between stones. The ground is soft, yielding; every step sinks slightly, and the stone paths are laid not to keep you dry, but to give you a firm place to stand. The marsh is alive, but it is a quiet life: the slow growth of algae in the puddles, the patient work of earthworms in the mud, the gradual shift of the water table as the season changes.

The built world is inseparable from the natural. Drains are not engineered; they are *allowed*, channels carved into the stone where water naturally flows. The city is a drainage system made visible. Rain falls, and it does not run off—it is absorbed, channeled, and finally exhaled through the stone itself. The city *drinks*.

The rules of this place are simple: nothing is wasted. Water is not a problem; it is a resource, a constant. The people—there are few, perhaps a hundred—live in close quarters, their homes and workshops and storerooms packed together. They do not build wide; they build tall, but not very tall, and the spaces are narrow. Corridors are not corridors; they are gaps between buildings, open to the sky, often covered with a second story that turns them into covered ways. The air is always damp, and fire is precious. They burn peat, and the smoke is low and heavy, settling in the covered ways and giving the air a smoky tang.

The weirdness is subtle. The water is not just water; it seems to have a memory. It carries impressions—faint, ghostly images of what it has touched, like the shadow of a leaf on a stone, or the outline of a hand pressed into wet clay. These are not mystical; they are simply the way light refracts through the mineral-rich water, but the effect is uncanny. And sometimes, the ground itself seems to *shift*, not violently, but with a slow, deliberate motion, as if the stone is still settling into its new shape. The city is not static; it is *becoming*.

The dynamic is the slow pulse of the marsh and the city together. The water table rises and falls with the seasons, and the city adjusts: doors are raised, thresholds are rebuilt, storage pits are sealed or opened. The people move with this rhythm, their lives measured in the depth of the puddles and the clarity of the air. There is no rush. The pace is biological, geological, patient. The city is not a machine; it is a living thing, and it is young. It has not yet decided what it will be.

This is a place where the boundary between building and growing has dissolved. The city is not *on* the marsh; it *is* the marsh, given a new form.
