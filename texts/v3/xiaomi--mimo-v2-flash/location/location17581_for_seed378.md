The air is thick, a constant visible weight. It is not the humid blanket of a jungle but something more mineral, more ancient in its wetness. Water condenses not on leaves—there are none—but on the black, glassy faces of the rocks that make up the terrain. These are not mountains in the classical sense, but a high, eroded plateau, a once-volcanic field smoothed by eons of some liquid, perhaps water, perhaps acid, into rolling hills and shallow, basin-like valleys. The stone is basalt, obsidian, and a strange, porous white rock that drinks the light and weeps a constant, slow seep of water from its pores.

The landscape is sparse. The rocks are scattered, not piled, leaving vast expanses of a fine, dark sand between them. The sand is not silica; it is the pulverized remains of the same stone, a dust that holds no memory of life. There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a fungus, not a single blade of a non-recognized biology. This is a post-life, or perhaps pre-life, world of pure mineral and water. The only color comes from the minerals: rust-red veins in the basalt, the occasional gleam of pyrite in the white rock, and the shifting, prismatic film of oil-slick rainbows on the standing water in the basins.

The urban element is a ghost in this machine. It is not a city of flesh and stone, but one of industry and abandonment. Great, skeletal structures of rusted iron and stained concrete rise from the plateau, spaced far apart, their purposes forgotten. They are factories, or perhaps water-pumping stations, or weather-control arrays now defunct. Their interiors are empty, cavernous, and echo with the slow drip of water. Pipes, thick as a man’s torso, snake across the sand and vanish into the rock, some broken and weeping, others silent. The technology is industrial, but it is a dead industry. The engines are cold, the gears seized with rust and mineral deposits. The only electricity is a ghost in the wires, a faint, static hum in the air on the driest of days, which are not very dry.

The temperature is high, a dry, baking heat that seems to radiate from the stone itself. Yet the atmosphere is wet, condensing on every surface, creating a constant cycle of evaporation and precipitation without ever forming a true cloud. The sky is a blinding, bright white, the sun a white hole in the heavens, its light filtered through the thick, mineral haze. There is no twilight here, only a shift from blinding day to a deep, star-pricked blackness where the heat of the day radiates back into space.

The fauna is abundant, but it is not life as we know it. It is the fauna of the mineral world. Crystalline entities, slow and deliberate, crawl across the stone. They are not animals; they are geologic processes given a semblance of mobility. They are prisms that drink light and excrete fine, geometric dust. They are slow, grinding things that move at the pace of erosion, their "bodies" composed of interlocking mineral plates. They communicate through shifts in vibration, felt through the stone, a language of geologic time. There are also the water-wraiths: shimmering, semi-solid forms in the standing pools, creatures of condensed atmosphere and dissolved minerals, their shapes shifting like mirages. They are the apex predators here, dissolving the crystalline grazers with a touch that is not physical, but chemical, a re-arrangement of their mineral structure.

The sound is profound silence, broken only by the sounds of the landscape itself. The whisper of fine sand shifting in a heat-driven breeze. The low, resonant hum of a distant, functioning pump, a mechanical heartbeat in the emptiness. The *plink* of a water droplet hitting a metal surface in an abandoned factory. The soft, gritty slide of a crystalline entity moving over rock. These are the loudest sounds. Voices, if they exist, would be a shock, an intrusion.

The rules of this world are simple. Water is the solvent of everything here. It carries minerals, shapes the stone, and is the medium for all interaction. The "ecology" is a cycle of dissolution and re-precipitation. The crystalline entities are born from supersaturated pools, grow by accretion, and return to dust when their internal structure is compromised by water or the touch of a water-wraith. The industrial structures are slowly being reclaimed by the same process; their iron skeletons are becoming a new form of mineral, rusting not into flakes but into a hard, coral-like shell.

It is a landscape of active decay and slow, mineral rebirth. The dynamic is not biological, but geochemical and mechanical. The weather cycles are driven by the intense heat and the constant, low-grade release of water from the porous rock, creating a perpetual, localized fog and drizzle. It is a world in balance, but a balance of stasis and slow transformation, where life is not a spark of energy but a property of matter itself.
