The place is a settlement, but a settlement that has been hollowed out and turned inside out. This is the Interstice, a vast, shallow, and entirely constructed basin carved into a landscape that never knew a natural ground. You stand on a floor of fused clay and powdered stone, a surface polished by generations of shuffling feet. The temperature is a constant, warm embrace—there is no direct sun here, only a diffuse glow that rises from the ground itself, a pale, silvery light that seems to be the breath of the place.

The architecture is not vertical, but horizontal and layered. The ground is a mosaic of platforms, sunken plazas, and low, vaulted corridors, all carved from the same pearlescent, vitrified clay. There are no free-standing walls; instead, the boundaries of spaces are defined by the rise and fall of the floor, by the curvature of a low archway, or by the dense clutter of furniture that is grown from the same clay as the walls. The scale is intimate—cathedral-like in its vaulted ceilings, but room-like in its immediate feel. The density is overwhelming. Every inch of the floor and walls is occupied: sleeping niches are carved into the plinths, market stalls are built into the curvature of the arches, and the air is thick with the hanging baskets of dried fungi and the taut, string-like vines that serve as both lines and borders.

The water is a constant presence, but not in rivers or pools. It is a dampness that breathes. A fine mist weeps from the high, vaulted ceilings, collecting on the broad, shallow ledges that serve as tables and seats. The mist condenses and runs in narrow, carefully guided channels carved into the floor, feeding into central cisterns that are themselves great, hollowed-out chambers. These cisterns are the heart of the Interstice, their water not for drinking—for that, they tap the mineral-rich condensation—but for life. Here, in the dim, wet dark, the ecology blooms.

The vegetation is sparse above ground, but in the cisterns and the damp corridors, it is a tangled, luxurious mat. The plants are not trees or grasses, but fungal structures and broad-leafed succulents that grow in a symbiotic mat with algae and lichen. They are the builders' primary material. The "wood" of the place is a tough, fungal mycelium that is woven, dried, and hardened. The "cloth" is a felted algae-fiber. The "rope" is the string-vine. The fauna is abundant and small: blind, pale fish in the cisterns; chirring, cricket-like insects that crawl on the fungal mats; and the furred, burrowing moles that are the Interstice's primary protein, hunted with simple spears and traps. There are no large predators here; the only predation is the slow, careful harvest of the ecosystem by its inhabitants.

The sound is loud, not with echoes, but with a dense, layered cacophony of life and craft. The constant drip of condensation is the bass note. The high, chittering call of the insects is the treble. And the human sound is a low, murmur of tools: the soft tap of a clay-modeller's mallet, the rasp of a fungal-wood saw, the rhythmic slap of a hand-weaver's loom. There is no silence here; the air itself is a medium of constant, low-grade activity.

The erosion is established. The rounded edges of every arch, the polished smoothness of the floor, the gentle undulation of the surfaces—these are the result of millennia of the same activity: the slow, patient carving of the clay, the flow of water, the brushing of bodies. The place is not decaying; it is being continually renewed by the same process that wears it down. The technology is primitive—stone, bone, and hardened fungal wood are the only tools. The "built" parameter is absolute; there is no memory of a natural substrate. The ground beneath your feet is the floor of a vast, man-made bowl, and the sky above is a distant, vaulted ceiling of the same material.

The weirdness is subtle, almost unnoticeable unless you look for it. It is in the way the light seems to emanate from the clay itself, a soft bioluminescence that has been bred into the material over generations. It is in the way the water in the cisterns seems to have a faint, greenish glow. It is in the fact that the entire Interstice is a single, interconnected organism, a living, breathing settlement that grows and repairs itself with the patient, silent work of its inhabitants. The dynamic is active; the mist rises and falls, the fungal mats spread and are harvested, the moles burrow and are caught, and the people move through the dense, warm, damp, loud, and beautiful maze they have made, and in which they live, utterly and completely.
