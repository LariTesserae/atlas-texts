The landscape is a hot, low-altitude desert, but not one of sand and stone. It is a desert of construction.

The ground is a single, uninterrupted plane—a vast, silent floor of pale, heat-radiating material that extends to the horizon in every direction. It is not flat in the human sense; it is engineered, with a gentle, almost imperceptible undulation that guides the eye toward distant, hazy vanishing points. The surface is cool to the touch, not from shade, but from its own internal geometry, a lattice of micro-channels that wicks away the oppressive heat, leaving the air shimmering but the ground itself dry and stable. There are no dunes, no cracks, no erosion marks—only the subtle, almost organic-looking seams where one massive panel meets another, stretching for kilometers in straight, unbroken lines.

Vegetation is sparse, a 1.1 on the scale, and it is of a single, strange kind. It is not plant life as we know it, but a crystalline flora. At scattered intervals, towering, faceted spires of translucent silica rise from the ground, some as tall as skyscrapers, others no larger than a person. They are not rooted; they are *grown*, extruded from the substrate itself in slow, geometric accretions. Their surfaces catch and refract the normal, harsh light, casting prismatic shadows that crawl across the ground as the sun moves. They do not photosynthesize. They do not breathe. They are silent, mineral structures that seem to exist only to break the monotony of the plane, and to channel the occasional moisture.

Water is a 1.3—damp, intermittent. There is no surface water, no rivers, no lakes. Instead, water is a secret, a hidden process. In the pre-dawn hours, when the hot air finally cools, condensation beads on the fluted surfaces of the crystalline spires and trickles down into hairline fissures at their bases. For a few hours, the ground around each spire is dark with moisture, a damp circle a few meters wide. By midday, the heat has reclaimed it all, pulling it back into the air as a faint, invisible vapor. The only evidence of this cycle is a slight darkening of the substrate in those circles, and a faint, saline smell that hangs in the air after the moisture has gone. Once every few weeks, a localized rain falls from a cloud that seems to form from nothing, drenching a small area for an hour before vanishing, its water sinking instantly into the thirsty ground.

The scale is vast, continental. This is not a place one walks across; it is a place one exists within. The horizon is a smooth, unbroken curve. The atmosphere is thick with heat, but the engineered ground keeps it from becoming a lethal oven. The weather is its own system: heat domes form and dissipate, and the sparse, crystalline vegetation interacts with them, their facets sometimes focusing the sun into brief, intense points of light that scorch the ground before moving on. There is no animal life, not a single insect, bird, or microbe. The only dynamics are physical and chemical: the slow growth of the spires, the daily cycle of condensation and evaporation, the occasional rain, the patient shift of sunlight and shadow.

The sound is a 0.5—silence, or near-silence. The wind, when it comes, is a low, breathy whisper that sighs across the vast, empty plane. It carries no dust, for there is no loose material to carry. The crystalline spires sometimes hum, a barely audible vibration when the wind strikes them at a certain angle, a sound like a distant, single note of a glass harmonica. Otherwise, there is only the immense, profound quiet of a world without life, without struggle, without decay.

The erosion is 0.3—emerging, embryonic. This world is not old. It is new, raw, under construction. The substrate shows no signs of age. The spires are pristine, their facets sharp and unweathered. The seams between the ground panels are crisp, not filled with dust or grit. It is as if the entire landscape was laid down yesterday, and the processes that will one day wear it down have only just begun to stir. There are faint, almost invisible patterns in the ground—like the ghost of a blueprint, a substructure just beginning to show through the surface. It is a world in its first moments of existence, and the rules of its making are still visible, still active.

The technology is 0.3—none. There are no devices, no tools, no signs of intelligent construction in the human sense. The spires are not machines; they are grown. The ground is not assembled; it is extruded. This is a biology of geology, a made-environment that operates by its own, unknown rules. It is a pure, natural system, but its nature is construction.

The world is sparse, dense with space. Between the spires, there is nothing but the pale, smooth ground and the shimmering air. The emptiness is a presence in itself. It is a landscape of balance, of emptiness and form, of heat and hidden moisture, of vast scale and microscopic detail. It is silent, active, hot, and damp, and it is entirely, utterly alone.
