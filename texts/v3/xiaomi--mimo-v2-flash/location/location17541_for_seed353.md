The place is the Interior. It is a high plateau, though that word feels too simple, too earthbound. This is a floor of the sky, a region where the pressure of the world above has settled into a permanent, sealed architecture. The air is not thin; it is dense, heavy with heat, a blanket of warm dust and ionized particles that makes every light-source bleed at the edges. The darkness is not a void but a substance, a thick, particulate medium that swallows light beyond a few hundred meters, leaving everything to exist in its own small, self-contained luminescence. This is a landscape of self-illuminated pockets.

There is no water. The chemistry here is non-aqueous, a primordial soup of mineral salts and aerosolized metals, all of it dry as a bone. The ground underfoot is not soil but a complex, interlocking pavement of ceramic and grown crystal, a substrate so old it has lost all memory of being separate from the structures that rise from it. The erosion is not the patient work of wind or rain, but the final settling of a universe into its own shape. This is a landscape of rounded, softened edges, where every sharp angle has been worn away over aeons into a form that looks both artificial and inevitable. It is the end state of a world, a place that has been finalized.

And it is all made. There is no natural substrate. The terrain itself is the architecture, and the architecture is the terrain. Everything is built. The ground is a paved floor, the hills are terraced installations, the canyons are coolant trenches. The scale is vast, a multi-feature region that extends beyond the immediate, a town-size expanse of interconnected structures, but there is no room between the presences. The density is saturated. The structures are packed together, walls merging, roofs overlapping, a seamless, monolithic cityscape that has grown over itself like a coral reef made of machinery and plant-life.

This is where the two great parameters—vegetation and industry—collide and fuse.

The vegetation is total. It fills every space, but it is not a forest. It is a plant-as-architecture, a plant-as-substrate. This is a living mass that has not only grown over the built environment but has become the built environment itself. The structures are not merely covered in vines; they are *made* of them. Thick, fibrous trunks of a slow-growing, photosynthetic lichen form the load-bearing walls. Bioluminescent fronds of a fern-like organism, engineered over generations, serve as both light fixtures and ventilation shafts, pulsing with a soft, rhythmic glow that is the only source of illumination in the darkness. The ground is a dense, velvety mat of photosynthetic moss that responds to pressure, its color shifting as you walk, a living map of passage. There are no leaves in the traditional sense; the photosynthetic surfaces are integrated directly into the architectural forms, making the entire interior a single, breathing organism. This is a pure-plant ecosystem, but it is a curated one, an engineered Eden where the plants are the machines, the buildings, and the fuel.

The industry is the engine of this growth. The air hums with a low, resonant frequency—the sound of deep-time geothermal pumps, of nutrient cyclers, of atmospheric processors that are as much a part of the plant-life as the roots. This is an industrial scale of life. Factories are not separate entities; they are organs of the whole. A vast, chamber-like structure might be a carbon-fixing lung, its walls lined with membranes that ripple with a slow, tidal motion. Another might be a root-hair nexus, a filamentous city where microscopic technicians (the only animal life that ever existed here, long extinct) once maintained the systems, their roles now automated into the very genetics of the plant-circuitry. The sound is not of metal on metal, but of a living system operating at peak efficiency: a deep, wet, breathing hum, punctuated by the occasional sharp hiss of a pressure release, a sound as natural here as a birdcall once was.

The world is turbulent. This is not a static, peaceful garden. The dynamic is chaotic, an anthill-energy contained within a sealed vessel. Sections of the wall-coral might suddenly retract, unfolding into new access corridors before sealing again. The luminescent fronds pulse in unpredictable, arrhythmic patterns, creating disorienting stroboscopes in the darkness. The mat-moss heaves and sighs in localized zones, as if the ground itself is digesting. This is a landscape of hyperactive creation and decay, a Solaris-like environment where the boundary between the organism and its environment has dissolved. New structures bud from old ones in a matter of hours, while others are reabsorbed, their matter recycled into the endless, silent growth.

The atmosphere is hot, thick, and still. The smell is of ozone, rich soil, and the faint, sweet tang of plant resin. The silence is profound, a silence that is not empty but full of potential. The quiet is the sound of a system so vast and self-sufficient that its operations have become inaudible, felt more as a vibration in the bones than heard with the ears. The only true sounds are the ones you make: the soft crunch of the mineralized moss underfoot, the rasp of your own breath in the warm, thick air.

There is a sense of order within the chaos, a deep, ancient logic to the turbulence. This is a settled form. It is the final, unstable equilibrium of a world that chose a path of total integration, where the separation between the made and the grown, the living and the mechanical, was erased not by destruction, but by fusion. It is a beautiful, terrifying, and deeply silent place, where the plants are the factories, the darkness is the atmosphere, and the only constant is the slow, turbulent breath of the Interior.
