The place is a high-altitude wetland, a basin cupped in the shoulders of a mountain range where the air is cool and thin, but thick with moisture. The water table sits just below the surface, and the ground is never dry; it is a sponge of peat and moss, a quilting of mosses, lichens, and carnivorous plants that work the air and the stone for what they need. The terrain is a soft, undulating plain of plant-substrate, a living carpet that rises into knolls of tangled root-mat and falls into shallow, tea-colored pools that reflect a sky perpetually on the verge of rain.

The atmosphere is heavy with condensation, and a fine mist clings to every surface, coalescing into rivulets that trace paths through the vegetation. The rocks weep; they are not stone so much as mineralized plant matter, their surfaces etched with the slow channels of water and root-fiber, their interiors pithy and damp. Where the ground is firmer, it is a lattice of fibrous root-hold, a woven mat that supports the weight of anything that moves across it without sinking.

The ecology is overwhelmingly alive. Fauna is not an intrusion here but the very pulse of the system: birds with iridescent plumage that dart through the canopy of aerial roots; amphibians that breathe through their skin and nest in the wet leaves of pitcher plants; swarms of insects that move in clouds, their wings beating a constant, low thrum. The plants themselves are the architecture and the inhabitants: trees are columnar growths of fungal-moss composite, their "bark" a living skin of lichen; vines hang in dense curtains, dripping with dew and alive with invertebrates; the ground is a mat of sphagnum and sundew, each droplet a trap for tiny prey. The air is loud with the chorus of frogs, the chirr of insects, the drip of water, and the sigh of wind moving through hollow stems.

The settlement is small but dense, a hamlet of organic construction woven into the living landscape. The buildings are not separate from the plants but are grown from them—woven frames of flexible vine and root, walls of packed moss and clay, roofs of overlapping leaf-crowns that shed rain in a steady patter. The paths are raised beds of root-mat, and the central square is a clear patch of ground where a great, ancient tree-moss has been allowed to grow unchecked, its surface a tapestry of micro-ecologies. The people here use hand-tools of bone and stone, and their crafts are of plant-fiber: nets, baskets, and the delicate weaving of water-catchment systems that channel mist into drinking pools.

The world's rules are simple but firm: water is life, and the balance is maintained by constant change. The landscape is in a state of turbulent equilibrium—storms of wind and rain sweep through, causing sudden growth spurts and minor collapses of root-mat, but the system absorbs and renews. The erosion is established; the water has carved slow, patient channels, and the plants have grown in response, their roots knitting the soil into a stable yet dynamic whole. The scale is intimate, bounded by the basin walls and the canopy of aerial roots, creating a room-like enclosure that feels both vast and enclosed.

Sounds are layered and loud: the constant drip of water, the chorus of frogs, the rustle of unseen fauna in the leaf-litter, the creak of plant-stems as they grow and settle, and the low hum of the wind through hollow reeds. The light is normal, but filtered through the mist and the canopy, casting the whole place in a soft, diffused glow. The temperature is cool, a gentle chill that settles into the bones, but the living mass of the vegetation generates a faint warmth, a microbial heat that makes the air feel alive.

It is a place of pure, living architecture, where every surface is substrate, every space is filled, and every breath is a communion with a world that is wet, loud, and in constant, turbulent renewal.
