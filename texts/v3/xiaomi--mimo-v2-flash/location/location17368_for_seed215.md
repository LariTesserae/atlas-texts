The place is a high, cold, wet plateau, vast enough to generate its own weather patterns—low, slow-moving clouds that spill mist across the basins and are fed by condensing vapor from the land itself. The elevation is high but not mountainous; it is a broad, continental uplift, a highland the size of a subcontinent, so immense that its horizons are not edges but gradations of atmosphere. The air is thin and sharp, and the cold is a constant, dry cold that settles in the bones. It is not a dramatic cold, but a pervasive one, like the stillness of a deep cellar.

Water is everywhere near the surface, but not as rivers or lakes. It weeps from the rock. The plateau is built on a raw, young geology—stone that is still settling, still forming. The erosion is minimal, not because the landscape is old, but because it is new. The rocks are smooth and unscarred, as if they were poured and cooled only yesterday. They are dark, fine-grained, and porous, and from their faces, water seeps in slow, constant sheets. It gathers in shallow depressions, forming mirror-like pools that reflect the low, gray sky. The air is heavy with moisture, condensing on every surface, so that even the stones are slick with a film of water. There is no true dryness here; the damp is a medium, a condition of existence.

The vegetation is sparse, but not barren. It is a low, hardy mat of mosses and lichens that clings to the rock, thriving in the wet cold. Grasses grow in scattered tussocks, their blades stiff and pale, and here and there, a stunted shrub, its branches gnarled and black, lifts a handful of leaves. The plants are adapted to the constant moisture and the cold; they are slow-growing, patient, and permanent. They do not compete so much as coexist, each finding its niche in the thin soil that collects in cracks and hollows.

The fauna is abundant, but it is shy and scattered. The space between presences is great. You might walk for an hour and see only the tracks of a small, furred creature in the mud, or hear the distant cry of a bird. But the life is there, in numbers. The cold does not deter it; it is a cold-adapted ecology. There are burrowers that live in the soft earth under the moss, and grazers that move slowly across the plateau, feeding on the tough grasses. Predators are few but efficient, their movements silent and patient. The ecosystem is in motion, but it moves at a biological pace, slow and deliberate.

The urban element is surprising in this raw landscape, but it is there: a settlement, or rather a network of settlements, scattered across the plateau. They are not cities as we know them. They are built from the same stone that forms the plateau, cut and shaped with a technology that is beyond modern—a technology that seems to work with the raw geology rather than against it. The structures are low and wide, following the contours of the land, their roofs curved to shed the constant moisture. They are connected by raised pathways of stone, and by a system of tunnels that run just below the surface, keeping the cold at bay. The infrastructure is sparse but efficient, designed for the vast scale and the slow, cold conditions. The settlements are not crowded; they are spaced far apart, each a self-sufficient node in a network that spans the plateau.

The light is normal, but it is filtered by the constant mist and the low clouds. The sun is a diffuse glow, never bright, and the shadows are soft and long. The day is not marked by a dramatic sunrise or sunset, but by a gradual shift in the quality of the light, from a pale silver at dawn to a deeper gray at dusk.

The sound is mostly quiet. The wind is a low hiss across the rock, and the water drips and seeps with a soft, constant patter. There is the occasional call of a bird, or the rustle of a creature in the grass, but these are sporadic. The quiet is not empty; it is filled with the presence of the land and the slow movement of life. It is a quiet that demands attention, that makes the smallest sound significant.

The world is normal in its weirdness. There is no magic, no alternate physics, no strange phenomena. The strangeness is in the scale and the rawness: the fact that this is a plateau the size of a continent, with its own weather and its own slow, cold ecology, and that it is young, still forming, and that human—or post-human—life has adapted to it with a technology that is advanced but not alien. The weirdness is in the quiet, in the constant damp, in the way the rock weeps water and the way the life persists in the cold. It is a place of slow, deliberate processes, of vast spaces and small lives, of a technology that has learned to live with the land rather than conquer it. It is a plateau of quiet persistence, a high, cold, wet world that is still coming into being.
