The place is a high plateau, a vast and densely woven canopy of life suspended in a warm, perpetual dusk. The air is thick with the breath of a million green things, a humid warmth that clings to the skin like a second layer. Water is the secret here—not in open lakes or rivers, but in the constant, slow exhalation of the forest. It beads on the enormous, waxy leaves of canopy trees, drips from the moss-slick bark of cycads, and gathers in the knotted cups of bromeliads that cling to every vertical surface. The ground is never truly dry; it is a sponge of decaying matter and living roots, releasing a faint mist when the sun—hidden somewhere above the endless layers of foliage—occasionally pierces through.

Everything is built. There is no soil, no raw stone. The very ground is a tapestry of interlocking root systems, fungal networks, and the hardened, fossilized resin of ancient, colossal flora that has been growing and dying for millennia. The structures are organic, but unmistakably engineered: great archways of living wood that knit together at the apex, walls of woven vines so dense they form a solid, flexible barrier, and platforms of layered, pressed foliage that bear the weight of the constant, slow traffic. The scale is immense, a regional ecosystem of constructed life, where the "town" is a single, continuous canopy crown and the "valley" is the gentle depression in the center of this living city.

The fauna is overwhelming, and it is the primary architect. The sounds are deafening not with noise, but with presence: the deep, resonant hum of a trillion insects, the constant rustle and click of chitinous limbs moving through leaf-litter, the low, guttural calls of large, soft-bodied grazers that move like slow-moving hills through the undergrowth.
