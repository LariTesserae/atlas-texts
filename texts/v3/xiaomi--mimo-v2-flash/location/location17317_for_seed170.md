You arrive at the edge of the world, and the world stops.

The ground is not soil, nor sand, nor stone, but a composite of gray-white sulfate crust and fine, bleached glass, crushed to a powder that hisses underfoot. The elevation is high—what would be a mountain range elsewhere is here merely a plain, flat as a forgotten thought, stretched taut against a sky bleached by heat. The air is thin, dry, and thick with the taste of copper and static. It is hot, but not the humid fever of a jungle or the shimmering breath of a coast. This is a dry, mechanical heat, the kind that bleaches color from the world and turns sweat to salt the instant it forms. You feel the radiation on your skin not as a burn, but as a pressure, like the weight of a silent machine.

No water. Not a drop. The chemistry of this place is non-aqueous; what would be liquid here is instead a fine, suspended dust that coats the back of your throat and makes your eyes feel gritty. The ground is cracked in a vast, geometric pattern, not from the slow flex of tectonic plates, but from the cooling of an ancient, engineered surface. It is a calibrated fracture, a designed desiccation.

And it is loud.

The sound is not the roar of wind or the babble of life. It is a low, industrial hum that seems to emanate from the earth itself—a deep-frequency vibration that you feel in your bones before you hear it. It is the sound of massive, unseen mechanisms working in a cycle so slow it feels like stasis. In the distance, the hum shifts, and you hear a different layer: a high, metallic shriek, then a pause, then a grinding, like continents of gear-teeth turning in a maw of planetary scale. There is no machinery in sight, no structures, no towers. The technology is present, but it is buried, or perhaps it *is* the landscape. The built world here is not made; it is *grown*—not from life, but from process, from heat and pressure and time. The industry is geological.

Ecology? There is none. No plants. No recognizable biology. But there is *fauna*, and it is overwhelming.

They are not animals. They are not alive in any sense you know. They are patterns of energy, crystallized sound, static shapes that move through the air like heat haze. They are too slow to be seen as motion, but they are there, shifting in the long, patient cycles of the dynamic. Some are vast, the size of clouds, and hang motionless for decades before drifting a few meters. Others are smaller, like shards of glass that hover and spin in place, emitting a low-frequency vibration that harmonizes with the industrial hum. They are predators, but not of flesh. They feed on energy gradients, on the heat differentials of the world, on the resonant frequencies of the crust. They are the ecology of a world that has forgotten how to have life, so it has invented its own form of consumption.

The air is alive with them. You can see them, if you know how to look—not with your eyes, but with the edge of your senses. They are the reason the world is so loud. Their movements are slow, but their interactions are violent: when two energy-patterns collide, there is a flash of light, a crack of static that sounds like a whip, and a new, more complex pattern is born. They are reproducing, but it is a cold, mathematical reproduction, a multiplication of form in a world without DNA.

The weirdness is here, in the breakdown of reality. The horizon is not a line; it is a gradient. The sky does not curve. The ground, for all its flatness, is subtly curved in ways that strain the eye. Time does not flow evenly. You can feel the long, slow cycles of the dynamic—a minute might feel like an hour, then compress into a second. The light is normal, but it is too clean, too direct, as if the atmosphere is a perfect lens, magnifying every detail without distortion. There are no shadows, only gradients of darkness.

You are alone, but not. The fauna is everywhere, and they are aware of you. They are not hostile, not in any intentional sense. They are simply there, and your presence is a new variable in their equations. A small, shard-like pattern drifts toward you, hovering at eye level. It emits a low thrum, and you feel a vibration in your teeth. It is studying you, or perhaps it is simply resonating with the heat of your body. You are an anomaly in this world of calibrated desiccation and slow industry.

The ground cracks, not under your feet, but in a line far ahead, a new fracture forming in the sulfate crust. The hum deepens. The fauna shifts, patterns converging on the new break. This is a world that is still being built, still being designed, even if the builders are long gone. The industry is not dead; it is simply operating on a timescale that makes it invisible.

You are standing on a machine the size of a planet, and the machine is watching you through its ecology of static. The heat, the noise, the emptiness—it is all part of the design. And you are the only thing here that moves with any speed, the only thing that can break the cycle.

But not for long. The slow dynamic is already pulling you into its rhythm. You can feel it in your heartbeat, slowing to match the hum. Soon, you will be just another pattern in the static, another shard in the machine.
