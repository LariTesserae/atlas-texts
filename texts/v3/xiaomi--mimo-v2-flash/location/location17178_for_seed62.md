The place is a high plateau, a broad, sloping shelf of granite and slate that rises into the thin, cool air, its rim a sawtooth of sharp, young peaks. The air itself is the first thing you notice: it is bright, almost painfully so, as if the sun is closer here or the sky is a paler, more reflective blue. The light does not diffuse; it strikes. It catches the edges of every leaf, the filaments of every moss, the glint of a metal rail or a glass pane.

The ground is a testament to recent creation. The features are sharp, the valleys are not yet softened by millennia of weathering, and the rock faces are raw and angular. It feels as though the land was lifted from the sea only yesterday, and the water still remembers its old shape. This is a place of intermittent moisture, not deep lakes or flowing rivers, but a dampness that is everywhere and nowhere at once. Condensation beads on the cold metal of abandoned machinery and drips from the ceilings of hollowed-out rock overhangs. In the low places, between the roots of massive trees and in the crevices of the stone, there are pools of still, black water, each a perfect mirror for the bright, silent sky.

And the trees—they are a forest of giants. The vegetation is lush to the point of saturation. Broad-leafed trees, their bark the color of wet slate, form a canopy so dense that the forest floor is a perpetual twilight, lit only by shafts of brilliant sunlight that pierce the leaves like spears. The trunks are so close together that they seem to be leaning into one another for support, their roots a knotted, writhing mass that breaks the surface like the ribs of a great beast. Ferns unfurl in carpets a meter deep, and moss, a living, breathing velvet, covers every surface not in direct, searing light. It is a jungle in the high, cool mountains.

Yet this is not a pristine wilderness. It is a landscape threaded with the ghosts of industry. The built elements are scattered, not a city or a town, but a series of outposts, each a monument to a scale of production that has since ceased. A skeletal conveyor belt, rusted the color of dried blood, climbs a cliff face for a mile before stopping abruptly. The roofless, windowless shell of a processing facility sits in a clearing, its concrete floors now a mosaic of moss and fern. A single, massive crane, its arm frozen in a mid-air gesture, stands on the shore of one of the still, black pools. The technology is not sleek or futuristic; it is heavy, iron-and-brass industrial, powered by steam that once hissed from forgotten vents and electricity that once hummed through copper veins now buried in the loam. It is all dead, but the scale of it, the sheer ambition of its placement in this impossible, high, lush place, gives the silence a weight.

The silence is the most profound element. The sound parameter is zero, but it is not an absence of sound; it is a presence of quiet. The only noises are the ones you make yourself: the crunch of your foot on a bed of fallen leaves, the rasp of your own breath in the cool air. There is no wind in the deep forest, only the occasional, imperceptible shift of air as a leaf turns its face to the light. No birds call, no insects hum. The fauna is rare to the point of vanishing. Once, perhaps, there were animals here, but now there are only signs: a feather caught in the moss, a subtle, clawed scratch on a tree trunk, the shadow of a large, swift-moving shape that is gone before you can focus on it. The ecosystem feels complete without them, a self-sustaining machine of growth and decay that has no need for a visible animal component.

The rules of this world are those of patience and persistence. The dynamic is slow, almost imperceptible. Change happens across seasons, not hours. The industrial decay is not rusting away so much as being *absorbed* by the vegetation, the metal framework slowly being strangled by roots and draped in moss. The pools of water evaporate and condense in a cycle that lasts weeks. The strangeness is a low hum in the background, a sense that the geometry is slightly off. The angles of the buildings don't quite meet the angles of the cliffs. The light, while bright, seems to emanate from the landscape itself as much as from the sky, as if the quartz in the granite and the waxy surface of the leaves are storing and re-emitting the day's brilliance. The forest does not feel alive in a biological sense, but in a *conscious* one—it is a single, vast, thinking entity, and the scattered industrial ruins are the thoughts it is slowly, deliberately forgetting.

This is a place of dense, layered presences—the presence of water in the air, of light on leaf, of metal in stone, of silence in space. It is a town-size territory where you can see for miles across the plateau, yet the forest canopy and the sharp, young topography hide most of it from view until you are upon it. It is a cool, bright, high, damp, silent, and deeply strange garden built over the bones of a forgotten industry, where the only movement is the patient, relentless growth of moss over steel.
