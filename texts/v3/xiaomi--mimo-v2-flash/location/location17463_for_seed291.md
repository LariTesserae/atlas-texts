The place is a vast, low-lying basin, a bowl scraped into the earth, and at the bottom of this bowl sits a city. It is not a city of stone or wood, but of a living, industrious resin, secreted by the billions of organisms that crawl, fly, and burrow through every square inch. The air is perpetually damp, chilled to the bone, and smells of ozone, wet metal, and the sweet-rot of vast fungal blooms. A fine mist hangs in the air, not from rain, but from the constant condensation on every cold surface—the gargantuan, half-built skeletal structures, the web of pipes and conduits, the very ground itself.

The geology here is embryonic. The earth is soft, a black, peaty loam underfoot, shot through with veins of luminous, cooling magma that pulse with a faint, sub-audible hum. This is not a land of ancient mountains, but of *newness*. The low hills are not eroded; they are still *forming*, their slopes slumping and readjusting, giving birth to raw, jagged outcrops of a mineral that resembles polished obsidian but is soft enough to carve with a fingernail. Water does not run in clear streams; it seeps. It wells up in circular pools that steam in the cold, it drips from the fibrous ceilings of cavernous spaces, it is trapped in the capillary systems of the city’s architecture.

The vegetation is a lush, aggressive tapestry that uses the city as its trellis. Vines as thick as a man’s arm, covered in phosphorescent moss, coil around steel girders and fibre-conduit. Great, fleshy leaves, ribbed like lungs, spread to catch the dim, normal light that filters through the perpetual mist. Trees are not wood; they are mineral-fibre trunks that grow in geometric lattices, their canopies a dense mat of razor-edged fronds that rustle with a sound like scraping metal. There is no space between things; the density is absolute. Every surface is a substrate for life, and every life-form is a component of the machine.

The city is industrial, but its industry is biological. Factories are not buildings; they are *organs*. Towering, pulsating sacs of tissue pump fluids through translucent veins into vast, chambered vats where processes of fermentation and synthesis occur. The air vibrates with the chugging rhythm of piston-driven hearts and the whir of gear-toothed mandibles. There are no roads, only pathways of compressed resin and bone, trodden by a fauna that is abundant and alien. Six-legged grazers with carapaces of chitin and copper browse the fungal mats. Flying creatures, more like manta rays than birds, glide silently through the mist, their bodies crackling with static electricity. The predators are sleek, multi-jointed things that move with a hydraulic precision, their calls a series of resonant clicks and low-frequency thuds.

The strangeness is in the seamlessness. There is no clear line between the built and the grown, the organic and the mechanical. A factory wall might breathe. A bridge might be a living tendon, tensing and slackening. The city is turbulent, a place of hyperactive, chaotic change. Structures are constantly being added, dismantled, and repurposed. A new tower might erupt overnight, a blossom of resin and bone, while an old district is consumed by a advancing wave of aggressive mycelium. The energy is anthill-like: a frenetic, purposeful swarm activity that is both creative and destructive. It is a city that is alive, and it is growing, and it is not entirely stable.

The sounds are mostly quiet. The dominant noise is the pervasive, low hum of the city’s metabolic processes, a vibration felt in the teeth more than heard. This is punctuated by the distant, rhythmic clank of industry, the occasional wet pop of a pod bursting, and the skittering of countless small feet on resinous surfaces. The mist swallows sharper noises. It is a place of immense, packed scale, yet it feels hushed, intimate, as if the city is holding its breath.

Rules of the world here are simple: everything is connected. Waste from one process is food for another. Death is not an end but a transfer of material. The landscape is a closed loop, a cycle of creation and consumption so rapid and efficient it appears as a single, turbulent organism. The cold is not a climate; it is the city’s resting state, the temperature at which its complex chemistry runs most efficiently. To walk here is to walk inside a living, thinking, industrial machine that is simultaneously building and dismantling itself. It is a place of immense, packed scale, of cold damp and biological machinery, where the very ground is under construction and the silence is the sound of a world breathing.
