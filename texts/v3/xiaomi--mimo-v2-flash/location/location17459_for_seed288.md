The place is a city, but the city is long gone.

It lies below the surface, not in a cavern but in a vast, subterranean basin whose ceiling is a solid vault of ancient rock, its stone ribs curving into darkness miles above. The air here is cool, still, and utterly dry. There is no water—not a drop, not a mist, not a trace of humidity in the air. The chemistry is non-aqueous, a sealed system of minerals and gases that has not felt rain in a billion years. The ground underfoot is not soil but a fine, pale dust of salt and crushed silicate, a desert that has never known wind to shape it.

And yet, it is lush.

Towering from this dust are forests of crystalline flora. They are not plants in the biological sense—no chlorophyll, no roots drinking water—but rather slow-growing mineral structures that draw energy from the faint geothermal heat bleeding through the rock below and from the dim, indirect light that filters down from distant fissures in the ceiling. Their “leaves” are faceted sheets of quartz and calcite, their “trunks” are spirals of banded agate, their “canopies” are canopies of opalescent fans that catch the faint light and scatter it in prismatic halos. They grow in scattered groves, standing in the vast emptiness between the ruins of the city, their spacing balanced by the slow, patient competition for mineral nutrients and space.

The city itself is layered, built of the same stone as the ceiling and walls, but shaped by hands long turned to dust. The structures are not towering—they are low, sprawling, built for a cool, dim world with no weather to speak of. Streets are wide avenues of packed ash and salt, their edges softened by millennia of settling. Buildings are single-story, many of them roofless, their walls thick to hold the coolness and the quiet. There are no windows, only open archways that look out onto the crystal groves. The architecture is simple, geometric, a language of rectangles and arcs, with no ornamentation except what the stone itself provides—veins of color, patches of luster, the occasional fossil etched into a wall.

The fauna is abundant, but it moves slowly. The creatures here are like the flora—mineral-based, or at least not water-dependent. They are the animals of a dry world: creatures with carapaces of chitin and silica, limbs of jointed crystal, eyes that are faceted lenses. They move through the groves and the avenues with a deliberate, patient gait, their metabolism tuned to the slow cycles of geothermal heat and the faint light. They are silent. Their presence is felt rather than heard, a shifting of shadows, a faint clicking of chitin on stone.

The sound is deafening.

It is not a loudness of violence or chaos, but a deep, resonant hum that fills the entire basin. It is the sound of the stone itself—of the slow, imperceptible shifting of the rock above, of the cooling of the planet’s core, of the pressure of a billion years of silence compressing into vibration. It is a sound that you feel in your bones, a pressure in the ears that never fades. There is no echo, because the sound is everywhere, inside and out. You cannot escape it. You cannot speak over it. You can only listen, and feel your own body vibrate in sympathy.

The dynamics of this place are slow. The crystal groves grow at a rate that would be invisible to a human eye, their facets sharpening over centuries. The ruins settle another millimeter every thousand years. The creatures move like glaciers, their paths carved by generations of the same direction. There is no day or night here, only the slow dimming and brightening of the distant light through the ceiling cracks, a cycle that spans years.

The rules of the world are simple: there is no water. Life here does not need it. The air is a sealed vessel, its gases cycled by the slow metabolism of the crystal flora and the mineral fauna. There is no tool-making, no fire, no metal—only stone and silica and the slow, patient building of structures through accretion and growth. The world is normal, because it has never known anything else. The weirdness is in its normalcy, in the fact that a world can be so utterly different and yet so complete.

You stand in the avenue, between two groves of crystal trees. The air is cool and dry on your skin. The light is dim, but it is enough to see the faceted leaves and the ruins around you. The sound is a pressure, a presence. You are in a city that has been abandoned for so long that even the memory of its people is gone, replaced by the patient growth of stone and the silent movement of creatures that have never known water. You are in a place that is alive, but not in a way you know. You are in the subterranean desert, the crystal forest, the ancient city, the dry world—all at once, and all of it is normal.
