The place is a low, cold, and deeply dark city, but the word city is too hard a thing for it. It is a settlement grown from and within a single, colossal organism. The ground is not soil but a spongy, living mycelial mat, and the air is thick with the smell of cold, wet bark and decaying leaves. The water level is high; it is not a landscape of rivers or lakes, but of seepage and condensation. Water weeps from the caps of giant, shelf-like fungi that serve as roofs, and it pools in the hollows of the mat, creating mirror-still pools that reflect nothing but the absolute dark above.

The architecture is all plant. Towers are not built, but grown—hollowed-out trunks of a long-extinct species of tree, their interiors carved out by generations of inhabitants. The streets are not paved, but are the smoothed, worn pathways of mycelium, their surfaces cool and slightly yielding underfoot. There are no doors, only archways of woven root and vine. The structures are sparse; they do not crowd each other. Each home or workshop is a significant distance from its neighbor, separated by stretches of open, dark mat where other, smaller fungal forms sprout like isolated islands.

The scale is intimate, almost secretive. This is not a grand city of vast avenues, but a network of pockets, of rooms-within-rooms. A "plaza" is merely a slightly larger clearing, its space defined by the way the fungal towers lean away from each other, creating a dome of negative space. The entire settlement is contained within a single, vast, shallow depression in the land, a bowl of living tissue no more than a kilometer across, hidden from the wider world by a rim of denser, thorned vegetation.

The temperature is a constant, damp cold. There is no sun, and the darkness is total, broken only by the faint, self-generated bioluminescence of the fungal towers—a soft, blue-green glow that provides just enough light to see the outline of your own hand. The atmosphere itself is a source of light, a faint, chemical shimmer in the air from airborne spores and gases.

The fauna is overwhelming, but not in the way of a jungle teeming with visible life. Here, the life is in the air, in the walls, in the very mat underfoot. It is a cacophony of the unseen. The air hums with the wings of countless insects, their bodies adapted to the dark, some with elaborate, glowing antennae. The mat is alive with burrowing creatures, their movements causing faint, rhythmic tremors beneath the skin. The fungal towers themselves are ecosystems; their porous surfaces are crawling with mites, springtails, and blind, pale spiders that spin webs of conductive silk. The sound is not of roaring beasts, but of a million tiny lives: the click of chitin, the drip of water, the soft rustle of things moving in the hollows, the almost-subsonic thrum of the city's own biological machinery.

The world's rules are simple and biological. All is connected. To cut a vine is to sever a communication line; to damage the mat is to cause a localized fever in the settlement. The inhabitants—small, pale, and with large, light-sensitive eyes—practice a technology of the hand and the tool. They use sharpened shards of fossilized fungus as blades, weave clothing from fungal silk, and craft vessels from gourds grown in specially tended patches of the mat. Their knowledge is of patterns, of seasons of growth and decay, of the symbiotic relationships that keep the city alive.

The strangeness is a quiet, pervasive thing. It is in the way the light seems to move slightly when you're not looking directly at it. It is in the fact that the "wind" is not air moving, but the slow, collective exhalation of the city's respiratory system. It is in the feeling that the place is not just inhabited, but is itself a single, dreaming entity, and the inhabitants are merely the thoughts that flicker across its mind.

The dynamic is constant, slow, and deep. The city is not static. The fungal towers grow millimeter by millimeter, their light shifting in intensity with the city's metabolic cycles. Predator and prey are not animals, but processes: the slow consumption of dead matter by the mat, the competition between different fungal strains for light and nutrients, the cycles of spore-release that can, for a few days, fill the air with a palpable, electric fog. It is a city in a state of perpetual, quiet germination.
