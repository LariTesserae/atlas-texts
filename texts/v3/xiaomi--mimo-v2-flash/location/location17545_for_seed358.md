I am standing in a place that is, first and foremost, *wet*. The air itself is a heavy, condensing medium; it clings to the skin and beads on the cold metal of the machinery that surrounds me. There is no horizon in the traditional sense, only a low, rolling expanse of mist and steam that limits visibility to a few hundred meters in any direction. The ground is not solid earth but a saturated substrate—a thick, dark loam mixed with clay and volcanic ash—that yields softly underfoot with a sound like a sigh. Water is everywhere, not as rivers or lakes, but as a pervasive saturation: it seeps from the rocks, rises from the soil, and condenses from the air itself. The stones here are "weeping," porous and cool to the touch, each one a small, natural wick drawing moisture from the deep, wet earth.

The scale is immense, a valley or basin perhaps, but it feels enclosed, its boundaries lost in the perpetual dimness. The light is a perpetual twilight, filtered through a canopy of low, heavy cloud and the steam that rises from the ground. It is a pale, diffuse glow, more suggestion than illumination, casting long, soft shadows that seem to pool in the hollows and around the bases of the structures.

And the structures are scattered, like the bones of some great, forgotten beast half-buried in the mud. They are industrial, but not new. Great, rusted cylinders—boilers or condensers—lie on their sides, their seams weeping rivulets of clean, cool water into the muck. Thick, insulated pipes, some as wide as a man is tall, snake across the landscape, some buried just beneath the surface, others exposed and coated in a slick, green biofilm. They connect to low, windowless buildings of corrugated iron and stained concrete, their doors hanging ajar or missing entirely. The air hums with a deep, resonant frequency—the sound of massive, slow-moving pumps and compressors, working tirelessly in the damp. It is a deafening, omnipresent thrum that vibrates in the chest and skull, a sound so constant it becomes a kind of silence.

The ecology is packed to saturation. There is no bare earth. Every surface is occupied. The sparse vegetation is not absent but adapted: low, rubbery ferns with waxy leaves that shed water in perfect beads; mosses in electric greens and deep olives that carpet every vertical surface, including the pipes and the rusted walls of the buildings; fungi in pale, ghostly clusters that thrive in the perpetual damp. And the fauna is abundant, not in large herds, but in a teeming, minute multitude. Insects—translucent, winged things—drift in clouds in the steamier hollows. Small, blind amphibians skitter through the leaf litter. The water in every puddle and pipe joint is alive with tiny, darting fish and the larvae of a thousand unseen things. The air is thick with the buzz of gnats and the slow, heavy beat of the wings of larger, beetle-like creatures that navigate by sound in the dim light.

The world here feels *emergent*. The erosion is not of decay but of formation. The ground is soft because it is still being made, the rocks are weeping because they are still cooling, the pipes are settling because the earth beneath them is not yet stable. This is a landscape in its first, slow infancy, a place where geology and industry are in a kind of patient, symbiotic infancy. The machinery is not abandoned; it is *growing*. The pumps cycle with a slowness that matches the turning of seasons, the pipes shift a millimeter a year as the ground settles, the buildings sag gently into the mud as if they are taking root.

The rules of this world are few but absolute: everything is wet, everything is slow, and everything is occupied. There is no empty space. Between the pipe and the moss, between the rust and the fungus, between the steam and the insect-cloud, there is no gap. The air is too dense with moisture and sound, the ground too saturated with life and water, the structures too entwined with their own slow decay and regrowth.

It is a normal place, in its own way. There is no magic here, no strangeness beyond the fact of its own existence. It is a basin of wet warmth, of industrial infancy, of packed and patient life. It is a place that is still becoming, and it will be a very long time before it is done.
