The place is called the Hollow by those who live there, though it is anything but. It is a high, cold plain, so vast that its own weather systems form and dissipate in a slow, continent-sized breath. The scale is difficult to comprehend from the ground; the horizon is not a line but a layer, a smudge of pale grey where the earth itself seems to thin into sky.

The ground is a hard, fractured shale, a young rock still sharp-edged from its recent emergence. It lies in great plates, tilted and buckled, leaving fissures and sudden drops that are invisible until one is upon them. The surface is a mosaic of textures: smooth, wind-scoured expanses; jagged, recently split faces; and everywhere, the fine, gritty dust of pulverized stone. The ground is cold to the touch, a deep, conductive cold that leaches warmth through any surface it contacts. Frost is the primary form of moisture here—not as snow, but as a delicate, crystalline hoar that coats every shadowed surface and sparkles in the weak sun. In the deeper fissures, where the air is still, the frost is thick, a carpet of white velvet. Here and there, a pocket of water persists, a dark, still pool in a stone basin, its surface a perfect, frozen mirror, or a slow, syrupy seep that coats the rock in a slick, dark skin.

The urban fabric of the Hollow is not a city in the traditional sense, but a vast, decentralized settlement built from the very stone it stands upon. The dwellings are low, angular structures of fitted shale, their roofs sloped to shed the occasional, thin rain. They are built into the natural folds of the land, their doorways low and their windows mere slits to keep out the wind. The infrastructure is simple: paths worn into the rock, cairns marking routes, and above all, the network of stone conduits that carry the slow, precious water from the high collection basins to the lower, inhabited fissures. The air is filled with the scent of damp stone and the faint, smoky smell of peat fires.

The ecology is sparse in its greenery but dense in its life. Lichens in jewel-toned greens, yellows, and oranges crust the rocks, the only true plants. Yet the fauna is abundant. Small, hardy mammals with thick fur and low bodies scuttle between the stones. Birds, dark and streamlined, ride the constant wind, their calls a series of sharp, dry notes that barely disturb the prevailing quiet. The most striking creatures are the ice-worms, translucent, slow-moving invertebrates that live in the frost layer, moving with a glacial pace over months. The air itself seems to hold a density of presence—not of things, but of a latent, patient energy. The silence is not empty; it is a held breath, a space in which the smallest sound—a pebble shifting, a breath from a nearby dwelling—is amplified.

The rules of this world are defined by patience and conservation. Everything moves slowly. The erosion that shapes the land is measured in generations, not years. The water moves in imperceptible seeps and seasonal thaws. The people, whose technology is limited to stone, bone, and woven fiber, understand this. Their tools are meant to last, to be used gently and repaired endlessly. Their society is organized around the sharing of resources—water, fire-stone, and the rare, heat-retaining hides of the larger mammals. There is a hierarchy of need, not of power. The light is a constant, flat, silver-grey, the sun a pale disc that casts long, weak shadows and provides little heat. It is a normal light, but it feels ancient, as if it has traveled a great distance to arrive here.

At times, a wind rises, not with violence but with a profound, low sigh that seems to emanate from the stone itself. It is the only sound that can drown out the silence, and in its passing, it rearranges the dust and the frost into new, intricate patterns. The place feels less like a landscape one passes through and more like a system one is within—a vast, cold, urban plain of stone and frost, where life persists through stillness and the careful, shared management of a severe and beautiful world.
