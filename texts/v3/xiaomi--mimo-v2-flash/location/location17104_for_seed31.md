The place is a high plateau, a highland basin ringed by sharp young peaks that have not yet learned the patience of erosion. The air is warm and wet, not like a lowland swamp but like a mountain that remembers the rain. Condensation beads on every surface: the dark volcanic rock, the lichen, the rough-hewn timber of the low walls and roofs of the settlement. The water is never far away. It seeps from the ground in a thousand slow springs, collects in shallow, reed-fringed pans, and runs in narrow, clear channels that the people have lined with flat stones. The stones are damp, always. The air itself feels heavy with moisture, as if the sky is a low ceiling pressing down on the basin.

The settlement is sparse, built of the same stone and timber that frame the landscape. The buildings are low and wide, with sloping roofs that channel the constant condensation into cisterns. The streets are not paved but are compacted earth and stone, polished by the slow passage of feet and the constant damp. There is space between the houses, and more space beyond the last wall: open ground where the rock is exposed, where the vegetation is a low, lush mat of ferns, mosses, and hardy grasses that thrive in the perpetual wet. The trees are few and stunted, their branches twisted by the wind that sweeps down from the peaks. They are not dense; the plateau is open, the horizon visible in all directions, a broad bowl of rock and sky.

The fauna is absent. No birds call, no insects buzz, no small mammals rustle in the undergrowth. The only sounds are the ones the water makes: the soft trickle of the springs, the drip from eaves and rock faces, the low gurgle of the channels. Sometimes the wind breathes through the reeds, a hollow, sighing sound. Otherwise, there is a profound quiet, a silence that is not empty but full of the presence of water and stone.

The people here live by simple means. Their tools are of wood and stone, with occasional bits of worked copper or iron that they trade for or find in the rare, dry veins of the higher peaks. They shape their world with hand and hammer, building their homes and their walls, carving the channels that guide the water. Their technology is primitive, but their understanding of the plateau is deep. They know where the springs will rise after a rain, which stones are safe to step on, how to read the subtle shifts in the mist.

The world is in equilibrium. The basin does not change. The peaks do not soften. The water does not flood or dry. The people do not grow or shrink in number. It is a place of stable, gentle wetness, of warm air and sharp rock, of sparse life and deep silence. It is a plateau that has learned to hold itself, and in its holding, it holds everything else.
