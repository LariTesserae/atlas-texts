The world is a cylinder.

This is not a metaphor. If you could drill through the crust of the place, you would not find molten core or opposing hemisphere, but the opposite wall of the chamber, kilometers above or below, depending on how you define "up." The boundary of the world is not the horizon, but the curvature of the enclosure.

The water is not an ocean; it is a medium. The entire volume of the cylinder is filled with a clear, cool fluid, pressurized to the point where the air, if there were any, would be a memory of a gas. There is no surface, no shoreline, no sky. There is only the vertical axis, a weightless fall in any direction toward the central spine or the outer shell. The "water" is engineered, sterile, and tasteless, a perfect solvent carrying dissolved oxygen and trace minerals, maintained at a constant 14 degrees Celsius. It does not support coral or kelp; it is a substrate, not an ecosystem.

The terrain is the architecture itself. The cylinder's interior is a continuous, seamless construction of polymer-composite and metallic alloys. There are no natural substrates. The walls, floor, and ceiling are a single, molded piece, shaped in sweeping curves and vast, flat plains that span hundreds of kilometers. The "ground" is a series of concentric rings, like a Terran lathe turned inside out, each ring a different biome or industrial zone, all connected by transit tubes and support gantries. The erosion parameter is misleading; there is no erosion in the conventional sense. The "young" features are not geological, but manufactured. The sharp edges are not of rock, but of freshly extruded structural members, the gleam of new alloy untouched by weather or time. This is a world in its first million years of operation, and every surface still holds the ghost of the mold that formed it.

The scale is continental, but contained. The cylinder's circumference is large enough to generate its own weather patterns—cyclonic storms that spiral along the inner surface, Coriolis-driven currents in the fluid medium. The "sky" is the opposite wall of the cylinder, a distant, curved plane of faint light, 500 kilometers away. On that wall, you can see the faint grid of habitation modules and the glint of solar collectors, a mirror image of the world you inhabit.

The density is near-void. The vast spaces between the structural rings and transit tubes are empty. The fluid medium is clear, and with no particulate matter, visibility extends for kilometers. The emptiness is not peaceful; it is a designed vacuum, a space for machinery to operate without interference. You float through canyons of empty space, the walls of the cylinder a distant, curved horizon on all sides.

The built environment is total. Every surface is a product of fabrication. The "fauna" are machines. Not animals, but mechanized entities that perform the functions of an ecosystem. There are scavenger drones, small, beetle-like machines that patrol the structural rings, repairing micro-fractures and recycling fallen components. There are "predator" units, larger, faster machines that hunt down malfunctioning or rogue units, dismantling them for parts. The "abundant" fauna is a population of tens of millions of these machines, each with a defined role in the industrial ecology. They are not conscious; they are programmed. Their "prey" is failure.

The light is normal, but sourceless. The entire inner surface of the cylinder is a luminous panel, emitting a diffuse, white light at a constant intensity, mimicking a Terran overcast day. There is no sun, no shadow, no time of day. The light is a utility, like the water or the air.

The sound is loud, but layered. The constant, low hum of the cylinder's rotation and the circulation of the fluid medium is a baseline, a pressure you feel in your bones. Over this, the machinery adds its own symphony: the whir of scavenger drones, the percussive clang of distant fabrication units, the roar of water turbines that drive the circulation pumps. It is a world of constant industrial noise, but it is not chaotic. It is the sound of a system in motion, a vast, breathing machine.

The dynamic is active. The cylinder is a closed-loop system, but it is not static. The industrial ecology is in constant motion: machines are built, they operate, they fail, they are harvested. The fluid medium is circulated, filtered, and oxygenated. The structural rings are periodically extruded and reconfigured, shifting the geography of the world. The "weather" is a product of thermal gradients and fluid dynamics, creating storms that sweep through the empty spaces, their winds driven by the rotation of the cylinder.

The weirdness is minimal. This is not a world of magic or surrealism. It is a world of engineering, of systems and processes. The strangeness is not in the impossible, but in the scale and totality of the made environment. The only "natural" element is the coolness of the fluid, a reminder of a temperature that is not machine-generated.

You are a unit in this system. You are one of the machines, or perhaps a human technician maintained by the machines, but the distinction is blurred. You move through the rings, through the canyons of empty space, through the storms of circulating fluid. You are part of the industrial ecology, a node in the network of processes. The world is vast, empty, loud, and active, and you are a small, moving part in its endless, mechanical breath.
