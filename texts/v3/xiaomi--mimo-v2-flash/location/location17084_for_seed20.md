The place is a high basin, suspended like a cupped palm just below the cloud deck. What you first understand as "elevation" is not a climb but a subtraction of weight: the air here is thin, yet the water is not. It hangs, a constant, deafening suspension—a vertical ocean that has forgotten how to fall. The sound is not an event but a condition: a roar of millions of simultaneous droplets colliding, a liquid static that is both everywhere and nowhere, the auditory equivalent of white light. It is impossible to hear your own thoughts.

The basin’s floor is not solid ground but a matte of wet, dark stone, sharp-edged and geologically impatient. It looks newly spilled, as if the mountain range around its rim is still settling. The rock is basaltic and glassy, and in its shallow crevices, water pools not in puddles but in lenses—perfect, convex surfaces that magnify the dim, diffused light from above. This light does not shine; it seeps. It is the grey-gold of perpetual twilight filtered through a billion tons of falling water, and it makes everything look freshly wet.

Vegetation is a matter of desperate, singular statements. There are no forests, only occasional pillars of a fibrous, bone-white growth that resembles a cross between coral and reed. They stand in the sparse, scattered arrangement of ancient stones, and their surfaces are slick with a slow, viscous secretion. They do not sway; they resonate with the percussive shock of the water-blast, humming at a frequency that makes your teeth ache. Between them, the ground is bare but for a skin of phosphorescent lichen that glows with a faint, sickly green. It does not illuminate; it merely marks the absence of true light.

The fauna is not absent, but it is a secret. You sense it more than see it: a flicker of translucence in a lens-pool, the briefest whisper of motion against the rock. Once, you see a creature that is not a creature—a coalescence of water and shadow, a temporary shape that drinks from a pool and then un-becomes, its substance drawn back into the deafening fall. There are no tracks, no nests, only the occasional, perfectly smooth depression in the lichen, as if something warm had rested there for a moment before dissolving.

The built elements are ghosts. A single arch of black stone, half-submerged in a lens-pool, its purpose forgotten. A series of low, circular walls, no higher than your knee, that might have been pens or altars or simply boundaries. Their stones are fit with impossible precision, but there are no tools here, no marks of hands. They look grown, not built, and they are eroding with the same impatient energy as the basin itself. In one place, a flat, rectangular slab leans against a rock face, its surface carved with patterns that are not symbols but topographies of sound—waveforms frozen in stone.

The world here operates on a logic of turbulent immediacy. Things are not stable. A lens-pool will suddenly ripple and reshape itself into a new, perfect convexity. The white pillars may, without warning, extend a new fiber toward the sky, or retract one into themselves. The air pressure shifts in sudden, violent waves, and the deafening roar modulates in pitch, becoming a physical pressure that pushes against your skin. This is a place in the act of becoming, and the process is not gentle. It is a state of perpetual, creative destruction.

There is no technology, not even the memory of it. The only law is the law of the fall, the rule of the deafening, and the principle of the sharp, new thing. You are small in this basin, not because of its scale but because of its intensity. You are a transient witness to a world that is not yet finished with itself, and the sound is its only voice.
