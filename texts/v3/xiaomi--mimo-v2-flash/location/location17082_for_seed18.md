The place is a hollow. You would not call it a canyon, for the walls do not rise; they fall away. You stand on a floor of fused slag and porous iron, a surface that is entirely made, a dark, matte plate kilometers in diameter, and beneath your boots there is no earth, only the metallic shell of the world, a skin over an immense cavity. The air is warm, a constant 28 degrees Celsius, humid, not from rain but from a pervasive, weeping condensation that slicks the metal with a film of water, beading in the grooves of the constructed plates and dripping in slow, rhythmic patterns into hidden drains that gurgle somewhere deep below. The sky is a normal, hazy daylight, filtered through a perpetual industrial haze, but it is far above, a distant ceiling; you are at the bottom of a hole in the world, and the light that reaches you is thin and diffuse.

The walls are vertical, sheer, and astonishingly high—extreme elevation measured not from sea level, but from the floor of the basin to its distant, unseen rim. They are not rock, but built: enormous, monolithic pillars of concrete and steel, ribbed with conduits and latticework, their surfaces weathered by millennia of slow erosion. This is not a recent ruin; the erosion is established, a patient abrasion by wind and condensation that has smoothed the sharp edges into gentle curves, leaving the entire structure looking ancient, settled, in its own state of balance. Moss and algae, lush and green, cling to the damp vertical faces, forming vertical gardens in the sparse light. Strange, phosphorescent fungi pulse in the shadows, their cycles slow, shifting from blue to violet over days.

The scale is small in the sense of a contained, bounded area—a single, defined cavity, a room carved from the planet’s crust. Yet within this smallness, the emptiness is vast. The floor is almost entirely bare. Structures stand at great intervals: rusted gantries, silent smokestacks, the shells of warehouses, all spaced hundreds of meters apart, their functions long obsolete. The space between them is a near-void, a hollow silence broken only by the deafening sounds of the place. This is not the noise of life, but of mechanics. A low, subsonic hum vibrates through the metal floor, the ghost of a planetary engine. Water gushes in pipes that run along the walls, a constant roaring of hidden rivers. The air is filled with the clang of distant, automated hammers, the hiss of steam vents, and the groan of metal contracting and expanding with thermal cycles. It is a factory that never stops, but whose purpose is forgotten, operating on its own logic.

Ecology is sparse but present. Fauna is abundant in number but not in variety. Swarms of metallic beetles, their carapaces gleaming with condensation, scuttle across the floor, feeding on mineral deposits. Birds—perhaps birds, creatures with leathery wings and chirping, gear-like vocalizations—nest in the high gantries, their calls echoing in the vast space. In the damp shadows, colonies of blind, pale crustaceans cluster around the weeping pipes. The flora is more visible: lush mats of moss, vines that dangle from the ledges above, and strange, flowering plants that grow in the rusted joints of machinery, their roots seeking moisture in the metal.

The rules of this world are implied by its construction. Everything is made; there is no natural substrate. The floor is a plate, the walls are a shell, the water is piped, the air is circulated. The technology is industrial but archaic—steam, gears, electric arcs that flicker in the distance, all operating in a slow, patient cycle. The dynamic is slow; change happens over centuries. The condensation weeps, the metal rusts, the engines turn, but nothing happens quickly. The weirdness lies in the total integration of the made and the living. The environment is not a backdrop for nature; it is a system that has incorporated life as part of its function. The beetles clean the metal, the birds nest in the machinery, the fungi feed on the rust. The place is a single, coherent artifact, and you are a temporary anomaly within it.

As you walk, the floor plates shift slightly underfoot, groaning. A distant, rhythmic pounding marks the cycle of some hidden piston. The air is thick with the smell of ozone, wet metal, and organic decay. Light filters down in shafts, illuminating motes of dust and condensation. The walls rise, immense and silent, toward a sky you cannot see, and the space between structures feels both intimate and infinite. You are in a made hollow, a engineered depression, a place where the world has been carved out and replaced with itself, and it hums, and drips, and endures.
