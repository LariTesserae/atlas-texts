The wind does not howl here. It sings, a constant multi-tonal hum that vibrates through the rock itself. This is the Wailing Scarp, a place where the upper atmosphere is thin and bright, and the ground is a fractured, vertical world of impossible elevation.

**Terrain and Materials:**
The landscape is a sheer ascent, a series of immense, stacked mesas and plunging ravines that vanish into a misty, unseen base. The rock is not one solid mass but a dense, chaotic layering of mineral deposits: dark basalt, milky quartz veins, and strange, phosphorescent strata that glow faintly in the perpetual daylight. The world is built of stone and mineral, a complete absence of organic soil. The ground is a mosaic of shattered plates, polished by wind and the ceaseless seep of water, which is everywhere. Water weeps from every crevice, condenses on every surface, and pools in hollows that never fully drain, creating a landscape of perpetual dampness and mirrored pools that reflect the unbearably bright, cloudless sky.

**Atmosphere and Light:**
The light is a constant, direct, and piercing brilliance. There is no sun, no discernible source, only a diffuse, directional luminosity that casts no soft shadows. Every edge is razor-sharp, every surface gleams with wetness. The air is cool and heavy with the scent of wet stone and ozone, a clean, metallic smell that clings to the back of the throat. Condensation is a constant process; a fine mist hangs in the air, not from clouds, but from the very atmosphere itself coalescing on the cold rock faces.

**Soundscape:**
This is a loud place. The primary sound is the harmonic hum of the wind moving through countless narrow canyons and over sharp ridges, creating a dissonant, cathedral-like choir. Beneath that is the constant drip and trickle of water, a percussive rhythm against stone. Occasionally, a deep, groaning crack echoes through the escarpment as a mineral shelf shifts, or a cascade of pebbles spills down a slope, the sound sharp and immediate.

**Ecology and Life:**
There is no plant life, no animal life. The world is mineral, post-biological, or perhaps pre-life. What passes for ecology here is a slow, mineralogical dance. Crystalline structures grow, not by biological process, but by precipitation and deposition from the super-saturated water. These formations are the landscape's only "living" features: delicate, fractal gardens of gypsum and calcite that form in the damp hollows, glowing with stored light. They are fragile, and will shatter if touched. The only movement, besides the water and wind, is the slow creep of these mineral deposits, a process measured in centuries.

**The Built World:**
Scattered across the mesas and sheltered in overhangs are the remains of a primitive, vanished civilization. These are not buildings, but markers: single standing stones carved with geometric spirals, low walls of fitted rock that now serve only to channel water, and occasional platforms of flat stone that might have been used for observation or ritual. The tools are simple: hand-chipped flint and obsidian shards found in sheltered ledges, and crude metal objects—not iron, but a strange, resilient copper-arsenic alloy—that show no signs of corrosion. The people who made them are gone, leaving only these faint marks on a world that seems to have always been.

**Rules and Dynamics:**
The world operates on a different set of physics. The **weirdness** is foundational: space is slightly non-Euclidean here. Distances feel wrong; a short climb can take an hour, a straight path may curve back on itself. Gravity seems to shift minutely on certain slopes, and water sometimes flows upward for a moment before resuming its downward course. The bright light and constant motion are part of a cycle: the brightness seems to intensify with the condensation, and the wind's song shifts pitch with the pooling of water. It is an active, balancing system—a world in a permanent state of damp, luminous, resonant equilibrium. The dense layering of mineral formations, the scattered artifacts, and the sheer scale of the escarpment all contribute to a feeling of profound, ancient, and strangely beautiful isolation.
