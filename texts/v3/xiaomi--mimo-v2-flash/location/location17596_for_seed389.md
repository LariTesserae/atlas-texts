The place is called Sinter, and it is a city built on a plateau of sheer, unbending altitude, where the air is thin and the heat is a thick, still weight.

From a distance, it appears as a smudge against a bleached and endless sky, a geometry of rust and slate that climbs rather than spreads. There are no roads in the conventional sense, only a lattice of suspended walkways, gantries, and pneumatic tubes that stitch the structures together. The buildings are not clustered; they are fused, sharing walls, pipes, and foundations, rising in staggered tiers like a mineral deposit growing in slow motion. The scale is immense, but it is not sprawling—it is compressed, vertical, dense. The density is such that the spaces between structures are not voids but filled with a secondary architecture of conduit, scaffolding, and dripping heat exchangers.

The ground is not earth. It is a bed of black, vitreous slag, a pre-geologic material that has not yet been weathered into soil. It is hot to the touch, not from recent fire but from a deep, persistent geothermal seep. There is no vegetation, no lichen, no moss. The world is post-biological here, or perhaps pre-biological—a place where the usual cycles of growth and decay have never taken hold. Instead, there are mineral efflorescences: delicate, fractal crusts of copper salts and iron oxides that bloom on shaded surfaces, feeding on trace moisture and the slow exhalations of machinery.

Water is present but elusive. It is not a river or lake, but a system of capillary seepage. Condensation forms on the cooler underbellies of structures, trickling down into narrow runnels that are not channels but hairline fractures in the slag. The water is faintly alkaline, carrying dissolved metals, and it pools in shallow basins that are more like stains than ponds. Rain is rare but violent, a sudden, warm deluge that flashes off the hot surfaces and vanishes within minutes, leaving behind a shimmering haze and the smell of ozone.

The air is still, but not silent. The sound is a low, industrial hum—the thrum of generators, the hiss of steam valves, the distant, rhythmic clank of a forge that never sleeps. It is a sound that feels embedded in the stone itself, a vibration more than a noise. Occasionally, there is a drip, a crackle of static, the whisper of a vent releasing pressure. The silence is not empty; it is saturated with this mechanical breath.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. There are no animals, but there are constructs—automata that move with a slow, deliberate patience, their gears lubricated by the same mineral seepage that coats the walkways. They are not intelligent, not alive in any recognized sense, but they maintain. They sweep slag dust from the platforms, adjust the angle of solar collectors, and repair the occasional fracture in the piping. They do not speak, but they emit a faint, rhythmic clicking as they move, a sound that blends with the industrial hum.

The temperature is high, but not blistering. It is a dry, constant heat, the kind that radiates from the ground and the structures, making the air shimmer above the walkways. There is no shade in the traditional sense, but the dense layering of the city creates pockets of cooler air, where condensation collects and the metal does not burn.

The weirdness is subtle, emergent. It is in the way the mineral crusts seem to grow in patterns that echo the city’s layout, as if the geology is learning from the architecture. It is in the slow, almost imperceptible shift of the structures—not settling, but adjusting, as if the city is breathing. It is in the fact that the automata, over centuries, have begun to follow paths that are not quite logical, their movements tracing patterns that seem to align with the condensation runnels, as if they are participating in a water cycle that has no organic component.

The dynamic is slow. Seasons are not marked by temperature but by the intensity of the geothermal seep, which causes the slag to expand and contract in cycles measured in decades. The city does not grow in the way a forest grows; it accretes, like a crystal, adding layers of infrastructure and conduit that are, over time, absorbed into the whole. Change is patient, almost geological.

The light is normal, but it feels filtered, because the air is filled with a fine, metallic dust that catches the sun and turns the sky a hazy gold. At dawn and dusk, the dust glows, and the city is bathed in a soft, amber light that makes the rust and slate glow as if from within.

This is Sinter—a hot, high, dense, industrial world of mineral and metal, where water is a secret and life is a pattern, and the only thing that moves with urgency is the steam that sighs from a thousand valves, whispering to a sky that has never known a cloud.
