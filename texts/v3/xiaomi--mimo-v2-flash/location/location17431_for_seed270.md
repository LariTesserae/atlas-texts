At first glance, the place seems to be nothing—a floor of pale, heat-cracked salt, utterly flat and silent, stretching to a horizon that is at once immediate and infinite. The air is cool and still, carrying a mineral sharpness that catches in the back of the throat. It feels like a memory of a desert, a negative space where moisture and life have been chemically erased. But the silence is a trick of the ears, a failure to parse the density of what is here.

The ground is not salt, not exactly. It is a fine, granular substrate of desiccated silicate and metal dust, a dust that moves with a sluggish, granular fluidity underfoot, as if the entire landscape is a single, settled dune of metallic ash. This is the erosion’s work: the ancient, primordial grinding of a world that has been worn down to its final, stable state. There is no wind, but the dust shifts in slow, microscopic avalanches, a perpetual, near-motionless settling. The scale is intimate, claustrophobic; you can see the curvature of the ground in your immediate vicinity, a bowl of reality only a few hundred meters across, though the horizon suggests otherwise. This is a pocket, a sealed chamber in the crust of a dead planet, or perhaps a single, perfect sample extracted and placed under glass.

Scattered across this pan are the artifacts of industry. Not buildings, but their ghosts: the skeletal frames of conveyor belts, half-buried in the dust; the rusted ribs of immense vats, their interiors scoured clean; the upright, fan-like blades of a broken turbine, each one a meter wide, etched with intricate, meaningless patterns. They are not active. They are fossils of a previous cycle, a time when this place was a factory of some kind. The technology is industrial, but of a strange, organic design—the pipes curve like vertebrae, the control panels are covered in a lattice of non-standard, tactile buttons. It is a technology that has forgotten its own purpose.

And then, the fauna. It is not a thing you see with your eyes, not at first. It is a pressure, a presence in the granular dust itself. The overwhelming fauna is the dust. It is alive.

It is a colonial, particulate organism. Each grain, each mote of silicate and metal, is a microscopic entity—a spore, a cell, a single-minded unit. Together, they form a distributed intelligence, a slow-moving consciousness that occupies the entire volume of the pocket. They are not plants; they are not animals in any recognized sense. They are post-biological, a mineral ecology that has replaced the carbon cycle with a silicon-and-iron one. They do not eat; they transmute. They absorb the faint residual energy of the cool, normal light, and they slowly, over centuries, rearrange the atomic structure of the world.

This is the source of the sound. It is not a roar, but a billion tiny clicks, a granular static that is always present, a white noise of shifting dust. The ancient vats and machinery are not being buried; they are being *digested*. The fauna is slowly, inexorably breaking down the industrial relics, rearranging their atoms into new, crystalline structures that sprout from the ground like fungi. These are not plants—they are geometric lattices, fractal spirals of rust and quartz, growing at a pace that is just at the edge of human perception. A visitor who stays for a day will see a new, hair-like filigree of metal have grown on a railing. A visitor who stays for a month will find a conveyor belt has been transformed into a garden of silent, metallic flowers.

The rules of this world are simple. There is no water, no aqueous chemistry. The only solvent is time and the collective action of the particulate life. The temperature is cool, a stable, equilibrium state that is perfect for the slow, patient work of transmutation. The light is normal, a gift from a distant, pale sun that can barely penetrate the haze of the dust, casting the entire scene in a perpetual, soft twilight.

This is a sealed environment, a terrarium of deep time. The overwhelming fauna is the landscape itself. The built environment is a meal for it. The sound is its voice. And the dynamic is its slow, eternal, and utterly alien life cycle, playing out in a pocket of reality that has been forgotten by everything else.
