The place is a vast, low basin, so extensive that its horizon is a soft, heat-hazed blur where the dim sky meets the unmade land. The sky itself is not a sky in any familiar sense; it is a solid, bruised dome of violet-grey cloud that moves in slow, turbulent eddies, its underside streaked with veins of phosphorescent green that pulse like slow lightning. There is no sun, only a diffuse, perpetual twilight filtered through this churning ceiling, casting everything in shades of deep amethyst and tarnished silver.

The ground is not ground. It is a constructed substrate, a single, seamless material of fused basalt and glass, poured and set in an age before memory. It is hot to the touch, radiating a dry, penetrating heat that warps the air into shimmering columns. The surface is not smooth; it is a record of its own making, a landscape of emerging form. Great, raw fractures split the plain, not as erosion but as ongoing creation—new fissures yawn open with a sound like grinding teeth, and from them exude threads of molten silica that cool instantly into jagged, translucent spires. Other areas are subsiding, the ground sinking into itself in slow, silent whirlpools, leaving behind shallow basins that fill not with water, but with a viscous, opalescent condensate that drips from the air itself. This is the water, the 1.7/3 parameter: not a hydrological cycle, but a constant, weeping precipitation from the saturated atmosphere. The condensate gathers in these basins, forming placid, mirror-like pools that reflect the tortured sky, but the pools are ephemeral. They are drunk by the thirsty ground, absorbed back into the substrate, or they simply sublimate back into the air, leaving a crystalline residue that glitters like crushed diamond.

There is no vegetation, no biology as we recognize it. The world is a mineral kingdom. What life exists is of a simpler, more terrifying order. From the fractures, there occasionally extrude slow-growing filaments of a non-carbon crystal, a lattice of pure silicon that hums with a faint, internal light. These are not plants; they are geological processes that mimic growth, a form of mineral ecology that feeds on thermal gradients and electromagnetic whispers in the air. They are sparse, scattered like lonely sentinels across the vastness, and between them is only the raw, made stone, the glinting condensate, and the oppressive, silent space.

The soundscape is one of profound quiet, punctuated by the noises of creation. The dominant sound is a low, pervasive hum, the vibration of the world's own structure settling and rearranging. Then, the crack of a new fissure, the soft hiss of condensing atmosphere, the plop of a droplet hitting a pool. Sometimes, the air itself will ripple with a sound like distant thunder, not from the sky, but from somewhere deep in the substrate, a tectonic groan of the world reconfiguring itself. There are no animal calls, no wind in leaves, only the sounds of a place building and unbuilding itself.

The rules of this world are ones of chaotic equilibrium. The turbulence is not a storm to be endured, but the fundamental state of being. Everything is in flux, yet the flux itself is stable. The constructed nature of the place is total; there is no "natural" substrate to erode. The erosion that exists is the erosion of *idea*, the slow dissolution of the world's own blueprint. The primitive technology is evident in the scars of its making: the places where the substrate was poured, the crude, hand-hewn look of some of the newer spires, the fact that the entire landscape seems to be the result of a single, immense, and very old act of fabrication, executed with tools that were more about imposition than finesse.

The scale is continental, but the weirdness makes it feel planetary, even cosmic. The reality breakdown is not a sudden cataclysm, but a persistent, low-grade unraveling. The laws of perspective seem to bend at the edges of vision; a spire that looks a mile away might be a foot tall, and the horizon sometimes appears to curve upward, as if the basin is the inside of a colossal, shallow bowl. The atmosphere is thick with not just moisture, but with glitches—flickers of impossible color, patches of air that seem to be made of static, the occasional visual echo of a spire that no longer exists.

This is a place of immense, sparse emptiness, where the presences are the events themselves: the opening of a crack, the condensation of a pool, the slow, humming growth of a crystal filament. It is a world in the moment of its own becoming, a vast, hot, constructed basin where reality is not a fixed stage, but a participant in its own turbulent, silent, and endlessly creative dance.
