The air is cool and carries a dampness that feels less like weather and more like an exhalation of the ground itself. It is a high place—somewhere on the broad, worn shoulders of a mountain range so ancient it has lost its peaks, smoothed by eons into a vast, rolling plateau. The sky is a normal, pale light, filtered through a perpetual haze of fine mist that catches in the throat and glitters on every surface.

What defines this place is the vegetation. It is not a forest, nor a meadow, but a total plant ecosystem. Every surface is living mass. The ground is a dense, spongy mat of mosses, liverworts, and a thousand tiny, nameless plants, so thick that it swallows footfalls and breathes with a soft, cool pressure underfoot. From this substrate rise the structures: great, gnarled trunks of what might be trees, but they are not wood. They are woven, fibrous pillars of bark and lichen, their interiors hollowed by slow decay and repopulated by fungi. Their branches are not rigid but sinuous, laden with broad, waxy leaves that channel condensation into narrow runnels dripping to the ground. Between them are not clearings but denser thickets of cane, of flowering vines that pulse with slow, bioluminescent rhythms, of fungal shelves that glow with a soft, blue light. The plant does not simply fill the space; it *is* the architecture, the walls, the roads, and the weather. It grows in the cracks of abandoned concrete and coils around the skeletal remains of machinery.

And there is machinery. Scattered across this green expanse are the signs of a settlement-scale civilization—towns, perhaps even a city, now mostly reclaimed. The built environment is industrial in scale: low, sprawling factory complexes with corrugated metal roofs now draped in flowering vines, smokestacks that stand silent, their apertures clogged with nests. Railway lines cut through the vegetation, their tracks warped and buckled, the spaces between ties a riot of fern and moss. Pipes and conduits, some still humming with a low vibration, snake through the landscape, their surfaces a tapestry of lichen and algae. The infrastructure is layered, showing ages of construction and decay, but it is all being actively consumed. The industry is not dead; it is being digested.

The sound is loud. It is not the clamor of a living city, but a constant, layered hum. There is the drip of water from a million leaves, the creak and groan of plant-fiber structures settling, the low-frequency thrum of geothermal vents hidden beneath the moss, the buzz of insects that are the only fauna one sees with any regularity—great, iridescent beetles and slow-flying moths. Occasionally, a deeper sound shudders through the ground: the collapse of a long-rotted support beam, or the grinding of tectonic plates far below this worn plateau. The air is thick with the smell of wet earth, decaying leaves, and the sharp, metallic tang of ozone.

The ecology is sparse. There are no herds, no flocks. The animals are few and secretive. A glimpse of a pale, six-legged creature scuttling under a leaf. A distant, haunting cry that might be a bird or the wind in a hollow pipe. The scarcity of fauna makes the plant-life seem even more dominant, more conscious. It is a world where the animals are mere passengers, and the plants are the true citizens, builders, and historians.

This is a place where reality is breaking down. It is not a violent tear, but a gentle, pervasive unraveling. The laws of physics seem to bend to the will of the vegetation. Light filters through leaves that should not be able to grow at this altitude, and shadows fall in impossible geometries. Sometimes, looking down a long aisle of fungus-laden trunks, the perspective stretches and compresses, and you see a memory of the place as it was—a street, a plaza—superimposed on the green present. The built environment is not just being reclaimed; it is being reinterpreted. A factory wall, now a canvas for a vast, slow-growing moss, occasionally flickers with the ghost of an old advertisement. The air shimmers at the edges, and the sound sometimes carries echoes of voices or machinery that have no source.

The dynamic is active. The plant-world is in constant motion, not in a rapid, visible way, but in a slow, relentless growth and decay. Vines tighten their grip, new shoots push through cracks, and the whole landscape seems to breathe. The dampness is not constant; it cycles, with periods of heavier mist followed by brief, dry stillness. The ground itself feels alive, shifting subtly, resettling. It is a world in a final, settled form—ancient, eroded to its bones—but it is also a world in perpetual, quiet transformation. It is a monument to an end that refuses to be still.
