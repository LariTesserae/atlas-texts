The Climb of Venkarr rises from a base of silent, table-like mesas into a needle-scarred sky. This is a place of extreme elevation, where the air is thin, cool, and perpetually damp, though rain falls only in ragged, intermittent sheets that condense instantly upon the dark, volcanic stone. The moisture here is not a river but a seep, a mineral sweat: it drips from overhangs, gathers in shallow, mirror-still pools on wide ledges, and slicks the faces of the cliffs with a constant, cold film. There is no true ground, only a vertical geography of terraces, spires, and wind-scoured platforms, each level connected by a lattice of bridges, gantries, and spiral stairways that seem both ancient and freshly welded.

The city—Venkarr itself—is not built upon the land but woven into it, a dense, industrial tapestry clinging to the sheer rock. Factories burrow into the mountain, their exhaust vents breathing steam into the cool air. Foundries glow with a dull, internal fire, their chimneys stacked vertically along the cliffs, their smoke mingling with the low-hanging mist. The infrastructure is layered and chaotic: rusted iron walkways bolted to stone, cable cars grinding along tracks that switchback up impossible grades, power lines strung like spider-silk between peaks, humming with a low, industrial drone. The architecture is a patchwork of soot-stained brick, corrugated metal, and raw, unworked basalt, all connected by a web of conduits, pipes, and pneumatic tubes that rattle with the movement of goods and messages.

The scale of Venkarr is open, but its openness is vertical. From a high terrace, one can look down into the chasm below—where the base mesas are lost in a perpetual dimness, a twilight zone of shadowed stone and sparse, hardy lichen—and up toward the higher spires, where the air grows so thin that the sky is a deep, bruised violet even at midday. The light is always dim, filtered through the perpetual mist and the layered smoke of industry, casting the entire city in a monochrome palette of slate, iron, and wet ash. The sun is a forgotten concept; illumination comes from the glow of foundries, the flicker of arc lamps strung along walkways, and the rare, pale shaft of light that pierces the cloud deck.

Despite the verticality and the industrial density, the place feels sparse. The city does not crowd; it distributes itself across the cliff face in clusters and isolated outposts, with vast empty spans of raw rock between. The silence between the industrial sounds is profound, broken only by the drip of water, the creak of metal, and the low, constant wind that whistles through the spires.

The ecology is abundant but strange. Fauna here has adapted to the vertical world and the peculiar conditions: great, silent moths with wings like wet slate flit between vents; six-legged goats with hooked claws cling to sheer faces, grazing on mineral-rich moss; thin, translucent fish swim in the condensation pools, their bodies shimmering with internal light. And then there are the weirder creatures—things that seem to phase in and out of visibility, their forms sketched in temporary static, their sounds not quite heard but felt in the bones. The weirdness of Venkarr is not a single event but a constant, low-grade reality breakdown. The stone itself sometimes hums with a subsonic frequency, and at the edges of vision, the geometry of the cliffs seems to fold and unfold, as if the mountain is still under construction, its final form not yet decided. Mirages of impossible structures—towers that aren't there, bridges that lead nowhere—flicker in the mist. The industrial machinery seems to sometimes operate without power, its pistons moving in a slow, dreamlike rhythm, and the sounds of the city echo slightly out of sync, creating a disorienting lag between action and noise.

The world’s rules are still being written. Erosion here is not a process of wearing down but of coming-into-being: new rock formations bud from the cliff faces like slow-growing crystals, and fresh fissures open with a grinding sound, revealing raw, unweathered stone within. The city’s engineers incorporate these raw geologies into their designs, building supports around new spires and rerouting walkways as the mountain reshapes itself. This is a place of patient processes; change is measured in decades, not days. The dynamic is slow, but the industry is loud: the clamor of hammers, the roar of furnaces, the hiss of steam, the clang of metal on metal—all of it reverberates through the stone, layered and persistent, a soundtrack to a world that is both permanent and perpetually unfinished.

In Venkarr, one learns to move with the vertical, to accept the dimness, to listen to the hum of the mountain and the machinery that has become part of its body. It is a city of extreme elevations and extreme contradictions: abundant life in a thin, cool air, industry in a place of raw geology, sound in a landscape of vast silence, and a reality that is always just a little bit broken, a little bit unfinished, a little bit more than what it seems.
