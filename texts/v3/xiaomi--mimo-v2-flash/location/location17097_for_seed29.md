Here is the place:

It is the throat of the world.

You are standing on a high plateau of heat and packed dust, but the scale is wrong. The world is a room, a cavern, a throat. The ceiling is not sky but a low, vaulted ceiling of hot, vibrating rock, pressing down with a weight that feels less geological and more like the diaphragm of a sleeping giant. The air is thick, not with moisture—there is none—but with pressure and sound. It is a density of presence, a saturation of heat and vibration.

The floor is not soil. It is a mosaic of interlocking mineral plates, a tessellation of dark, glassy basalt and shimmering, heat-warped salts that crunch underfoot like broken ceramics. There is no vegetation, not a single spore of fungus or blade of mineral. This is a world before life, or after it, a purely geological chamber where chemistry happens in the dark. The air smells of hot metal and ozone, a sharp, clean scent of industrial processing, but there are no machines you can see.

The light is a memory. It is 0.5/3, a darkness that is not absolute but is a deep, smothering twilight. The only illumination comes from two sources: a faint, pulsing bioluminescence that seems to emanate from the very rock itself—a slow, geological glow—and the intermittent flashes of electrical arcs that jump between towering, natural spires of conductive ore. These spires, packed together with no room to spare, form a dense forest of raw, industrial geometry. They are the world's engines, its nervous system. They hum with a low, resonant frequency that vibrates through the soles of your boots and into your bones.

The sound is a constant, loud roar. It is not a wind, for there is no air movement. It is the sound of the planet's inner machinery: the grinding of tectonic plates far below, the hiss of superheated gases venting through minute fissures in the rock, the crackle of electrical discharge, and a deep, sub-audible thrum that feels like the world's own heartbeat. It is industrial, but natural—a factory that built itself.

Fauna is abundant, but it is not life as we know it. It is fauna of the rock and the current. Shards of magnetic stone, shaped like swift, flat fish, school in the dense air currents that flow between the spires, their movements guided by the planet's powerful magnetic field. Glassy, crystalline arthropods scuttle across the mineral plates, their limbs clicking with a sound like falling gravel. They are drawn to the heat and the electrical fields, feeding on the energy that crackles in the air. They are the planet's living components, its organic gears in a machine of rock and fire.

The erosion is emerging, but it is slow, a patient process. The edges of the mineral plates are smooth, as if worn down over millennia by a gentle, constant pressure. New formations are rising, however—tiny, hair-like crystals growing from fissures, a slow, upward creep of the planet's crust. It is a landscape in a state of constant, gradual becoming.

There are no structures, no artifacts of any made thing. The industrial quality is inherent, a property of the geology itself. The planet is an engine, and its inhabitants are its mechanics, its fuel, its moving parts. The dynamic is slow; the changes here happen across epochs. The electrical storms that flash between the spires may last for centuries, the slow grind of the plates for eons.

You are in a pocket-scale world, a single, immense chamber. The ceiling is low enough to touch if you raise your arms, yet the space feels infinite because it is so densely packed. There is no horizon, only the next spire, the next rock formation, the next layer of packed, humming presence.

It is a world of heat, pressure, and sound, a sealed environment where chemistry and geology have replaced biology, and where the industrial is not a made thing but a fundamental law of the place. It is loud, hot, and packed, a throat singing its own deep, eternal song.
