The place is a basin of low relief, a shallow bowl carved not from rock but from a dense accumulation of sediment and mineral precipitate. It is a landscape of water and stone, where the water does not run but clings.

The ground is a mosaic of dampness. At the edges, where the basin slopes gently inward, the soil is dark and wet, a sponge of peat-like mineral fiber that yields underfoot with a soft sucking sound. Further in, the surface hardens into a crust of crystalline salt and silica, a white-grey rind that fractures under pressure to reveal a damp, gritty substrate beneath. The water table is not deep; it is the surface itself. In the low-lying flats, a thin film of water sheathes the ground, reflecting the dim sky like a mirror of mercury. In other places, the water has pooled into shallow lagoons, their surfaces placid and scummed with a fine, iridescent dust of micro-crystals.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen. The world is mineral, but it is not inert. The "vegetation" here is a slow, geological bloom. Stalactites of pure, clear calcite weep from the ceilings of low, overhanging shelves of rock, each drop leaving behind a minuscule ring of deposit. On the flats, the ground is not flat but dimpled with thousands of tiny, perfect cones of accreted mineral, each one a record of a single, slow-growing drip from the condensing atmosphere. They form a dense, field-like covering, a landscape of stone corals. They are cool to the touch, holding the ambient temperature of 1.4/3, a persistent, deep coolness that seeps into bones.

The structures are few and mysterious. Scattered across the basin are tall, slender spires of a dark, glassy material, like obsidian or volcanic glass, but perfectly smooth and seamlessly joined. They are not buildings in any human sense; they have no doors, no windows. They are monolithic, perhaps thirty feet high, and their surfaces are etched with patterns that seem to shift when not looked at directly. One is vertical and sharp; another is gently leaning, its tip brushing the surface of a lagoon. A third is fractured, its top half lying in a thousand razor-edged shards around its base, as if it had been struck by a silent, immense force. They are the only things here that are truly sharp, their edges cutting against the dim light.

The atmosphere is heavy with sound. It is not the sound of life—there are no birds, no insects, no rustling leaves—but of process. The basin is a vast, resonant chamber. Water drips with a slow, metronomic rhythm from the mineral concretions and from the weeping rock faces, each drop a clear, bell-like note that merges into a constant, shimmering curtain of sound. This is punctuated by the deep, subsonic groan of the ground itself as it settles, a sound felt more in the chest than heard with the ears. There is a constant, soft hiss, the sound of the cool air condensing on every surface, a million tiny collisions. From the dark, glassy spires, a faint, harmonic hum emits, a frequency so low it is a pressure in the air. The sound is loud, not in a chaotic way, but in a layered, overwhelming way. It is the sound of a world in a state of perpetual, slow formation.

Ecology, in the biological sense, is absent. But there is a different kind of system. The fauna is rare, and it is not made of flesh. In the deepest parts of the lagoons, where the water is a foot deep and perfectly still, shapes move. They are not fish. They are complex, shifting geometries of light and shadow, like schools of silhouettes that have detached from the rock and learned to swim. They are slow, patient, and they react only to the sound of drips, moving toward the source of a new rhythm. They are the only things here that have a will, however faint.

The rules of this world are clear in their strangeness. Time is measured in accretion, not decay. Things are built from the bottom up, drop by drop, layer by layer. The sharp spires are the exception, and their presence suggests a different rule: that something here can also be subtracted, can be cut away. The world is young in its formation, the features fresh and sharp, yet the processes are ancient and slow. It is a landscape of patient saturation, of dense, layered presences that are all mineral, all water, all sound. The light is dim, not from night, but from a permanent, heavy cloud cover that filters the sun to a soft, grey luminescence. It is enough to see the sheen on the water, the glint on the crystal cones, and the dark, absolute black of the glass spires. It is a world that feels like it is still being born, but in a way that is utterly alien—a birth not of life, but of pure, patient, and loud substance.
