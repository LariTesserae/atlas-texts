The place is a basin, but one of such immense age that the word "basin" feels misleading. There is no rim or edge, only the fact that the ground is low, a shallow bowl smoothed to near-perfect flatness by time so great it has become its own kind of absence. The floor is not soil but a single, continent-scale sheet of lichenous crust, moss-and-fungus mat interwoven with hair-fine rootlets, all compressed into a resilient, slightly springy carpet that sags only a few meters across kilometers. It smells of damp stone and cold chlorophyll, a scent that has nowhere to dissipate.

The air is cool, a constant twelve degrees, and humid enough that every surface beads with a fine mist that does not drip but simply exists, a liquid sheen held in suspension. The water here is not a river but a permeation. The ground itself is the water table, spongy and alive, and in places the crust breaks into shallow, clear pools no deeper than a boot-sole, each reflecting a rectangle of pearl-gray sky. The sky is always overcast, a permanent ceiling of stratiform clouds that diffuses the light into an even, shadowless illumination—bright enough to see, soft enough to have no time of day.

The vegetation is total. There is no bare ground, no rock outcrop, no dust. The plant-mass is architecture: great, slow-growing lattices of what might be called trees, if trees grew in columns of porous, cork-like wood and canopied in fractal fans of leathery leaves the size of bedsheets. But there are no distinct species, only a continuum of form, from the towering spires (seldom higher than two stories, this being a low place) to the creeping mats underfoot. Vines hang in dense curtains, not of individual strands but of braided, living ropes that sway in the faintest air movement. Moss grows in bulbous, erose towers, its spores drifting in the humid air like a quiet snowfall of green dust. The ecology is a single, interdependent organism: the trees feed on the mist, the mat retains the moisture, the fungi break down the inevitable deadfall, and all of it grows so slowly that change is not noticed in a human lifetime. It is a closed system, an orchard of stone and leaf that has never known a predator or a plow.

And yet, the sound is loud.

It is not the sound of waterfalls or storms, but a mechanical hum that seems to emanate from the air itself. This is the industrial element: a distant, omnipresent drone of engines and generators. There are no factories visible, no smokestacks, no tracks. The technology here is not a surface presence but a buried one, or perhaps a distributed one, as if the machinery is woven into the root-mats or hums in the bedrock kilometers below. The sound is low-frequency, a vibration felt in the chest as much as heard, the promise of immense power doing work of which no evidence is ever seen. It is the sound of a world running, of a cycle that requires no caretaker.

Fauna is abundant but small. No large mammals or birds, only insects, amphibians, and a profusion of tiny, blind crustaceans that skitter across the pools and through the soil-mat. Their movements are the only dynamic element in a landscape of frozen equilibrium. They do not grow old; they are born, they scavenge, they die, and their bodies are immediately reabsorbed into the plant-mat. The ecology is perfectly efficient, perfectly closed. There is no waste.

The scale is intimate. One can walk from one end of the visible basin to the other in a day, though the boundaries are soft, curving away into mist. It feels like a room, a cathedral of moss and hum, where the pillars are living wood and the hymn is the engine drone.

Time does not pass here; it is spent. The erosion is ancient, not as erosion of rock but as the wearing away of novelty. Nothing changes shape. The spires have stood for millennia, the pools have been shallow for epochs. The world has settled into its final, perfect form. It is a place at the end of a cycle, a quiet, cool, damp, green, humming, endless room where the machinery of the world ticks on in the dark, and the only law is to persist.
