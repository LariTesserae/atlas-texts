The place you enter is a grotto of impossible scale and intimate enclosure. At first glance, it is a city, but one that has forgotten the concept of ceiling and sky. It exists within a hollow geode the size of a small continent, its walls a cyclopean, translucent crystal that glows with a dull, internal luminescence. This is the only light, a perpetual twilight of bruised violet and bruised amber, trapped within the stone and filtered through the dusty, stagnant air. The temperature is a dry, baking heat, a heat that has nothing to do with a sun and everything to do with deep geothermal breath and the slow, chemical exhalation of the crystal itself.

There is no water here. Not a drop. The "rivers" that snake between the low, sprawling structures are beds of polished, dry obsidian, their surfaces scarred with the faint, rippled patterns of some long-vanished, non-aqueous flow. The air is thick with a fine, silicate dust that hangs motionless, making every distant sight a soft-focus dream. The silence is profound, but it is not empty; it is *loud*. The sound is a low, resonant hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet, a chord struck by the planet's own slow heartbeat. It is the sound of a million tons of crystal settling, of thermal vents sighing, of the city itself breathing in its sleep.

And the city is alive, but not with animals. It is a city of flora. Lush, aggressive, and utterly silent, the vegetation has claimed every surface. Vines thick as a man's arm, with waxy, black leaves that drink the dim light, coil around the carved facades of buildings. From the dry riverbeds, great columns of fibrous, porous fungus rise like buttresses, their caps spread wide to catch the mineral dust that settles from the air. There are no flowers, only vast, fleshy pads of moss that glow with a faint, sickly bioluminescence in the deep violet shadows, their light the only source of color in this monochrome world. The ecology is a closed loop of decay and regrowth, powered by geothermal heat and the slow breakdown of the crystal itself. There is no animal life—no insect, no bird, no scavenger—so the plants perform all roles. They are the architects, the builders, the scavengers, and the gardeners.

The structures of the city are low, sprawling, and built from the same materials as the landscape: stone carved from the geode's inner walls, hardened fungal matter, and polished obsidian from the dry riverbeds. The technology is primitive—hand-tools of sharpened crystal and bone, woven baskets of fibrous root, simple levers and pulleys—but the engineering is sophisticated. The buildings are not stacked; they are *grown*. Walls curve organically, doorways are perfect arches that seem to have been eroded rather than built, and stairways spiral up the inner faces of the crystal walls, their steps worn smooth by a million silent footfalls. The city is vast, but empty. You can walk for an hour between structures, your footsteps echoing in the cavernous space, and see no one, no movement, save for the slow, deliberate sway of a fungal tower as it adjusts its posture to the subtle shifts in geothermal pressure.

The erosion here is not the work of wind or water, but of time and chemistry. The edges of the obsidian rivers are rounded and glassy, as if polished by an eternal, dry river. The crystal walls are pitted and etched with patterns that look like frozen lightning, the result of millennia of acidic mineral deposition. The city itself seems to have settled into a state of equilibrium; it is not growing, not decaying, but simply *persisting*. It is a place that has reached its final form.

The weirdness is a quiet, pervasive thing. It is in the way the light seems to pool in certain areas, creating pools of deeper shadow that defy the geometry of the cavern. It is in the fact that the hum of the earth can sometimes be heard to resolve into a pattern, a rhythm, before dissolving back into noise. It is in the knowledge that every plant here is both individual and part of a single, sprawling organism that owns this hollow world. The dynamic is active but slow; the fungal towers grow by a hand's-width each year, the obsidian rivers shift their courses in inches over a lifetime, and the great crystal walls continue their imperceptible, eternal settling.

This is a place of profound solitude, of deep time, and of a life that has no need for eyes or ears or voices. It is a garden grown in the belly of a stone, lit by its own internal fire, and it is utterly, completely, and loudly alone.
