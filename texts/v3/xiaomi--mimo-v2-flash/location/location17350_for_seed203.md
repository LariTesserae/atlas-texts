The place is called the Hollow Range. It is a vast, subterranean cavern system on a planetary scale, so immense that it generates its own weather. The air is perpetually cold, the rock walls sweating with a damp chill, and the only light comes from the bright, searing glare of a central sun-stone—a colossal, floating crystal of captured daylight that drifts slowly through the highest vaults of the cavern, casting sharp, bleached shadows that stretch for miles.

The floor of the cavern is not rock, but a living, breathing mat of vegetation. This is not a forest, but a single, sprawling organism: a mycelial-plant matrix that carpets every surface, its root-hairs delving into fissures and its broad, photosynthetic fronds turning the cavern’s air into oxygen. The plants are architecture. In the dimmer zones, away from the direct glare of the sun-stone, they have grown into hollow, cathedral-like structures—skeletal arches of fibrous cellulose, suspended ceilings of leaf-lattice that drip with condensed moisture. The dampness is constant but uneven; in some zones, water beads on the fronds and falls in slow, silent drips into shallow pools that ring the base of the plant-pillars. In others, the air is so saturated it feels like walking through cold breath.

The sound is mostly quiet. The only regular noise is the faint, hollow whistle of wind moving through the plant-channels, and the occasional deep, tectonic groan as the cavern shifts—a young system, still settling. But the quiet is deceptive. The dynamic is turbulent. The sun-stone’s path is not stable; it is pulled by magnetic fields and by the massive, slow currents of the air itself. As it moves, it causes explosive growth and die-back in the plant-mat. In its wake, the vegetation withers into brittle, ash-like fibers, creating vast clearings of near-emptiness—empty spaces where the plant-mat has retreated, leaving the stone floor exposed and sharp with fresh, unweathered edges. Before it, the plants surge upward in chaotic, spiral towers that reach toward the light, only to collapse under their own weight within days. The ecosystem is in a constant state of creation and decay.

The scale of emptiness is profound. Between the towers of plant-architecture and the clearings, there are kilometers of open space—vast, hollow air where the only presences are dust motes caught in the sun-stone’s beams and the occasional, rare fauna. The fauna are simple: blind, translucent insects that scuttle along the plant-roots, and a single species of leathery-winged glider that nests in the highest vaults, descending only to sip from the dripping condensation pools. They are silent, and their populations are sparse, limited by the unstable food sources.

Scattered artifacts hint at a primitive intelligence. On the walls, faint carvings—simple geometric patterns—have been etched into the plant-flesh itself, not by tools of metal, but by sharpened stone. Small, abandoned structures of woven plant-fiber and stacked stone dot the clearings: temporary shelters, now collapsing, their builders having moved on or vanished. The technology is pre-metal; they used what the cavern gave them—stone, fiber, and the light of the sun-stone for warmth and direction.

The atmosphere is cold, bright, and hollow. The air tastes of damp stone and chlorophyll. You can see for miles in any direction, but what you see is the same: plant, emptiness, light, and the distant, grinding motion of the cavern itself. The world feels both ancient and newborn—a place where the land is still forming, where erosion is sharp and recent, and where life has written itself over the stone in a language of living architecture. It is a world of vast, silent spaces, lit by a moving sun, and haunted by the quiet drip of water and the slow, turbulent breath of the earth.
