At first, you might think the world was carved from bone, but it is too dark for that. It is a terrain of basalt and salt-scarred obsidian, stretched across a scale that breaks the eye. The horizon is not a line but a permanent condition—a haze of distance where the heat bends the air into slow, shifting domes. The elevation is extreme; you stand on a plain that is already the roof of the world, and from here, the ground falls away in terraces and sheer cliffs into canyons so deep they swallow their own echoes. There are no rivers, no lakes, no moisture of any kind. The air is dry, mineral, tasting of ozone and static. It is warm, but the warmth is not a blanket; it is a pressure, a weight that settles in the joints and makes the skin prickle.

The city—if it can be called that—is not a thing of streets and squares. It is a lattice of industry set upon the plateau, a slow, patient architecture of rust and oxidized copper. The buildings are not houses; they are refineries, processors, and storage silos, all interconnected by elevated tracks and pressure pipes. There is no green anywhere, no moss, no fungal bloom. The only biology here is fauna, and it is abundant, but strange. It is not animal life as we know it. It is a mineral-based ecology: creatures of crystalline growth and slow, tectonic movement, beings that feed on thermal gradients and static charge. They scrape the rock with silicate mandibles, they nest in the hollows of cooling slag, and their calls are not sounds but vibrations that travel through the stone.

The light is normal, a pale gold that bleaches the edges of things, but it is filtered through a permanent haze of industrial particulate. The sky is a sheet of milky white, unbroken by clouds, because there is no water to form them. The sun is a smudge, a diffuse source of heat without direction.

The sound is mostly quiet. The wind is a low hum, a pressure sighing through the canyons. The industry is not loud; it is a deep, resonant thrum, the vibration of engines and the movement of immense gears, all slowed to the pace of geology. You can hear the fauna, but only as a distant, rhythmic clicking, the sound of crystals growing and fracturing in the heat.

The erosion is established. The landscape is not new. It has been weathered for eons into a state of balance. The cliffs are smooth, the canyons are rounded, the plateaus are flat as if they have been planed by a colossal hand. The city is built upon this oldness, its foundations sunk into the rock, its structures shaped by the same forces that carve the canyons.

The dynamic is slow. Nothing happens quickly. The heat cycles between day and night, but the change is gradual, taking hours to shift a few degrees. The fauna moves with the patience of geology, migrating across the plateau in response to thermal plumes that rise from deep fractures in the earth. The city operates on cycles that last seasons, its machines processing raw ore into refined material in rhythms that match the slow sweep of the sun.

The weirdness is in the details. The city is not built for people. It is built for the fauna, or by them, or in partnership with them. The industrial structures are not just metal and stone; they are grown, seeded into the rock and coaxed into shape by the creatures’ slow, deliberate work. The pipes are not just conduits; they are the circulatory system of a living landscape, carrying thermal energy and mineral slurry from one part of the plateau to another. The fauna are not separate from the city; they are the city, and the city is the fauna, and the landscape is the body of a slow, dreaming intelligence that has no name and no need for one.

The air is thin, but breathable. The gravity is normal. The world is sealed, but not by design. It is sealed by nature, by the absence of water and the presence of a thousand mineral cycles that keep everything in a state of dry equilibrium. The salts are everywhere, crusting the rocks, glittering in the light, sharp as glass underfoot. The dust is fine, a powder of silica and metal that coats everything in a thin, uniform layer.

The space between things is vast. The city is scattered, its structures set far apart, connected by threads of track and pipe that span kilometers of empty plain. The fauna, too, is scattered, moving in loose herds across the plateau, their movements dictated by the slow pulse of the earth.

This is a world of industry without pollution, of life without water, of city without people. It is a place where the lines between natural and artificial have dissolved into a slow, mineral dream.
