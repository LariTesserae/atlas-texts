The high plateau of **Karah Vesh** does not so much rise from the world as sit upon it, a vast, flat-topped island of stone adrift in an atmosphere so saturated it feels like breathing under water. The altitude is high enough that the air is thin, but the warmth is constant and close, a humid, mineral heat that clings to the skin and does not dissipate. There is no sun, only a diffuse, even light that seems to emanate from the cloud-ceiling itself—a permanent, overcast sky of pearlescent grey, lit from within by slow, electrical discharges that crawl across its underbelly.

The ground is not soil. It is a dense, interlocking mosaic of stone, slick with a perpetual film of moisture that weeps from every surface. The rocks are not rough; they are polished smooth by aeons of condensation and micro-erosion, their faces faceted like dark, wet gems. Water is everywhere, but not as rivers or lakes. It is a near-surface presence, held in capillary networks within the stone, emerging as slow seeps, as a constant dew, as a fine mist that rises from fissures and hangs in the air. The atmosphere itself is the primary reservoir—a vast, warm ocean of vapor that presses down, condensing on every cool surface, pooling in the minute depressions of the landscape until it is re-absorbed into the stone.

There is no vegetation. Not a single spore of moss, not a blade of crystalline grass. The world is a post-biotic mineralogy. The ecology is one of stone, water, and energy. The dense, layered urban settlement is not built *on* the plateau; it is *of* it. The city, also called Karah Vesh, is carved directly from the bedrock. Towers are not erected; they are excised, leaving sheer, weeping walls. Streets are not paved; they are polished grooves in the stone, designed to channel the constant seepage into subterranean cisterns. The infrastructure is industrial in scale—great, humming factories of stone and metal that process the mineral wealth of the plateau, their engines powered by geothermal vents and the slow, relentless pressure of the condensed atmosphere. Conduits of copper and basalt carry not electricity, but pressurized vapor and heated water, driving pistons and turning wheels in a low, constant rhythm.

The fauna is overwhelming, but it is not biological. It is a mineralogical menagerie. **Slag-wraiths** drift through the air, entities of condensed vapor and particulate metal, their forms shifting like smoke but their cores solidifying into temporary, sharp-edged shapes. **Basalt leviathans** move with glacial slowness across the plateau, their bodies composed of interlocking stone plates that grind with a sound like distant thunder; they are not alive, but they are presences, following paths of thermal gradient and mineral density. **Chalk-moths**—clusters of fine, white dust that swarm in response to electrical discharges—fill the air in dense, pulsing clouds. The city's inhabitants, whatever they are, have adapted to this. They are likely constructs themselves, or beings of stone and water, their movements deliberate, their communication a series of resonant taps and low-frequency hums that travel through the rock.

The sound is loud, but it is a layered, patient loudness. The constant, soft hiss of condensation. The deep, resonant grind of the leviathans. The industrial thrum of the factories, a bass note that vibrates through the stone. The crackle of atmospheric discharge. The echo of a tap, traveling for seconds through the dense, wet rock. It is not chaotic; it is a symphony of established processes.

Erosion is not a destructive force here; it is a formative one. The landscape is in balance, settled into its own state over millennia. Every surface is weathered into its optimal form for water retention and thermal regulation. The city is not decaying; it is *maturing*, its structures slowly re-polishing themselves, its channels slowly deepening. The dynamic is slow. Change happens across seasons that last centuries. A new factory vent might take a thousand years to fully integrate into the stone. A leviathan's path might shift by a few meters per century.

The weirdness is subtle, woven into the fabric of the place. It is in the fact that the water does not evaporate, but is perpetually recycled through the stone and air. It is in the fauna, which is not alive by any conventional definition, but is undeniably present and responsive. It is in the light, which has no source. It is in the sound, which seems to have weight and texture. The world operates on principles that are familiar in their parts—condensation, erosion, industry—but assembled into a whole that is alien, patient, and deeply, wetly alive in its own mineral way.
