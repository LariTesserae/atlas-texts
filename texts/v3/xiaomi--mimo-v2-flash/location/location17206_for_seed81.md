The high plateau is a basin of heat and silence, a place where the sky presses down with the weight of a hot lid and the ground radiates a dry, mineral heat that shimmers above the surface. This is a city built upon a geothermal skeleton, a metropolis of hollow spars and vast, echoing chambers, all carved from the black and rust-red rock of the highlands. The air is thick with the hiss of steam and the smell of wet stone and ozone, a condensing atmosphere that weeps from the sheer faces of the canyon walls and pools in shallow, saline sheets that never quite evaporate.

There are no plants here. No moss, no grass, no lichen. The ground is bare rock, scoured by aeons of wind and thermal stress, and in its place are crystalline growths—geodes that sprout from the rock like candelabras, their interiors glowing with a faint, phosphorescent light. They are not alive, not in any biological sense, but they are built, constructed by the slow, deliberate chemistry of the plateau’s high heat and mineral-rich vapors. They hum with a low, resonant frequency, a sound that is felt more than heard, a vibration in the bones of the city.

The city itself is a sprawl of open space and sparse structures. Buildings are not clustered but scattered, each one a monolith of carved stone and rusted iron, connected by suspended walkways and narrow causeways that span the chasms between them. The scale is regional, but the density is near-void; you can stand on a balcony and look out over a kilometer of open air, with only the occasional spire or tower breaking the horizon. The infrastructure is industrial—old engines of steam and heat, rusted pipes that vent scalding vapor into the sky, and silent, motionless cranes that once moved materials across the chasms. Now, most of the machinery is still, its gears frozen in place, its wires sagging and frayed.

The light is dim, filtered through the perpetual haze of condensing vapor and the high, thin clouds that never quite clear. The sun is a dull, coppery disk, seen only in brief moments when the wind shifts the steam. The glow from the crystalline growths is the primary illumination, casting long, shifting shadows that move like slow, silent predators across the ground.

Sounds are rare and deliberate. The hiss of steam, the drip of water from the canyon walls, the low hum of the crystals, and the occasional distant, metallic groan as the city settles under its own weight. There is no wind here, only the slow, convective rise of hot air, which carries the sound in strange ways—sometimes a noise seems to come from everywhere at once.

Ecology is a strange, non-biological system. The crystals grow and recede in cycles tied to the geothermal vents below, and the few fauna that exist are constructed—small, insect-like machines of iron and wire that scuttle across the rocks, feeding on the mineral deposits and the occasional pool of saline water. They are rare, seen only in the deepest, quietest parts of the city, and they move with a jerky, mechanical grace.

The rules of this world are simple: heat rises, water condenses, and the city is built on the bones of a forgotten industry. The past is layered here, not in strata of soil, but in the rusted girders and the silent engines, in the hollowed-out chambers where once there were crowds and commerce. Now, there is only the open space, the hum of the crystals, and the slow, steady drip of water in the heat.
