The floor of the abyss is a place of crushing silence and immense pressure, a landscape defined not by what is present, but by the sheer, suffocating weight of the medium that surrounds it. This is not a seabed in the traditional sense—there is no distant surface, no memory of sunlight. The water here is the universe, a cold, still, and absolute medium that has settled into its final, most profound state. It is ancient beyond measure, a liquid that has been compressed and unmoving for eons, eroding not by friction but by the simple, relentless passage of geological time. The topography is one of profound flatness, a plainscape of sediment so fine it behaves almost like a fluid itself, sculpted into dunes and basins by the subtle, tidal pulls of unseen gravitational forces. There are no rocks, only the accumulated dust of a world’s slow decay, packed so densely that to step upon it would be to sink into a substance with the consistency of wet clay.

The vegetation is a memory of life, not life itself. Here and there, in the vast, dark expanse, are the skeletons of forests. Great, petrified trees stand in silent groves, their trunks mineralized into quartz and calcite, their branches delicate lattices of crystal that catch the faintest luminescence. They are not growing; they are enduring, their forms preserved in the cold, chemical stillness. Between them, the ground is bare, save for the occasional, slow-growing mat of chemosynthetic bacteria that paints the sediment in faint, ghostly hues of purple and green—a sparse, cold fire that feeds on minerals and the faint energy bleeding from the planet’s core.

The light is not an absence but a presence of its own: a total, unbroken darkness that is palpable. It is a physical substance, a velvet weight against the skin. Yet, it is not absolute. The darkness is punctuated by a cold, blue-green bioluminescence that emanates from the fauna and the mineral deposits. It is a dim, starless night-sky reversed, with the light coming from below and within. The creatures that inhabit this realm are rare and strange, having evolved in a world without predation or need for speed. They are slow, phosphorescent things: gelatinous, bell-shaped swimmers that pulse with gentle light; armored, trilobite-like scavengers that drift like falling leaves over the sediment; and vast, silent leviathans that are rarely seen, only sensed as a displacement of the water, a pressure change that signals their passage. They are solitary, ancient, and unconcerned.

The atmosphere—if it can be called that—is one of profound, turbulent stillness. The water is so dense that movement is a violent act. A flick of a tail creates a shockwave that propagates for miles, a ripple that disturbs the sediment for days. The turbulence here is not of storms, but of energy—a hyperactive, chaotic exchange at the molecular level. Currents are not wind-driven but are born from geothermal vents that breathe plumes of superheated, mineral-rich water into the cold expanse. These vents are the heart of the world, and around them, the density of life and energy is almost saturated. The water around a vent is a blizzard of microorganisms, a swirling galaxy of light and motion, a frantic, silent carnival of existence that exists for a moment before being swept away into the immense, cold plains.

There is no technology, no artifact of any hand. The only structures are natural: the vast, cathedral-like spires of mineral deposits that grow from the vents, the perfectly smooth basins carved by ancient, settled currents, the intricate, lace-like patterns of crystal that form on the petrified trees. The rules of this world are simple and absolute: pressure, cold, and the slow, chemical clock of deep time. Life is not about growth or competition, but about endurance and efficiency. Sound is not a wave but a vibration felt in the bones, a deep, sub-aquatic hum from the planet’s core and the distant, grinding shifts of the planetary crust.

This is a place at the end of things, a landscape that has already completed its story. It is packed with existence—every cubic inch of water teems with microscopic life, every square foot of sediment holds a billion dormant spores—yet it feels profoundly empty. It is a cold, dark, silent, and eternal plain, where the only turbulence is the silent, energetic chaos of life persisting at the edge of impossibility.
