The place is a high, thin plateau of granite, a tableland lifted so close to the sky that the air itself is a different substance—rare, sharp, and brilliant. The sun, unfiltered by haze or humidity, pounds the rock with a relentless, almost clinical light. There are no shadows here, only sharp-edged silhouettes and the glint of exposed quartz veins that run through the stone like frozen lightning.

The ground is not soil but a coarse, granular regolith of crushed rock, the product of millennia of freeze-thaw cycles. It crunches underfoot with a sound like stepping on a bed of dry bones. The plateau is immense, a regional-scale expanse that seems to curve gently at the horizon, not with vegetation, but with the slight, almost imperceptible warp of distant rock faces. There are no trees, no shrubs, only the occasional, stubborn cushion of a hardy lichen, a grey-green film clinging to the lee side of boulders, or the startling flash of a purple saxifrage blooming directly from a crack in the stone, its petals thin as paper.

Water is the secret, pervasive presence. It does not flow in open streams; the scale and erosion have long since channeled any such thing underground or away. Instead, it is a dampness of a different order. Each dawn, a fine, mineral-laden fog condenses on every surface, a process so precise it seems engineered. By mid-morning, this fog has evaporated, leaving behind a glittering crust of dissolved salts on the rock. In deep crevices, where the sun never reaches, permanent seepages weep from the stone. These are not pools, but slick, dark patches of biofilm, cool to the touch, where tiny, blind crustaceans scuttle in the perpetual twilight of the fissure. The air holds the smell of wet stone and ozone after the rare, violent thunderstorms that crack the silence for minutes at a time.

The sound is the defining characteristic. It is not a sound of life—there are no insects humming, no birds calling—but of the world itself. It is a constant, deafening roar of wind. This wind is not a gale, but a steady, high-altitude current that shears across the plateau without cease. It is the sound of a thousand empty spaces rushing to fill itself. It screams over rock spires, moans through narrow slots in the cliff faces, and hums a deep, resonant note in the larger amphitheaters of stone. To stand in the open is to be immersed in a wall of noise so complete it becomes a kind of silence, a pressure on the eardrums that drowns out all internal thought.

Fauna is abundant but sparsely distributed. The creatures here are masters of emptiness. Great, solitary raptors—the color of storm clouds—ride the thermal currents with barely a flap of their wings, their eyes scanning the rock for the scuttling movement of rock hyraxes or the flash of a scaled reptile. The hyraxes themselves live in vast, communal warrens dug into the soft sedimentary layers between granite sheets, their barks echoing strangely in the wind. On the high, wind-scoured ridges, herds of thin-legged, sure-footed ungulates move like shadows, their hooves making no sound on the stone. They are always moving, always with the wind, their coats the exact grey of the rock.

There are no structures. No cairns, no ruins, no handprints. The only markings are natural: the long, parallel striations carved by glaciers that retreated eons ago, the circular pits where water whirled sand in a vortex for centuries, the deep, smooth grooves where rock has been rubbed by the passage of animals for millennia. The world feels ancient, settled, and in balance, not through harmony, but through a kind of immense, slow-wearing stasis.

The weirdness is subtle, a matter of perception. Because the wind never changes in its constant, deafening force, and the light never dims from its brilliant peak, time feels suspended. The shadows do not lengthen; they are etched in place by the angle of the sun, which seems locked at its zenith. A visitor might feel that they have stepped into a single, eternal moment. The only change is the rare, almost ritualistic flash of the storm, which arrives, batters the rock with sound and light, and is gone, leaving the same deafening wind and the same brilliant, empty sky in its wake. The world is a perfect, hollow bell, ringing forever with the sound of its own making.
