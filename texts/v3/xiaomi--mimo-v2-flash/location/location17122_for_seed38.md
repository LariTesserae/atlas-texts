This is a plateau the size of a continent, a single, endless capstone lifted high into the atmosphere. It is an old place, so old that the concept of a mountain has been forgotten; the world here has not been built up but worn down, settled into a final, patient form. The air is thick and warm, a constant, close breath that tastes of wet stone and chlorophyll. It is the wetness of a place that has not seen the sun in a thousand years, a dampness that rises from the ground and condenses on every surface, a perpetual, gentle weeping. The sound of water is everywhere, not as a river or a fall, but as a pervasive, liquid whisper: the drip from a hanging leaf, the gurgle of a hidden channel beneath a carpet of moss, the soft, constant susurrus of water moving through the very substrate of the land.

This is not a landscape of soil and rock, but of life and decay. The ground is a living mattress, a dense, spongy mat of vegetation so total that the distinction between plant and earth has dissolved. What you walk on is a complex, springy architecture of roots, rhizomes, and fungal mycelium, a black, peaty loam that is itself composed of the slowly digested remains of the ecosystem. Above this, the vegetation builds the world. Great, arching buttresses of liana and vine form canopies and tunnels, their trunks not of wood but of fused, fibrous growth. The trees are not separate things; they are the pillars of a single, continuous structure, their branches woven together into a ceiling that filters the dim light into a perpetual, green-tinged twilight. The air is saturated with spores and pollen, a visible haze that hangs in the unmoving air.

The scale of it is vast, so vast that the horizon is a curve of indeterminate green and grey, and weather systems form and dissipate over seas of canopy. To be here is to be inside a world-sized organism. The density of life is staggering; every cubic foot is teeming. You cannot place a hand on a surface without touching a dozen species of moss, lichen, and creeping vine. The fauna, though abundant, is small and slow, perfectly adapted to this saturated, dim, and ancient realm. Insects with translucent, fungal-veined wings drift in the warm air. Small, amphibious creatures, their skin perpetually moist, blink slowly from beneath broad leaves. There are no predators here, not in the traditional sense. The struggle for existence has been replaced by a profound, patient competition for space and light, a slow-motion ballet of growth and entanglement.

There are no tools here, no technology, no mark of a thinking hand other than the ecosystem's own, impossibly complex design. What structures exist are not built but grown: hollows in the colossal, woven trunks, archways formed by fallen trees that have been colonized by new growth before they even hit the ground, faint, circular patterns in the moss where a larger animal might have rested a century ago. These are the only artifacts, and they are ephemeral.

The light is dim, the colour of a deep forest in perpetual late afternoon. It filters down in shifting columns, illuminating motes of dust and spore in a constant, silent dance. The temperature is a stable, warm blanket, the high elevation moderated by the blanket of vegetation and the constant evaporation. There is no sharp day or night, only a gradual dimming and brightening as the cloud-cover above the canopy shifts over epochs.

The strangeness of the place is not in any single horror or marvel, but in its totality. It is the feeling of being inside a lung, or a single, vast cell. The rules of this world are the rules of biology and geology, writ large and slowed to a pace that is barely perceptible. Change happens not in storms or earthquakes, but in the slow settling of a new layer of detritus, the gradual invasion of a more successful moss, the patient, millimeter-by-millimeter growth of a root tendril. It is a landscape at the end of a long, long process, settled into its final form, a world where life and stone have become one, and the only sound is the soft, eternal weeping of a place that has forgotten how to be dry.
