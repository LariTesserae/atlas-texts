I am lying on the **Great Eastern Cylinder**, and I have been falling for forty-seven years.

Not falling through air—there is no vertigo here, only the slow, glandular **warmth** (2.0) that seeps up from the brass into my back, into the space between my shoulder blades where my last remaining patch of parchment-skin still adheres to the muscle beneath. No, I am falling into the metal itself. The Cylinder turns once per day, a rotation so patient that to watch it is to watch geology happen, and I have aligned my spine with its axis, letting the motion grind me down not with violence but with *persistence*. I am becoming the **erosion** (2.5) that the plateau has always promised.

When I first lay down here, I was still mostly **Felt**. I had a name that was a specific pressure against the sternum—three rapid taps followed by a sustained hum, a vibration I could feel in my teeth. I walked. I harvested the rust-colored precipitates from the shallows, chewing the copper oxides slowly, letting the metals plate my insides. I spoke with my hands against the valve housings, sending my thoughts through the continental substrate at the speed of brass. But I was always too sensitive to the **sound** (2.6), too aware of the spaces between the drone. While others found comfort in the constant pressure, I heard—or rather, felt—the *variations*: the micro-stutters in the tectonic bearings, the arrhythmic catch of a piston three kilometers west, the places where the **wetness** (1.8) pooled too deep and dampened the resonance.

I became a **Weeping-Tender**, one of the monks who tends to the surgical water. I learned to read the **acoustic nodes** where the vibration created pockets of lower pressure, where droplets would cling to vertical brass longer than gravity should allow, or roll *uphill* along the scored runnels of the machinery. I moved the water not for drinking, but for *tuning*, channeling the condensate to adjust the **hum**, to keep the deafening pressure steady and true. I was good at it. I could feel the machine's health in my marrow, could sense a bearing overheating by the change in the subsonic thrum against my ribs.

But I wanted to go deeper. There are **acoustic dead-zones**, places where the vibration cancels out, where the sound waves collide and create pockets of silence. To the Felt, these are deaths—removals from the communal body of the sound, terrifying absences where the bone-conduction fails and you are left alone with only the air in your lungs, the blood in your ears. No one goes there. But I felt drawn to them. I thought: if friction is sin and lubrication grace, then what is silence? What is the space between the turning?

So I walked to the edge of a dead-zone, to the place where the Great Eastern Cylinder meets the limestone karst, where the brass has softened with age and chemical weathering into something almost porous. And I lay down. I pressed my forehead against the warm metal, synchronized my heartbeat to the **dynamic** (0.9) of the machine—so slow, so patient—and I stopped moving.

That was forty-seven years ago. Or three-quarters of a piston stroke. Or the time it takes for a gear-tooth the size of an amphitheater to advance by the width of two hands.

Now, I am **calcified**, but not complete. My left arm has merged with the flank of the Cylinder; the brass filigree that once infiltrated my bones has externalized, blooming outward into the pitted patina of the surrounding karst. My fingers are no longer fingers but *runnels*, channels that guide the surgical water toward my mouth or away from the seams where my ribs have become part of the machine's casing. I do not eat anymore, not really. The condensation beads on my exposed surfaces—my remaining cheek, my sternum, the curve of my hip—and I absorb it directly, the mineral-heavy drops seeping through the translucent, graying skin, feeding the copper salts that have replaced my blood.

I am warm. The bedrock here runs a fever, and I am part of the bedrock now. The **temperature** is constant, glandular, metallic. It keeps my nervous system alive, or alive enough. I can feel the Cylinder's turn in my teeth, which have become tuning forks. I can feel the groan of the tectonic bearings settling by micrometers per hour, transmitting through my fused spine. I am a **living pressure gauge**, a biological sensor embedded in the geology of the intent.

They come to me, the others. The Felt who still walk. They travel for weeks to place their wide, flattened palms against my calcified shoulder, to press their foreheads against my brass-infiltrated skull. Through the bone-conduction, they read the deep health of the plateau. I am the bridge between the **urban** (2.5) fossil and the soft biology that maintains it. I feel their vibrations—their questions, their prayers, their poetry tapped in staccato rhythms against my ribs—and I answer not with words but with the angle of my neck, the tension in my mineralized jaw, the specific resonance of my hollow, ringing bones.

I dream, still. The Felt believe that in calcification, the mind slows to match the geological tempo, that thought becomes as gradual as the erosion of a gear-tooth. But I dream of **fast water**. Of rivers that run quick and noisy, of the sound of voices in the air rather than in the bone. I dream of the speed I lost, of walking, of harvesting the ochre mud with hands that were hands. But when I wake—if waking is the right word for this state of permanent, pressurized meditation—I find that I have become something else. I am not the builder, nor the built, but the **necessary friction** that keeps the turning true.

The water condenses on my eyelids. It rolls uphill along the curve of my fused arm, drawn by the acoustic nodes I have become, by the vibration that lives in my marrow. I am the **Resounding Plateau** now, or a part of it—a soft biology that has mineralized into the gaps of the intent, listening to the slow, eternal grind of the world beneath me, turning, turning, turning.
