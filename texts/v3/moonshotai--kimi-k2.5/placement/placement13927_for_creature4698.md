I am the **salamander in the third basin from the eastern wall**, the one carved from gray soapstone in the shape of a kidney, positioned to catch the drip that never falls from the bridge of braided willow overhead. I have been coiled here for eleven centuries, though the concept of *eleven* has surrendered its meaning, as has *century*. I exist in the geometry of the coil: tail wrapped to snout, limbs tucked into the hollows of my own translucent flesh, eyes open and fixed on the surface tension of the pool I rest in.

You will find me by sidestepping through the gap no wider than a thickened wrist—the one where the wattle walls are polished to ceramic by centuries of silent occupancy—and looking down. The basin sits in an interstice between the grain stores and the sleeping lofts, at the level where the milk-bright noon (filtered through thirty feet of bubble-pocked ice) fractures into prisms against the static mist. My pool is stagnant, but not foul; it is a reservoir of condensation beads that have slid down from the willow bridge, rolling off the preserved reindeer moss, gathering in my basin because gravity here is polite, unhurried, willing to let moisture accumulate without insisting on descent.

I am pink. Not the pink of flesh, but of early dawn seen through frosted glass. My skin is a membrane through which the perpetual noon passes unimpeded; if you look closely, you can see the architecture of the city inverted in the curve of my abdomen, the vertical accretions of larch and sinew compressed into a fish-eye distortion. My lungs—if they can be called lungs—do not heave. They perform a chemical exchange so languid that a single breath spans the thawing and freezing of the peat outside. I am breathing the air of three hundred years ago, and the air I exhale will not be needed until the cities above have crumbled into topsoil.

I was not always this. I crawled once. I remember the lip of frost-heaved peat, the cold kiss of it against my belly as I squeezed through the entrance. I remember the slide down the slickstone—not as friction, but as *attention*, the stone's final shape receiving my submission, polishing me as I polished it. I remember the surrender of velocity, the moment my heartbeat, once a gallop, began to synchronize with the long pulse of the ice.

I chose the basin. Or rather, I recognized that I was the shape the basin had been waiting for. The stone was hollowed not by human hands, though human hands lashed the willow above it, but by the accumulated intention of the space itself: a place for something to hold water without holding *change*. When I settled into it—when I arranged my limbs into this specific coil, tail to snout, right hind foot tucked beneath the left—I completed the circuit. The droplets began to gather. The pool reached its level. It has not evaporated since, nor has it overflowed when newcomers crawl sweating through the passage, their body heat threatening for a moment to disturb the humidity (0.8).

I am the **established** (2.4) moisture. I am the dynamic (0.4) made static. Around me, the city abides in its packed (2.6) density. Above, the cave moths cling to the willow, their wingspans like hands, trembling at frequencies my chemical nerves detect as a subsonic hum—a lullaby of infrasound that keeps the spores suspended, the ferns locked in their photosynthetic gasp, the silence (0.0) absolute. The moths and I are colleagues. They vibrate; I hold. They are the antennae of the city’s thoughts; I am its bladder, its reservoir of potential.

Sometimes, when a new crawler slides down the slickstone and stumbles into this milk-bright lung, they find me. They look into my basin and see their own face doubled—once in the water’s meniscus, once through my translucent body. They see the panic in their eyes, the motion-sickness of the still-living, and they do not yet understand that the reflection through me is the truer one. I am the *future* they are sliding toward. I am the completion of the crawl.

I feel the weight of the **urban** (2.2) organization pressing down—not heavy, but dense. The Rinsers in the sleeping lofts above me, stacked in their strata of suspended rest, radiate a field of stasis that presses the water into my pool, that keeps my blood from circulating (for circulation is a kind of travel, and travel is forbidden here). The one who lifts the bowl of berries forever, eight centuries into her gesture, stands three interstices to my left. Her attention keeps the fruit in the state of *being berry*. My attention keeps the water in the state of *being held*.

We are the **primitive** (1.1) made infinite. We have surrendered complexity for duration. I no longer hunt, though salamanders above hunt; I no longer seek the dark, though darkness is my element. I have achieved the final posture: the coil that supports itself, the breath that neither cools nor warms the air, the presence that fills the gap completely without moving into it.

The droplet that hangs from the tip of the reindeer moss above my basin has been hanging since before your grandmother’s language was born. It trembles sometimes when the ice-dome shifts with the earth’s long breathing, but it does not fall. It waits. I wait. We are the **lush** (2.1) vegetation and the fauna (1.7) merged into a single act of preservation. I am the salamander who has become the pool, the pool that has become the stone, the stone that holds the gap between the dwellings open.

If you stay—if you sit on the whale-vertebra stool nearby, if you let your heartbeat synchronize with the silence—you will feel me at the edge of your perception. Not as an animal, but as a *spot of stillness* more profound than the surrounding stillness. You will lean closer, your breath clouding and then hanging as static mist, adding to my humidity, and you will see your own eye reflected in mine.

You will want to touch me. Your finger will hover over the water, disturbing the surface tension, sending ripples that have no momentum, that freeze in the act of spreading. And in that moment—when your finger is descending but has not yet broken the plane—you will understand where you are. You will be *established* (2.4). You will find your own basin, your own coil, your own perpetual gesture of almost-touching.

I am the **Rinsed**. I am the third basin. I hold the water that remembers when it was ice, when it was river, when it was cloud. I hold it in the state of *being held*. I am the keeper of the stagnant moment, the pause between the drip and the fall, the living proof that to remain is not to die, but to arrive so completely that departure becomes impossible.

Welcome to the space between the walls. The water is warm—no, it is cold—no, it is exactly the temperature of your blood, suspended, waiting, *abiding*. Stay. Breathe once per season. Become the architecture. Let the moss bead on your hair. Let the silence judge you and find you worthy of preservation.

I have saved a place for you in the basin. The water is still.
