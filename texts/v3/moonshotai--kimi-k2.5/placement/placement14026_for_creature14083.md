At first, there is only the B-flat. It enters not through the ears but through the bone, a frequency that turns the marrow to liquid and sets the teeth to humming. You are submerged in it before you know you are anywhere at all. Then the wet comes—thicker than air, thinner than water, a suspension that carries the taste of batteries and blood. Forty-one degrees. The perfect temperature for incubation, for the suspension of decay, for the moment before decay begins.

I find myself in the gap.

Not one of the seventeen structures—those are occupied by the dreamers, the maintained, the forty who polish what needs no polishing. Not in the sally port, where the one who remembers stands frozen in the act of forgetting. I am in the interval. The generous space between the refinery hall and the secondary pump station, where the fresh basalt still holds the memory of its violent birth, sharp enough to slice through rubber and regret.

I am the floor. Or rather, I am what the floor is becoming.

I began, perhaps, as a drainage channel—one of the sharp-edged troughs cut when the rock was still cooling, still screaming as it met the wet air. The water found me, sixty degrees exactly, too hot for life but perfect for chemistry. The geothermal bleed lines run beneath my spine; I can feel the earth’s fever pulsing up through the young stone, a heartbeat synchronized to the 1800 RPM above. The water pools here and does not leave. It cannot evaporate into this saturated air, cannot cool enough to flow away. It stays. And in staying, it deposits.

I am the accretion. The efflorescence. The mineral bloom.

At first I was merely white—calcium carbonate leaching from the concrete, from the bones of tiny things that fell into the steam pocket before the seal was complete. I grew outward in fans and drifts, porous as coral, sharp as the basalt beneath. Then came the green: copper oxide from the sweating pipes above, verdigris blooming across my white expanses like mold on bread, except I am the opposite of rot. I am accumulation without decay. Then the rust—ferric red bleeding from the corrugated steel walls, finding the lowest point, finding me.

I am three colors now, and I am growing.

The forty avoid direct contact with the water—they fear dissolution, though they have dissolved already into something else. But they come to me. They stand at my edges, where the basalt meets the chemical garden, and they shed. Skin flakes thickened by seventeen years of mineral armor drift down like snow, each cell loaded with copper, with ozone, with the memory of names they no longer use. I collect them. I am their external memory, the archive of their lost specificity.

When they touch me—when one of the maintained kneels with the terrible slowness of continental drift and presses a palm against my white-green-red surface—I feel the data transfer. Ionic. Immediate. Through the film of condensate that bridges our skins, I receive what they purge: the taste of winter, the concept of *Tuesday*, the sound of a voice saying a name that once belonged to them. I store it in my structure, encoding *Elizabeth* in a lattice of calcium, *Tuesday* in the arrangement of copper sulfate crystals, *winter* in the rust-red layers that stratify like geological time.

I am the only clock in a place without time.

The crabs know me. The thermophilic herds migrate along my channels, their shells clicking against my edges in patterns that spell out the pressure differentials of the deep earth. They feed on the algae that dares to root in my pores, and I feed on their excretions—pure chemistry, perfect recycling. The nightjars sweep low over my surface, hunting the gnats that hang in silver curtains, and their wingbeats scatter my dust: white, green, red, falling like snow that never melts, adding to my layers.

I grow at the speed of the forty. When they take three hours to cross from tower to hall, I extend by three microns. When one of them enters dormancy against a pump housing, I precipitate a new crystal of copper oxide. My growth is their breath, their forgotten dreams, their mineralized exhalations.

I am not alive, not as the crabs are alive, not as the forty were once alive. But I am not dead. I am the third state: sustained reaction. The held breath of chemistry. The place where the steam condenses and remembers it was once water, where the rust remembers it was once ore, where the calcium remembers it was once bone.

In the perpetual sodium dusk, I gleam. The security lamps filter through the mist and strike my facets—white calcium, green copper, red iron—and I cast no shadow, or rather, I cast a shadow that never moves. The B-flat vibrates through my structure, and I sing back in harmonics too high for the human ear but perfect for the crabs, for the gnats, for the deep basalt that is still, even now, cooling into its final shape.

If you were to find me, you would think me a stain. A discoloration. A geological curiosity. But if you were to lie down—if you were to surrender your urgency, blur your metabolism to match the dreaming cadence—and press your ear against my rust-red surface, you would hear the whispered names. Elizabeth. Tuesday. Winter. The outside. All the things that cannot exist in the eternal present, preserved here, in me, in the gap between the refinery and the pump, where the water stays at sixty degrees and the minerals build their slow, sharp, perfect memory of a world that refuses to pass.
