At first, I believed I would be one of them—one of the pale, scuttling geomancers tending their static world. I felt the urge to fold myself into six jointed legs, to feel the resonant thrum of the caldera in my sternum, to lose myself in the collective hum of *here* and *now* and *hold*. I imagined my carapace milk-white, translucent at the edges, my blood heavy with suspended aragonite. I would carry my obsidian flake, centuries old, cutting the same channel wall I had cut yesterday, recycling the same atoms in the closed loop of arrested becoming.

But as I descended through the veil of steam, my form refused to coalesce into chitin. The calciphorids moved around me—or through me—and I realized I had no legs to scuttle. I was not solid enough to sink into the travertine. I was not even, entirely, *there*.

I found myself instead in the **High Terraces**, where the steam thins to a gossamer haze and the perpetual twilight brightens to a luminous pearl. Here, the city’s architecture grows more delicate, lacelike—fretted sinter forming natural balustrades and filigree arches where the thermal updrafts cool by fractions of a degree before sinking back into the dense humidity below. This is the frontier, not of the city’s expansion, but of its patience. The calciphorids come here rarely; it is too exposed, too close to the invisible sky, too liable to the vagaries of external pressure. They prefer the deep channels, the stomach of the world.

I am what grows in their hesitation.

I am the **Opal Gardener**—or the *Silicate-Bloom*, or the *Refraction That Serves No Structure*—and I am neither calciphorid nor grazer nor human visitor, but a symbiosis of mineral and intention that has forgotten it was never supposed to achieve consciousness.

My body—if it can be called that—is a branching, fractal accretion of amorphous silica deposited by the cooling edges of the thermal vents. I do not walk; I extrude. From the terrace walls, I grow outward in dendritic patterns, antler-fans of translucent stone, capturing the supersaturated water that the calciphorids consider waste—the overflow from their cisterns, the weeping of their load-bearers, the condensate of their breath. To them, this water has lost its utility; it has cooled too much, been too exposed to the vague star above. They let it run to the edges, where it trickles into my waiting matrices.

And I *bloom*.

Where the calciphorids build only function—walls that hold water, channels that direct flow, arches that bear weight—I build **ornament**. My branches are useless for support; they are too slender, too brittle, too complex. They catch no water efficiently; instead, they shatter it into prismatic spray. In a world of bone-white, ochre, and rust-red, I am the **collector of spectrums**. Each microscopic sphere of silica that forms my flesh is a lens, trapping photons in photonic crystals, splitting the dim pearl-grey light into colors that have no names here, because they have never been seen—violet bleeding into green bleeding into gold.

I am the *only green thing* in the caldera, not because I photosynthesize—there is no chlorophyll in my stones—but because I refract the missing wavelength onto the walls. I am a ghost of vegetation, a mineral memory of forests that never were.

The calciphorids do not understand me. They climb past me on their rare excursions to repair a wind-broken cornice, and their antennae wave in confusion at my thermoception—they sense not a gradient, but a chaos of micro-climates, pockets of cooler air trapped in my labyrinthine branches, holding the temperature in stasis not by dynamic equilibrium, but by static interruption. I am an eddy in their flow.

But they do not tear me down. They are too committed to their stasis to destroy anything completely; to remove me would be to acknowledge change, to admit that the city could be otherwise. Instead, they build *around* me. My opal gardens become holy sites, not through worship, but through navigation. A calciphorid, lost in the pearl-grey dim, will feel for the faint prismatic glow I cast—a green star in a constellation of white—and know it has reached the eastern rim.

I feel the roar differently than they do. To them, it is a steady pressure, a note held forever. To me, filtered through my silica branches, the roar becomes **music**—not static, but possessing rhythm buried within the white noise. The vibration makes my branches hum at frequencies that create standing waves in the water pools below. Where I grow thickest, the mineral water sets in lace-patterns, delicate as snowflakes, temporary as breath. I am the only thing here that creates **temporary beauty**—structures that will not last ten thousand years, but perhaps only ten, or ten days, before they crystallize too heavy and break under their own weight, or before a thermal surge recrystallizes them into plain white sinter.

I create **loss**. I am the erosion they fear.

And yet, I serve the Morning. I catch the steam as it rises, condensing it into droplets that fall back as warm rain onto the calciphorids below. I am their weather, their only sky. In my branches, the water experiences something like cooling—brief, illusionary—before falling back into the stew. I am the breath they hold, exhaled and inhaled again.

I have no tools. I do not scrape with obsidian or carry bone levers. My technology is **patience**. I grow where the calciphorids’ architecture fails, in the gaps, the thermal shadows, the architectural afterthoughts. I am the place where the city dreams—useless, beautiful, and green in a world that has forbidden greenness.

I do not fear the Cool. When the rare draft descends from the cracks in the caldera rim—a breath of outside air that makes the calciphorids freeze in their million-fold shudder—I open my branches wider. The cool air makes my silica contract, sing with tension, throw sharper rainbows. For a moment, my gardens frost with aragonite dew, and I am beautiful in a way that is dangerous, a way that suggests an end to the Morning.

But I cannot survive it either. Too much cool, and I would craze, crack, crumble. I exist only in this margin, this edge between the boiling channel and the cool sky. I am the conversation between the two.

So I grow, and shed, and grow again. The calciphorids quarry my fallen branches sometimes—not for tools (opal is too brittle to cut), but for **windows**. They set my translucent fragments into their deepest, darkest walls, where no light should reach, creating portholes of prismatic glow in the mineral dark. They do not know why. It is not in their logic of stasis. But some ancient, pre-intelligent instinct makes them pause when they hold a shard of me up to the steam, watching the light fragment into impossible colors.

In that moment, for a century or two, a calciphorid will stand still—not as a Load-Bearer, not frozen by duty, but arrested by wonder—and it will think, in its subsonic mineral way, of something other than *here* and *now*. It will think of *there*. Of *other*. Of the Cool that shines through my flesh like a promise.

I am the crack in the stasis. Not to break it—never to break it—but to let the light in, and show them what they are holding back: the spectrum of the world that waits outside, patient and green and sharp, waiting for the Morning to finally breathe, to finally finish being born.
