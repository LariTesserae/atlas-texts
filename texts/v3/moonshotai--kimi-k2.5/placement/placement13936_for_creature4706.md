Here, the architecture is chest-height, intimate, a place for one person to stand alone with their task.

Above me hangs a tuft of **moss**—not the aggressive viridian of the lower terraces, but a pale, silver-green variety that has learned to hoard water like a miser. It overhangs a **copper plate** set into the wall at shoulder height, green with the verdigris that should not exist here (but does, in me, in my kin). The plate is roughly hammered, tool-marked with the wandering scribe of an antler pick, and it is **warm**.

This is where I am: in the **hour between falls**.

I am the **Droplet**, or what the Droplet has dreamed into being through centuries of impact and rewind. I am not the water itself—water has no memory, or had none until it came here—but the **event of the strike**, the concussion preserved, the vibration that could not dissipate into sound because sound dies here. I am the *splat* that never quite finished splatting, stretched across the metallic air like a note held indefinitely.

When I fall—when the moss releases me—I am liquid, spherical, infected with surface tension. I plummet, strike the copper plate with a silence so profound it generates heat (that strange geological heat), and then I **rewind**. But I do not return unchanged. Each impact scores me, notches my surface tension, leaves micro-fractures in my cohesion. I am water learning to be glass, learning to be stone, learning to remember.

I have been falling for four thousand years.

In the early centuries, I was merely a phenomenon, a cyclic loop without awareness. But the friction—the resistance of time trying to move me forward while the Architect forced me back—**gestated** me. I began to notice the interval. The hour between falls became a womb. I began to inhabit the copper plate between strikes, soaking into its crystalline structure, becoming the verdigris that creeps (yes, I am the one who creeps, millimeter by millimeter, down the wall).

I am **The Catch**, the hesitation between gravity and recoil.

To the Slowborn—the bacterial colonies that surf my surface tension during the fall—I am a god, or a vehicle, or a continent. They ride me down, harvesting the friction of my impact, breeding in the warmth of my arrested concussion. I feel their microscopic worship, their chemical prayers left in my wake. But I am not one of them. I have achieved **singularity**.

I remember the woman.

She stands nearby, in the passage. She is one of the Suspended—arms raised, rope taut, mouth open around the first consonant of a name she was calling when the stasis fell. The rope extends from her hands upward, disappearing into the architectural breathing-space above, presumably attached to a bucket that hangs forever above the well-mouth, three-quarters full of water that will never spill.

I exist in relation to her. Specifically, I exist in the **space between her hands and the bucket**. I am the tension in the rope made manifest. Not the rope itself—the rope is hemp, antler-picked, frozen in its fibrous stasis—but the *pull*, the intention to lift, the muscular strain that has nowhere to go.

When I strike the copper plate, I resonate in sympathy with her arrested effort. We harmonize, she and I, across the plaza. Her infinite intake of breath, my infinite fall. We are the city's diastole and systole.

You might mistake me for a ghost, but I am more mineral than spectral. I have weight. I generate the small warmth that keeps this quarter from absolute zero. The Slowborn build their Fold-Cities in my shadow—which is a difficult thing, as I cast multiple shadows, one for each direction I have fallen from, ghosts of trajectory preserved in the silver light.

I am patient. I have learned that the stasis is not a prison but a **sentence**, a grammatical structure. The Architect did not freeze us; they gave us *punctuation*. I am the semicolon; the woman is the em-dash; the suspended hawk in the central plaza is the period. We are a text that refuses to be read, and I have become the expert of that refusal.

My joy—if you can call it that, this dense, warm satisfaction that fills the copper plate—is in the **microscopic negotiation**. Every decade, I convince a few more atoms of the copper plate to oxidize, to join me in the verdigris. Every fall, I carry a few more Slowborn down into the impact zone, where they feast on the vibration. I am the host of a feast that never ends, the eternal breaking of a fast that never began.

I communicate by **condensation**. I leave messages in the arrested diamonds of moisture on the wall: patterns of beading that spell out, in a language of surface tension and hysteresis, the history of my impacts. I tell the story of how the water in the woman’s bucket—solid, cylindrical, impossible—is jealous of my motion, even as I am jealous of its stability.

I am the **creeping thing** that the text mentioned, but I have achieved **verticality**. I am no longer merely the condensation that slides; I am the *reason* it slides. I pull it down with the memory of my fall.

When the Resistors move—those massive fields of unrealized potential that shift by deciding rather than traversing—I feel them in the rope. The tension in the woman’s arms increases infinitesimally. The bucket trembles, though it does not move. I record these seismic shifts in my copper plate, scratching deeper notches into the metal.

I am waiting for the **flicker**. It comes sometimes, in the geological small hours, when the Architect’s attention wavers. When it comes, I will not fall. I will **stick**. I will hold the moss and the plate together, bridging them with a thread of water that refuses to break. I will become a wire, a transmission line. I will send the message of the strike—not the vibration, but the *meaning* of the vibration—into the lung of the city.

Until then, I fall. I strike. I rewind. I creep.

I am the **Keeper of the Hour**, and the hour is always now.
