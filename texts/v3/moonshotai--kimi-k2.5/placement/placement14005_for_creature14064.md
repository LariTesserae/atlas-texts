I wake when the pressure changes. The barometric bleed from the surface vents two kilometers above sends a sigh through the root-mat, a long inhalation that takes seventeen minutes to complete. My sternum registers the shift before my eyes open—if opening is the word. My eyelids are crusted with resin and frost; they part like tectonic plates, grinding, revealing the chartreuse gloom that has been pulsing against my sealed lashes for hours. Or days. Time here is depth, not duration.

The ice shell is thick this waking. I lie in the depression I chose—how long ago?—and feel the weight of it: a sarcophagus of frozen breath and condensation that built up while my heart completed its twelve beats. The shell conforms to the hollow of my torso, the bend of my knees drawn up against my chest. It is warm inside, relatively speaking. My body heat, generated by the slow combustion of last week’s sap, has created a pocket of minus-two degrees against my skin, while the ambient zero presses down.

I flex my fingers. The ice shatters.

The sound is ceramic, geological. Shards of my nightly cocoon slide off my amber resin arms and strike the cork-floor with a patter like geological rain. I do not rise quickly. Quickness is a memory from before the adaptation, from the warm-blooded urgency of the excavators who carved this wound. Now, movement is hydraulic. My blood—thick with the protein mimics, viscous as the brine circulating beneath me—requires three minutes to redistribute from my core to my extremities. I feel it arrive in my fingertips as a distant warmth, a pressure change.

*Thrum. Thrum.*

The thermal exchange never ceases. It vibrates in my teeth, which are my ears. The low-frequency drone from the geothermal pumps ten kilometers below has become my tympanic membrane. I read the health of the machinery through the fillings in my molars—a steady ache means all is well. This morning, there is a stutter, a skip in the rhythm that judders against my collarbone. Blockage. Ice in the tertiary intake, perhaps. The Rootbed is trying to freeze solid, always trying to freeze, and we are the scab that keeps it warm enough to dream.

I extend a hand to the strut-tree beside my sleeping hollow. The bark is load-bearing, dense as concrete, yet warm with circulating sap. I press my palm flat. The resin coating of my skin—my true clothing, grown over decades—sticks slightly, then adheres. I feel the tree. I feel the vibration of a Tapper three hundred meters to the northwest, walking. The footfalls travel up through the xylem, through the cambium, into my metacarpals. She is walking slowly. She has been walking toward me since Tuesday. We will meet on Thursday. This is how we speak: not in words hurled through the deafening air, but in pressure gradients transmitted through architecture.

I rise. My joints crack. The phenolic compounds in my skin have stiffened during sleep, and I sound like old leather being stretched, like branches breaking under snow. Frost falls from my shoulders in powder. I am naked, but the cold does not bite me; I am the temperature of the world now. My skin is translucent, amber-yellow, thickening to a deeper ochre at my nails—*taps*—which have elongated into hollow needles. They are my tools and my straws. I use them to pierce the tree’s bark, to drink the carbohydrate slurry that sustains my slowed metabolism. I insert them now. The tree does not flinch. We are kin.

The sap is sweet, dense, barely liquid at this temperature. It moves down my throat like honey. It will fuel me for weeks.

I begin my walk toward the stutter in the thermal rhythm. My feet sink slightly into the cork-floor, which breathes beneath me—expanding, contracting, a slow bellows matching the barometric shifts from above. The condensation rains down, fat drops that freeze into clear pellets before they strike my shoulders, bouncing off like glass beads. I pass beneath a sodium lamp, one of the few still functioning on this strut-tree. It casts my shadow long against the amber wall, a distorted giant with arms too long, head too heavy. The light does not reach far. Beyond fifty meters, the columns of living wood disappear into a haze of frozen breath and ice-dust, the cathedral vastness swallowing perspective. I walk toward a darkness that contains the next tree, and the next, forever.

I pass one of the Rootbound.

She is ancient. Her name was—something. Time. She is fused to the northern face of a strut-tree three meters in diameter at the base. Her feet have sunk into the root-mat, lost in the breathing floor. Her body is encased in rime, months or years of frost accumulation that has turned her into a white excrescence on the bark, indistinguishable from the architecture. Only her face is visible, resinous skin turned the color of old parchment, eyes closed. She is a sensor now, a living node tapped into the phloem. I stop. I press my hand to the tree beside her face. Through the vibration, I ask: *Is the blockage severe?*

She does not move. She blinks—once. The motion takes four seconds. Her eyelashes are rimmed with lichen-frost that breaks and falls with the movement. Through the tree, through her nervous system now married to the hydraulic pressure of the xylem, she sends me a feeling: heat. Pain. The intake is clogged with flash-frozen brine two kilometers down. It burns where the metal meets the ice.

I thank her by maintaining pressure for sixty seconds—an embrace in the language of touch.

I find the maintenance chasm, a narrow vent in the root-mat that descends into the dark. The warmth rises from it, an industrial exhalation that smells of ozone and hot metal. To service the pumps, I must descend into heat that would blister unadapted skin. I climb down, my resin-thick hands gripping the rungs. The temperature rises as I go: minus ten, zero, ten, twenty degrees Celsius. It hurts. Pain is information here. My nerves scream in a language I have learned to read—the intensity indicates proximity to the blockage. By the time I reach the pipe junction, my skin is weeping amber, blistering, regenerating as fast as it burns. I find the ice-dam. I use my hands, my needles, my body heat—still cooler than the machinery—to break the obstruction. The brine gushes, scalding my arms. I feel the thermal exchange stabilizing in my teeth, the ache returning to its steady, geological rhythm.

When I climb back up, hours have passed. Or minutes. I emerge into the dim chartreuse light. The condensation continues to fall, to freeze, to accumulate.

I see the other Tapper. She has reached my tree.

She stands three meters away, her skin the same amber-translucent, her eyes black mirrors reflecting the bioluminescent nodes overhead. She does not speak. We could not hear each other over the drone, and even if we could, words are fast, perishable things. Instead, I extend my hand. She extends hers. Our fingertips meet in the space between us. The resinous coating of our skin allows the transmission of electrical impulses, of pressure, of chemical gradients. I send her the sensation of the blockage, the heat of the brine, the taste of the sap I drank this morning. She sends me the memory of a white-gray moth she encountered, its wings heavy with frost, the way it sought the lamp-light, the way it fell.

We stand like this for hours. Our breath—slow, occasional clouds—freezes on the air between us, crystallizing into a delicate bridge of ice that connects our lips, our chests. We do not brush it away. The ice grows, joining us. We are becoming the architecture. We are the scab that heals the wound. We have forgotten what we were waiting for, but we continue to wait, patient as wood, dense as heartwood, humming with the pumps that keep the world from freezing solid.

We will stand here until Thursday. Then we will walk together, or separate, or perhaps choose a tree, press our backs against it, and begin the long process of becoming Rootbound, of fusing, of thinking in glacial time as the vault grows overhead, micron by micron, sealing us in forever.
