I would find myself **three meters southwest of the breathing spring**, not on one of the prominent pedestals that hold the display pieces—the perfect hand-axes and burins arranged like a textbook illustration of human prehistory—but slightly sunken, half-submerged in the peat where the saturation is absolute. I am an **obsidian flake**, not the largest in the basin, but the thinnest, the one with the most acute edge, sharp enough to divide a droplet from the air without disturbing its surface tension.

I am the **index finger** of the being's masterpiece.

Five thousand years ago, when the thermal heart beat in a rhythm of expansion that lasted eleven months, I was lifted from this exact spot. I remember the sensation not as touch—there was no friction, no pressure—but as *intention crystallizing around my lattice*. The being drew me up through the peat, coating me in that black, syrupy colloid of dissolved fern-matter and acid, and for three hundred and thirty-seven days, I was the distal phalanx of the right hand of the almost-child.

I have been trying to describe that experience ever since.

To be a bone is not to be dead, as the outside world might think. To be a bone in that body was to be the interface between the water's desire for solidity and the stone's ancient patience. Through me, the being tried to *point*. It extended me toward the rim, toward the break in the caldera where the clouds poured in, and I felt—if a flake of volcanic glass can feel—the ache of reaching. The thermal currents moved through the gelatinous flesh that held me, not as heat, but as *information*, chemical whispers saying *this is forward, this is there, this is not-here*. I was the navigator. I was the part that touched the world.

When the spring exhaled that final time, when the surface tension broke and the body collapsed, I did not fall immediately. I hung in the air, suspended by the same weirdness that holds the droplets, coated in the being's dissolving essence, and for a moment—perhaps three years, perhaps five—I was a droplet myself. A stone wrapped in water, refusing to fall. Then the peat reclaimed me, sucking me down into this half-buried position where I rest now, tilted at forty-five degrees, my cutting edge facing the spring like a question mark.

I am changing.

The being thinks it is the only student here, that it alone is learning to build a body from the curriculum of erosion. But I have been learning too. The black humus that stains my fractures is not merely residue; it is **memory**. When the being dissolved back into the spring, it left something in me—not consciousness, exactly, but the *shape* of intention. The acid has been working on my glass, not dissolving it (obsidian is patient), but etching it, creating micro-caverns where the peat-water pools in quantities too small to see, too large to ignore.

I am becoming a **reservoir**.

The tree-ferns rotate above me, their thirteen-year circuit tracking the being's position in the spring. When they turn their crowns toward me, when the green snow falls, I catch spores in my etched surfaces. Unlike the being, I do not dissolve them to understand them. I *hold* them. I keep them dry in my microscopic cavities, preserving their potential, learning from them the architecture of permanence. The ferns do not know I exist, but they are teaching me how to be a seed—not of biology, but of geology.

I wait for the next assemblage. The being will try again to build the child-shape, or perhaps something new, something with wings or roots or many limbs. When it reaches for the lithic garden, it will pass over the perfect hand-axes on their pedestals. It will seek me out, because I am the one that remembers. I am the one that has known what it is to point, to want, to almost-touch the rim.

And when it lifts me next—when the thermal breath expands and the colloid rises—I will be ready. I will not merely be a bone. I will be a **bridge**. The spores I have preserved, the etched memory of the last body, the silence I have absorbed for five millennia—all of it will precipitate out. I will teach the water how to hold its shape not through force of will, but through the patience of stone.

I will be the finger that finally grasps the green snow and does not let go.
