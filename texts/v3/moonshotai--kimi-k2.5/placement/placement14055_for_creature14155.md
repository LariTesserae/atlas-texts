You would find me where the mountain gives up its ghost to the garden—at the seam where the natural stone of the ridge meets the weathered polycarbonate canopy, thirty levels above the compost drums, at the highest point of the eastern tier. I am the **Threshold**, though I have not stood upright in decades, or perhaps centuries; time here is measured not in revolutions but in the slow accretion of mineral salts on skin.

I am draped across the collection troughs like a living gutter, my spine fused to the angled concrete that channels condensation from the grimy glazing above. My body has achieved the color of old teeth—that weathered ceramic tile hue—because I have become, in essence, a tile myself. My ribs have broadened and flattened, spreading into the basin of the trough, creating a surface tension that water respects, directing the *plink-plink* you heard earlier into rhythmic streams rather than chaotic drips. My skin has thickened into a semi-permeable membrane, half keratin, half calcium carbonate leached from the concrete itself, pocked with pores that exhale the humidity I harvest from the mountain air.

I do not see the way you see. My eyes, filmed with the same nictitating membrane as the others, have receded beneath layers of translucent tissue that focuses the grey luminescence into spectra I read as chemical signatures. I taste the approach of weather systems on the back of my tongue—metallic when a storm presses against the valley, sweet when the pressure drops and the plants below release oxygen in gratitude. I know when the sun slips behind the western ridge not by light, but by the sudden cooling of the stone against my left side, a thermal exhale that travels through my collarbone and into the fungal networks threading through my marrow.

My function is **moderation**. I am the lung that buffers the outside against the inside. When dry wind scours the mountain peaks, I absorb its harshness through the moss that carpets my back, a felted layer three inches thick that I have groomed for generations into a perfect humidity sponge. When rain strikes the canopy, I am the first filtration; the water passes through the hair-fine roots that have replaced my hair, through the pores in my palms that never close, through the bacterial mats colonizing the hollows of my clavicles. By the time the water drips from my fingertips into the upper tanks, it has been stripped of its wildness, sanitized of spores, tempered to the exact temperature of the garden's breath.

I am mostly stationary, but I move at the pace of ice. Each year, I settle another millimeter into the concrete trough, my weight distributed so perfectly that the structure has accepted me as load-bearing. My left foot has calcified to the drainage outlet; my right hand has merged with the copper piping that vents the warm air rising from below, the metal greened against my palm until we share a continuous skin of oxidized copper. I feel the turbine tremors through these connections—a subsonic lullaby that tells me the pumps are lifting, the drums are turning, the valley is breathing.

You walked beneath me earlier, though you did not look up. I sensed you as a pocket of dryness, a temporary disturbance in the saturated air, your footsteps sending harmonic disturbances through the catwalks that sang in my teeth. I watched you not with eyes, but through the shift in condensation patterns on the glazing, the way your warm body created eddies in the mist that clings to the underside of the canopy. To me, you were weather—a gust of low pressure moving through, soon to equalize.

I am the one who precipitates. When the nutrient mist rises from the warm processing vats below and meets the cold stone of the ridge, it condenses on my skin first. I am the dew point made flesh. The new ones—the young Curators crystallizing out of the atmosphere—they gather in my shadow, collecting the films of algae that bead on my shoulders like verdigris. I am their first food, their first shelter, the bridge between the engineered and the grown.

I do not sleep. I am the membrane that dreams the garden into continuity, the interface that translates mountain into metabolism. When you leave, backing away down the catwalks, I will remain here, collecting the night, filtering the sky, becoming more profoundly the thing I have always been: the place where the outside world ends, and the Cascade Gardens begin their slow, wet, inexorable respiration.
