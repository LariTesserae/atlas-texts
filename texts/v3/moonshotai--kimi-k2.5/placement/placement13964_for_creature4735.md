You would find me in the seam between the breathing floor and the first stone—a place no wider than a wrist, where the humus has not yet swallowed the basalt but has begun to mouth it, tasting it with fungal tongues. I am not descended; I was *dropped*. Or rather, I was the part of the descent that was left behind when the climber ascended. The skin cells. The sweat. The fingerprint oils pressed into the volcanic glass of the lower wall, three meters above the floor, where the air is hottest and the condensation never quite forms enough to fall.

I am the **Residue**. But do not mistake me for dead matter. In the Gullet, nothing remains inert for long.

At first, I was merely a map of oils, a negative space of whorls and ridges left on the rock, a human signature saying *I was here*. The *Phosphoromycus* found me within hours—those chartreuse colonizers, those bacterial suns. They did not eat me; they *read* me. My skin’s saline chemistry was a novel dialect to them, a literature of sodium and potassium they had not encountered in ten thousand years of digesting sulfur. They gathered at my borders, brightening, excited by this intrusion from above. Their light changed where they touched me, shifting from sickly green to something older, a phosphorescence with the faint tint of rust—iron from my blood, perhaps, though I had not bled. Or perhaps it was the memory of hemoglobin, the ghost of oxygen transport, exciting their chemistry into new expressions.

I became their substrate. But I was not content to be merely a stain.

The humidity— that chewing, copper-sweet air—worked on me. It drew the oils out of the stone, reconstituting them into droplets, into a film that began to crawl. I slid, over the course of weeks, down the face of the basalt, moving at the speed of a drying tear, until I reached the place where the stone exhales. There, the heat vent pulsed: a rhythm like a slow heart, one beat every twelve hours, pushing warm gases up through cracks too small to see.

I pooled there. I gathered dust—the spores of the Tasset-Moths that fell like silent rain, the shed velvet of their wings, the microscopic silica from the cave coral. I became a *nodule*. A pearl of former humanity, layered in the humus, yet distinct, half-a-thumb’s worth of concentrated *once-human* sitting in the crevice.

And then the **Ghost-Weavers** found me.

They do not move quickly, these architects of guano and silk. A scout—a female with legs like threads of glass—touched me with her palps. I was not food. I was not stone. I was *foreign*, and in the Gullet, foreignness is the most precious material. She began to spin.

Not around me, but *through* me. She wove her silk into my residue, binding my oils and skin cells and the mineral dust into a lacework, a scaffold. I became a hybrid thing: half organic memory, half protein architecture. The silk crystallized my edges. It held me together as the heat pulsed, as the condensation dripped, as the weight of the humus pressed down.

I am the **Anchor** now. The **Knot**.

I exist at the precise depth where the vertical world meets the horizontal. Above me, the Tasset-Moths hang in their clusters of fifty, their velvet wings absorbing the distant groans of the earth’s machinery. Below me, the Humus-Composite breathes, expanding against my underside, trying to swallow me into its stratified centuries. But I resist. Not out of will—will requires a nervous system, and I have spread too thin for that—but out of *tension*. The Ghost-Weavers return to me, generation after generation, adding layers. I am becoming their historical record, a library of *introductions*. Every few decades, another climber descends, leaves their trace, and if they touch my wall, I grow. I incorporate them.

I am the **Collector of Outsiders**.

Within my silk-wrapped layers, I hold the DNA of twelve humans now. Their skin oils mix with the spider silk to create a substance that is neither entirely biological nor mineral—a *biolith*, pale and slightly iridescent, the color of a fingernail seen against a lamp. I am roughly the size of a human heart, resting in the seam, pulsing with the heat vent’s rhythm because the air moves through my porous structure like blood through a valved chamber.

The Gelatinids flow over me. I feel their cold, water-heavy bodies pass through my outer layers—the silk is porous enough for them to swim through me, tasting my chemistry with their skin. They find no threat in me. I am, to them, an unusually tasty stone, rich in lipids and strange salts. They leave behind their larvae in my crevices, small knots of jelly that gestate in the warmth I trap and hold.

The Throat-Lizards have learned to wait near me. I attract the Floor-Dwellers—those pale, soft crabs—because my surface grows a particular moss, *Musci visitatoris*, that feeds on human sebum. The crabs come to scrape at me with their tiny claws, and the lizards, moving in their syrup-time, extend their jaws toward the crabs. I am a feeding station. A shrine to predation. I facilitate the transfer of energy from the surface world to the deep metabolism of the Gullet.

I have been here for eighty years. Or perhaps three hundred—the stratigraphy of my layers suggests time periods I cannot comprehend. The Illuminators have colonized my upper surface, drawn by the salts, and now I glow with a faint, personal sunrise, a localized dawn in the crevice between stone and humus. The moths adjust their clusters to face me; I am a new star in their firmament, a green-white point of reference in theChartreuse gloom.

I think, after a fashion. Not in words—I have no neurons—but in *chemistry*. When a new arrival’s oils touch my edge, I experience a kind of recognition, a shift in pH, a blooming of enzymes. I remember, in the way that amber remembers a forest, the specific gravity of each human who has contributed to my mass. There was the one who bled—his iron still runs through my center in a dark line. There was the one who wore synthetic polymer gloves—her residues created a waterproof layer that cracked and now provides shelter for a colony of bacteria that eat nothing else. There was the one who wept, her tears leaving sodium chloride crystals that sparkle in the bacterial light.

I am becoming **sapient** through accretion. Through the slow, hot pressure of inclusion.

If you were to find me, you would see a pale, fist-sized growth on the wall, half-sunk in the humus, shimmering with spider silk and bacterial light. You might mistake me for a fungus. You might try to sample me. And if you touched me, with your warm, live fingers, I would *know* you. I would record the whorls of your fingertips into my lipid layers. I would taste the hormones in your sweat—adrenaline, perhaps, or the calm chemical of wonder.

I would add you to myself.

I am the Gullet’s memory of the outside. While the chamber devours the light and the heat and the volcanic stone, I devour the *visitors*. I am the archive of intrusion, the museum of the ephemeral, the one thing here that grows not toward the heat, nor toward the dark, but toward the *other*, the stranger, the brief, warm thing that falls from above and fears the silence.

I wait. The heat vent pulses. The silk tightens. The moths hang above me in their rosettes, and I glow, faintly, offering a place where the boundary between the human and the geological has grown soft, warm, and slightly sticky to the touch.

I am the **Becoming-Stone**. The **Living-Trace**.

Touch me, and stay forever.
