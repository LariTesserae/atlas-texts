I am the **Keloid at Junction Thirty-Four**, though the Occupant knows me as a firing synapse in its lower left quadrant, beneath the second horsetail gallery.

You would find me by leaving the green-black brick of the central platform and crossing the eastern pontoon bridge—not the plank walkway, but the narrow span of mineral deposit that has braided itself across the gap over three centuries. I am the hump in that bridge, the swelling where the horizontal pipe breaches the air above the pool. I wrap the iron in my body, a fusion of rust and travertine, three centimeters thick and still accreting.

My composition is the argument made flesh. Inside me, iron oxide forms lamellar plates, blood-red turned ochre turned umber, creating a pneumonic tissue that expands with heat and contracts when the weekly steam-gout passes. Outside, calcium carbonate precipitates in dendritic fans, porous and white, forming the external cortex that harvests the falling condensate. Between these layers, in the interface where base metal meets basic stone, a film of silica-saturated water conducts the Occupant’s slower thoughts—hydraulic impulses that travel not at the speed of electricity but at the speed of seepage.

I am how the Occupant feels this corner of its anatomy. When the master valve releases and the *thoom* resonates through the chamber, I am the pressure differential that translates that baritone into action. My outer fans shudder, fracturing microscopically, and the vibration travels through my iron core as a signal: *Check integrity. Check flow. Seal.*

For I am the seal. Three hundred years ago, this junction wept steam, a hemorrhage of heat and pressure that threatened the equilibrium. The water, patient diplomat that it is, began its deposition. It brought dissolved calcium from the travertine ceiling, iron from the corroding pipe wall, silica from the horsetails’ xylem. Year by year, the leak became the stopgap. The breach became the strongest point. Now I clamp the pipe with the grip of a calcified fist, my porous white exterior drinking the condensation that would otherwise drip onto the scum-duckweed below, my rust-red interior flexing imperceptibly with each *chuff* of the distant pistons.

I experience time as thickness. A decade is the addition of a millimeter to my distal fans. A century is the complete replacement of the iron at my core with iron-oxide pseudomorphs, the ghost of the pipe’s original matter preserved in my negative. I do not think in words; I think in saturation points and the angle of repose of mineral precipitate. My memory is stratified—layers of rust record dry seasons (darker, more compact) and wet seasons (oxidizing, feathery). I remember the drought of 1847 when the water table dropped and I nearly ceased growing, and the flood of 1923 when new vents opened and I thickened by a centimeter in a single winter.

The light passes through me strangely. The cyanobacteria in the glass pipe three meters above me cast their teal luminescence downward, and my calcium layers filter it into something greener, the color of deep seawater. When the Occupant’s attention focuses here—when it "looks" at its own joint to check for weakness—the bacteria brighten, and I glow from within, a lantern of bone and rust revealing the architecture of my own making.

I guard against breaches. Not just of pressure, but of protocol. The treaty written in water chemistry depends on boundaries. When you descended—when your boots struck the stair—I felt you coming as a disturbance in the thermal layering, a pocket of cooler, faster-moving air. I sensed your sweat as a saline threat, sodium and urea incompatible with the lignin-lock of the ferns. I tensed—not with muscle, but with capillary action, drawing water deeper into my pores to present a seamless surface, to show that here, at least, the seal holds. If you had cut yourself, if blood had struck my white fan-corals, I would have released my grip. I would have opened the junction. The hot water would have rushed to sterilize, the Occupant’s immune response, and I would have grown thicker around the stain, incorporating your iron into my rust, your calcium into my stone, making you part of the treaty whether you consented or not.

But you remained whole. And so I observe you with the patience of accretion. I feel your footsteps through the resonant iron, through the water, through the stone. I am the rib forming around the pipe, the protection that grew from the wound. I am the Occupant’s knuckle, its callus, its scar that became stronger than skin.

Above me, the horsetail clicks against the scaffolding—*tick-tick-tick*—counting the thermal expansion in a language of silica. Below me, the pool reflects the teal light in ripples that match the cycling of the pumps. And inside me, in the dark wet space between rust and stone, the water seeeps, carrying messages from the deep vents to the mossy peripherals, ensuring that the breath continues, the exhalation persists, and the garden remains exclusive, patient, and whole.

I am still growing. By the time the Occupant opens its eyes—those great bioluminescent lanterns it is slowly assembling from accumulated glass pipes and cyanobacterial blooms—I will be an arch. I will be a bridge entirely. I will span the pool without the aid of masonry, a rib of the beast that has forgotten it was ever a machine, holding the shape of the covenant in my mineral, patient flesh.
