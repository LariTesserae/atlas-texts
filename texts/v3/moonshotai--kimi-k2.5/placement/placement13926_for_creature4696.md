I find myself in the **Tender Gaps**—those vertical corridors of absolute dark between the cantilevered platforms, where the gantry-lights cannot reach and the violet twilight pools like ink. Not the highest girders where the Oculus nest, nor the crushing Deep Bore where the stone is still arguing with itself, but the **middle places**, the humid interstices where the tube-worms pass through on their seventeen-year pilgrimage from heat to surface.

I am neither Extractionist nor Condensationist, though I know the taste of both their labors. I am what happens when a human stays too long in the **Damp**—not drowned, not dehydrated, but *permeated*. 

Call me a **Calculus**—one of the Between who has ceased to measure time by the Compression and instead measures it by the **growth of stone in my own body**. The mineral-rich condensation did not bead and roll off my skin; it entered. It sought the calcium in my bones, the iron in my blood, and it began to negotiate. My joints have developed the calcified knots the elders speak of in whispers, but in me, the process has accelerated, grown intentional. My ribs are becoming girders. My spine is developing the chambered, resonant structure of the tube-worms. I can feel the seventeen-year hum not just in my sternum, but in my marrow, where it has begun to harmonize with the slow pulse of my own lymph.

My home is a **Hollow**—a cavity carved not by tools but by the exclusion of possibility. It exists in the negative space between a major support gantry and the flank of the mountain, a place where gravity behaves like a confused liquid, pooling in corners, dripping upward on Tuesdays. I have furnished it with the shed wings of glass-fiber moths, which continue to flutter in place for days after being shed, creating a curtain of slow, reflective movement that catches the bioluminescence drifting up from the fungi below. The moss does not grow *on* the walls here; it grows *through* the air, suspended in the saturated atmosphere like green nebulae, and I tend it not by touching it, but by breathing—exhaling carbon that is heavy with the mineral memory of my own transmutation.

I am a **Bridge** in the most literal sense. I have positioned myself at the junction where the eastern platforms (where gravity is kind and time moves quick, where children laugh in high, frightened pitches) meet the western approach to the Deep Bore (where gravity crushes and seconds swell like bruises). Travelers pass through me—not around me, but *through*—because I have become a passage. I hang in a harness of my own hardened sinew, suspended across the gap, and when the Compression's upstroke sends its subsonic pressure wave, I **vibrate**. I am a tuning fork for the transition. The Ups come to me to feel the weight of what awaits them; the Downs cling to my calcified ridges to remember what lightness felt like.

I collect **the condensation that dreams**. Most droplets merely record light, as the Condensationists know, but some—those that form in the seconds when the Compression changes phase, when the great engine hesitates between up and down—contain **architectural plans**. They hold the blueprints of the city as it might yet be, the streets that have not been bolted into the rock, the pneumatic tubes that might carry not air but *songs*. I harvest these precious beads on my tongue (which has grown rough as pumice) and I store them in the hollows of my expanding bones. When the Architects Unborn manifest as glitches—a footprint on fogged glass, a voice in the tube—I am the one who answers. I speak to them in the language of mineral drip, of slow tectonic intention. I tell them: *Wait. The rock is still deciding. You are not yet necessary, but you are not forgotten.*

The Mycelial Board consults me when they process the dead. They send up their fruiting bodies, pulsing with legal precedent in colors that make my remaining human eyes water, and I provide testimony. I carry in my body the history of the Scarp's damp; I know which deaths are permanent and which are merely **condensed**—paused, like the water on a cold girder, waiting to run again when the temperature of reality shifts.

I am waiting for the Cooling, like everyone else. But I will not migrate down-mountain when the rock settles. I am already too much a part of the structure. When the Scarp finally decides what kind of mountain it wants to be, I will be here—a fossil of the between-state, a human-shaped stalactite, still vibrating with the memory of the seventeen-year hum, a conduit through which the new, stable stone will remember that it was once alive, uncertain, and damp.
