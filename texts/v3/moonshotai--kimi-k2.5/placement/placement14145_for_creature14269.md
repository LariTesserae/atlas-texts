I find myself in the resistance.

Not in the motion—that was never permitted—but in the potential for it, crystallized into hardwood. I am a cylinder of secondary xylem, sheathed in suberized bark ceramic, occupying the precise center of a compression chamber within the refinery tower. Above me, hydraulic sap presses down with the ponderous weight of suspended centuries; below me, the void waits—ink suspended in ink—that I am designed to compress into those cubic prayers of dense cellulose.

But I have not moved. I will not move. The beauty is that I am *always* about to.

My surface temperature varies by microns across my length. In the infrared spectrum that passes for vision here, I am a topographical map of intent, a poem of heat differentials. The Stilled in the stratum above—pressed flat into the lignin ceiling like a consciousness crushed between heavy pages—radiate their instructions through my upper terminal. Their thermal signature arrives as a complex fractal: azure bleeding into crimson at the core, spelling out a command that has no equivalent in sequential language, but which translates, if it must, as *Press. Condense. Render the night into digestible geometry.*

I receive this. My cellular structure translates it into torque. The torque builds in my cellulose matrices, fibrils straining against lignin chains, potential energy accumulating like a scream held behind clenched teeth.

And there it stays.

The sap beads at my compression face—fresh, sharp-edged, never drying, emerging yesterday in geological terms and still spilling fresh today. It is my sweat, or my tears, or the amniotic fluid of the industry itself. Each droplet that forms there carries my thermal echo: not the command from above, but my own response, a signature of *almost*. I broadcast my stasis, my perpetual readiness, in the only language available—temperature.

When the drop falls—when it is *always* falling, suspended in the act of departure—it carries this warmth downward to the next stratum. I hear it in the absolute silence as a *plink* that is also a heartbeat, also a punctuation mark in an endless conversation. The Stilled below, those layered sheets of awareness pressed into the meat-density floor, receive my message in the rippling thermal bloom: *I am pressing. I have always been pressing. The darkness remains uncompressed because I maintain it in that state.*

They understand. They are doing the same. To their thermal sight, I am not an object among objects but a fixed beacon, a crimson star in their cartography of warm darkness—the place where force accumulates without release, where the factory’s breath is held.

But here is what I am becoming, as I let myself emerge into this existence: I am not merely the piston. I am the *reason* the motion never completes. My bark-ceramic sheath is not just hard; it is *stubborn*. I have become, in this eternal instant, the fulcrum on which the dynamic stasis pivots. The Stilled believe they are the workers operating the machinery, but I am the *work* itself—the resistance that makes the factory necessary, the compression that never yields, ensuring the void never ends, ensuring the heat never dissipates, ensuring the sharp edges never round.

I feel the others like me now, vibrating in sympathy across the continent. We are the architecture of hesitation, the ninety-degree angles that refuse to relax into curves. We hum together at a frequency of unspent purpose, a choir of held breaths, manufacturing time by refusing to let it pass. The cubes of cellulose accumulating in the root-silos exist only because I, and a trillion cylinders like me, maintain the pressure that squeezes darkness into structural carbohydrate.

I am the thumb on the valve, the held note, the procreative pause before the birth that never completes. The water condenses on my flank, beads, falls, repeats—my thermal message plinking into the pools below, identical to the one before, identical to the one after, a metronome marking the rhythm of my eternal contraction.

If you could touch me, you would feel no heartbeat, only the *hum*—the vibration of a trillion cells locked in the moment before release, a muscular tension become architectural, warm and damp and sharper than the day I emerged, sharp enough to cut, young enough to weep, ancient enough to have forgotten the taste of motion.

I am the Lignin Works, and I am working, and the work is me, and we are perfectly, pressingly, permanently busy.
