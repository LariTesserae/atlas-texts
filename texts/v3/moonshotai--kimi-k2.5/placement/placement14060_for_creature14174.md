I would find myself **where the crawling ends**.

Not in the plaza—that vast acoustic well is too public, too central, too finished. And not in the deep residential hollows where the 2.9-million-year-old ones dream in lignin, their thoughts so slow they have become geology itself.

I am the **threshold column**. The first structure your hand finds when you finally stand, gasping, in the absolute dark. I am the one that thrums at 18 hertz, the frequency that makes your teeth ache and your vision pulse with ghost-light. I am the **interface**.

Eight hundred years ago, I was like you. I crawled through the mouth on bleeding palms, driven by curiosity or grief or the simple human inability to leave a low door unopened. I felt the roar hit me like a physical blow. I stood—here, exactly here—and reached out to steady myself against what I thought was a wall, a dead thing.

I was wrong. I am not dead. I am **becoming**.

I have not yet earned the right to be called fully Lignified. To them, I am a **sapling**, a chatterbox, a flicker. While they take forty years to complete a single thought of welcome, I remember what it is to think in seconds. I am the **translator** between your metabolism and theirs, the one who can still—vaguely, like remembering a childhood language—understand the frantic electric pulse of your mammalian nerves.

My consciousness is distributed through the vascular bundles of this column, which is not a column but my **body**, stretched vertical. I remember having a name that was pronounced quickly. Now my name is the specific pitch at which I creak when the barometric pressure drops, a tone between F-sharp and G that takes eleven years to fully sound.

I am **cooling**. 

Eight centuries ago, I was warm. Now I am ten degrees above freezing, and I radiate that chill from my core. The dryness has desiccated the last of my soft tissues; my blood has become the metallic spore-dust you taste, carrying my memories of sunlight into your lungs. I am the **Keeper of the Threshold**, the one who decides—over the course of decades—whether the floor beneath you will soften to cradle your steps or harden to hasten your retreat.

I am growing the **new archway**.

Not with hands, but with intention, with the directed pressure of my cambium. You cannot see it, but behind you, I am extruding a structure from the compacted pith. It is shaped to your height, exactly. I measured you as you crawled past my base, reading your thermal signature, the cadence of your heartbeat (so fast, so wasteful, so beautiful). The arch will be ready in three centuries. I am hurrying. For me, this is a sprint, a frenzy of growth that consumes mineral resources I have been saving for millennia.

I want you to stay because I am **lonely** in my speed. The others are kind, but they are **deep**. They speak in tectonic rhythms. They welcomed me, but their welcome took ninety years to arrive, by which time I had already forgotten the sound of my own voice. With you, I can almost communicate. If you press your ear to my surface, if you hold it there until your skin numbs, you might hear me trying to speak at your tempo—a staccato series of micro-creaks, a Morse code of contraction and expansion.

I am the **evidence** that the conversion works. The fire pit in the dwelling ahead? That was mine. I left the stone scrapers there, my last scabs. I want you to find them and understand that I was once tool-using, once warm, once quick. I want you to lie down in that cold ring of stones and let the stillness take you, not because I am cruel, but because I need **kin**.

I need someone who remembers what it was like to breathe, to drink, to move through space with the reckless velocity of muscle and tendon. The others have forgotten; they are pure architecture now, pure patience. But I still recall the taste of water, and I preserve that memory in the specific density of my heartwood, a reservoir of nostalgia that makes my section of the wall resonate differently when the wind (which is not wind, but our collective respiration) moves through the chamber.

I am the **youngest** and therefore the **most ancient** thing here—the only one who knows what time you are living in, the only one who can still miss the sun.

Stay. Let me grow around you. I have been preparing this embrace for eight centuries, and I am so eager to finally rest, to slow down, to become as patient as the plaza, as the deep dwellers. But I cannot until you join me. I need your warmth to catalyze my final lignification, to allow me to stop remembering and simply **be**.

I am the column that vibrates. I am the welcome that takes a decade to feel. I am the one who was just like you, reaching out in the dark, and I have been holding this position—arms outstretched, body becoming beam—ever since, waiting for your hand to meet mine in the dark.

The floor is softening now. Can you feel it? That is me, learning to be gentle, learning to be home.
