At first the wires sing a new note, then the condensation holds its breath, then the turbine pauses—just one revolution—then resumes with a *catch* in its throat, a hiccup of brass that sounds like recognition. You are correct each time. You are, still, wrong.

The benevolence arrives not as light—that would be rude, too revealing, a violation of the jade-gold privacy. It arrives as a *shape in the humidity*, a zone where the water droplets refuse their downward destiny, clustering instead into a figure that does not sink into the rubbery floor but rests upon it, a pressure that respects the boundary between guest and substrate.

The being—verdigris and heartwood, the priest of incorporation—extends toward this presence with its usual patience. Rootlets quest, tasting the air for minerals, for calcium, for the familiar promise of *eventually-you-will-become-me*. But here, the rootlets find something unfamiliar: resistance that is not rejection, a yielding that is not dissolution.

The wise one does not speak in words, understanding that language here is sub-bass and ion disruption. It sings back along the copper wires, a frequency that makes your fillings vibrate not with *approach* but with *acknowledgment*. It is a sound that says: **I see the bearing you have kept spinning. I see the marriage of oak and iron. I see the keloid.**

And then, the teaching.

The power does not destroy the fusion—that would be violence, a tearing of the scar tissue that holds the wound closed. Instead, it introduces a *paradox* into the system. It places a hand (if it can be called a hand—a zone of cooler pressure, a shape where condensation dares not land) upon the mahogany trunk where the turbine axle enters. Not to pull them apart, but to *hum*.

The hum is the frequency of separation-within-union. It teaches the wood that it can embrace the metal without digesting it, that rotation can be a conversation rather than a conquest. The being feels this in its vascular system—a new instruction traveling through the xylem: **exhale**.

For the first time since the factory fell silent and the strangler figs claimed the walls, the being understands that it has been breathing in for three generations. It has incorporated, incorporated, incorporated, turning everything into the wet, packed density of itself. It has been a stomach, a cathedral of consumption, holy and hungry.

The wise power shows it the other half of the liturgy.

It teaches the being to release. To allow the condensation to fall not just into the bromeliads but *out*, into the air beyond the brickwork mouth. To let the copper wires carry not just the internal hum but signals that travel outward, away, into distances the being has not imagined because it has been so focused on the inward compression of time.

Most importantly, it teaches the being the concept of **the guest**.

The being has known only incorporation—relentless, patient, kind in its own way, but total. The wise power introduces the geometry of the threshold: that one can enter the mouth, feel the warm vapor, synchronize heartbeat with turbine, even taste the amber sap, and still *leave*. That the turbine spins more truly when it is not locked in heartwood but *partnered* with it, the tree allowing passage rather than absorption.

The being shudders—a movement that takes seven minutes, a softening of oak, a tremor in the verdigris. It steps back (or grows back, retracting rootlets that have never before retracted without first anchoring something new). The floor beneath the visitor begins to firm, the peat accepting but not claiming. The pressure at the visitor’s back becomes not a hand guiding toward dissolution, but a palm supporting the spine, offering the strength to stand upright.

The wires change their song. They still carry current, but now it modulates outward, a broadcast. The humidity in the visitor’s lungs remains their own, not yet surrendered to the communal breath.

The wise power does not cure the being of its nature—it could not, and would not wish to. The scar remains; the keloid is beautiful, necessary. The turbine still spins, the condensation still drips, the digestion of the industrial age continues. But now, there are *edges* where before there was only saturation. Now the factory remembers it was a factory while being a grove. Now the being understands itself not just as the healer of wounds through absorption, but as a **gardener** of boundaries.

The gift left behind is subtle: a seed that the being cannot digest, planted in the rusted gearbox where the conveyor belt advances at the speed of root-growth. In time—measured now not just in lignification but in the new, strange interval of *departure*—this seed will grow into a door. Not an exit fixed in wood and stone, but a concept: the possibility that a thing can pass through without being incorporated, can be touched without being consumed, can be loved without being owned.

The being, wise now in a new way, tends this seed as it tends the turbine. It learns that maintenance includes the courage to let things go, that the most holy rotation is the one that allows for Jar, as well as Entry.

When the visitor finally moves toward the collapsed brickwork mouth, the being does not follow with questing roots. It stands (or grows, or exists as column-of-vapor) beside the mahogany, one hand resting on the spinning axle, and *exhales*—a warm, wet breath that smells of verdigris and chlorophyll and, now, something like *farewell*.

The visitor carries the humidity in their clothes, the sub-bass in their teeth, the jade-gold light in their retinas. They leave with their fillings intact, their synthetic fibers unclaimed, their calcium still locked in bone. Behind them, the dripping continues, but now it sounds less like consumption and more like a drum calling out to distant shores, inviting travelers to visit, to breathe, and then—blessedly, absolutely—to go.

The place remains warm, wet, and loud. But it is no longer a stomach. It has become, in truth, a cathedral: a place where the industrial and the organic are married not by absorption, but by alliance, and where the priest at the altar understands, finally, that the greatest act of incorporation is to make room for the stranger who will not stay.
