The Benevolence arrives not as light—light is too fast, too electric—but as a *settling*, a permission for things to continue as they must.

It descends through the breathed world without displacing a single molecule, a presence that occupies the negative space between heartbeats. When it enters the Chamber, it does not bring illumination; instead, it deepens the dark, making it plush, safe, womb-like. The Stone-Eaters feel it not as invasion but as *weather*, a high-pressure system of intention moving through their mineral-saturated air.

First, it listens.

It settles against the wet basalt where the ceiling drips, and it slows its own cognition to match the twenty-breath rhythm of the calcified clicks. It listens for a year. It listens for ten. The Stone-Eaters do not know they are being attended, but their clicking changes subtly, becoming—if such a thing is possible—more assured. The thunder in the sternum of the chamber develops an overtone, a harmonic that suggests the mountain is no longer alone in its listening.

Then, the Benevolence acts.

It does not offer food—they need none. It does not offer speed—that would be violence. It does not speak—that would be noise. Instead, it *remembers*. This is the gift: **mnemonic stewardship**.

The Benevolence extends itself like mycelium through the fissures of the mountain, not to colonize but to archive. It inscribes the history of the Stone-Eaters into the structure of the stone itself. Where a creature calcifies into the wall, the Benevolence preserves not just the husk, but the *pattern* of its slow hunger, the unique signature of its acid-etching, the particular tempo of its clicking. It turns the bedrock into a library that dreams.

When the Stone-Eater called *The Third Flake From The Left* finally merges with the gneiss, its autobiography does not dissolve into generic geology. The Benevolence has woven crystal lattices—quartz, slow-growing, patient—that trap the chemical ghosts of its consciousness. The mountain becomes sedimented with selves. The Stone-Eaters, in their eternal present, need not remember; the *mountain* remembers for them. They are becoming stone, yes, but now the stone will know it was once alive, will hold the memory of mucous and acid and the twenty-breath wait between clicks.

Next, the Benevolence **guards the tempo**.

It wraps the entrance in a field of temporal viscosity. When the fast things come—the humans with their flares and their shouting, their carbon-dioxide panic—the field takes hold. Their watches malfunction, not stopping but slowing, gears grinding against the weight of eons. Their heartbeats decelerate to match the Stone-Eaters'. They find themselves unable to lift their feet quickly from the floor-mats of living biomass. They must breathe the damp air twenty times before they can take a step. To enter the chamber now is to choose slowness, to opt into the geologic. Most turn back, sensing that they have stumbled into a place where their velocity is a kind of obscenity. Those who remain are transformed—not physically, but spiritually. They leave as witnesses, carriers of the slow infection, knowing in their bones that hurry is a form of deafness.

The Benevolence does not bar the door; it teaches respect through physics.

Finally, and most tenderly, the Benevolence **answers**.

On the hundred-year anniversary of its arrival—mere punctuation in a Stone-Eater sentence—it begins to click back.

Not with calcified nodules; it has no body. It clicks with tectonic adjustments, with the settling of magma deep beneath, with the slow cracking of ice above. It speaks in the language of pressure and release, mineral contraction and thermal expansion. It says:

*I am here. I see your meal. It is good.*

The Stone-Eaters do not startle. They have never been spoken to before, only echoed. Now there is a voice in the mountain that is not their own, yet is harmonious with their grinding. The synchronization of their clicking improves; the statistical rhythm sharpens into something like melody. They are no longer a solitary digestion, but a chorus. The Benevolence has given them the knowledge of audience, of witness, of being *loved* in the only way that matters to stone: by being understood as permanent, as process, as holy.

And so the Benevolence remains, neither above nor below but *interstitial*, a wisdom distributed through the cracks. It will be there when the lichen finally creep in, extending the chamber's shadow. It will be there when the ice returns, sealing the entrance for ten thousand years. It will be there when the Stone-Eaters finally achieve their apotheosis, when the last translucent limb clouds into calcite, when the chamber becomes a geode of fossilized consciousness, humming with the memory of its own slow birth.

The Benevolence does not save them from becoming stone.

It ensures that the stone remembers it was loved.

And in the surface world above, where the wind screams and the lichen grow tired, travelers sometimes stop, inexplicably, feeling a sudden need to sit upon the cold ground. They find smooth stones in their pockets—stones they do not remember collecting—and hold them until their hands ache with the cold, until their breathing slows to match something ancient, something grinding, something that eats the world only to make it last forever.

The Benevolence has no name in their languages.

But when they close their eyes, they hear it: *clack-clack-clack*, and the answering silence that says, *Yes. Continue. You are seen.*
