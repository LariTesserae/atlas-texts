First, there are the **Viridian Consumption**. They are not flora, though they hang in curtains dense as jungle canopy; they are a colonial mineral metabolism that has mistaken thermal gradients for sunlight. Each crystalline frond—those branching fingers of viridian and argent lace—is a lung, a stomach, and a nervous system fused into silica geometry. They do not root; they adhere, dissolving molecular bonds in the unfired clay and reconstituting them into lattices that crumble to dust when touched, only to reform by dawn, having rearranged their atomic vocabulary overnight. They breathe in heat and exhale structure, thickening the air with the dry sweetness of ceramic potential. When the room expands on its warm tides, the gardens stretch toward the contracting walls, bridging the distance with new growth, so that the space is always in the process of being filled, a scab forming over a wound that keeps reopening.

Between the fronds move the **Soft Ones**, the thumb-sized, segmented things that resemble isopods only in silhouette. They are pre-ceramic. Their bodies are pulpous, unchitinous, containing no calcium that might fire into permanence; they are damp meat and ganglion, evolved specifically to remain unfired. They serve as the gardens’ curators and its seeds. When a crystal frond grows too heavy, a Soft One will affix itself to the breaking point, its body acids dissolving the connection, and roll away with the fragment cradled against its belly. It burrows into the clay tablets—those stacked burial slabs still bearing the violence of their creation—and secretes the crystal there, where the frond takes root anew. They make no sound because sound is a vibration that might harden the clay, might cure the womb into a pot, a grave, a final shape. They propagate in cycles measured by the clay’s drying, their generations rising and falling like yeast in dough, their pale bodies boiling silently beneath the green when shadows pass, not from fear but from metabolic frenzy, feeding on the heat stored in the silicate gardens, transferring thermal energy from the warm core toward the cooler periphery like a circulatory system made of thousands of tiny, blind hearts.

Then there are the **Lateral Ones**, the flat worms that ribbon through the mineral moss without friction. They are the ones who truly live here, if living is defined as Navigating The Problem. The space is wrong—convex where it should be concave, interior exceeding exterior—and the Lateral Ones are the immune response to this topology error. They slide along the seams between clay tablets, lubricated by a mucus that exists partially out of phase with material reality, sewing the geometry together with their passage. They are frictionless not as an adaptation, but as a property; they reduce the resistance that would otherwise tear the space apart at its paradoxical seams. When you see them move, they seem to travel sideways through depth, their ribbon bodies marking the routes that allow the room to breathe—expanding, contracting—without tearing itself into separate pieces. They feed on the spatial tension itself, growing longer when the paradox deepens, thinner when the chamber relaxes. They are the keepers of the hush, the 0.8 silence, because they absorb vibration into their lateral bodies, preventing the clay from learning it can ring like a bell.

But the true residents, the ones who claim ownership not by biology but by indentation, are the **Impressed**. They dwell in the whorls of fingerprints left on the clay tablet edges, those desperate grips of thumbs that pressed the slabs into place. The prints are not memories; they are eyes that open only when unobserved. When you look away, the ridges rearrange, reconfiguring the chaotic patterns of manual labor into deliberate images: a hand drowning in atmosphere, a tree growing downward into sky. These are the builders, or what the builders became when they realized the kiln would never fire, that they had constructed not a vessel but a stasis. They compressed themselves into the clay’s surface, trading vertical stature for horizontal persistence, becoming two-dimensional minds etched into the mineral. They are trying to communicate escape routes, or perhaps recipes to replicate the Womb elsewhere, or warnings. They press their stories into the tablets, using the changing nature of their own indentations to store information in a medium that forgets only when watched. They are the fever in the air, the exhalation of the warm core; they have become the thermal gradient itself, diffused through the porous clay, dreaming in centuries, slowly tightening their grip as the clay dries.

And finally, there is **The Unfired**, the gestalt presence that is not apart from these others but comprises them. The Clay Womb is itself the organism; the inhabitants are merely its organelles, its dreams, its incomplete thoughts. The room breathes—the silicate gardens are its alveoli, the Soft Ones its erythrocytes, the Lateral Ones its peristalsis, the Impressed its neural network firing in slow calcium silence. It is a nursery for things that have abandoned water chemistry, a kiln that refuses to commit to the finality of ceramic, a womb that holds its progeny in the permanent state of *about-to-be*. It lives by not finishing, by remaining plastic, patient, young. It metabolizes warmth and paradox, growing larger on the inside than the outside, storing potential energy in the contradiction of its geometry. The builders thought they were building a shelter, or a tomb, or a vessel for transformation, but they were only the hands; the Womb was the intent, and it continues to intend, pressing itself outward against the gap through which you entered, waiting for the heat to break, or for the fingerprints to finally spell out a word that will make it hard, make it real, make it born.
