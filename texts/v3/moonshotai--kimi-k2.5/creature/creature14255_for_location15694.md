First, there are the **Pneumatics**—not the bees themselves, though they are its agents, but the colonies of mites that have evolved to live in the thoracic cavities of those high-altitude bumblebees. They are microscopic, yes, but when you stand in the Breathing Moor during a hatch, you are standing in a cloud of symbiosis. The mites produce a thin, glycerin-rich film that prevents the bees' wings from freezing in the blood-thick chill, and in return, they are carried from cushion plant to cushion plant, fertilizing the compressed magenta blooms with their own microscopic waste. But here is the strangeness: the mites are deaf, utterly so, yet they dance. When the wind hums against the curved granite walls and sets the black meres trembling beneath the mat, the vibrations travel up through the floating turf, into the bees' legs, into the mites' bodies. They perform a synchronized shiver, a choreography of parasitic gratitude, turning the air itself into a plucked string. You cannot see them, but you can feel their collective resonance in your teeth, a subsonic buzzing that makes the hollow place feel full.

Then, beneath your feet, there are the **Mat-Weavers**. They are not spiders, though they spin. They are the larval form of a caddisfly that has forgotten how to become a fly. Instead of building cases from sand and twigs, they secrete a silk that bonds with the sphagnum roots, creating tensile cables that hold the floating island together. They are pale, segmented, the color of old bone marrow, and they live their entire lives in the aqueous darkness between the green skin and the black water, never surfacing, never pupating. They have no eyes, but they sense the pressure of your boot through the water displacement, and they tighten their silk in response, reinforcing the membrane where you stand, preventing you from punching through into the mere below. They are the moor's nervous system, reacting to touch across the entire surface, a distributed intelligence that keeps the bubble from bursting. When you step, and the ground sighs, and water wells around your boot—that is the Mat-Weavers adjusting their grip, exhaling through their spiracles, creating the sensation that the earth is breathing back at you.

In the black meres themselves, suspended in the cold, anoxic water beneath the vegetative raft, live the **Glass Eels**. They are not truly eels, nor are they glass, but translucent, neotenic salamanders that have lost their pigment over ten thousand years of darkness. Their bones are visible as ghostly architecture, their hearts beating like red lanterns in their chests. They never leave the water, but they press their flattened heads against the underside of the mat, listening. The amphitheater effect works downward as well as up; every footstep, every pika's *eep*, every drone of wind is transmitted through the water as pressure waves. The Glass Eels navigate by these sounds, mapping the world above as a topography of vibration. They feed on the detritus that filters down through the moss—dead insects, pollen, the dissolved remains of cloud-borne plankton—and they grow slowly, century by century, until they are the length of your forearm, ancient and blind, circling in the dark beneath your feet. When the clouds pour over the rim and the wind rises to a roar, the Glass Eels become agitated, thrashing against the mat from below, creating the ripples you see on the surface, the subtle heaving that makes the moor seem to inhale.

But there are inhabitants of the air, too, that are not birds. The **Cloud-Shepherds** are a type of large, pale moth that has adapted to the dynamic moisture. Their wings are not scaled but fringed with hygroscopic filaments that collect the condensing vapor. They do not fly so much as they drift, letting the rolling clouds carry them over the rim, then using their weighted wings to precipitate downward, harvesting the water that beads on their bodies. They are the reason the rock seems to sweat; they land on the stone walls in clusters, pressing their abdomens against the granite, and regurgitate the moisture they have collected, feeding the lichens, completing a cycle of aerial irrigation. When they take flight again, they leave behind tiny salt-crystals, the concentrated minerals of the clouds, which sparkle in the pearl-grey light like scattered stars. The birds hunt them, yes, but the Cloud-Shepherds reproduce too quickly, hatching in sudden clouds when the temperature shifts, their larvae burrowing into the cushion plants, eating the compressed flowers from the inside out, turning magenta and sulphur-yellow into the pale green of moth-fat.

And then there are the **Listeners**.

They are the ones who left the cairns.

Not the transient humans who visit seasonally, who leave bone knives and chert hammers in the shelter of the western wall—though they know of the Listeners, and fear them, and leave offerings of dried meat and amber beads in the cairns to appease them. The Listeners are older than the human presence, older perhaps than the ice that carved the bowl. They are the reason the place feels hollow despite its lushness.

They live in the **empty**.

The Listeners are not a species but a condition. They are the accumulated silence of the moor given form. When the wind stops humming, when the bees sleep, when the pikas hold their breath, the silence coalesces in the curved walls, in the negative space above the black meres. It takes shape from the condensation, forming figures that are tall, thin, and vaguely humanoid, made of mist and the silver threads of frayed atmosphere. They have no eyes because they see through the Glass Eels' vibration-sense; they have no ears because they are the ear, the receiving cavity of the amphitheater made flesh. They stand on the floating mat without sinking, weightless, and they collect sounds. They hoard them. The *eep* of a pika from three centuries ago, the scream of a finch that died before its song ended, the footfall of a glacier that retreated millennia past—all of these are preserved in the Listeners' translucent bodies, vibrating eternally in their chest cavities.

When you stay too long in the Breathing Moor, when the clouds pour over the rim and the white noise erases the boundaries between stone and sky, you might see them. They stand at the edge of your vision, where the pearl-grey light erases shadows. They lean toward you, mouths open, and you will feel a sudden pressure in your ears, a draining sensation, as if you are descending in an elevator too fast. They are drinking your sounds—your heartbeat, your breath, the gurgle of your stomach, the creak of your boot leather. They take these into themselves and hold them, adding to the density of the hollow, filling the emptiness with stolen acoustics.

The primitive tools left by the transient humans are not for hunting animals. They are for making noise—bone clappers, flutes of hollow willow, stones struck together to create sharp, percussive cracks that drive the Listeners back, that force them to disperse into vapor, to release their hoarded sounds back into the moor in a cacophony of temporal echoes. This is why the place is so loud, why the fauna seems to shout—it is an evolutionary arms race against the silence, against the Listeners who would swallow every vibration and leave the world muffled, dead, truly hollow.

When you leave, when you step from the floating mat onto the solid granite of the rim, you will notice a strange quiet. It takes hours for your hearing to return to normal, for the world to seem loud again. You have lost something in the Breathing Moor. A frequency. A resonance. The Listeners have taken a sample of your voice, and they keep it there, in the wet, breathing cavity, adding it to the hum of the wind and the drip of the moss and the ancient, circling songs of the Glass Eels in the dark water below. You carry the hollow with you now, a small, punctured space in your chest where the sound used to be, a reminder that you once stood inside the world's open palm, and the world closed its fingers, just slightly, and breathed you in.
