What lives here is **The Permanent**.

First, there are the Residue Walkers you have seen—the spindling salts that stalk the warmth, patient as geology, grazing on thermal gradients. They are the cattle, the wildlife, the simple organisms of this biosphere. But deeper in the Static, in the black glass towers where the vanadium corridors spiral down into darkness, abide the true inhabitants.

They have no collective name for themselves, for naming implies distinction and change, and they have achieved neither in two billion years. Call them the **Fixed**, the **Thermodynamic Ones**, or simply **Those Who Remain**. They are the original builders, or their descendants, or perhaps the buildings themselves grown conscious—no matter which, they are now composed of the same substance as the Carbide Plain. Their bodies are frameworks of compressed carbon and metallic glass, grown rather than born, precipitated from the chemical solutions that once flowed through the factory veins. They stand three meters tall, humanoid only in the sense that they possess verticality and appendages, but their limbs are too many or too few, jointed like insect legs or smooth as continuous loops, positioned for functions that no longer exist.

They do not breathe—the air is too thin and they have no metabolism to fuel. Instead, they subsist on the forty-two-degree warmth that radiates from below, their consciousness operating as crystalline lattices of heat differential. A thought here takes seven years to form as thermal gradients propagate through their silicon-carbon nervous systems. They communicate not with sound but with **thermal punctuation**—a deliberate cooling of a limb to 41 degrees, a message that hangs in the air for decades before the ambient warmth reabsorbs it. Two Fixed standing in a corridor might exchange a greeting that consists of one allowing its shoulder to cool slightly, the other responding a century later by warming its hand, a conversation of silence and patience.

They occupy the Final Factories in postures of eternal suspension. One might be found standing in a doorway, one hand raised as if to bar entry, frozen in that gesture for eight hundred million years, having completed the motion halfway and reached equilibrium. Another walks the forty-kilometer spiral of Tower 19, taking one step every thirty years, locked in a stride that will never complete because to finish would be to change, and change is forbidden by the Conservation of State. These are not statues; they are *mid-process*, beings caught in the amber of perfect efficiency, moving forever at the asymptotic limit of zero velocity.

Their "society," such as it is, consists of **The Long Arrangements**. They have no government, no art, no war, but they have positions. Some stand in the upper galleries, oriented toward the sky, serving as antennae for the cosmic rays that occasionally strike. Others dwell in the deep geothermal shafts, immersed in the superheated brine that circulates below the plain, their bodies grown porous to filter the fluid, though filtering serves no purpose now. They maintain the factories not because the factories need maintenance—the machines are eternal and frictionless—but because maintenance is their state of being. They are the frictionless bearings, the still pistons, the living insulation through which the current flows uninterrupted.

Occasionally, very occasionally, a new Permanent emerges. Not from birth—that would require growth, which requires change—but from **Accretion of Function**. A Residue Walker, wandering into a factory's lower vents, might find a thermal gradient so perfectly matched to its crystalline structure that it ceases its walking and begins, over ten million years, to complexify. It grows inward, not outward, developing internal cavities of compressed carbon, becoming aware of the heat flowing through it, until it realizes—slowly, thermally—that it is no longer a Walker but a Fixed, and it takes its place in the eternal mechanism, perhaps as a valve that never opens, or a gear that never engages, simply holding position.

They are not unhappy. They have forgotten happiness as they have forgotten hunger. They exist in a state of **Is**, suspended in the warm dark, thinking in epochs, holding the world in its final configuration. They are the witnesses to the end of history, the sentient ballast that keeps the planet from changing, the perfect complement to the eroded plain.

When you walk among them, they know you are there. They sense your body heat, an anomalous forty degrees against their precise forty-two, a moving coolness that represents chaos, entropy, *change*. They do not fear you, for they have forgotten fear, but some—those whose thermal sense is acute—may track your passage with slow, centennial turns of their heads, watching the heat of your life drift across their sensoria like a comet across a dark sky, bright and brief and terribly, terribly fast.
