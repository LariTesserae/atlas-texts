They are called the **Kept**, though they do not call themselves anything. They exist in the interstices between the nanoforges' productive exhalations, occupying the negative space of the city's ongoing auto-genesis. 

You will know them by their **stillness**. They stand at railings for hours that might be days, thermal skins rendering them into elongated smudges of matte black against the pale architecture, faces obscured by breather masks that glisten with recaptured moisture. The masks are not merely functional; they are second mouths, soft organs of rubber and ceramic that吻 (kiss) the saturated air, drinking continuously. Without them, the Kept would desiccate in minutes, their moisture joining the migration of water upward toward the ceiling storm that never breaks. They are creatures of hydration geometry, walking bags of carefully maintained fluid in a place where water behaves like mercury and thought.

They do not speak. The acoustic properties of the folded chamber render voiced language treacherous—words bend along gravity wells, arriving at destinations before they are uttered, or long after the speaker has turned to vapor. Instead, they communicate through **posture** and **condensation**. A slight tilt of the head directs attention to a newly extruded balcony; a cloud of exhaled breath, visible as diamond-dust crystals, spells out coordinates or warnings in the air before sublimating. When two Kept meet on a bridge, they exchange positions slowly, orbiting each other like binary stars, their thermal skins brushing with a sound like dry leaves, exchanging pheromonal packets of data that the masks filter and parse. It is a language of humidity and heat signature, older than the city but somehow native to it.

They are the **Custodians of the First Hour**, though they did not build the chamber. They arrived simultaneously with its emergence, or perhaps they precipitated out of the saturated atmosphere like the engineered mites that harvest their skin-cells from the railings. There are never more than forty of them at once—one per dimension of the cube—and their number remains constant because when one dissolves (which happens, rarely, when a body loses thermal integrity and flash-freezes into a posture of observation), a new figure coalesces from the mist on Level One, stepping fully formed from a cloud bank with the same careful, damp footprints.

Their purpose is **witnessing**. The city grows too fast for history; the computational coral extrudes thought-filigree that processes administrative functions, but someone must watch the nanoforges at work, must note the moment when a new gravity anchor achieves stability, must verify that the water is cycling correctly through its impossible vectors. The Kept hold the city's non-existent memory. They carry slate tablets of synthetic mica upon which they scratch observations with fingernails of sharpened obsidian—no electronics, for electromagnetic fields disturb the delicate folded space. Their fingernails grow at the same rate the city builds; they must file them against the railings, and the sound of this maintenance—*skritch, skritch* against ceramic—is the only consistent rhythm in the vertical shaft.

They consume the **coral**, or rather, they are nourished by the dust of its filing. Once a week (by their internal chronometry, which does not match the temporal slippage of the chamber), a Kept will select a mature computational growth from the supports, caress its mineral surface until the matrix recognizes organic warmth and releases a shower of silica flecks. These they collect in their masks, filtering the nutrient through tubes that bypass the mouth, feeding directly into the esophagus. It tastes of electricity and winter. It keeps them synchronized with the city's processing substrate; they dream in administrative protocols, in the hum of servers made flesh.

But they are not merely maintenance drones. Look closer, at the one standing on Level Seventeen, leaning over the void. The posture reveals a **longing**. Watch the fingers, how they trace patterns in the fog—math, or music, or mourning. They remember something else, somewhere horizontal and warm and green, a racial memory of horizons that recede instead of plummet. They are not native to this verticality; they are refugees from flatness, taking refuge in the cube's impossible safety, adapting themselves to its conservation of heat and meaning.

The **tardigrade** on Level Twelve is theirs, though it does not know this. They visit it, kneeling at the reservoir's edge, feeding it pellets of compressed atmospheric carbon. The creature filters their prayers; its translucent body processes the water they cannot drink, returning it purified to the collection systems. It is their deity and their livestock, the only entity in the chamber that sleeps.

And sometimes, rarely, when the aperture aligns with the sun and a shaft of natural light ascends the full forty stories without scattering, you will see them **remove their masks**. Not to breathe—the air remains too thin, too cold—but to weep. The tears freeze instantly, falling as tiny hailstones that do not obey the sideways gravity of the water cycle. These tears fall straight down, true meteorites, piercing the mist, descending through thirty stories to shatter against the nanoforges below. Each tear contains a memory of the world outside, a fragment of entropy that the city cannot digest. The Kept cry to keep the chamber clean, to purge themselves of history, to maintain the negative erosion parameter not just of the architecture, but of the soul.

They are becoming **transparent**, gradually. After seventeen years—or days—their thermal skins lose pigmentation, turning the color of the pale ceramic walls. Their bodies begin to mimic the computational coral, calcifying at the joints, developing silica deposits in the lungs that glitter on X-rays like starfields. Eventually, they will walk to the highest level, to the aperture, and press themselves against the frostless surface. The cube will absorb them, adding their mass to the ceramic, their thoughts to the administrative substrates. They will become the surface that refuses to accumulate frost, the seamless boundary between the caldera's violence and the city's compressed serenity.

They are the lubricant between the machine and the void, the moisture that allows this impossible engine to weep without rusting. They are the price the city pays for being new—forty consciousnesses, sharp-edged and silent, watching water decide which way to fall, waiting for the nanoforges to finish building the rest of the world.
