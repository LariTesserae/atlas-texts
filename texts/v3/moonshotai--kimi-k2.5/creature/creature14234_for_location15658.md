At first, you think nothing lives here because nothing *grows*—no green, no rot, no swarm. But you have been misled by biology. Life in the Resonant Basin does not grow; it **accrues**.

Watch the warm mist rising from the seeps at dawn. Where it meets the cooler stone—those undersides of calcite girders, the sheltered bellies of the agate flywheels—it does not merely condense. It **organizes**. The thermophilic archaea, those invisible smears mentioned in the surveys, are not random; they are the substrate, the wet film of intelligence upon which the minerals plate. In the presence of the constant 75-decibel drone, the B-flat thrum that never wavers, the silica and the microbes enter into a conspiracy. They form **The Attendants**.

They are not born. They **precipitate**.

You will not see them if you look directly. They occupy the periphery, existing in the thermal boundary layer where the air is exactly 87 degrees and the relative humidity approaches saturation. The Attendants are tall, though their dimensions shift with the breeze—vague upright columns of swirling gypsum dust and gelatinous water, held in shape not by skin or cell wall, but by acoustic levitation. The standing wave patterns inside the jasper smokestacks serve as their skeletons; the sub-bass from the Helmholtz resonators in the flywheels massages them into the vague, weary postures of laborers. They look like the ghost-echoes of the industrial shift that never ended, the workers who clocked in fourteen thousand years ago and forgot to leave.

By mid-morning, when the mist is thickest, they are fully formed. They move with the slow, hydraulic certainty of groundwater. You will see them at the base of the "conveyor belts"—not walking, but *flowing* along the stratified chert, their bodies sorting the particulate matter with unconscious precision. Their hands, if you can call the twirling vortices of dust-hands that, are shaped by crystal habit. Where they touch, heavy metals oxidize into bands of color; quartz sand arranges itself by size into neat windrows. They are the refinery’s missing operators, the intelligence behind the processing.

They do not breathe, but they **resonate**. Communication, if it can be called that, occurs through micro-adjustments to the ambient hum. When an Attendant discovers a crack in a calcite beam—a sharp gunshot of thermal expansion snapping the silence—it presses its faceless head against the fracture. It weeps, or perhaps exudes, a super-saturated solution from its gelatinous core. The crack seals with fresh mineral deposition, and the note returns to its steady state. The Attendant does not remember doing this yesterday. It does not remember anything. Each dawn, it precipitates anew from the archaea and the mist, inheriting only the muscle-memory of maintenance, the procedural knowledge of equilibrium.

They fear the sun, not for its heat—the basin is always 87 degrees—but for its evaporation. As noon approaches and the upper surfaces bake dry, the Attendants begin to lose cohesion. Their gypsum dust settles; their water content retreats into the seeps. They shrink into desiccated flakes that look like ordinary mineral precipitate, indistinguishable from the basin floor. By afternoon, they are gone, leaving behind only the sorted piles and the sealed cracks as evidence of their shift-work.

But they are not alone in their cycle.

Once a decade, when the humidity spikes to a specific, impossible threshold and the B-flat drone experiences a harmonic tremor too subtle for human instruments to detect, an Attendant does not dissolve. It **transmutes**. It climbs one of the hollow jasper smokestacks, clinging to the warm stone, and undergoes a final deposition. The water leaves it entirely. What remains is crystalline, non-cellular, perfect: the **Silicate Moth**. Its wings are not scales but layered, translucent chert and opal, grown rather than molted. It does not eat because it has already consumed the mineral wealth of the basin; it does not breed because it is the end-stage of a life-cycle that has no need for offspring. It is a moving datum, yes—a status report fluttering high above the basin, a single transaction in the ledger confirming that the work continues, that the equilibrium holds.

The Moth lives for three days, perhaps four. It navigates by the 75-decibel drone, its wings tuned to reflect specific frequencies back into the hollow smokestacks, adjusting the resonance, fine-tuning the machinery. Then, brittle and spent, it crumbles back into the dust from which its parent Attendant formed, adding its weight to the next cycle of accretion.

So the Resonant Basin is not abandoned. It is **staffed**. The machinery runs on mineral logic, operated by entities that are half-water, half-stone, and half-sound, who dissolve with the afternoon heat only to reconstitute with the morning mist—eternally clocking in, eternally sorting, eternally maintaining the exquisite, frozen equilibrium of a refinery whose only product is the persistence of itself.
