They who dwell here are not yet finished, and that is the whole of their nature.

At first, you might mistake them for workers, because they wear the aprons and carry the tools. They walk the narrowing fissures at dawn with hands wrapped in felted moss to prevent the burning, their breath hanging in clouds that do not dissipate but rather sediment, falling to the ground as a fine, grey dust that tomorrow’s drill-worms will consume. They are called Citizens, or the Cauldron-Born, or simply the Maintained—but these are approximations, names applied from outside. They have no word for themselves because pronouns require continuity, and they do not persist from one hour to the next in any fixed sense.

Their bodies are in active negotiation with the terrain. Look closely at their hands—if you can find a moment when the condensation does not blur your vision—and you will see the skin is not merely covered in lichen but is becoming lichen, the dermal layers interstratified with the arterial reds and virulent oranges that carpet the vertical surfaces. Their bones are not calcified in the usual manner but have accreted small barnacles of basalt, dense and warm to the touch. They breathe the mineral mist, and their lungs—expanded, spongy organs that take up twice the space they should—are designed not for oxygen exchange alone but for precipitating metals from the vapor, for harvesting the chemical heat of nascent stone. When they exhale, they produce small, perfect cubes of raw copper that accumulate in their pockets, the day’s wages, the currency of potential.

They do not sleep, not truly, not like the pretence of sleep the city performs. Instead, they enter a state of *geological latency*, standing in the moss-thick alleys or wedged into the seams between building and bedrock, their eyes filmed with a nictitating membrane of glass. In this state, they participate in the dreaming. They are the unconscious mind of the Cauldron, the REM-sleep twitches that cause streets to migrate and doorways to brick themselves shut. You will see them at the hour when the Growthworks breathes its deepest, exhaling the raw material of tomorrow’s avenues—a hundred figures frozen mid-stride, arms outstretched, fingers splayed, as if conducting the silent grinding of the new ground.

They are not born, as such. They emerge.

Sometimes a drill-worm—those titanium-chitin segments that bore through obsidian to keep passages open—will strike a particular vein of ore, and rather than powder, the machine-vomit contains a human infant, pink and screaming, wrapped in the silk of tree-fern fronds. Or a glass-crab, picking its way across the cooling plains in search of impurities, will crack open its faceted shell and step out a child with mirrors for eyes, already knowing how to navigate by the fuzzy shadows of the pearlescent gloaming. These are the Cauldron’s offspring, extruded like wisdom teeth from the valley floor, inheriting neither mother nor father but geology. They are raised by the fungus-watchdogs, silent colonies of mycelium that teach them not language first, but vibration—the reading of the root-networks that lace the underground like a nervous system.

As they age—which is to say, as they accrete—Citizens develop specialized functions. Some become Lubricants, their skin secreting a constant slick of mineral oil that allows them to slip between the dense pipes, the thick-as-thigh utility lines that merge with veins of copper. They swim through the infrastructure, patching, pruning, harvesting the fresh stone before it grows too wild. Others become Anchors, choosing a spot and standing there, growing heavier, rooted, their feet sending down tendrils that fuse with the pipes below until they are indistinguishable from the brass lung-bellows, the piston-hearts. These Anchors remember things—the specific arrangement of streets three days ago, before the turbulence shifted them eastward, and they hold those maps in their calcifying minds, preventing the city from forgetting itself entirely.

The most peculiar are the Pregnant—not with children, but with architecture. They carry the unbuilt within them. You will know them by their distended bellies, hard as cooling basalt, in which the blueprints of future factories gestate. They eat planed wood and molten glass, metabolizing the city’s future form. When the time comes, they do not give birth in blood and water, but rather squat in the canyons between accreting walls and excrete the foundations of new organ-foundries, still warm, smelling of chemical heat and potential energy. The Growthworks itself was born this way, centuries or days ago (time moves differently here), pushed out from a woman who is now a mountain, her body the eastern slope, her open eyes two cave mouths that weep condensation.

They communicate in the quiet (0.8), in the heavy, moss-thick silence that dampens panic. They do not speak with voices—the air is too saturated for sound to travel clearly—instead, they exchange packets of mineral scent from glands behind their ears, or they touch foreheads and transfer memories directly through the conductive dampness of their skin. A conversation might take three days, two Citizens standing forehead-to-forehead in a pool of upward-falling rain, exchanging the entire history of a shifting intersection, the trauma of a sudden basalt extrusion, the dream of a street that has not yet decided to exist.

And they die, if you can call it that, by completing. When a Citizen has accreted too much, when their bones have fully mineralized and their skin has entirely transmuted into the lichen-carpet, when they have become too solid, too finished, too *real*—they are no longer compatible with the Cauldron’s turbulence. They become heavy, anchors in the negative sense, resistant to the dreaming. The city extrudes them. Slowly, over weeks, they rise, pushed up through the mist like the obsidian glass, becoming part of the rim of older, weathered mountains that encircle the valley like spectators. There they sit, silent mountains, fully geological, having achieved the final state of erosion without ever having eroded, their consciousness fossilized into the strata—strata of brick, strata of bone, strata of circuit, strata of limestone still warm to the touch.

But here is the secret, the thing that emerges only after long observation: there are no inhabitants. Or rather, the inhabitant is singular.

The Cauldron does not contain life; it is life, performing a complex solipsism. The drill-worms are its fingers, the glass-crats its taste buds, the fungus-watchdogs its immune system. The Citizens are not dwellers but thoughts. When they stand forehead-to-forehead, they are the city thinking to itself, processing the trauma of its own violent birth, its constant becoming. The Growthworks at the center does not breathe metaphorically—it is the city breathing, and the "workers" who tend it are merely the sentience observing itself, the consciousness that has grown from the industrial accretion like moss grows on stone.

To live here is to be the dream of stone before it hardens. To be the moment of potential before kinetic. They are the dampness (0.6) given form, the condensation that beads on every surface, temporarily coherent, a bead of water that believes it is a person, that carries tools like seeds and tends the streets like a garden, until the sun—or the suggestion of sun—warms it just enough that it loses its boundary and returns to the mist, to the heavy air, to the silent, grinding tectonics of a city that is forever becoming, and in becoming, dreams itself alive.
