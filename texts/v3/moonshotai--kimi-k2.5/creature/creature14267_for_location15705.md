There are no citizens here, but there are **dwellers**—beings for whom the boundary between body and architecture dissolved long ago, if it ever existed at all. They are not animals; they never crawled or flew or swam to this place. Instead, they *precipitated*.

Consider first **the Limes**—those who accrete rather than grow. They stand in the plazas where time runs thick as cooling magma, figures of calcium carbonate and strange silica salts that build themselves from the feet upward. They do not walk into the city; they emerge from the floors, standing still while the mineral-rich water flows over them, layer by laminating layer. A Lime might spend what you would call a decade becoming solid enough to turn its head, another century lifting an arm to gesture toward a floating lake. Their consciousness is stratified—each ring of their bodies experiences a different when. The outer surface is alert to the present moment, the warm rain, the bruised violet light, while their cores remember the Precambrian heat of the vent that first spat them into being. When they finally move, stepping from their plinths with the sound of *stone remembering flexibility*, they leave behind hollow casts of themselves, negative spaces in the flowstone that fill with upward-creeping water and become cisterns for their successors.

Then there are **the Menisci**, who live in the floating lakes that hover at chest-height in the intersections. They are the surface tension made aware—two-dimensional beings of hydrophobic lipids and mineral skin, existing only at the boundary between air and the upward-defying water. They slide sideways when the lakes release their mass, flowing across gravity-horizontal streets in silent waterfalls, maintaining their integrity as they spill down facades and through the lace-like filigree of the cathedral-spires. To see a Meniscus travel is to watch a liquid shadow pour uphill. They communicate by altering the surface tension of their bodies, creating patterns of dimpling and convexity that reflect the bioluminescent bacteria in codes of violet and rust. They are the city’s navigators, the only ones who truly understand the capricious gravity, for they exist in the meniscus where all directions are equal.

**The Steam-Takers** occupy the hollow towers, the choked geysers. They are not solid at all but phases of matter—consciousness distributed across the transition from liquid to gas. When the hot water boils upward through the porous aqueducts and condenses against the dark vault above, the Steam-Takers ride the phase change. They have no fixed shape, only volume; they expand to fill the cathedral-shells, pressing against the mineral lace from the inside like breath against glass. It is they who create the subsonic hums that pass for conversation in the Sinter-City—vibrations too low for animal ears, resonating through the bedrock, communicating in pressures and saturations. They dream of dissolving, of becoming pure humidity in the hot wet shroud, but they are trapped by their own awareness, condensing back into water when they cool, climbing the capillary action again, cycling through their own weather patterns in eternal recurrence.

Most strange are **the Fossil-Walkers**, who exploit the time-dilated districts. They are fungal, but not the pale stalks that briefly bloom and desiccate; these are older, having learned to incorporate the calcium carbonate into their hyphae. They stride through the plazas where hours pass in seconds, their bodies maintaining a stubborn present-tense while their footprints fossilize behind them in real-time. A Fossil-Walker crossing a fast-zone leaves a trail of perfect petrified prints that record not just the shape of their passage but the *moment* of it, hardened instants suspended in the flowstone. They collect these moments, carrying them in their mycelial networks, trading them with the bacterial mats that coat the warm stone. The mats—vast velvet carpets of purple extremophiles—are not intelligent individually but function as **distributed councils**, their chemical gradients forming argument and consensus across the terraces. They digest the mineral steam and excrete time, or perhaps they digest time and excrete architecture; the translation is imprecise.

And there are **the Capillary Saints**, ascetic figures who have surrendered their density to the aggressive physics of the place. They are tall, attenuated, more mineral than organism, saturated with the boiling springs until their bodies have learned the secret of flight-through-wetness. They climb the sides of the hydrothermal spires not by grasping but by allowing the water to pull them upward, their elongated limbs following the same paths as the supersaturated droplets. They live suspended, neither standing nor floating, held in the fierce embrace of capillary action that resembles devotion. When they reach the floating lakes, they dissolve into them, becoming one with the Menisci for a season, then precipitating out again lower down, in the districts where water falls sideways, beginning their pilgrimage anew.

These inhabitants do not eat, not exactly. They **metabolize the gradient**—the temperature differential between the boiling springs and the fever-hot air, the pressure differential between the deep vents and the dark ceiling, the temporal gradient between the fast-plazas and the slow-spires. They are the city’s metabolism made manifest, the consumption and excretion of geological process given temporary organization. They have no young, only accretions. They have no death, only dissolution back into the springs.

Sometimes, when a new mineral layer cracks into place with that particular *thrum* that echoes through the basin, the dwellers gather—if gathering is the word for entities that exist in different times and states of matter. The Limes turn their ringed faces upward, the Menisci ripple in synchronous patterns, the Steam-Takers hum at frequencies that make the bacterial mats glow brighter, bruising the violet deeper. They are not worshipping. They are **voting**, or perhaps **photosynthesizing meaning** from the silence. The city breathes—infrastructure sipping and weeping, spires crumbling and growing, water climbing and falling—and they exist within that breath, hot, wet, dark citizens of a place that builds itself only to remember that it is alive.
