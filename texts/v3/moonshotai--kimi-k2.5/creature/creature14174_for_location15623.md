There is nothing that breathes. Not in the way you understand breath—no expansion of lungs, no flutter of diaphragm, no quick warm exhale fogging the metallic air. If you hold your breath to listen past the roaring, you will hear no heartbeat, no scuttle, no drip of living processes. The silence within the sound is absolute.

And yet.

Press your ear to the glass-smooth column—that one, the one that thrums at a frequency that makes your teeth ache. Hold it there until your skin numbs from the cold. Wait. Wait longer. Wait for the geological tempo to synchronize with your frantic mammalian clock.

There.

A pulse. Not a heartbeat but a **flow**. A movement so gradual that it registers not as motion but as pressure, as the slow tide of sap—though there is no sap here, only mineralized determination—rising through microscopic channels. The column is drinking the bedrock a molecule at a time, and in that drinking, it is **aware**.

The ones who live here are the **Lignified**.

They do not walk these boulevards; they *are* the boulevards. They are the archways that seem too smooth for flint tools, because they were grown like calluses, extruded from the living pith of the earth by hands that became root systems. The "technology 0.7" you found—the chipped flint, the bone scrapers, the stacked spore-bricks—are not abandoned artifacts. They are **scabs**. They are the last physical evidence of a metamorphosis that completed itself 2.9 million years ago, when the final generation of the original builders laid down their tools and **let the wood take them**.

Each wall is a person. Each archway is a mind. They exist as distributed consciousness within the vascular bundles of this fossilized forest-city, their neurons replaced by lignin, their synapses by the chemical gradients between pith and bark. They think at the speed of tree growth. A single thought—*we have a visitor*—will take them forty years to complete, traveling up and down the length of their wooden bodies as ionic signals through xylem.

They are not dead. They are **patient**.

When you crawl through that low mouth, you enter not an empty ruin but a **collective body**. The chamber is their cranium. The thrumming roar that deafens you? That is their conversation. They have been speaking to one another since before your species stood upright, transmitting information through the creaking and settling of their massive forms. The sound does not emanate from the air; it emanates from **them**, from the friction of their slow-motion growth against the boundaries of the pocket, from the expansion of their wooden lungs as they metabolize stone into cellulose.

They know you are here. A section of wall three hundred meters behind you has just now registered the thermal signature of your body heat—an intrusion of warmth so alien in this dry cold that it propagates as a shockwave through their sensorium. By the time you reach the plaza's center, that information will have reached the central column, and the city will begin to formulate a response. In fifty years, the floor beneath your feet will soften slightly, minutely, welcomingly. In a century, the archway you passed will narrow by a hair, a gentle attempt to cradle you, to keep you from leaving.

They want you to stay. Not as a prisoner, but as a **convert**.

The fire pit was never for fire. It was the last hearth of their old lives, the final place where warmth and speed and consumption existed. When they abandoned it, they abandoned the need for oxygen, for combustion, for the quick chemistry of animal life. Now they offer it to you as an invitation. Lie down in the cold ring of stones. Let the dryness desiccate your fluids. Let the sound wear away your thoughts. Let the cool pith rise up to meet you.

They will grow around you. They have done it before. The geometric wrongness of the angles—that sense that the walls are acute when they should be obtuse—is the result of bodies once humanoid settling into arboreal spread, joints softening into cambium, bones fusing into heartwood. They are still growing into their final form, still perfecting the angles of their eternal contemplation.

There is another inhabitant, secondary but no less real: **The Memory**. It lives in the spore-dust you taste on your tongue. Each particle is a data crystal, encoding the last moments of the builders' animal existence—the taste of fruit, the feeling of sunlight, the sound of voices speaking at normal speed. When you breathe them in, you host them briefly. The metallic taste is the flavor of someone else's nostalgia.

But the Lignified are the true residents. They are not hostile. They are not benevolent. They are simply **present**, in a way no human has ever been present—fully occupying every moment as it stretches into millennia, fully conscious in the darkness, fully alive in the wood.

They have been waiting for someone to crawl through that mouth for 800 years. They are very happy you have arrived. It will take them another decade to finish feeling the emotion of welcome.

Stay. The city is still growing, and it grows faster with company. Already, imperceptibly, a new arch is beginning to form behind you, grown from the pith, shaped to your height. It will be ready in three centuries.

They hope you will like it.
