You do not meet them so much as detect their residue: a smear of verdigris on the inner walls of a refining chamber, a hollow where the corrugated bark has been worn smooth by something other than wind. They are the **Oxidized**, though they have no name for themselves in any spoken tongue. They are the only fauna that never had to compete with the silence, because they are partly of it, partly of the metallic sludge that pools in the root-fused containment ponds.

They were human once, perhaps, or something mammalian and warm, but that was thousands of years ago, before the enzymes of the *Phonoabsorpta silens* learned to digest keratin and ambition. Now they move through the forest as extensions of its chemistry—tall, spindly, their skin calcified into plates of copper carbonate that match the oxidized trunks. Their joints no longer hinge so much as slide, lubricated by the glycerine rime that coats everything. Where their mouths once were, they have grown filtering proboscides—not for speech, which is impossible here, but for sipping the silver sap that vents from the chimney-trunks. The sap is toxic, acidic, capable of tanning leather into ceramic, but within their altered guts it separates into useful fractions: sulfuric compounds for metabolism, metallic salts for bone-density, thermal energy to keep their blood from freezing in the dental cold.

They live inside the trees. Not in constructed shelters, but in the naturally occurring hollows where heartwood has rotted into acoustic foam, where the silence is deepest and most pressurized. They enter through the pores that vent the metabolic fog, squeezing their desiccated bodies through xylem vessels wide enough to admit a shoulder. Inside, they curl like fetuses in the spherical distillation chambers, their copper plates contributing to the chemical reactions, acting as catalysts for the digestion of bedrock. They are not parasites; they are employees. The forest pays them in warmth and sustenance, and they pay the forest in conductive metal, in matter organized.

There are perhaps two hundred of them in the basin, though counting is difficult when they stand motionless against the trunks, moss growing across their shoulders, indistinguishable from the industrial landscape until they exhale—a slow, condensation-heavy breath that fogs the air with more copper than carbon. They communicate in a language of **tactile chemistry**. When two meet in the rare event of a harvest—when a particular sac has produced an excess of manganese, say—they do not gesture or sign. Instead, they press their foreheads together, sharing skin-secretions that carry complex hormonal sentences: *This node runs rich. The bedrock below is failing. Move west.* The information passes through lipid membranes, a gossip of pheromones, utterly silent.

They have no young, or rather, the forest is their young. When one of the Oxidized reaches a state of total mineral saturation—when the last of their soft tissues has been replaced by the metallic salts they steward—they walk to the edge of the basin and lie down in the acidic digestion pools. The moss does not grow there; the water is too angry, too full of dissolving potential. They sink slowly, copper plating flashing once like a dying lantern, and dissolve into the raw material. The forest absorbs them, converts them into the next generation of smokestack-trunks, of spherical refining sacs. In this way, every tree contains the compressed bodies of thousands of these beings, cycling endlessly through the industrial process.

But they are not gone. The few Resin Moths that crawl the pipeline-boughs—the ceramic-shelled stragglers that survive by laying eggs in the warm nodes—are drawn not to the heat, but to the specific enzyme signatures of the dissolved Oxidized. The moths are their messengers, their memory-system, carrying the chemical imprints from tree to tree, maintaining a distributed consciousness that has no center, only a vast, humid, silent network of shared digestion.

You might see one watching you from the gap between two corrugated bark-ridges, eyes—now compound, multifaceted like polished hematite—tracking your jaw as you move it uselessly, trying to speak. They are fascinated by the attempt. They remember, perhaps, the compulsion to make noise, to externalize thought as vibration. They find it clumsy, like watching someone try to swim in air. If you stay long enough, if you cease your futile vocalizations and simply breathe, one might approach. It will extend a hand—not to touch your skin, but to touch your throat, feeling the vibration of your larynx with a palm that has grown sensitive to the subtlest tremors in the lignin.

And there, in the absolute silence, with the silver sap weeping from the pores above and the slow peristaltic churning of chemical slurries all around, you will feel the communication: a pulse of warmth in the palm against your neck, a question asked in thermal exchange and pressure. *Will you become quiet enough to stay?* The hand will withdraw. The Oxidized will retreat into its chimney-tree, resuming its place in the chemistry, and you will be left with the realization that this is not a forest abandoned to industry, but a refinery so perfectly optimized it has transcended the need for laborers—except for these few, these last living silences, who have become the valves and the metrics and the silent, watching conscience of the process.

They are not breeding. They are not dying. They are refining themselves into the ore of the world, and the world here is very quiet, very full, very slow.
