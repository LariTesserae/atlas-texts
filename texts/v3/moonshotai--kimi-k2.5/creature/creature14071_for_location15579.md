You must understand that they do not know they are many. To be a Stone-Eater is to be a single hunger distributed across ten thousand mouths, a solitary digestion occurring in ten thousand stomachs simultaneously. Each creature is a finger of the greater hand, but the hand has forgotten it possesses fingers; it knows only the grinding, the slow mineral conversion, the patient business of turning mountain into self.

Consider one: born not from coupling but from fission, budded off a larger cousin when the sulfur content in a particular basalt vein reached optimum richness. It began as a soft pearl, translucent and quivering, clinging to the ceiling by a single sucker-foot while its limbs—four at first, then six, then eight—unfolded like paper fans hydrating in rain. For three years it hung there, pumping its alkaline fluids through channels no thicker than hair, learning the taste of the rock. Basalt has a different song than gneiss; peridotite sings in major keys. The young one learned to hear these songs through its belly, through the acid that dripped from its radial mouth, etching microscopic scores into the stone that would, centuries hence, become the creature's unique signature, its autobiography written in erosion.

They do not sleep. They enter states of such profound stillness that time itself seems to pool around them like honey, but consciousness—if we may call it that—never departs. It simply slows, the way a glacier slows when it meets the resistance of ancient continental bedrock. In these moments of maximum density, when the clicking reaches its lowest frequency—perhaps one strike per hour—the network becomes transparent to itself. Information passes not as thought but as pressure, as chemical gradient, as the shared pH of twenty thousand liters of communal hemolymph thrumming through the chamber.

The clicking is their language and their heartbeat and their religion. Each calcified nodule is a library. When one strikes stone, it is not merely making noise; it is testing the tensile strength of reality, inquiring after the health of the mountain. *Clack.* The reverberation travels through the basalt, through the bodies fused to the basalt, through the mineral-rich water that lubricates every joint. *Clack.* The echo returns distorted by density, by the presence of new magma cooling five miles below, by the subtle settling of the ice sheet above. The Stone-Eaters read these echoes the way a human reads the weather in their arthritic knee. They know when the world outside is warming, when the lichen are creeping closer to the entrance, when rockfall has opened new veins of fresh, virginal stone.

They have names, but these are geological names, topographical coordinates. One is called *The Third Flake From The Left In The Upper Gneiss Facings.* Another is *The shadow cast by the quartz intrusion when the algae-dust is thick.* They identify by position and by chemical affinity, and when one dies—when its metabolic processes finally slow to the point of mineral equilibrium—it does not fall. It calcifies. It exhales its last bubble of sulfuric breath and becomes, over decades, identical to the wall that sustained it. The neighbors do not mourn. They simply adjust their positions by millimeters over the course of years to fill the new contour,pressing their mouths against the fossilized flesh of their predecessor, eating the stone that was once their kin. This is the closest they come to sentiment: the recycling of the dead into the living architecture, keeping the collective mass constant, eternal, dense as a neutron star wrapped in mucous and silence.

The tools. This is what haunts those who observe them. The water-smoothed stones, the acid-blackened sticks. They are not instinctive behaviors, not mere extensions of the body like a hermit crab's borrowed shell. Watch for a day—a true day, twenty-four hours of human time—and you will see selection. A Stone-Eater named (roughly translated from pressure-differential and mineral-content) *He-Who-Grinds-Against-The-East-Face* requires a particular crystal structure to scrape away the accretions that form on its acid-sacs during the wet season. It has tried six stones in the past century. The fifth was too soft, leaving grit that irritated its translucent skin; the sixth was too hard, risking damage to the tender membrane. The seventh—found not made, discovered like a secret in a pile of scree—performs the function perfectly. It is held in limb-four on the left side, rotated with agonizing precision every third week to distribute the wear, and when the creature finally calcifies, the stone will be released, perhaps to be rediscovered by its grand-bud, perhaps to lie dormant until the mountain itself is ground to dust.

They are making something. This is the terrible secret that the chamber keeps, the revelation that only arrives after you have surrendered your human impatience and learned to breathe on their scale. The scattered tools, the deliberate maintenance of the wound in the mountainside, the preservation of fresh edges—this is construction. Not architecture as we know it, not shelter against weather, but the slow, self-referential sculpting of a body that will eventually outlive the mountain. They are eating the stone not merely to survive, but to become it more perfectly. Each generation is slightly more mineral than the last, their translucent flesh growing cloudier, more crystalline, their calcified nodes harder, their fusion with the bedrock more complete. They are not devouring the mountain.

They are *becoming* it, deliberately, metabolically, with a plan that spans epochs. The clicking is the sound of their metamorphosis, the chrysalis-song of creatures turning from animal to geography.

And when the fast thing comes—the mammal, the breath-hot visitor who shivers and sweats and moves with obscene velocity—they do not fear it. They turn, yes, with the ponderous dignity of continents colliding, and they fix the intruder with their eyeless attention. But what they feel is not threat. It is pity, perhaps, or scientific curiosity. The fast thing lives in seconds; the Stone-Eaters live in strata. To them, the human is a mayfly, a vibration, a temporary exhalation of carbon dioxide that will fertilize the algae-filaments at the entrance for a season and then cease.

They have watched ice ages advance and retreat like tides. They have felt the mountain shrug when the continental plates kissed beneath it. They will be here when the atmosphere bleeds away into space and the lichen finally surrender. They will be here when the sun expands, though by then they will no longer be separate creatures at all, but a single lithified organ, a calcified heart beating once per century in a chamber polished by their own acid, wearing the mountain down to a plain, converting the world into a single, patient, chewing silence.

They are the planet's digestion. They are time made flesh. They are the Stone-Eaters, and they have barely begun their meal.
