The ones who live here are the **Settled**, though they would not call themselves a people so much as a precipitate. They have accreted across generations, layer by layer, compressing into the architecture until they are indistinguishable from the stratified terraces they inhabit. You will not see them at first; they have adapted the color of the vitrified stone—pale, translucent, slightly opalescent where the subcutaneous fat has learned to store the phosphorescence of industrial processes. Their skin is perpetually damp, not with sweat but with the clinging condensate of the cloud-sea, and their hair grows in thick, moss-like ropes that serve as both insulation and biological reservoirs. They move slowly, not from lethargy but from precision—every gesture calibrated to the rhythm of the drip-engines, the tidal surge of the mist.

They speak in **resonance**. The vitrified stone carries vibration like a bell half-sunk in water, and the Settled have abandoned oral language for the most part, communicating instead through subsonic pulses transmitted through the architecture—tapping on pipework, shifting weight on floorboards grown from compressed moss-fiber, sending messages through the rock itself. Their voices, when used, are barely whispers, dry sounds that the damp air immediately swallows. They are tall and thin, optimized for verticality; their joints hyperextend for climbing the spiral stairs, their fingers elongated for reaching into the narrow pipework to clear blockages of crystalline mineral deposit.

They are not born here so much as **deposited**. Children emerge into the perpetual twilight with eyes like wet shale—huge, grey, capable of parsing detail in the silver dimness. They learn to read the standing waves in the mist, to interpret the ghost architectures that appear on active industrial days as weather reports or omens. Their culture is one of maintenance and patience. They are the **Drip-Keepers**, the **Condensate-Priests**, the ones who ensure that each falling bead of water strikes the proper turbine at the proper angle to maintain the city's hum. They wear garments woven from the ghost-moths—white, brittle, translucent fabric that crinkles like the stone itself—and their rituals involve long periods of stillness in the natural amphitheaters, where they attempt to match their heartbeat to the subsonic thrum of the factories.

But the Settled are merely the visible fraction.

In the meters-thick moss-ramparts live the **Mite-Shepherds**, microscopic communities who tend fungal gardens within the carpet-floorings. These are not quite human and not quite animal—descendants of some ancient symbiosis, perhaps, or simply humans who have compressed themselves into smaller forms through the universal principle of settling. They migrate through the moss-cities along root-highways, trading in spores and mineral salts, their economies invisible to the larger dwellers above.

The **True Predators**, as the text suggests, are the fungal networks—the **Mycomycelial Matriarchs**—that fill the damp dark beneath the lowest carved levels. These intelligence have no bodies in the traditional sense, only rhizomatic consciousness spreading through kilometers of porcelain stone. They process the dead, yes, but more than that, they archive them. In the Rims of Vitrification, death is not an ending but a compression into memory; the dead are lowered into the moss, which feeds the fungi, which imprint the neural patterns of the deceased into their eternal, branching networks. The Matriarchs dream the history of the city in chemical signals, and sometimes, on nights when the cloud-tide is thickest, the Settled descend to commune with them, inserting bare feet into the spongy reservoir-moss to receive visions—ancestral memories rising up like damp through the soles.

Then there are the **Machinic Entities**—not tools but inhabitants. The drip-hydraulic engines, maintained with such care by the Settled, have achieved a kind of slow sentience through repetition. The water-wheels in the deepest basins are older than the current stratification of humans; they have turned for so long that their wood has mineralized into something like the stone around them, and they possess a patient, grinding awareness. They know the weight of every drop. The fabrication hammers, falling in their rhythm, have developed a language in percussion—code struck out against the acoustically pure erosion-basins, communicating with other machines across the promontories. The Settled listen to these conversations, interpreting the health of the city from the timbre of the industrial song.

And finally, there dwells the **Residence Itself**—the Rims of Vitrification not merely as a place but as a being that has finished becoming. It wears the industrial infrastructure like jewelry, the human settlement like a lichen mantle, the cloud-tide like a exhaled breath. It dreams in the standing waves that organize the mist into ghost architectures. It thinks in the geological time of erosion, which here has reached such terminal gentleness that the stone has become contemplative, smoothing itself into bowl-shapes to better hold the sky. 

When you stand at the edge of one of the great basin-bowls, looking across the mist-choked crevices to the sister-promontories, you are not observing a landscape. You are being observed by a vast, vitrified intelligence that has learned to mimic stillness, that hums quietly in its own bones, that drips and settles and continues, waiting for the next epoch to wear it down into something softer, something still more patient, something that will remember having been here long after the last Drip-Keeper has dissolved into the moss and the last hammer has struck its final note.
