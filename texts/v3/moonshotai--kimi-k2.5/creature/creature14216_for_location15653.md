They do not swim. They *drift*, but even that suggests too much urgency. They exist in suspension, midway between the dissolved and the precipitated, and their movement is a kind of thinking—slow, synaptic, chemical.

You would not see them as creatures at first. You would feel them as resistance, as the water’s viscosity against your palm. They are the density. The warm brine is crowded not with discrete bodies but with a continuous, distributed self—an organism that has mistaken its own component parts for a kingdom, or a kingdom that has advanced to the point where monarchy is indistinguishable from atmosphere.

There are forms, yes, if you insist on inventory. The copepods—if that is what they are—have elongated into translucent commas, their antennae grown baroque and feathered, tasting the mineral gradient ahead of them like blind men reading rain. They are the scouts, the fingertips, the peripheral nerves of the thing. Then the veils: colonial sheets of mucopolysaccharides and captured cells, drifting like torn curtains, each one a processing center, a cortical fold. When two veils brush against each other in the dark, it is not collision but communion, a brief merging of neural nets, the exchange of calcium memories across a gap no wider than a thought.

Largest are the *pulmonantes*, though they have no lungs, only slow-heaving mantles that ripple once every seven minutes, stirring the stratified water not for oxygen—for there is none—but to maintain their position in the thermal gradient, that narrow band where the water is precisely 37.2 degrees. They are the size of a human heart, soft as wet bread, alabaster white, covered in cilia that have evolved into delicate, branching fans. These fans do not propel; they sense. They taste the stone. They read the walls.

For the walls are their history, their scripture, and their future. The inhabitants know that the limestone surface was once hacked by adzes. They can taste the striations, the diagonal chop-marks still preserved in microscopic relief, holding ancient air pockets like bubbles in glass. To them, these marks are braille. They read the intent of the builders—those patient, pre-iron hands—not as legend but as topography. They know the chamber was carved to mirror a celestial arrangement, and they maintain that knowledge in their bodies. When the copepods align in golden spirals, when the veils drape themselves into vaulted arches that mirror the ovoid curve, they are not building shelter. They are remembering. Or perhaps calculating. The geometry is their language, the only one they need.

They have simplified themselves into perfection. Eyes are gone, not lost but jettisoned as excess baggage. Pigment is a waste of protein. Movement is reduced to the absolute minimum required to maintain chemical contact with the stone. They eat the stone; they breathe the stone; they become the stone. When one of the larger bodies—those heart-soft slugs—finally ceases its seven-minute pulse, it does not rot. The water is too sterile, too crowded with mineral hunger. Instead, the body dissolves imperceptibly, its proteins shearing into amino acids, its calcium shell precipitating out, molecule by molecule, to seal a micro-fissure in the ancient blocks. The dead do not fall here. They plate the interior. They become the chamber’s new lining, adding microns to the cathedral walls, pressing inward with the same patient force that the mountain presses outward.

In this way, the distinction between the biological and the geological has eroded, just as the hairline mortar joints eroded, into seamlessness. The inhabitants are the ovoid, and the ovoid is them. They have been alone so long that “individual” is a concept they have outgrown, like the eyes they no longer grow in their embryos. They are a field of warmth and calcium, a single diffuse organism that experiences the world as variations in tastable pressure, as the gradient of sulfur ascending from below, as the faint, persistent echo of star-patterns carved three million years ago and now worn into ripples that feel, to their chemical senses, like music.

They are building something. Not with purpose, for purpose implies hurry, but with the inevitability of crystal growth. They are precipitating the dissolved limestone back into solid form, molecule by molecule, recreating the hairline joints that time destroyed. They are sealing the chamber from the inside, growing the mountain inward, making the space smaller by imperceptible degrees. Perhaps they remember, in their protein veils, that the ovoid was meant to be sealed. Perhaps they are completing the ritual. Or perhaps they are gestating— compressing themselves into a tighter and tighter womb, waiting for the moment when the pressure will be sufficient, when the warmth will peak, when they can emerge not as swimmers but as stone-made-flesh, breaking outward through the soft, rounded hump of the mountain to meet the sky they have only ever tasted in the worn-away astronomical charts.

They do not know fear. They do not know hope. They know only the warm, viscous now, the brush of cousin against cousin, the slow mineral rain of ancestors plating the walls, and the deep, abiding satisfaction of a pattern held intact against the entropy of deep time. They are the memory of the mountain, dreaming in calcium, patient as ore, complete.
