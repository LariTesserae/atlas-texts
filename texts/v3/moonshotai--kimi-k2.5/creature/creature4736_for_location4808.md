The one who lives there has no name that would sound like a word in your mouth. You would have to click your tongue against the palate three times, soft-hard-soft, to approximate what the Glass-Wing Bats call her when they flutter past her earholes in the dark. But if you must have a name for the shape of her, call her **the Keeper**, or **the Spiral-Walker**, or simply **she-who-does-not-rise**.

She is small. The Vault was built by hands, but it was built for small things. Her shoulders are narrow enough to slide between the terracotta cylinders without disturbing the bats clustered inside; her spine has compressed over decades of ducking under low-hanging ropes of agave fiber, giving her a permanent forward tilt, as if she is always listening to the floor. Her skin is the color of the Grotto Moss that creeps between the bricks—pale, luminous green-grey—and it is perpetually damp, not with sweat but with the chamber’s own breath. Condensation beads on her forearms and runs in rivulets down her elbows, dripping to feed the lichen that grows in the whorls of her ears.

She is old, but age is difficult to measure in a place where the temperature never shifts and the light comes only in shafts. Her hair is not hair anymore but a fine, downy pelt, thick as the under-fur the bats shed into the air; it floats around her head in a nimbus, catching the beams from the periscope-shafts and glowing like dandelion seed. Her eyes are huge, lidless in the way of deep-sea fish, filmed with a nictitating membrane that she can draw across to protect her vision from the spore-snow. She sees in grey-violet, in the trembling heat-shimmer of the air, in the ultrasonic bloom of bat-speech that paints the terracotta in her mind as topography.

She does not sleep, not as you understand it. She enters torpor. She climbs into an empty cylinder—there are always a few, reserved for her by some consensus of the colony—and wraps herself in a hammock of braided leather thong. There, her body temperature drops to match the blood-warm ambient, and her heart slows to four beats per minute, thrumming in counterpoint to the *tonk-tonk* of water drops falling into the clay bowls. The bats cluster around her, filling the tube with their parchment bodies, their translucent wings folded over her like shrouds. They keep her warm. They click softly against her eardrums, telling her the news of the lower pits: a beetle has overturned, a pseudoscorpion has molted, a blind snake has fallen through Shaft Four and been overwhelmed by the scarabs.

She tends the **Writing**. This is her primary function. The Glass-Wing Bats are born with pigment patterns on their backs—irregular splashes of umber and rust that the Keeper knows to read. Each pattern is unique, a history written in melanin. She catches the bats gently as they hang, cupping them in her damp palms, and traces the markings with her thumbs. She knows which lines indicate a strong hunter, which spirals denote a female ready to breed, which dark spots warn of congenital weakness. When a bat dies—when its cylinder grows cold—she retrieves the body before the beetles can claim it. She scrapes the wing-membranes clean, stretches them on bone frames, and adds them to the **Scrolls** that line her private corridor: a library of translucent maps, each one a record of a life lived in the Vault.

She maintains the **Song**. The bats sing constantly, a sizzling, crackling pressure that would drive a surface-dweller mad, but the Keeper hears the music in it. She knows when the pitch shifts upward that the humidity has climbed too high; she knows the particular staccato of alarm that means a predator has breached the light-shafts. When the colony grows too loud, too dense with anxiety, she climbs the bone ladders to the central dais. There, she sits beside the shallow soapstone basin—that impossible pool where water collects but never spills—and she hums. Her voice is low, subsonic, a vibration she sends through her hands into the stone. The bats respond. Their clicks modulate, harmonizing with her drone, and the panic subsides. The rhythm of the dripping water reasserts itself. The Vault exhales.

She feeds on **white**. In the composting pits, where the scarab beetles roll their perfect spheres of guano, she harvests the albino mushrooms. She breaks the bone-porcelain stems with her teeth and chews the spongy flesh, which tastes of iron and fermentation. She drinks from the central basin, cupping the water that never overflows—water that has filtered through twenty meters of clay and memory, water that tastes like the earth’s own saliva. She does not eat meat, though she could; the bats offer her mites and gnats in grooming gestures, pressing the tiny morsels to her lips, and she accepts these offerings as sacraments.

She is not alone, though she is the only one of her kind. There are others like her, or there were. She finds their traces in the petrified bricks: fingerprints pressed into the clay before it fossilized, small footprints worn into the tabular stones where generations of Keepers have paced the same spiraling paths. She is the latest in a lineage of stewards, perhaps the last, bred for this heat and this darkness, selected for smallness and silence and the ability to read the mutations of isolation.

She is repairing a rope when you find her. You have come down through Shaft Three, breaking the seal of dust, and the bats have exploded into a cyclone of alarm around you, filling the air with parchment thunder. She does not startle. She ties off the agave fiber with a knot perfected five thousand years ago and turns her huge, filmed eyes toward the sound of your breathing. She smells you—sweat and soap and metal, the sharp alien scent of iron—and her nose wrinkles. She has never smelled iron.

She clicks her tongue, soft-hard-soft. A question. The bats fall silent, waiting. The water drips. The beetles pause in their grooves.

She extends one damp, moss-colored hand toward you, palm up, not in threat but in invitation. She wants to see if you, too, have the Writing on your back. She wants to know if you have come to take her place in the cylinder, or if you are just another predator, briefly descending through the light, soon to be rolled into the compost by the scarabs.

The Vault holds its breath. She holds hers. The water in the basin trembles, but does not spill.
