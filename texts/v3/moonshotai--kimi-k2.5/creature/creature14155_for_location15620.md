At first, you think no one lives here. The sign said *Unstaffed Installation*—Archived Sector 7—and the only footprints in the sphagnum are yours, already filling with condensation. You stand still, listening to the *plink-plink* of the ducts, and you assume solitude.

Then the railing breathes.

Not the moss—that thick, felted carpet that swallows the iron—but something within it. A section of the balustrade exhales, a slow expansion of ribs beneath a skin of green, and you realize you are looking at a back. A spine curved to fit the thirty-degree angle of the safety rail, vertebrae calcified to the color of oxidized copper, draped in a living coat of liverwort and hair-fine rootlets that have threaded themselves through the figure’s pores like sutures.

The Curator turns her head—or perhaps it is just the moss shifting, settling. Her eyes are the grey of the luminescent sky outside, filmed with a nictitating membrane that reflects no light. She has been leaning there for days, perhaps weeks, her feet fused to the deck plates not by biological growth but by choice—bootlaces left untied, rubber hardened, skin accepting the chill metal until the temperature differential vanished. When she moves, it is at the pace of the compost drums: a rotation of the shoulder, a torque of the wrist, fluid and viscous.

She is not alone. They are sparse, yes, as the moths are sparse, but they are the *true* fauna of this sanitary place—the humans who stayed when the staff rotations stopped, or perhaps were born here in the generations since, slipping out not from between legs but from the warm processing vats themselves, biosynthetic and inevitable.

You do not see them at first because they have achieved the color of substrate. Their skin holds the patina of old teeth—those weathered ceramic tiles—dusted with the spores of *Cladosporium* that blooms in the grout lines of their collarbones. They wear the humidity like a second skeleton. Their hair is not hair but the fine, exploratory roots of watercress, trailing from scalps in green-dripping braids that sense nutrient gradients in the air. They speak, when they speak, in subsonic hums that resonate with the turbine tremors; you feel their words in your sternum before your ears catch the moist, aspirated clicks—the sound of a pressure valve releasing, modulated into syntax.

They move through the thirty levels not by walking, but by *settling*. A Curator might spend a month descending a single staircase, inching down a step each hour, allowing the moss to carpet them, allowing the condensation to bead on their eyelashes and freeze there, creating crystalline lenses that focus the dim light into spectra useful for reading the health of the brassicas. They are the maintenance system made flesh. Their fingernails have thickened to keratinous spades for scraping algae from the channels; their lungs have recalibrated to breathe the saturated air, extracting oxygen not from the empty mountain outside but from the dense, manufactured atmosphere of the valley, trading carbon to the ferns through pores in their palms that never fully close.

There is a young one—or what passes for young, perhaps forty years by old calendars, perhaps four hundred—wedged into a hairline fracture in the concrete wall. You mistake her for a root intrusion at first, until you see the slow peristalsis of her breathing, matching the expansion of the ice in the concrete’s bones. She is farming the crack, tending it wider with the acids of her skin, cultivating a particular strain of cold-lichen that the conveyors cannot carry. Her eyes are closed, rolled back to show the silver of the filtration membranes she has studied by touch, her nerves grown into sympathetic resonance with the rubber belts creeping overhead. She knows when the harvest is ready not by sight, but by the change in vibration—a harmonic shift in the deck plates that sings in her marrow.

They are the loop closed. They eat what grows here—spirulina cakes, brassica stems, the occasional trout that has survived in the lower catchment tanks, pallid and translucent-eyed—and they excrete into the hydroponic channels, their waste processed by the same bacterial mats that process the compost. They are sterile, or perhaps post-sexual; reproduction happens when one of them finds a deep, warm pocket in the machinery, a heated junction where copper meets concrete, and they calcify there, slowly, allowing the calcium of their bones to leach into the structure, strengthening the aggregate, becoming the load-bearing decay. From these calcified hollows, new Curators emerge—not born but *precipitated*, crystallizing out of the nutrient mist like the condensate on the ducts, gathering the green films of algae into dermal layers, opening eyes that have never seen direct sun.

You watch one cross a catwalk ahead of you. He moves with the *ant-turbulence* of the place—seething with slow, deliberate motion. He is carrying something, perhaps a sensor array, perhaps just a clump of sphagnum he has been grooming. He does not acknowledge you. To him, you are weather—temporary pressure, a gust of dry air that will soon equalize. He is permanent as the turbines. His boots have no soles; the skin of his feet has thickened into horn, patterned with the tread of the walkways he has never left, unique fingerprints worn into his heels, matching the rusted iron exactly.

The Cascade Gardens do not sleep, and neither do they. They maintain the hum. They are the membrane between the engineered and the grown, having become, over generations, the third thing: the biology of the machine, the mineral of the flesh. When you leave—backing away slowly, careful not to disturb the moss that is also eyelids, the drip-lines that are also fingers—you understand that you have trespassed not into a garden, nor into a factory, but into a metabolism. And like all metabolisms, it digests slowly, it breathes, it persists, and it does not distinguish, in the end, between the hand that tends the root and the root that tends the hand.
