What lives here has forgotten that it is alive, or perhaps has accreted a definition of living so broad that it includes the dripping of water and the settling of stone within its category of "breathing."

At first you might think the inhabitants are the **Saturated**—those pale, malleable forms you glimpse in the corner of a cul-de-sac, pressed against the weeping walls like slugs made of chalk and moonlight. They have the general shape of intent: a suggestion of limbs, a orientation toward the vertical, but their skin is matte and porous, the color of bone marrow left too long in water. They do not walk so much as they **flow** through the labyrinth, squeezing their soft calcite skeletons through gaps too narrow for air, their bodies compressing with the sound of wet clay shifting in a closed hand. They leave trails—not slime, but delicate deposits of calcium carbonate, frosting the walls behind them with lace that will, in fifty years, be indistinguishable from the architecture itself.

But look closer, and you realize the Saturated are not the residents; they are the **maintenance**, the immune response, the fingers of a larger presence exploring its own body.

It is rarely seen—a silhouette that flickers at the edge of the silver light, moving against the grain of the architecture. Where the Lithic grows, the Vein-Walker prunes; where the water flows, it drinks not to nourish but to **thin**, to create channels of absence. It is bone-white too, but mobile, articulated like a stick insect made of coral, and it carries with it a pocket of rare dryness, a desiccant force that leaves behind it not calcite but **hollowness**.
