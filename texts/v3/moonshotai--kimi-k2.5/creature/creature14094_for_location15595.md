At first, you think the inhabitants are only the obvious ones: the rust-colored crayfish testing the chemical composition of the newborn water with their antennae; the swallows who have discovered that the hollow tubes—the petrified smokestacks—muffle their songs into private whispers; the bats threading the spires at dusk; the stoneflies scraping algae films with mandibles that click like knitting needles against the calcite. They are the squatters, the testers of real estate, the opportunists who arrived to find the stone still warm from its formation.

But stay longer. Stand in one of the plazas until your breathing slows to match the geological rhythm of the drip. Watch the undersides of the terrace lips, where condensation beads and falls. Look closely at the surfaces you took for bare, granular stone.

**The Precipitants** live here.

They are not easy to see because they are in the process of becoming the architecture itself. Each one is small—no larger than a thumbnail, flat and pale as a drop of skimmed milk. They are crustaceans of a sort, or perhaps mollusks defying classification, existing in the liminal state between organism and mineral deposition. They secrete the travertine not as waste but as body, extending their own edges in the same chemical frenzy that raises the towers.

You will find them clustered in the "streets"—those channels where the mineral-rich water flows fastest. They adhere to the stone with discs of microscopic suction, their bodies fluttering like pale leaves in the current. When the hydrostatic pressure pulses upward (that subsonic hum you feel in your feet), the Precipitants open their carapaces, exposing feathery gills that are white as bone and iridescent with silica. They filter the heated water, extracting nutrients so minute they might be imaginary, and in return, they deposit a layer of crystalline calcite—just a few molecules thick—onto their own backs.

They grow outward, not upward. They are the architects of the horizontal, the builders of the terraces and balustrades. A colony of them, adhered to a wall for a century, will have built a bench, a cornice, a windowsill—though they know nothing of sitting, nothing of looking out. They build because the building is their metabolism.

In the hollow tubes—the vertical shafts that resemble smokestacks—they live in strata. The uppermost Precipitants are young, translucent, barely calcified, still capable of detaching and drifting to new construction sites when the water shifts. Below them, their elders are half-stone, their lower bodies fused to the tube walls, their upper limbs still waving in the thermal currents, still feeding, still secreting. At the bottom of the deepest tubes, you will find the Ancients: completely encased in white travertine, perfectly preserved, their final shell-layers forming the fluted acoustic properties that the swallows now use for their nests. They are dead, yet they are the structure. They are the ancestors who became the load-bearing walls.

There are other things, too, that emerge from this collaboration between biology and geology. In the lower basins, where the horsetails grow thickest, you may glimpse **The Dampeners**—gelatinous sheets that spread across the pooled water like living oil slicks. They are colonial organisms, billions of individual zooids acting as one membrane. They drink the mineral water and excrete pure silica, weaving glassine threads that bind the reedy grasses together into hummocks. They are the reason the plazas remain flat and smooth; they are the pavers, the carpet-layers, filling in the gaps between the Precipitants' terraces with a mortar of organic glass.

And in the shadows, where the moss grows thickest on the northern faces, move **The Rafters**—creatures that might be insects or might be something older, armored in the same emerald velvet as the moss they consume. They do not build; they curate. They prune the ferns to maintain the canopy’s lattice, ensuring the light remains diffuse, the twilight perpetual. They harvest the condensation before it can drip, storing it in abdominal bladders that swell like crystal grapes, then releasing it in controlled rivulets to feed specific colonies of Precipitants. They are the gardeners of the stone, the ones who ensure that the biological-geological collaboration does not choke on its own abundance.

Together, these inhabitants form a metabolism that is indistinguishable from the city’s growth. The Precipitants raise the walls; the Dampeners pave the floors; the Rafters manage the climate; the crayfish, swallows, bats, and stoneflies are the weather they endure. There is no council, no planning, no intention—only the shared fever of the warm water, the shared patience of accretion.

They are quiet. The Precipitants make no sound as they extend their mineral bodies. The Dampeners absorb vibration. The Rafters move with the sticky deliberation of things that live in a world without predators, only collaborators. When the wind flutes through the hollow tubes, it is not the place testing its voice—it is the breath of the Ancients, the fully calcified Precipitants, resonating in their stone throats, singing the low, subsonic note that calls the water up from the depths.

They do not know you are there. They are too busy becoming. They have been building this city for ten thousand years, and they have only just laid the foundation. Tomorrow, the towers will be a fraction of a millimeter taller, and the youngest Precipitants will have sealed themselves to the stone, beginning their long, slow surrender to architecture. They are the residents, yes, but they are also the real estate, the infrastructure, the urban plan. They are the living blueprints of a civilization that will never need tools, because their bodies are the trowel, the brick, the mortar, and the patience of stone itself.
