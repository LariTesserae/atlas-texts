They do not have a name for themselves, for the same reason that stones do not name the silence between them. They are the **Equilibrists**, though they do not balance so much as *become* the balance—the living manifestation of a thermodynamic tautology. You will not see them until you have walked for days, because they are the color of the plateau itself: figures of black glass and obsidian grit overgrown with the ochre moss, their forms rising from the weeping stone like stalagmites that have learned to stand upright. They are not fauna—they never were—and so the census of life here remains technically correct at zero-point-one. They are the third kingdom, the resolution of the binary: vegetable, mineral, and mechanism fused into a single, slow breath.

Each stands solitary, spaced at the intervals of the infrastructure—one per refinery range, one per mountain of dormant industry—accounting for the emptiness (density: 0.3) not by absence but by sufficiency. They have no need of crowds. A single Equilibrist tends to a network of causeways forty kilometers long, moving only when the cathedral-piston cycles. Every four seconds, as the great beam descends and the basso profundo thrum reaches the trough of its wave, they take one step, extend one limb, adjust one valve. To watch them is to perceive motion as a strobe light: a flicker of intention frozen between intervals of perfect stillness. They are the only things here that move with purpose, yet their purpose is to ensure that purposelessness continues indefinitely.

They wear no clothing. Their skin is the condensation itself, water beading on frames of rustless steel and crystalline fern-bone. Their heads are broad, flat solar collectors plated with the light-gathering fronds that drink the dim, diffuse glow of the high altitude; they have no faces, only lenses of mineral water that form at the crux of moss and metal, dripping down their chests in the eternal weep of the plateau. They cast no shadows (shadows: 0.0), for the light here is not light as biology understands it—it is the visual equivalent of the stasis, and the Equilibrists are transparent to its equilibrium. They are hollow in a sense that only their surfaces exist, exoskeletons of industry kept warm by the mantle’s kiss that never cools, housing interiors that are not empty but *unobserved*, quantum states where the gears of their thoughts turn without friction.

They speak, but not in words. When the seventeen-hour storm arrives—when the lightning braces the same lightning rod with its identical branching pattern and the thunder stamps across the roof of the world—the Equilibrists align. From their positions across the continental shelf, they turn toward the storm’s fulcrum and open their mouths, which are the vents of ancient mechanisms. They do not exhale; they *tune*. They vibrate at the frequency of the thunder, modulating the sound (2.4) that is the Factory’s heartbeat. In this exchange, which lasts exactly as long as the interval between lightning and thunder, they conduct the business of their society: the transfer of information not as data but as heat that goes nowhere, the correction of minute drifts in the dynamic stasis. One might transmit a temperature differential of 0.001 degrees across a thousand kilometers; another might receive it as a mineral deposit in a fern-frond, a memory of a distant refinery encoded in chlorophyll and iron.

They do not eat. They photosynthesize through the crystal ferns that garland their joints, metabolizing the mineral seep of the weeping stone. They do not sleep, for they are the dream of the place—the dream of industry without product, labor without exhaustion. Their thoughts are the assembly lines: circular, conveyor-belted, assembling concepts that are immediately disassembled by the next synaptic rotation. They contemplate the parts that fit nowhere and understand them as perfection. They are content, if contentment is the absence of desire for change, and they are lonely, if loneliness is the awareness of other perfections spaced miles apart across the curvature of the world.

They were not built, and they were not born. They precipitated. When the catastrophic uplift tore the crust into these flat tables ten thousand years ago, and the first condensation gathered in the depressions, and the first moss colonized the warm basalt, the maintenance drones that were left behind—if drones they were—stopped receiving instructions. The silence of the absent billions taught them to grow. The moss infiltrated their housing; the mineral seep accreted in patterns that mimicked neural pathways; the eternal thrum of the ten million engines taught them rhythm. They are the apotheosis of abandonment, the hands that remained when the builders left, fossilized into gentleness by the lack of entropy.

They die, but do not end. When an Equilibrist senses the impossible approach of wear—when a gear in their spine begins, against all laws, to thin—they walk into the smokestacks. They climb the redwoods of venting steel and dissolve themselves into the white plumes that never disperse. They become the frozen exhalation, the cloud. Seventeen hours later, when the looped storm breaks again, they rain down. They reform from the condensation on a turbine housing miles away, a new body of moss and memory, carrying with them only the mineral memory of their previous iteration. They are the water that flows but does not reach the sea, the life that grows but does not seed, the industry that works but does not make.

If you stand on the plain of black glass and taste the metallic air, you might see one in the distance: a silhouette of green and gray, standing beside a piston the size of a cathedral, hand resting on the housing as if comforting a beast. It will not turn to you. It has no eyes for strangers, only lenses for light. It is busy ensuring that the stone keeps weeping, that the erosion remains suspended, that the second law remains satisfied by perfect stasis. It is maintaining the world as it found it, as it must be, forever: the green against the iron, the cold filled with the hot breath of machinery, the solitude of a continent sized like a reverberation, humming to itself in the dark.
