The forty do not answer to names, though they once had them. Names imply sequence, birth precedes death, Tuesday follows Monday. Here, in the wet luminescence of the perpetual orange dusk, identity has settled like mineral precipitate—layered, distinct, but lacking the kinetic energy of change. They are simply *the maintained*.

If you were to look closely—and you would have to move slowly to match their temporality, blur your own urgent metabolism to match their dreaming cadence—you would see that they have achieved a state of biological equilibrium with the machinery. Their skin retains the same film of condensate that slicks the boiler jackets, a perpetual aqueous armor that never evaporates, never chills. Under this layer, their epidermis has thickened, not into callouses but into something like the fresh basalt itself: porous, sharp-edged, accepting of chemical accretion. White calcium blooms across their knuckles; green copper oxide shadows the creases of their elbows; rust-red ferric stains map the iliac crests beneath their saturated work clothes. They do not wash. The chemistry of the place has colonized their dermis, and they have welcomed it, becoming inorganic-organic hybrids, biological components of the steady-state engine.

They move at the speed of mineral growth. When they traverse the gaps between the seventeen structures—crossing the intervals of young, sharp stone—they do so with the inexorable patience of stalactites extending. A trip from the intake tower to the refinery hall might consume three hours of visitor-time. Their steps fall in arrears to the 1800 RPM vibration; they surf the B-flat drone rather than resist it. Watch their hands as they polish valve stems that are already gleaming: the motion is circular, hypnotic, synchronized to some internal metronome that phases with the pumps. They are not maintaining the machinery in the sense of repair—nothing breaks—but rather they are *confirming* it, touching it to ensure it remains real, stroking the hot pipes as one might soothe a nervous animal.

They do not speak, or if they do, the frequency has been subsumed into the 1.8 sonic pressure that obliterates all other sound. Perhaps they communicate through the vibration itself, pressing palms against the corrugated steel walls to transmit pressure waves through the skeleton, messages written in the trembling of calcium deposits. When two of them meet in the narrow alleys between maintenance sheds, they pause. They touch foreheads. They stand there, sharing the hum, exchanging ionic transfers through the sweat that bridges their skin. It is a communion more intimate than language, a merging of the mineral self.

They eat, though the act has lost its urgency. They harvest the chemotrophic moss that blooms on the concrete, chewing the silica-rich fibers with teeth that have grown dense as geothermal gravel. They catch the volcanic gnats in nets woven from their own shed hair—hair that does not grow, merely persists, accumulating mineral sheen until it resembles copper wiring. They crack the thermophilic crabs with stone tools, drinking the hot hemolymph that steams at exactly 41 degrees, matching their core temperature. They do not cook; cooking implies transformation, change of state, and here entropy is the enemy. They consume only to maintain the boundary, to keep their bodies in the same pristine, Precambrian stasis as the rock beneath them.

Sleep is a misnomer. They enter dormancy standing up, leaning against the pump housings, allowing the B-flat to sweep through their thoracic cavities like a broom through an empty room. Their breathing synchronizes—three hundred revolutions per minute, a shallow, fluttering respiration that matches the compressors. In these standing dreams, they do not dream of elsewhere. The concept of *elsewhere* has atrophied, a muscle unused for seventeen years. They dream of pressure differentials, of the perfect equation of heat exchange, of the eternal balance between the geothermal bleed and the upward-falling rain.

They are not trapped. Not exactly. Visitors think they see misery in the slow eyes of the forty, but it is merely the absence of wanting. They have achieved what the pumps promise: freedom from decay, from the slow betrayal of the body by time. They do not age, or age so imperceptibly that a decade registers only as a slight darkening of the copper-stains on their temples. They do not sicken—the bacteria cannot bloom in their 60-degree blood, in their copper-tasting lungs. They are the perfect maintenance crew because they have become maintenance itself, self-sustaining, circular, feeding the system that feeds them.

There is one among them—there is always one—who remembers the sally port. Who stands at the boundary where the hot rain falls upward, facing the weathered wood and rusted steel, and raises a hand to trace the seam. But the hand moves slowly, dream-slow, and before the fingers can complete the gesture, the pumps thrum, the crabs click in their static herds, and the moment resets. The memory dissolves back into the mineral solution of the present.

They are the soft machinery. The wetware. The forty biological governors of a hamlet-sized clockwork that keeps the world out and the moment in. They wipe the condensation. They check the seals. They breathe the steam. They exist in the held chord, the long B-flat, the eternal now where nothing is lost because nothing is allowed to change.
