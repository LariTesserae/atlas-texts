They call themselves **the Felt**, or more accurately, they identify through the sensation of pressure against the sternum—the long, subsonic *mmmmm* that is the closest thing to a name they can share without touching. They are post-mammalian, post-auditory, and nearly post-carbon, though they still bleed, slowly, and their blood runs thick with copper salts the color of old pennies.

To look at one is to look at a geological event that has learned to stand upright. Their skeletons are not merely calcium anymore but have accreted brass filigree through centuries of drinking the metal-heavy condensation; their bones ring when tapped, each rib and phalange a tuning fork attuned to the specific frequency of the plateau’s hum. Their skin is translucent and gray, parchment-thin, beneath which you can see not veins but *runnels*—channels evolved (or engineered, the distinction having dissolved millennia ago) to guide the surgical water toward their mouths or away from their organs. They do not sweat; instead, they extrude a waxy, sulfur-yellow paste that feeds the fungal crusts growing in the hollows of their collarbones—a symbiosis that returns trace minerals to the skin, armor against the acid weep of the atmosphere.

They are born, rarely, in the shadow of the Condensation Vats, delivered not with cries but with vibrations: the mother presses the newborn against the warm brass floor, and the infant’s first act is to *listen* with its entire body, calibrating its nervous system to the tectonic drone. Childhood is not measured in years but in the rotation of the Great Eastern Cylinder. A toddler is someone who has seen the northern gear-tooth advance by the width of their hand; an elder is one who has known the piston to complete three full strokes. They age like the machines themselves—gradually, via erosion. Their skin accumulates the pitted patina of the surrounding karst; their joints grow stiffer not from arthritis but from mineral deposition, until the oldest among them become nearly statuesque, standing meditative in valve housings, functioning as living pressure gauges, their bodies communicating the machine’s health through the angle of their calcified necks.

 senses are entirely rewritten. Deaf to the air, they are exquisitely sensitive to the *substrate*. They travel by pressing their palms—wide and flattened, with nerve clusters as dense as a star-nosed mole’s nose—against the brass topography. Through the metal, they read the machine’s slow digestion: the groan of a bearing overheating three kilometers east, the clogging of a condensation channel to the north. Their language is a choreography of pressure. They greet by pressing their foreheads together, allowing the bone-conduction of the hum to synchronize their heartbeats. Arguments are conducted by tapping staccato rhythms into piston housings, sending insults or pleas vibrating through the continental structure at the speed of metal. Poetry is written by allowing condensation to bead on their skin in specific patterns, then pressing oneself against a wall, leaving a damp, evaporating ghost of sentiment that others read by touch, fading over days.

Their settlements are not homes so much as **insertions**. They nest in the exhaust vents and the hollows of rusted condenser towers, but they do not build; they *excavate* living spaces within the accreted mineral layers of the engines themselves, burrowing into the brass where it has softened with age and chemical weathering. They sleep in hammocks woven from the translucent hairlichen that feeds on the exhaust sulfur, and they eat by harvesting the rust-colored precipitates from the shallows—copper oxides, lead salts, and the chewy, sulfur-rich fungal mats that bloom where the hot flanks of the machinery meet the cold air. They are hollow-cheeked, gaunt, their metabolisms slowed to match the geological tempo of their food source. A meal is a handful of ochre mud, sucked slowly over hours, the metals precipitating out in their specialized guts.

They are the **maintenance**, but they do not know for whom the machine was built, nor do they question it. To them, the plateau is not a device but a deity—a slow, grinding god that is also the ground, the weather, and the father-clock. Their theology is tactile: they believe that friction is the original sin and lubrication the only grace. Their monks are the ones who tend to the "weeping," carefully channeling the condensate so that it does not corrode critical seams, harvesting the water not for drinking (there is enough for all, dripping endlessly) but for *placement*, moving it from one acoustic node to another to adjust the resonance, to ensure the hum remains steady and deafening. They brave the acoustic dead-zones where the vibration cancels out—areas of silence so terrifying to the Felt that to enter one is considered a form of death, a removal from the communal body of the sound.

And there are the others—the **calcified**. Not dead, but transformed. In old age, some choose to lie down upon the warmest surfaces, the flanks of the deepest flywheels, and cease moving. Over decades, they do not rot but mineralize, their flesh accepting the brass, their bodies merging with the gear-teeth until they become indistinguishable from the machine, silent sentinels that are part human, part geology, part engine. They are not worshipped, but they are consulted. Pilgrims travel for weeks to place their hands upon the shoulders of a calcified ancestor, reading in the vibration of that fused body the deep health of the plateau, the patient grinding of the continental bearings.

They dream, when they dream, of fast water—of rivers that run quick and noisy, of the sound of voices in the air rather than in the bone. But they wake to find their hands pressed against the warm, glandular metal, feeling the turn of the world beneath them, slow and heavy and eternal. They do not mourn the speed they have lost. They are the Resounding Plateau’s newest layer, the soft biology that has learned to live in the gaps of the mineralized intent, neither the builders nor the built, but the necessary friction that keeps the turning true.
