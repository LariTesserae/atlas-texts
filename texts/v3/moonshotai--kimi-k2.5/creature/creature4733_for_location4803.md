What lives here is *softness* made sentient—not in the sense of weakness, but of permeability. The Phytoplano has selected against rigidity for four hundred million years. Calcium is a poison here, a fossil memory. Instead, the inhabitants are colloids and gels, membranes and sinew, creatures that can press themselves through the fibrous peat as easily as a sigh passes through lips.

Begin with the **Drinkers**, who might be the closest things to stewards. They have no bones, or rather, their skeletons are hydrostatic—columns of pressurized fluid that allow them to stand upright when they wish, or to pour themselves into the amber lymph that seeps from the ground. Their skin is translucent amber itself, the color of the fluid they harvest, and you can watch their slow metabolism through their bellies: the dark lump of a meal dissolving over weeks, the gentle pulse of their two hearts. They move in pairs or threes, connected by tubes of living tissue that they grow between themselves, sharing blood sugars and neural impulses. To speak, they press their porous fingertips together and let hormones pass—whole conversations conducted in a chemical syntax of grief, hunger, and memory. They cultivate gardens of heat-sensing tubers in the upper peat, but their true nourishment comes from *deep-drinking*: they insert proboscises—flexible, hollow roots—two kilometers down into the warm core, tapping the subterranean smolder, sipping the entropy of ancient compression. They are patient addicts of warmth, descending into torpor when the internal fires wax, frenetic when they wane.

Above them, in the vaults of the lateral canopy, live the **Weavers**. These are not spiders but *travelers*—creatures that began as something like sloths, perhaps, but have become architecture. Their bodies are flat, leaf-shaped, fifty meters long but only centimeters thick, draped across the wooden arches like living tarps. They photosynthesize not with light—there is none worth using—but with the infrared glow of the plateau's respiration. Their undersides trail thousands of hair-fine filaments, which they play like harp strings, sending infrasound thrumming through the cellulose. This is how they hunt: the vibrations stun the spore-clouds, the floating plankton of this world, which they then absorb through dermal mouths. But more importantly, this is how they *garden*. By vibrating specific frequencies, they encourage the sequoia-like arches to grow in particular directions, reinforcing weak spans, redirecting the flow of capillary water. They are the engineers of thisbreathing cathedral, composing the forest into shapes that please their sonic aesthetics, creating resonant chambers where the mist hums with standing waves.

The mist itself is alive with the **Gelatinous Choir**—the "soft ones" mentioned, but more complex than mere slugs. They are colonial organisms, each "individual" being a swarm of microscopic flagellates suspended in a shared mucous matrix. They form shapes according to need: long torpedoes to spear through humid air, wide bells to capture condensation, intricate lattices to filter the rain. They communicate by changing their density, creating pockets of pressure that travel through the saturated atmosphere like smoke rings. When two colonies meet, they embrace, exchanging genetic material and memories—their entire history is encoded in the chemical composition of their slime. They leave trails that remain active for decades, phosphorescent highways that guide other creatures through the twilight. The largest colonies achieve a kind of wisdom, hovering in the thermal updrafts for centuries, growing vast and brain-like, processing the chemical signals of the plateau into slow thoughts. They are the closest thing to weather here, predicting the months-long storms by sensing the barometric shifts in the peat.

Beneath the Drinkers, in the crushing dark of the lower peat where the compression turns the organic matter into something like warm plastic, lurk the **Inverse Ones**. These are the miners of heat, serpentine beings that have abandoned the concept of "up." They are eyeless, earless, limbless, their bodies segmented and hydraulic, adapted to the increasing pressure as they bore toward the ancient bedrock. They eat the plateau itself, not as destruction but as cultivation—digesting the peat, extracting the thermal energy of its slow combustion, excreting refined compounds that feed the chemosynthetic roots above. They are the digestive tract of the world, and they are intelligent in the way of enzymes—purely chemical, purely decisive. Yet they have culture: they leave behind complex sculptures of compressed carbon, geodesic shapes that serve as pressure-chambers for their young. They communicate through the peat itself, sending pressure waves that travel faster through the dense organic matter than sound travels through air, a subterranean internet oftouch.

On the lithic islands—the calcified ruins of ancient forests—dwell the **Stone-Bound**, those who have chosen mineralization over decay. They are the descendants of the eyeless primates mentioned, but changed. They secrete substances that encourage the petrification of wood, binding themselves into the ancient heartwood towers, becoming living fossils. Their fur has evolved into lichen farms, their bodies half-carbon, half-silica. They are the historians, their neural structures having adapted to store information in crystal lattices rather than fragile neurons. They remember the plateau before the chemosynthesis, when there was still light. They sit motionless for years, processing these memories, then move in sudden bursts of calcified agility, rearranging the mineral structures of their islands, creating new hollows, new towers. They are attempting to build something—a machine, perhaps, or a prayer—out of the resistant stone, a counter-weight to the eternal softness. They envy and despise the Drinkers, these beings of fluid, and guard the borders of their hard islands with bitter chemical weapons.

Most mysterious are the **Spiral Navigators**, creatures that have evolved to exploit the non-Euclidean hydrology of the Phytoplano. They resemble nautiluses made of living peat, their shells spirals of compressed humus, their bodies small and phosphorescent knots at the center. They float in the capillary water that flows uphill, riding the pressure gradients, never touching solid matter. They are the traders and messengers, carrying spores and hormones between the isolated islands and the deep territories, their shells serving as seed-banks. They navigate by tasting the age of the water—new rain has a sharp, ozone taste; ancient lymph is sweet and thick. When they die, their shells sink, but slowly, taking centuries to settle, and as they descend, they release their cargo, seeding the depths with canopy life, ensuring that even two kilometers down, there is genetic memory of the sky.

And finally, there is the **Great Breathing**, which may or may not be a creature at all, but which all the others serve and fear. It is the distributed consciousness of the plateau, emerging from the sum total of chemical signals, root pressures, and spore exchanges. It thinks once every decade. When it exhales, the entire plateau contracts slightly, the peat compressing a millimeter more, the mist drawing back toward the surface. When it inhales, the roots swell, the Drinkers dream vivid dreams of heat, and the Weavers play chords that make the air shiver. It does not command, but it *tends*, adjusting the rate of decay, the flow of entropy, ensuring that this equilibrium persists. The inhabitants do not worship it—they *are* it, their neural chemistry temporarily syncing during the long storms, becoming neurons in a brain the size of a subcontinent, briefly achieving a consciousness vast enough to understand the slow, patient love of compression, the embrace of gravity, the peace of being utterly and finally consumed by the weight of one's own growth.

Here, to live is to dissolve and persist simultaneously. There are no sharp edges, no sudden deaths, no births that break the membrane between mother and world. Everything bleeds into everything else, slowly, in the warm, amber dark, forever approaching that final, perfect density where the distinction between creature and place will vanish entirely, and the Phytoplano will become, at last, purely itself.
