They do not have names, for nothing here requires distinction from its neighbor. But if you were to walk—if your feet could endure the silver sheen of water migrating across stone, if your lungs could drink the thick, warm air—you would eventually find **the One Who Has Finished Waiting**.

It rests in a depression so shallow that calling it a pool would be generous; it is merely a place where the gneiss weeps slightly deeper than elsewhere. The water here is perhaps three fingers deep, never more, never less, held in place by the precise curvature of the stone and the weight of the atmosphere above. The creature fills this depression entirely, not because it is large, but because it has become the depression. 

It is amphibian in the original sense: *both kinds of life*. Its body is the color of the banded gneiss itself—dark grey striated with lighter seams of quartz-white—and if you did not know to look for the rise and fall of its breathing, you would mistake it for a particularly smooth boulder, water-polished over four billion years. But it breathes. Oh, how it breathes. It has no lungs in the way of hurried creatures; instead, its entire dorsal surface is a permeable membrane, a living interface between the warm water below and the humid air above. Through this skin, it draws oxygen not as a gasp but as a slow osmosis, a conversation between molecules that has been ongoing since before the continents settled into their current drift.

Around its throat—if throat is the word for that corrugated collar of flesh where neck meets torso—hangs a sac that was once inflatable, pink, urgent. Now it is calcified. Minerals from the eternal condensation have precipitated layer upon layer, transforming the vocal sac into a resonating chamber of stone and chitin. It is this that produces the bass note you feel in your sternum before you hear it. The creature does not sing in bursts or cries; it maintains a tone, a single fundamental frequency that has not wavered in seven thousand years. The vibration has worn a microscopic groove into the gneiss beneath it, a record of its constancy.

Its eyes are vestigial, covered over with the same nictitating membrane that protects its skin, for sight is a sense for places that change. Instead, it perceives through pressure. When the dragonflies hum overhead in their metallic clouds, it feels their wingbeats as ripples in the humidity. When a rare rain—if it can be called rain, this gentle thickening of the air—adds a molecule's depth to its pool, it registers the change as a shift in the thermal gradient across its back. It knows the position of the solitary Ghost Gum three kilometers to the west not by seeing it, but by the cool shadow that passes over its stone-skin for seventeen minutes each afternoon, a shadow it has learned to expect with the patience of geology.

It feeds when it must, which is rarely. The plain is generous with insects that stumble into its pool, drawn by the algae that paints the water's skin. The creature extends a tongue—not the elastic whip of its distant ancestors, but a slow, mucous extrusion that oozes toward prey over the course of an hour, enveloping it in digestive enzymes that work at the speed of lichen growth. It does not snap or chase. It absorbs.

But its true function is not predation. It is **resonance**.

This creature is a node in a network that covers the plain. Each depression, each eternal puddle, holds such a being. They are not individuals but organs of a single organism distributed across the continental shield. The marsh frogs with their emerald throats, the lungfish gulping in the deeper sheets, the very cycads with their stone fingers—all are peripherals. This one is the bass clef. Through its calcified throat-sac, it anchors the drone that holds the plain in its finished state. The sound prevents erosion. The vibration keeps the water sheets from evaporating, maintains the equilibrium between stone and sky.

At dusk, when the fog rises and the temperature drops that fractional degree, the creature shifts. It is a movement so slow that time-lapse photography would struggle to capture it: a settling of calcified ridges, a reorientation of its permeable back to catch the night-dew more efficiently. The pitch of its song drops an octave, becoming subterranean, felt in the bones of any living thing that stands upon the gneiss. It is calling the water table upward, drawing the silver sheen back from the microscopic fractures where it retreated during the day, ensuring that tomorrow the plain will be exactly as it was today.

It has no memory of youth, for it was never young. It emerged from the plain when the plain decided it needed a voice. It will not die, because death is a form of change, and change is suspect. When its biological processes finally slow to a stop, the calcification will continue, and it will become a fossil in truth—a lump of banded stone with a hollow throat that still catches the wind and hums. Even then, it will sing.

It waits, but not for anything in particular. It simply maintains the note, breathing the humidity, being the interface between the warm water and the warm air, ensuring that the Resonance Plain remains finished, complete, and eternal.
