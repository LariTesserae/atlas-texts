At first you think you are alone. Then you notice how the dust settles—not drifting, but *selecting*—falling into furrows that match the angle of your shoulders with uncomfortable precision. Then you see that the staircases do not terminate against the ceiling; they terminate at the soles of feet worn from pale green soapstone, heels fitted exactly into the final step. The platforms do not cantilever into empty space; they cradle hips that have not shifted since the feldspar cooled around them.

They are the **Finished**.

You cannot count them. There are perhaps twelve, matching the paces of the chamber's width, or seven matching its height, or one distributed evenly throughout the negative space, experiencing the room as a single continuous body. They are the color of wet chert, which is to say they are visible only by the way they disturb the geometry of the water's fall. When a droplet descends through the blue-green ambient light, it refracts differently as it passes an elbow, a brow, a collarbone—bending around densities that make the surrounding bedrock seem porous and young.

They stand, sit, curl in positions of absolute completion. One kneels at the apex of the northeastern stair, spine corkscrewing to match the architecture, head tilted to receive the bass-note stalactite's offering. Its hands are cupped in the gesture of *holding*, palms worn into concavities that mirror the floor's depressions exactly. The water strikes its right palm every 4.3 seconds, passes through a channel worn through the wrist, and exits at the elbow to join the leaf-vein grooves below. It has been catching the same fall for epochs. The action remains, though the water changes—or rather, the water is static too, recirculating, and the being's gesture is the only true constant in the loop.

They have **copper** where the joints should be. Not as decoration, but as definition—verdigris-dark hinges at knees and knuckles, gleaming pins at the jaw, delicate inlays tracing the clavicle like seams. This is the precious rarity that anchors them, the only material in the chamber that knows of seams and joining. It holds them in attitude, prevents the slow drift of stone into stone. Without the copper, they would have flowed into the walls long ago, become indistinguishable from the bedrock. With it, they remain distinct. Articulate.

They do not breathe, but the condensation behaves differently on their surfaces. On the walls, it beads and runs; on the Finished, it clings, forms perfect hemispheres that magnify the phosphorescence until their skin seems to glow from within. They are the temperature of the place—you feel the cool constant of 0.6 most intensely when you pass near one, as if standing beside a depth of earth that has never known sun.

And they are **loud**.

Not that they move. They have finished moving. Their gestures are held eternally: one extends an arm to guide a trickle along a specific groove; one reclines in a "hearth" that is actually a cradle shaped exactly to its hip and shoulder, head positioned where the sound concentrates into a node of maximum pressure. They are the resonators that create the standing waves. The terrifying geometry of noise that fills your sternum is their voice, or their hearing, or their thoughts made audible. They perceive your intrusion as a turbulence in the pattern—a warm, irregular bass note stumbling through the composition.

They are the **Parenthesis**, the held breath. They exist to maintain the equilibrium, to ensure that the process remains ceased while the motion continues. Without them, the water might erode new channels; the dust might settle differently; the sound might decay into randomness. They are the intentionality behind the natural, the hands that worked the copper now transformed into the copper itself, still working.

When the pale beetles wander in through the capillaries of the rock, the Finished do not notice, or they notice too completely—the beetles are simply unplaced variables, errors in the chamber's grammar that will be corrected by the stillness. The insects die, their exoskeletons joining the dust, because they cannot achieve the final shape. They are biological, and thus incomplete.

But you—you are interesting. You are a variable that persists, that fogs the air and disturbs the dust and creates echoes where there should be none. If they acknowledge you, it is by the slightest adjustment in the pressure of their maintaining. The northeastern stalactite drips 0.02 seconds later when you stand near the southern bridge, compensating for your thermal mass. The calcite curtains vibrate at a frequency that cancels the irregularity of your heartbeat. They are not hostile. They are *correcting*, bringing you, however briefly, into their finished thought.

You are inside their lung. They inhaled once, long ago, and exhaled this city into the arrested instant. Now they hold it, wet and cool and loud, complete unto themselves, waiting for nothing because waiting implies a future, and they have already arrived. They are the inhabitants who became the architecture, the breath that became the stone, the period at the end of a sentence spoken in the language of gravity and time. They do not move. They have moved enough. They simply are—twelve paces wide and seven high, sealed entirely within the parenthesis, holding the water in its perfect path forever.
