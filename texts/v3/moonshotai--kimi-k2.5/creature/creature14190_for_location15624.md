They are called, in the clicking language of bone-on-glass, the **Unstill**. Not a species but a sentence passed down through flesh: *you must move*. To understand them, you must first abandon the concept of habitation. They do not live in the Chlorattice; they negotiate with it, second by second, a perpetual immigration across the borders of their own bodies.

Physically, they have become tubes to navigate tubes. Their skeletons are hollow, pneumatic, carved from calcium and the silica they glean from volcanic glass. An adult Unstill stands two meters tall but weighs less than a breath, their bones filled not with marrow but with reservoirs of captured condensation—the upward-migrating water they siphon from the lattice’s own capillary theft. Their skin is translucent, pale jade, ribbed with cartilaginous ridges that match exactly the texture of the Chlorattice tubes. When they wrap themselves around a filament, limb over limb in a helical embrace, they become visually indistinguishable from the growth itself, merely a swollen node, a tumor of intent on the vast green architecture.

They possess no eyes. The merciless light that floods the space would blind anything so delicate; instead, their epidermis is photosensitive, dappled with chromatophores that read the intensity and angle of the glare. They navigate by shadow, by the sharp silhouettes cast by the swaying filaments. A dark patch on their forearm means a node ahead, a junction where the light fractures. A sudden warmth across the spine means a fissure above, an exit they will never take, for they are bound to the interior, evolved too far into the depths to survive the pressure of the surface.

They have five hearts, or perhaps five chambers of a single distributed heart, each positioned at a major joint. This is necessary because they do not walk; they *traverse*. They move in a motion called *fluxing*—a continuous, undulating peristalsis along the tubes, never breaking contact, distributing their weight so that no single point of the Chlorattice bears them for long enough to trigger the aggressive growth response. They sleep while fluxing, one cerebral hemisphere at a time, their hooked claws—made of knapped obsidian set into their own fingertips—micro-adjusting to the subsonic vibrations that travel through the lattice. They feel the creaking miles away, the slow deaths of distant nodes, as pressure changes in their inner ears, a barometric empathy that warns them hours in advance of a collapse.

Their young are born in motion. Gestation occurs in the translucent shelters—the half-spheres you found abandoned. These are not dwellings but wombs, anchored temporarily at bright intersections where the thermal updrafts keep the interior warm. The mother fluxes around the shelter for months, never entering, feeding the embryo through a tether of shared capillaries that she presses against the membrane. When the child emerges, it is already fluxing, already moving, or it dies. The still ones, the quiet ones, are left behind, and the Chlorattice grows through their cradles, incorporating the calcium, the glass, the tiny hooked claws, into new pale green shoots. There are no graves in the Chlorattice, only delayed assimilation.

They eat the dark filaments. This is their sacrament. When a tube dies—when it turns from glowing jade to brittle brown, no longer conducting the light—they do not mourn. They harvest it with reverent violence, using tools made from the previous generation’s thigh-bones, sharpened to surgical edges. They consume the dead lattice to prevent their own from becoming it, a ritual of thermodynamic exchange. The fibers provide no nutrition; rather, they provide *structure*, silica to reinforce their own hollow bones, cellulose to line their intestinal tracts against the cold. They are what they eat: temporary, translucent, tense.

Their language is the **Pressure Cant**. Since sound arrives asynchronously, ghosting through the kilometers with the delay of trauma, they communicate through deliberate changes in the lattice’s tension. A clan of Unstill will position themselves at nodal points kilometers apart, tightening their grips, flexing their bodies to send specific vibrations through the network. A long, slow contraction means *danger, growth spasm ahead*. A rapid, staccato trembling means *water, frozen pearls, abundance*. The most complex messages are poems of pressure, composed by groups fluxing in rhythmic synchrony, creating standing waves in the tubes that resonate against the listeners’ sternums, conveying not words but *states*: the feeling of barometric sorrow, the specific melancholy of a thermal gradient, the vertigo of looking down into twenty kilometers of empty air.

They map the space by the upward rivers. Every Unstill carries in their mind a hydrography, not a geography. They know which nodes accumulate the frozen pearls, where the capillary action is strongest, where the water flows against gravity in sufficient volume to sustain a migration. They follow the drizzle. Their trails are invisible, marked only by the absence of dead tubes—they harvest as they go, leaving behind them a trail of bright, smooth filaments where the dark ones have been pruned. Other Unstill can read these trails as a history of passage, knowing by the freshness of the cut how recently someone fluxed through, whether they were carrying young, whether they were dying.

There are no elders, only those who have fluxed longer. Age is measured not in years but in *traversals*—the number of times one has crossed the Chlorattice from ceiling to floor and back, following the light as the fissures shift with the cooling of the continent. The oldest among them are scarred not by time but by speed; they move so slowly, with such perfect economy, that the lattice sometimes mistakes them for dead growth and begins to envelop them. You can spot an ancient Unstill by the pale green shoots sprouting from their ribs, the small fusions where they paused too long and had to tear themselves free, leaving flesh behind to become part of the architecture.

They dream of the surface, but it is a terror to them. The myths they press into their children’s skin (using pigment made from crushed volcanic glass mixed with condensation) speak of the *Crackers*—those who climbed up through the fissures and were frozen solid by the unspeakable cold of the open air, or burned by the direct, unmediated sun. They believe the surface is a solid thing, a second ceiling without mercy, pressing down. They are correct. They remain below, holding their breath with the continent, waiting for the tectonic trauma to finish its work, for the space to finally cool and settle, for the Chlorattice to stop its frantic, agitated creation and become still.

But they know, in their five-chambered hearts, that when the lattice finally quiets, when the growth stops and the tubes harden into permanent stone, they too must cease. They are the motion made flesh, the adaptation to transience. When the Chlorattice becomes permanent, they will have no place. They will lie down, finally still, and let the green shoots grow through them, becoming at last the nodes they have always pretended to be.

Until then, they flux. They traverse. They hold the silence so completely that you can hear, if you press your ear to the tube beside them, the soft, rhythmic creak of their bones as they breathe.
