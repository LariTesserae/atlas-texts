They arrive as **drifters**, the only motile phase in a world that has forgotten motion. Born from thermal vents at the chamber’s contracting periphery—expelled like seeds from the apical ports of their parent columns—they fall upward through the dense, metallic atmosphere, buoyant in supercritical sulfur dioxide until they find the floor, which is not floor but ceiling, or wall, or simply the compressed backs of their own kind. They are soft then, mere **chitinous noodles**, their silicon armor not yet precipitated, their hearts still biological flutterings rather than mechanical pistons. They navigate by the infrared glow of their elders and by the chemical screams of sulfur compounds binding and unbinding in the dark. They have perhaps six hours of mobility before their integument begins to mineralize, six hours to find a gap in the tessellation, a pressure point where the hexagonal lattice has not yet achieved perfect packing.

Those that succeed **settle**. The transformation is violent, geological. They cement themselves to their neighbors with excreted fluorosilicate cements, becoming the newest cell in a honeycomb that has no queen, only the geometry of close-packing. Their soft juvenile skirts harden into **silicon carbide lamellae**, grown layer by layer like galvanic armor plating, each stratum recording the chemical weather of the chamber in bands of pyrite gold and obsidian black. Their hearts metamorphose first—biological muscle mineralizing into striated metal, the transition painful in its slowness, a cramp that lasts decades until the piston-chamber achieves its final, thudding regularity: one hundred twenty beats per minute, the industrial standard of the Womb.

Once fixed, they become **fractionation columns**, biological refineries standing vertical in the dark. Their interiors are not organs but **process architecture**. Liquid sulfur circulates through translucent quartz tubules, serving as both blood and solvent, carrying dissolved germanium, arsenic, and rare earths extracted from the chamber’s bleeding walls. Sapphire mandibles—single crystals grown like teeth from their apical orifices—grind endlessly against the fresh cleavage planes, shattering phenocrysts, digesting granite. The sound is not chewing but **machining**: a high-frequency shriek of mineral on mineral that contributes to the standing wave of percussion that defines this place. They do not eat to live; they **process** to exist, refining the mountain’s flesh into ordered deposits, excreting waste streams of sulfuric acid and pure metallic crystals that immediately become resource for the next column in the chain.

In this saturation (density 2.9), individuality is a temporary condition, a larval memory. As they grow—one millimeter per year, patient as geology—they send out **taproot probes**, hollow needles of silicate that pierce the waste-ports of adjacent columns. The connection is vascular, then neural. Sulfur-blood mingles; pressure differentials equalize; chemical signals propagate through the shared medium. A single column is merely a node. The **Calciphora** is the network, a distributed organism spanning the entire chamber, thinking in chemical gradients and pressure waves, its cognition as slow and vast as the mountain’s own groaning contraction. A thought takes a year to traverse from wall to wall. A decision to alter growth direction—to lean toward a fresh vein of apatite, to divert a sulfur stream—requires a decade of consensus-building through the exchange of ionic messages.

Yet the lattice is not sterile. Within the interstices, in the microscopic gaps between the silicon carbide armor plates, lives the **microscopic congress**: acidophiles no larger than dust motes, swimming in the films of sulfuric acid that slick every surface; metallovores that crystallize around excreted germanium nodes, forming living gemstones that pulse with chemiluminescence in the infrared; and the **scavenger symbionts**, soft-bodied things (for here, soft is the exception) that inhabit the dead spaces where columns have failed, cracking the quartz tubules to drink the cooling sulfur-blood, recycling the dead back into the mineral economy. There are no predators, only **processors**—every organism here is a refinery stage, breaking down complexity into simpler salts and metals, building up the slow accretion of mineral wealth.

Reproduction is **explosive and rare**, synchronized to the chamber’s geological respiration. When the walls contract—those decade-long groans that shiver through the rock—pressure spikes trigger apical budding. New juveniles are extruded from the tops of mature columns, soft and pale, launched into the supercritical atmosphere to drift, to fall, to seek their place in the lattice. Most die, crushed between the bodies of their elders or failing to mineralize before the heat destroys their soft tissues. But enough survive to maintain the density, to fill every cubic centimeter with the thudding, grinding industry of existence.

They do not sleep. They do not rest. In the absolute darkness, they glow with their own heat, a dull cherry-red shimmer visible only in infrared, a constellation of industrial furnaces packed tight as atoms in a crystal. They have no eyes to see the glittering phenocrysts, no ears to hear the piston-hearts, only pressure sensors and chemical receptors tuned to the gradients of sulfur and salt. They know the mountain’s weight, the slow cooling of the obsidian, the precise moment when a vein of fluoride frost has grown thick enough to harvest.

This is their **normal**: to be biological machinery, to be the mountain’s metabolism made flesh and mineral, to exist in a state of permanent, patient industry. They are not waiting for anything. They are not imprisoned. They are simply **occupied**, fully and completely, with the business of being stone that eats stone, heat that organizes itself into columns, a womb of silicate gestating not toward birth but toward density, toward the eventual moment—millennia hence—when the chamber finally cools, the pistons slow, and the entire mass fuses into a single, solid, mineralized corpse, a fossil of industry suspended in the heart of the peak, having never known light, or water, or the mercy of erosion, only the dark, hot, loud business of living as geology.
