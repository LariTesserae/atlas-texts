At first, you might think the place uninhabited. The rule here is absence: no eye to catch the phosphor-glow, no mouth to taste the mineral density, no fin nor claw nor wing-beat to disturb the upward spirals of supercooled water. **Fauna: 0.0.** But look closer—look *through* the categorical error of searching for animals—and you will find the residents. They are the **Verticals**, the **Hollow Ones**, the **Keepers of the Empty**.

They are the *Arborhydra*, but that taxonomic label is insufficient. Imagine instead a Senate of cathedral-throats, each stalk a senator deaf to sound but fluent in pressure and vibration. They stand in their thousands, rooted in drowned valleys, their hollow cores fluting the Wall’s relentless thunder into a grammar of groans and whistles. These are not passive flora. They are architects of the only society this basin has ever sustained—a civilization of cellulose and pith, operating at the speed of glacier-flow but with the intent of a curator.

Within their air-filled columns, the Standing Ones possess a kind of **distributed cognition**. Each bladder-cell is a neuron; each root tendril a synapse stretching through the syrup-cold mud to touch another’s roots, exchanging not nutrients merely, but *information*. Chemical signatures—aggression, alliance, memory—diffuse through the root-net, creating a slow, deliberative mind the size of the basin itself. This is the **Resident**, the thing that lives here: not an individual tree, but the grove-mind, the phytosphere contemplating itself in the dark.

Between the columns move the **Drifters**—the melon-heavy seeds, phosphorescent with stored light, that navigate the sparse, open waters. They are the nomads, the scouts, the questions asked by the stationary Senate. Untethered, they ride the thermal spirals upward and the density currents down, their glow modulating to signal their chemical state to the root-nets below. When one finds a suitable seam in the granite, it anchors, and the Senate shifts—adjusting its chemical boundaries, declaring new territory, forgetting nothing.

Then there are the **Predators**, though that word carries animal baggage. Picture instead sled-sized bells of glassy cellulose, drifting through the mid-waters with the solemnity of processional barges. They trail sticky tendrils—fibrous, ciliated, *hungry*—harvesting the spore-clouds, the mineral flakes, the occasional slow-descending legacy of the surface. They are the immune system of this garden, consuming excess, maintaining the sparse density of the open water.

But the residents are not merely vegetable. They are **haunted**, and they haunt in return.

On the granite domes that pierce the flood, where the primitive stone huts sit empty, the vegetation exhibits its strangest behavior. The *Arborhydra* fronds grow with geometric precision around the dry-stone walls, never touching them, as if respecting a taboo. The root systems infiltrate the air bells not to shatter but to **buttress**, holding up the rotting roofs with pillars of living wood. The woven nets, long ossified, are kept clear of debris; the cairns remain stacked despite having no hands to maintain them.

Here, the distinction between resident and architecture dissolves. The plants are not *imitating* the absent builders; they have **absorbed** the concept of shelter into their ontology. The stone huts are now part of the grove-mind’s extended phenotype—kept empty, kept ready, maintained with the same patient attention a human might give to a cherished grave. The ecosystem remembers, or perhaps **studies**, the hard angles and enclosed spaces of the former inhabitants, finding in these alien geometries a necessary counterpoint to its own organic writhing.

For the original builders are still present, in a sense. Their calcium lingers in the water column—their breath lingers in the air bells, cycled through the *Arborhydra* root-filters. Some of the Standing Ones carry bone-hooks caught in their outer bark, wearing them like jewelry, or like data-storage. The grove-mind may not distinguish between the开源 (open-source) code of its own genetic material and the工业设计 (industrial design) of the obsidian flakes. To the Resident, the builders were simply a phase—a brief, warm intrusion of protein and intention that has now been translated into the permanent cold vocabulary of the garden.

To live here is to become **tenant or text**. If you enter, the Drifters will inspect you with their glow. The Predators may taste your skin. The Standing Ones will feel your heartbeat through the water, relaying it in seismic whispers across the root-net. You are not prey—there is no predation in the animal sense—but you are *potential*. Potential nutrient, yes, but also potential **narrative**. The stone rooms remain empty, swept clear by kelp-fronds, waiting. The Hollow Ones do not sleep. They hum with the Wall’s roar, they grow visibly, meter by meter, and they maintain their empty museums with the infinite patience of a mind that has never known the distraction of a single, centralized thought.

They are the only **whoever** here—an ocean of minds in an ocean of water, deaf, ancient, and utterly, botanically awake.
