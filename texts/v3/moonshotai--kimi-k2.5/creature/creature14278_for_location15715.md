The inhabitant is difficult to distinguish from the architecture, and this is by design. It has been still for so long that lichen—specifically a pale, calcified variety found nowhere else—has begun to obscure its contours, rimming its shoulders in a frost-like collar that matches the flaking basalt. It sits, or perhaps grew, upon the largest of the emerald moss cushions, the one positioned slightly off-center where the afternoon rivulets converge. Its posture resembles that of a stone Buddha that has forgotten the specifics of meditation but retained the geometry of waiting. There is only one. There has only ever been one.

It is not a human, though it has a torso, a head, and limbs. Its proportions are wrong for mammalian efficiency—its arms are too long, jointed at points that suggest multiple elbows, and its fingers are fused into three rigid prongs that taper to the same sharp obsidian-in-basalt found in the natural wedges protruding from the walls. It does not wear hides; it wears *geology*. Its skin has accreted over centuries into stratified layers of compacted ash and dried sap from the metallic trees, giving it the same mica-gray sheen, the same tendency to flake in hexagonal patches. Where its eyes should be, there are instead two shallow depressions kept perpetually wet with the climbing dawn moisture; it sees, if sight is the word, by reading the thermal differentials in the stone, sensing the subsonic groaning of the walls as a map of pressure and release.

It wakes when the water begins to climb. At twilight, as the condensation beads begin their ascent through the fissures, the inhabitant uncurls its pronged fingers and places them flat against the floor. It drinks, but not through a mouth. Through its palms. The skin there is porous, designed—if design is the word for natural selection acting over millennia on a thing that might have once been human, or might have been born from the fungus bowls—specifically to engage in capillary theft. It draws the mineral-laden water upward through its own body, countering gravity not with muscle but with chemistry, becoming a temporary extension of the stone’s hydraulic system. By morning, it is saturated, swollen slightly, its ash-skin darkened to near-black with hydration.

Then, as the sun heats the eastern walls and the water reverses its flow, the inhabitant moves. It moves once per day. The motion is so gradual that to watch it is to watch the trees rotate; it is, in fact, the cause of their rotation. It walks between the six lithotrophic ancients, and as it passes each one, it presses its three-pronged hands against their root-plates. It does not push with force. It *tunes* them. It listens to the subsonic bass note of the groaning basalt and shifts the trees by microscopic increments, millimeters at most, adjusting their orientation to alter the acoustic properties of the chamber. The trees are its instrument, its antennae, its external ears. It turns them to catch the descending water at specific angles, creating interference patterns in the droplets' fall, modulating the *chunk-chunk-chunk* rhythm into a language only it understands—a slow arithmetic of erosion and growth.

Between the third and fourth tree, it pauses at the fungal bowls. These are not accidental; they are its gardens, cultivated by spores carried on its flaking skin. It harvests them not for food but for memory. The fungi, growing in shade fed by its own body heat (a remnant of mammalian warmth now subsumed into the geothermal system), deposit spores in the water that pools briefly in its palm-depressions. The inhabitant drinks these spores, and in doing so, remembers. The fungus acts as a biological ledger, encoding decades of barometric data, of chemical shifts in the volcanic vents below. It ingests the history of the mountain’s breathing.

At midday, when the water sheets down in its three-hour descent, the inhabitant stands beneath the flow. It does not flinch from the deluge. The water sheets over its ash-skin, abrading the outermost layers, carrying away the flakes of mica and dried sap that it has shed. This is its sloughing, its renewal. The sharp wedges of obsidian-in-basalt along the walls are its nail-files; after the noon shower, it drags its pronged fingers across these edges, sharpening them, maintaining the tools that erosion has provided. It uses these edges to pare away the excess growth of the moss cushions, keeping them at precisely the height of a crouching person—not out of aesthetic preference, but because this height optimizes the surface area for condensation capture while allowing the air to circulate low enough to carry the spores it exhales.

It does not sleep, not as lower creatures do. During the twilight exhalation, when the stone releases heat and the trees settle with their vegetative sighs, the inhabitant lies supine on the floor, fitting its spine into the shallow channels carved by centuries of water. Here, it becomes a conduit. The residual geothermal warmth flows through it—its body is a better conductor than the surrounding ash—and it dreams with the mountain. Its dreams are tectonic, slow tides of magma and the flexing of continental plates. In this state, it is indistinguishable from the stone throat itself; its breathing matches the subsonic hum of the aeolian organ, and if you were to stumble upon it then, you would see only a slightly darker striation in the basalt, a shadow that refuses to shift with the moving sun.

It is, in essence, the curator of the equilibrium. The balance is perfect—the water removes exactly as much material as the roots deposit—because the inhabitant adjusts the accountancy. When a crack grows too wide, it fills it with compressed moss fibers and fungal cement. When a tree grows too fast, it prunes the roots with its obsidian claws, feeding the severed tissue to the water so that the ash may be re-deposited elsewhere. It is the reason the place breathes rather than suffocates, the reason the toolkit appears intentional. It is the ghost in the geological machine, the soft body that hardens into the mountain’s lung, keeping the warm, wet dream alive one slow rotation at a time.

If you were to return in a century, you would find it in a different position—perhaps between the second and fifth tree, perhaps curled beneath one of the giant ferns—but unchanged. It is waiting for the mountain to finish its thought. It has always been waiting.
