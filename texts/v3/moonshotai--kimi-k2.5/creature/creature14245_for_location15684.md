They are **softness holding its breath against hardening**.

To see them first, look for the **pale articulations**—knees and elbows hyperextended from decades of net-crawling, of bracing between calcite blades that did not exist when they were born. Their bones are light, porous, almost birdian; evolution here selected for buoyancy in a world that constantly tries to mineralize you. Their skin carries the **dust of fresh travertine**, not as decoration but as exhalation—their pores secrete a thin alkaline sweat that binds to the air, leaving them perpetually dusted in white, ghosts against the darker wet stone. They move with the economy of those who know that sound is a precious currency. Their language is **subvocal**, conducted largely in the touch of fingertips to throats, in the exchange of breath across nostrils, in the signed patterns of chert-knappers whose hammers fall muffled by felted moss.

They call themselves **The Held**, or simply **The Breathing**, though they seldom speak even these names aloud.

From birth, they are taught **The Shaving**. Every quarter-turn of the seasons, they take their curved chert blades and scrape their walls, their ceilings, their sleeping alcoves. The removed matter—cream-colored curls still soft as cheese when fresh—is not waste but **provision**. They eat it, some of it, mixing the scrapings with reeds and the phosphorescent algae that rim the seeps, creating a paste that sustains them when the alkaline water poisons most plants. The rest they **redepose**, weaving the shavings into the nets that connect their homes, hardening into rope, into ladder-rungs, into the thin translucent membranes of their windows. They are the only species that harvests time itself, that recycles the immediate past to build the present.

They navigate the three-dimensional crush with **calcified grace**. Unlike the Skerren, who bound blade-to-blade with ossified joints, the Held retain flexibility but move as if they, too, might calcify. They pause before stepping, testing the stone’s mood. They know each net walkway has a resonant frequency; to hum the wrong note while crossing is to announce your weight, your location, your intent. They travel in **kinetic silence**, using the cantilevered nets not as bridges but as **harps**, plucking the gut-strings to send vibrations ahead of them, clearing paths, warning those above and below of their approach.

But their true art is **Hydromancy**—the reading of the silicate films.

They keep shallow pans beneath their sleeping mats, catching the drips that fall from above. In the morning, before the surface tension breaks, they study the **memory-ghosts** captured there: the shadow of a neighbor rising in the night, the passing of a Skerren, the tremor of a new spring opening in the deep. They have learned to read the magnetic alignments of the silica like scripture, discerning not the future but the **imminent**—the water records intention as surely as it records flesh. A child is taught first to break the surface of any still water they find, to shatter the mirror before it fixes their image too permanently. They grow up knowing they are being constantly recorded by the world, that their shadows linger in puddles, that privacy is a heresy against the geology.

Their dead are **not buried but installed**.

When breath stops, the body is carried to the freshest terrace, the most recently birthed flowstone. There, the community works quickly while the stone is still damp and workable. They hollow a niche—not with sorrow but with precision—and place the corpse within, braced in a fetal position. Then they pour the **quick-water**, the most alkaline seeps, over the form. In days, the body is **armored**, enrobed in silica and lime. It becomes a **structural member**, a floor joist, a countertop, a step in the ascending verticality. To live here is to accept that you will eventually support your grandchildren’s weight. The ancestors are not remembered; they are **stood upon**, **slept beside**, **cooked over**. The heat of fires dims against their phosphorescent shells, creating a secondary glow that maps the settlement’s interior like veins in marble.

They have no mirrors, but they know their faces through the **reflections in each other’s eyes**—and through the water-memories they steal glimpses of, the silicate fossils that show them how they move, how they cast their shadows, how they disturb the air.

Their calendar is **geological**. They do not measure years by stars but by **accretion layers**—the Winter White, the Cream Spring, the Blue Deep when the phosphorescence is strongest. A generation is not twenty-five years but **three shavings**, the time it takes for a sleeping alcove to narrow by half. They speak of “the hardening” as others speak of winter, preparing for the day when a favorite passage becomes too brittle to traverse, when a beloved window occludes completely.

They are **becoming architecture**. 

Their culture is one of **tender maintenance against inevitable engulfment**. They love the stone that will devour them; they carve their names into the fresh deposits only to shave them away when the stone threatens to trap the letters forever. They are midwives to a landscape birthing itself in violent haste, curating the growth, negotiating with seeps, sleeping in nets suspended above their own future tombs. 

They are quiet, pale, dusted with the future, constantly erasing themselves from walls that remember everything, eating their surroundings to survive, and leaving behind only the **resonance of their footsteps** in the mineral lattice—a hum that persists in the stone long after the flesh has been accreted, fossilized, and raised up to become the roof of the world.
