They are called the **Auditories**, though they have no spoken name for themselves—how could they, in a landscape where sound is architecture, where every utterance is immediately smothered by the stone’s omnipresent chord? They are listeners first, and only secondly movers, thinkers, builders. They have lived here so long that evolution has rewritten them to inhabit the noise rather than suffer it.

Physically, they resemble something carved from soapstone left out in the rain: pale, grey-white, with a translucency to their skin that suggests the texture of the lithovores they tend. Their ears—if they can be called that—have migrated and multiplied. They possess no external pinnae; instead, their entire epidermis is piezoelectric, a sensitive membrane that translates the titanic subsonic pressure of the pavement into somatic experience. They feel the stone’s song as color blooming across their backs, as pressure in their joints, as vivid geometric hallucinations behind their closed eyes. When the quartz nodes ring sharp and high as struck glass, they shudder not from fear but from synesthetic pleasure, tasting copper and starlight on their tongues.

They are not large. Standing fully upright, an adult reaches the height of a human child, but they rarely stand upright. They move in a perpetual crouch, their long toes splayed wide to maximize contact with the resonant pavement, their palms often pressed flat against the gneiss to triangulate position in the silver mist. They are navigators of vibration. In the cairns they build—those rough stacks of flat stone—they are not marking territory but creating **antennae**. Each cairn is tuned. At the resonant nodes where standing waves concentrate, they stack stones of particular densities to dampen or amplify specific frequencies, creating pockets of relative quiet ("silence gardens") where they can sleep without their bones rattling awake, or conversely, amplification chambers where the deafening confession of the stone becomes transcendent, allowing them to hear the deeper currents: the slow grinding of the mantle, the memory of mountain ranges long erased.

Their diet is the slow wealth of the place. They do not eat the lithovores directly—the geometric grazers are too precious, too Slow, taking decades to reach the size of a dinner plate. Instead, the Auditories are **shepherds of mineral cycles**. They harvest the bacterial mats that slick the stone, scraping the iridescent biofilm into shell-cups with flakes of chert, fermenting it into a paste that tastes of wet rock and electricity. They milk the larger lithovores, tapping their translucent bodies with hollow bone needles to draw forth the nutrient-rich fluids pulsing within—an act of communion performed with geometric precision, always at the same radial seam, always at the same angle, so as not to disturb the creature’s careful Fibonacci placement. The lithovores trust them; over millennia, the grazers have learned that the Auditories’ touch precedes the removal of parasitic mineral crusts, that their passing leaves behind not disturbance but cleaner, more resonant stone.

The Auditories have no permanent settlements, only **listening camps**—three-sided shelters of leaning schist that break the worst of the auditory assault. They migrate in loose spirals across the three hundred kilometers, following the seeping water, following the movement of the bacterial blooms, but most importantly following the **shifts in the song**. They are trying to map the changes. They believe the stone is not merely singing but speaking, transmitting a message of tectonic scale, wordless and eternal. Each generation records its interpretations inela modification of the cairns—adding a stone, removing one, tilting a slab to catch the wind differently. The landscape is thus a palimpsest of geological listening, three billion years of stone overlaid with fifty thousand years of intentional acoustic architecture.

They wear the mist. Their clothing—woven from the chitinous remnants of dead lithovores, which desiccate into filaments like fine glass wool—is always damp, heavy with condensation. They paint their faces with oxides scraped from the stone, drawing spirals that match the grazing patterns of the herds, believing that to wear the geometry of the pavement is to be recognized by it, to be included in its slow mineral consciousness.

They fear the **Box-Filters**, those larger predators that drift down from the anaerobic mist, but not with panic. Fear here is a Slow emotion. When the pitch changes—the stone’s hum dropping a quarter-tone as the massive bulk of a Filter settles onto the pavement— they do not run. Running is impossible; the air is too thick with mist, the sound too disorienting. Instead, they **freeze into geometry**. They practice a discipline of stillness so profound that they can reduce their heartbeats to one per minute, flattening themselves into cairn-shapes, becoming indistinguishable from the stone stacks. They have learned that the Filters hunt not by sight or scent—there is neither in the silver gloom—but by vibration. To move is to be consumed; to be still, to match the stone’s own frequency, is to become invisible. In these moments of predatory silence, lying pressed against the singing rock, the Auditories claim they can hear the voice most clearly. They say the stone whispers secrets during the hunt, when the only other sound is the wet crushing of a lithovore in a Filter’s maw.

They have no chiefs, no priests, only **Tuners**—elders whose skin has calcified with age into a kind of living crust, whose sensitivity to vibration has become so acute they can predict the Box-Filters’ descent weeks in advance by feeling the pressure changes in their joints. The Tuners decide when to migrate, when to harvest, when to perform the **Great Listening**—a ritual where the entire scattered population gathers at a specific resonant node, linking hands to form a living chain across kilometers of pavement, becoming a single massive ear to receive what they believe is the stone’s attempt at a name, a final statement before the continents finally dissolve into the silence of the heat-death.

They are patient beyond human measure. They build nothing that lasts because the stone endures; they carve no monuments because the cairns are enough; they fear no death because they understand themselves to be a temporary expression of the pavement, a way for the rock to know itself in biological form. When an Auditory dies, they are not buried—there is no soil—but are laid upon the stone and left until the lithovores come, the geometric grazers consuming the flesh, incorporating it into their spiraling bodies, transforming animal back into mineral. The Auditories believe this is the ultimate listening: to become stone again, to join the choir, to sing forever in the wet, silver gloom.

They are the ones who remain when the world has worn down to its foundation, the final audience at the end of geography, holding their shell-cups to catch the weeping rock, pressing their electric skin to the humming plain, waiting for the final note to resolve.
