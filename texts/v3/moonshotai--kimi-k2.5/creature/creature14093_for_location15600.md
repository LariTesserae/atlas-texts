What lives here has forgotten the sun in its bones. The primary inhabitants—you cannot call them a species in the Linnaean sense, for they are more a process than a taxon—are the **Tessellates**, though no human tongue has ever named them. Each is a fist of living geometry, a chitinous solid crystallized from sulfurous geochemistry. Their exoskeletons are not keratin but **metamorphosed chitin**, a ceramic harder than flint, etched with spiral growth-rings that record the thermal pulses of the breathing stone around them. They are blind, naturally; the amber glow that permeates this place bleeds from their own bodies, a bioluminescence of accretion. When a Tessellate extrudes its paste—when it voids its guts of the dissolved bedrock it has fermented in acidic chambers—the chemical wedding of carbonate and protein gives off that soft, steady light. They shine as they build, and in building they slowly die into architecture.

They are born mobile and die stationary, though the distinction matters little. A juvenile Tessellate, pale and soft as wet chalk, emerges from a temporary cyst bored into the flank of its parent (or was it a wall? the distinction erases itself). It begins eating immediately, mandibles scraping the basalt with a sound like a knife sharpening stone. Its metabolism is a furnace; the heat of its digestion is what keeps this realm fevered. It consumes the old to build the new, tunneling not through empty space but through its siblings, who calcify around the wound, sealing the bore-hole with their own bodies until the tunnel becomes a load-bearing arch. They do not avoid being eaten; they incorporate the eater. To be tunneled through is to be honored, to become structure. The dead do not rot here—they simply stiffen, their soft paste curing into travertine within the hour, becoming the pillars and vaults that the living climb over, crushing and grinding, in their endless migration.

They move in **rivers**. Not metaphors—actual kinetic flows, millions strong, pouring across ceilings in defiance of gravity, their collective weight sometimes exceeding the tensile strength of the calcified film beneath them. When they fall, it is not tragedy but redistribution. A ceiling-river collapses, and the Tessellates rain down in ceramic hail, shattering into fragments that the carpet below—a ten-meter-deep stratum of packed bodies—consumes immediately, dissolving the shards in communal stomachs, re-secreting them by afternoon into a new bridge spanning a new chasm. They are the weather and the erosion and the sedimentation. They are the only history this place has, their bodies forming strata that record hours rather than epochs.

They communicate in **grinding**. The susurration that fills the space is their language, a syntax of abrasion. A Tessellate announces its chemical needs by rasping its mandibles against its neighbor, signaling acidity or mineral content. They coordinate their architectural frenzies through vibrational resonance; when a critical mass begins secreting paste in unison, the entire region hums at a frequency that makes the tool-users’ teeth ache. They are eusocial but not hierarchical—each is mason and mortar, architect and blueprint, simultaneously.

And then, at the margins, where the swarm-density relaxes from absolute to merely suffocating, there are the **Gleaners**. 

They are small, these tool-users, adapted not to dominate but to surf the chaos. They are bipedal, mammalian, remnants of a surface evolution now vestigial—they have lost pigmentation, their eyes are enormous and black, adapted to the amber twilight, and their skin is permanently damp, hosting lichens that do not photosynthesize but chemosynthesize, a secondary symbiosis. They move like ghosts through the architecture, which is hostile to permanence. They cannot build homes; they can only **harvest nests**.

With hand-axes of chert—flaked from the rare intrusive veins that the Tessellates cannot digest—they chip hovels into the half-cured stone, living in the geological equivalent of wet clay. They reinforce these temporary burrows with mud and the shed carapaces of juvenile Tessellates, creating a lattice that breathes with the humidity. They sleep on platforms suspended above the carpet, for the floor is a ocean of living stone that shifts hourly. They wake when the grinding song changes pitch, signaling a collapse, and they flee with their few possessions: sacks of uncured travertine that will harden into tools by morning, blades fashioned from the chitin of the dead, and the fossilized droppings of the swarms, which burn slow and sulfurous in their hearths.

They are scavengers of the calcified future. They harvest the Tessellates not by killing—that would be impossible, for the creatures are harder than the stone around them—but by mining the freshly dead, the ones that have just fossilized, still warm and slightly soft. A Gleaner knows the sound of recent death: a particular hollow ring when struck. They quarry their building materials from the dying, carrying slabs of their predecessors on their backs, moving through the vastness like ants carrying leaves, establishing camps that last perhaps three days before the tessellated urgency swallows them, the floor rising, the walls extruding new growth that seals their doorways.

They have no names for themselves, only directional markers: *Upstream* and *Downstream*, *Toward the Glow* and *Away*. They mark their passage with charcoal scrawls on the fossilized backs of immobile Tessellates— dashboards of calendrical lines, counting the hours until the next migration forces them to move. They are the only things here that remember the concept of *outside*, though they have never seen it, carrying the memory in their genes like a scar.

Between these two—the mineral animals and the soft animal-miners—there is a third presence, if it can be called living: the **Air Itself** has become a participant. The supersaturated humidity, the warm saline rain, the copper-and-plaster taste—it is not empty atmosphere but a plasma of dissolved calcium, a medium in which the Tessellates swim as fish swim in water. The Gleaners must wear masks of woven chitin when they travel, not to breathe, but to filter the stone-dust that precipitates into their lungs, calcifying them from the inside out if they inhale too deeply. To breathe here is to risk becoming architecture premature.

What lives here, ultimately, is **Conversion**. The Tessellates are priests of the lithic mass, transubstantiating bedrock into biology into cathedral into bedrock again. The Gleaners are heretics, stealing the sacred substance for their own brief, soft purposes. And the space breathes them all in, a lung of warm stone, exhaling moisture that has nowhere to go but back down onto the backs of the builders, who catch it, who drink it, who piss it out as paste that glows amber in the dark, building the future faster than the past can remember to erode.
