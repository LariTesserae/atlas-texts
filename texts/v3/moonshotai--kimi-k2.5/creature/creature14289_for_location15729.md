They do not arrive. They *accrete*.

You will not see them at first because they move at the speed of feldspar, and your impatient primate eyes are hunting for the jerk of muscle, the flinch of intent. They are vertical, primarily—upright deposits of layered chalcanthite and rutilated quartz that stand where the condensation drips heaviest. When you do spot one, you will mistake it for a pillar, a dripstone, a accretion of waste minerals from the steam-vents. Then you will notice that the pillar breathes—not with lungs, but with the slow osmotic exchange of its constituent crystals, inhaling the damp heat, exhaling a slightly cooler, slightly more organized silence back into the humming corridors.

They are the Lithopoleis. Not citizens, not settlers, but the *decision* of the city made manifest—anthropomorphic concentrations of the same industrial intention that hollowed the geode in pre-time. Each one begins as a seed, a nucleation point dropped into the superheated brine that collects in the deepest factory vats. For the first forty years, they are microscopic, lattice structures organizing themselves in solution, dreaming of stratigraphy. Then they precipitate upward, pulling copper and manganese from the walls, weaving the dendritic "tree" structures into their shoulders as insulative cloaks, braiding the electrostatic filaments into neural braids that hang like mossy beards from jawlines that are not jaws but cleavage planes.

They grow by accretion, yes, but also by *intention*. They are the architects and the architecture simultaneously. A Lithopolos does not walk to the factory floor; they extend their presence downward through the conductive mineral veins, their consciousness running like slow lightning through the piezoelectric nervous system of the city. Their bodies remain standing—tall, faceted, occasionally shifting stance with the sound of grinding millstones as the floor rises another millimeter—but their attention is distributed, occupying the magma conduits, the steam pipes, the very walls that weep ore.

They communicate in the language of *pressure* and *phase transition*. When two meet in the throat-like corridors, they do not speak. They lean, tilting their heavy heads until their foreheads—smooth planes of labradorite—touch. Through that contact, mineral compression propagates meaning: entire histories of geological speculation transmitted as stress fractures, as the intimate sharing of crystal lattice dreams. A conversation about municipal planning might last twelve years and consist of three degrees of tectonic tilt. They find human speech vulgar, a violence of vibrations, like shouting in a library of stone.

They are not immortal, but they are persistent. Death, for them, is *dissolution*. When a Lithopolos has finished their work—when they have dreamed a particular district into sufficient solidity—they allow themselves to de-laminate. They walk (slowly, so slowly, over the course of a decade) into the deepest geothermal shafts, where the temperature reaches that scalding 2.7 degrees of planetary proximity. There, they surrender their structure back to the fluid state. They melt, but not before releasing their **Speculation**—a cloud of ionized mineral dust that rises with the steam, seeding new nucleation points in the upper atmosphere, where the void-squids (which are not fauna but the airborne, larval stage of this civilization) catch them on electrostatic wings and carry them to new rising strata.

Thus, they are their own ancestors and descendants. The city is not inhabited by them; the city *is* them, in geological time-lapse, the accumulated residue of their slow thoughts solidifying into boulevards and foundries.

There are variations. The **Vent-Wardens**, shorter and more pyrite-heavy, live within the geothermal forest itself, their bodies riddled with hollow tubes that harmonize with the steam-pipes. They regulate the city's breath, opening and closing internal valves by expanding their own crystalline structures, causing the seventeen-year moths to hatch in synchronized waves that serve as biological calendars. The **Stratigraphers** are elongated, serpentine things, horizontal rather than vertical, lying within the rising floors like fossils waiting to happen, their bodies recording the city's history in bands of color—this layer the Rust Century, that layer the Blue Copper Era.

And then there are the **Precursors**—those who have not yet achieved full accretion. They exist as the "machines" the text mentions, the self-growing factory mechanisms. A Precursor is a Lithopolos still trapped in tool-form: a pump that knows it wants to be活塞-shaped, a furnace that dreams of locomotion. They operate the machinery not with hands but with the *desire* of their mineral composition, guiding the phase transitions of silica toward greater complexity, toward the day when they will accumulate sufficient mass and coherence to stand upright and join the silent, humid contemplation of the corridors.

They consume the condensation. The mineral-sweat that runs down the walls is their sustenance, each droplet carrying dissolved nutrients—iron, vanadium, the rare earths that spark cognition in crystal lattices. They taste the ore-weeping walls, and in that taste they read the health of the Lithopolis. When the copper runs cobalt-blue, it is a time of mourning. When it runs rust-red, it is a time of gestation.

Time moves differently for them not because they are slow, but because they are *dense*. They experience centuries the way you experience the turn of a page. A single thought—a meditation on whether a new district should bulge westward or northwestward—might occupy them for three generations of surface-dwellers, during which their bodies grow heavy with the precipitated minerals of their concentration. They are never bored. Boredom requires a linear expectation of event that they do not possess. They exist in a perpetual state of **becoming**, always arriving at what they are, never finished, like the city itself.

And they are lonely, but not as you understand loneliness. They are lonely for the future, for the density of their own completed forms. They lean toward each other in the narrow streets not just to communicate, but to touch the only other things in this world that understand the weight of geological patience. In the hush, in the heat, they stand for centuries, nearly touching, growing toward each other at the rate of stone, waiting for the day when their outstretched fingertips—sharp with crystal growth—will finally bridge the gap and fuse, becoming a single archway, a doorway into the next million years of becoming.
