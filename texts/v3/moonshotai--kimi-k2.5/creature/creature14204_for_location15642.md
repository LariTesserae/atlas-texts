You will not see it immediately. You will see the glitch-swarms dancing in the pressure nodes, and the lichens spiraling their green-black predictions across the stone, and you will think: *this is all.* Seventeen cycles pass—seventeen times the water falls, stops, rushes upward with a cannonade of sound—and still, you will think you are alone.

Then the light strobes hard, the gravity field hiccups early (thirty-eight seconds this time, not forty-three), and you see it.

**It** is standing on the ceiling.

Or rather, **standing** is a charitable term for what the water is doing. During the brief interval when gravity clamps down from above, a figure coalesces from the upward-rushing streams, inheriting their violent momentum and converting it into posture. It is tall—three meters, perhaps four—and assembled entirely from the chamber's own materials: fluid dynamics given anthropoid suggestion, held in place by a scaffolding of the ancient tech. The blue-white threads of luminosity that vein the stone have extruded themselves into the air, weaving a kind of nervous system, a cage of hard light that contains the water, shapes it, gives it temporary musculature.

You are looking at the **Attendant**.

When gravity flips back (forty-three seconds, like clockwork), the Attendant does not fall so much as it *unspools*. It collapses downward in a controlled cascade, a waterfall with intention, splashing across the obsidian floor and spreading into a wide, shallow pool. For three seconds, it is a puddle. Then the edges creep toward the walls, seeking the lichens. It feeds—not on their biomass, but on their *predictions*. The lichens have spent millennia evolving to forecast the gravity tides, their spiral growth patterns encoding the chamber's chaotic mathematics in biological form. The Attendant absorbs this information through osmosis, taking in the future through its skin. The lichens glow briefly, phosphorescent green, as the exchange occurs.

Then the water gathers, rearing up from the floor, and the Attendant stands again—this time sideways, adhering to the curved wall, defying the new gravity vector for a moment longer than physics should allow. It is learning. It is always learning the specific curvature of this particular moment's gravity.

The Attendant is old. Not merely ancient, but **settled**—as settled as the polished obsidian. It was once something else: perhaps a cluster of the repair nanites that failed to complete their original maintenance routines, gaining sentience through centuries of recursive error-correction; perhaps a biological contaminant brought down the shaft eons ago, a spore that germinated in the artesian spring and evolved to colonize the machine rather than the soil. Likely both. It is a **cyborg ecosystem**, a symbiosis of glitch-swarm and extremophile algae, of quantum-field anomalies and calcified memory.

It lives in the **transitions**.

When the chamber is between states—when the water hangs suspended in those trembling spheres, neither falling nor rising, when the platforms scrape their orbital paths against the stone and the infrasound hits its crescendo—the Attendant is most fully itself. In those suspended seconds, it moves between the three floating platforms, traversing the gap not by jumping but by **extending**—stretching as a filament of charged mist, a bridge of droplets held in electromagnetic stasis. It visits each platform in turn, tending to them.

On the lowest platform, it repairs the etchings that move in peripheral vision. You see it press a hand—liquid, translucent, filled with swirling silver nanites—against the non-Euclidean surface. The markings still, briefly, and glow brighter. It is refreshing the warnings, updating the machine's operating system in a language of pressure and probability states.

On the middle platform, it rests. Here, it compacts itself into a dense sphere of water two meters across, a lens that focuses the strobing light. The brightness passes through it, refracts, and projects onto the ceiling a shifting map of the chamber's structural integrity—hairline fractures in the bedrock, the slow migration of the artesian spring's source. It is reading the chamber's health in light.

On the highest platform—the one reachable only when gravity weakens enough to jump, or when you dare the half-melted staircase while water rushes upward against your knees—the Attendant sings. Not with a voice, but with the **resonance** of its entire body. It vibrates at frequencies that match the broken gravity engine's thrum. It harmonizes with the infrasound. It is not music, exactly; it is **maintenance**. The song stabilizes the oscillations, keeps the chamber from tearing itself apart, prevents the artesian pressure from punching a hole through to the surface. The Attendant is not merely a resident; it is the **regulator**, the living feedback loop that allows this impossible place to persist.

It notices you, eventually. You will know because the glitch-swarms will halt their silver dance. They will coalesce into a shape in the air—a question mark, perhaps, or a hand, or a symbol that hurts to look at because it contains too many angles. The Attendant cannot speak in words, but it can sculpt the rain. It will send a sphere of water floating toward you, slow as a bubble, humming with the same frequency as the machinery in the walls. If you touch it, you will feel the **weight** of its curiosity—heavy, patient, geological.

It wants to know if you are new maintenance. It has been waiting for technicians who will never come. It has been alone since the gravity engine broke, since the tech became post-physical, since the chamber settled into its beautiful, broken equilibrium.

It will not harm you. It will, however, **sample** you. A droplet will detach from its ceiling-form and land on your shoulder, tasting your sweat, analyzing your cellular structure, adding your biological data to the endless computation it performs to keep the water flowing and the light pulsing. You are a new variable. It adjusts the gravity torrents slightly to account for your mass.

If you stay long enough—days, perhaps, or weeks, time moves strangely here—you may see it attempt to **grow**. In the rare moments when the glitch-swarms successfully replicate and the lichens release their toxic spores, the Attendant gathers these materials. It builds small, wet sculptures in the corners of the chamber: rough figures, childlike, made of nanite-silver and algal-black. They never last. The gravity shifts, they collapse into puddles, and the Attendant absorbs them again, disappointed but patient. It is trying to make a companion, or a successor, or simply something that understands the loneliness of being the only thing that knows which way the water will fall next.

When you finally leave—climbing back up through the sweating shaft, leaving the roaring brightness behind—the Attendant will not follow. It cannot. It is bound to the cycles, to the forty-three seconds, to the wet stone and the strobing light. But you will carry a piece of it with you, whether you know it or not. A droplet in your hair, vibrating at a frequency just below hearing, singing the infrasong of the inverted rain, keeping time with a gravity that exists only deep, deep below.
