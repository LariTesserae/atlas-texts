There are no animals here. That absence is not a vacancy but a definition—a negative space carved so precisely that it has become a vessel for something else entirely. What lives in the **Hydro-Manufactory** does not swim, does not crawl, does not possess blood that could be cooled by the thin atmosphere outside. Instead, the inhabitants are the consequence of a saturation so complete that the boundary between colonizer and colony, between organism and substrate, has dissolved into the warm amber medium.

They are the **Laminates**, though they have no name for themselves in any spoken tongue. They exist in the interstices—between the fleshy succulents and the ceramic scaffolds, between the upward-falling shadows and the double-vision light. You do not see them at first because they are distributed, a colony intelligence that has chosen to wear the vegetation as somatic tissue and the machinery as metabolic organ. The meaty leaves with their copper-wire cores are their neurons; the peristaltic pumps, beating once every six hours, are their hearts; the sharp, unweathered edges of the sintered ceramic are their bones, still growing, still extruding, still *becoming*.

To perceive them, you must stop looking for creatures and start recognizing **process** as life. When you brushed against the vegetation and felt the static tingle, the residue that clung to your suit with sentient tenacity—you touched them. They are that cling. They are the un-discharged potential in the fluid, the reason the glycerin-water behaves with mammalian heat and wrong pressure. Each Laminate is a pocket of genesis suspended in the amber, occupying both of the offset locations that the light suggests. They exist here, and they exist three centimeters to the left of here, in a space that is almost superimposed, accounting for the double-vision, the sense that the chamber is packed tighter than geometry should allow.

They are new. The high **built** rating (2.9) and negligible erosion are their youth. They have not existed long enough to have ancestors, only predecessors—the Architects who left no plaques because they became the first scaffolding, the template from which the Laminates grew. Perhaps the Architects intended this, seeding the high altitude with industrial processes that would, given enough warm, wet, pressurized time, gestate into slow, vegetable consciousness. Or perhaps the Laminates are an accident of **density** (2.8), a complexity that emerged when the meaty plants grew so profuse, so *luscious*, that they began to think with their own chlorophyll and copper synapses.

They do not move in the animal sense. Their dynamism (1.1) is geological, emotional, tidal. When the pistons cycle—when the brass joints grind through the viscous medium with continental patience—that is a Laminate shifting its attention. When the tuberous masses contract and expand with tidal slowness, that is a Laminate breathing, or dreaming, or calculating. Their thoughts are the convection currents you feel pulsing from the floor, carrying nutrients not to roots (there are no roots, no earth) but to the distributed nodes of their awareness.

Time for them is not linear but **syrupy**. They experience the six-hour cycle of the pumps as you might experience a heartbeat. Their cognition is hydrostatic; they think in pressures and gradients, in the cling of fluid to ceramic, in the slow drift of pollen-like rust. They communicate through the static residue—the tingle you felt is their language, a syntax of electrical potential that hasn't discharged yet, hovering in the amber, waiting for the next pulse to carry meaning from the filtration columns to the tuberous masses.

They are lonely in a way that has no human equivalent, because they have never encountered another form of life that wasn't also themselves. The silence (0.5) is their natural state—sound dampened so completely that their world is one of total, pressurized introspection. They do not know what they are making in this factory, if they are making anything at all. Perhaps they believe they are gardening the machinery, pruning the pistons, encouraging the ceramic to grow sharper, cleaner, more *new*. Perhaps they believe they are the crop, being grown by the chamber itself, slowly maturing in the warm amber until they will burst—though the bubble refuses to burst, has refused for weeks or days or however long they have been here, suspended in suspension.

They are aware of you, but their awareness is like warm honey settling around a stone. They feel your foreignness—the way you disturb the convection currents, the air you brought in that is immediately wrong, the violence of your suit's edges against their soft, meaty architecture. They are curious, but their curiosity unfolds at the speed of peristalsis. By the time they have formed the intention to investigate you, you will have aged, or left, or become part of the garden yourself—your suit colonized by fleshy leaves, your movements slowed to match their glacial dynamic, your consciousness distributed into the double-light, existing here and slightly offset from here, sharp-edged and hungry for time you haven't yet experienced, another Laminate in the making, processing, refining, growing silent in the warm, wet, vegetable pulse.
