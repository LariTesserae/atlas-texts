They do not occupy the city so much as they constitute its interstitial fluid—the **Saturated**, as they might call themselves, or perhaps they have no name for the collective, knowing only the specific pressure-frequencies of their immediate kin. They are the answer to a question of maximum density: what creature can survive when there is no empty space, only a gradient of occupancy from solid architecture to the microscopic pores where capillary water clings?

Their bodies are long—two, sometimes three meters—but folded into origami-like compressions, segmented with hydrostatic joints that can lock rigid or liquefy instantly. They are translucent, opalescent, skin like wet parchment through which you can see the dark shapes of organs suspended in gelatinous suspension. They have no eyes; in a world without shadows, where light oozes sourceless from every surface and solid objects press against you from all sides, vision is a redundancy. Instead, their entire epidermis is a tympanic membrane, alive to the subsonic vocabulary of the city. They "see" by the reflection of sound waves off nearby surfaces, mapping the shifting topology in real-time as a pressure-scape of densities and vibrations. They navigate the three-kilometer vertical not by sightlines but by the specific timbre of the grinding vaults above and the digesting machinery below.

They breathe the warm, wet atmospheric lung of the place, drinking more than they inhale—filtering the perpetual condensation through gill-slits that flutter along their flanks, extracting not just oxygen but the mineral nutrients suspended in the weeping water. They are lithovores by necessity, their digestive systems filled with enzymic acids that dissolve the salt-crystal forests and photovoltaic silica that bloom on the walls. They scrape these accretions with radulae made of hardened calcium phosphate, or simply embrace the warm ceramic-alloy surfaces, absorbing heat and light through dermal photovoltaic cells. They taste the city’s mood through its chemical effluent, detecting shifts in the programming of the aggregate matter by the metallic tang in the moisture.

Reproduction occurs in the micro-basins where water pools, the warm gelid sheets providing a medium for gamete exchange. Their young emerge as polyps, translucent and minute, drifting in the capillary channels alongside the tube-dwelling invertebrates, filter-feeding until they develop the muscular hydrostats necessary to cling to shifting walls. Childhood is a precarious clinging to the architecture, learning to predict the turbulence—the sudden cantilevers that might crush, the floors that rise to meet ceilings. They learn to flow into the available volume, to make themselves flat, to become fluid when the city decides to compress a chamber.

They do not build homes. There is no static space to claim. Instead, they carry their privacy internally, retreating into neural trance-states when the physical proximity becomes absolute—which is always. In a world of Density 3.0, privacy is not a spatial luxury but a biochemical discipline. They secrete hormones that create temporary barriers of scent, or they entwine in communal sleeping clusters where individual boundaries blur, sharing neural impulses through skin contact, their nervous systems temporarily merging into local collective consciousnesses. They are solitary and communal simultaneously, alone in their sensorium yet pressed against neighbors in every direction.

Their technology is their flesh; their flesh is the city. They interface with the programmable aggregate not through tools but through resonance. By vibrating specific frequencies against the walls—using the hardened palps at their limb-tips—they instruct the nanoscale machinery to extrude, recede, or reconfigure. They sing corridors open and close them behind them with dampened chords. They are shepherds of the lithovore fauna, cultivating the crystalline fungal analogs in gardens that are merely patterned mineral stains on the walls, harvesting the tube-dwellers from the water channels like aquaculturists. They hunt the acoustic predators not with weapons but by flooding local zones with canceling frequencies, neutralizing the hunters’ sensory abilities and collecting them as they lie disoriented.

Death, for them, is a dissolution. When an individual’s organs finally crystallize from mineral overload or their hydrostatic joints fail, they do not bury their dead—they compress them into the walls. The body is pressed against the programmable substrate, vibrating a final sequence that instructs the city to absorb it. The corpse becomes architecture, its calcium enriching the salt-forests, its phosphates feeding the photoreactive glaze. In this way, the Saturated never leave the city; they merely transition from animate fluid to static structure, their memories perhaps lingering in the acoustic properties of the walls they become, their voices joining the roar.

They know nothing of outside. The concept of "open sky" is a neurological error to them, a phantom limb sensation of the species’ ancient ancestry long edited from their genetic memory. They are content in the absolute interior, moving through the heaving darkness like proteins through a cell’s cytoplasm, communicating in a language of infrasonic booms and the high-frequency whine of mineral crystal growth. They are the Volumetric City’s neural tissue, its immune system, its digestive enzymes—thinking, feeling, eating, and being eaten by the structure that sustains them, packed so tight against one another and the architecture that the distinction between inhabitant and habitation has long since ceased to have meaning.
