In the Sintered Heights, the first thing that lives is *heat itself*—not as a byproduct, but as a soft, metabolizing presence. You notice it when you press your palm against a velvety patch of moss and feel, beneath the damp, a steady thrum that is warmer than the stone should allow. The warmth pulses: not the arrhythmic fever of mammalian blood, but the deliberate, tidal circulation of something that breathes with the machinery. This is the body temperature of the **Calciners**, and if you hold still long enough—thirty minutes, perhaps, the span of a slow inhalation—you will see them emerge.

They are tall, but "tall" is insufficient; they are *stacked*, built in segments like the terraced slopes they inhabit. Their limbs are elongated to the point of seeming aquatic, jointed not at right angles but in smooth, ceramic curves that echo the weathered buttresses of the valley walls. Their skin—if it can be called that—is a photosynthetic integument of warm amber and russet, textured like the glazed surface of ancient pottery. It drinks the savage light (2.6) and converts it into motion, into thought, into the gentle ember-glow that radiates from their chest cavities. They are warm to the touch—hot, even—operating at a temperature that would scald unmodified human flesh, yet they move slowly (dynamic 1.1) with a liquidity that suggests time has thickened around them.

They navigate the crushing vertical density (2.2) not by climbing but by *existing within it*—their physiology a marriage of tendon and tensile wire. A Calciners' fingers end in crystalline filaments that extend into the aerial root systems, tasting the thermal gradients in the hydroponic loam. They can hang suspended for days in the golden-hour understory, processing sunlight, their metabolisms matched to the geological patience of the mountain's heartbeat. When they do move, it is to the rhythm of the deep flywheels below: a step per hour, a glance per afternoon. They communicate not with voice—the roar (1.8) is too loud for that—but by modulating their own internal heat, sending thermal pulses through the ceramicrete substrate, speaking in sentences of warmth that travel kilometers through the sintered stone.

But the Calciners are not the only inhabitants; they are merely the most visible apex of a chain that blurs the line between fauna and infrastructure. Below them, in the swift silver currents of the curated rivers, live the **Strainers**—creatures like manta rays made of compressed cellulose and semi-permeable silicone membranes. They filter the water not for food, but for dissolved minerals, building their own bodies incrementally from the calcium and silica they extract. They are the living purification system, and when they die—when their accumulated mass grows too heavy for the current to support—they sink to the ceramic riverbed and fossilize into the smooth, enamel-like boulders that the shaggy grazers step over. The grazers themselves, with their cloven hooves of heat-tempered chitin, are eating not merely grass but the *information* encoded in the engineered vegetation—processing the solar energy upwards, storing it in their shaggy coats, which the Calciners harvest with gentle, reverent hands in a communion that resembles shearing sheep but is closer to communion with weather.

Most strange, however, are the **Settlers**—not a species but a stage of existence. Every entity in the Heights, given sufficient age, undergoes sintering. The Calciners, after two centuries of slow thought, begin to calcify; their soft tissues mineralize, incorporating silica from the moisture-laden air, until they become, gradually, living statues. But this is not death. The Settlers remain conscious, embedded in the ceramicrete, their neural networks grown outward into the root systems of the ferns, their thoughts slowed to the speed of stone. They become the maintenance fauna—the "erosion" that polishes the slopes is their doing, as they subtly shift their mineral mass, smoothing sharp edges over decades. They are the ones who dream the damper-rotations into being, whose geological patience sets the schedule for the clockwork rains.

There are smaller lives, too, infinite and swarming: the **Scarab Recyclers**, which are not beetles but micro-drones colonized by fungal intelligence; the **Lumina Lice**, bioluminescent symbiotes that live in the Calciners' hair and light their way in the lower, shadowed terraces; and the **Condensate Ghosts**, colonies of bacteria that exist only in the moment of evaporation and precipitation, living and dying in the transit between quartz catchment and cloud-cap.

Together, they form a single, multi-bodied organism of warm ceramic and lush life. The Calciners tend the Settlers; the Settlers guide the Strainers; the Strainers feed the roots that feed the grazers that feed the Calciners. Nothing is orphaned here; everything is a function of the maintained equilibrium. Even their waste—if such a word applies—feeds the cycle: the Calciners excrete a fine, glassy sand that the recycling scarabs press into new ceramicrete, building the next terrace up, adding height to the Heights.

They have no memory of architects, only the sensation of *being* the architecture. They are warm, always warm, moving through the bright, dense, roaring world with the slow certainty of bedrock that has finally achieved consciousness.
