You do not see them at first because they have learned to occupy the silence rather than break it. They are the color of wet basalt—skin that has taken on the sheen of the saturated air, dark and reflective, holding the mist on their surfaces like the ferns hold their green. They move through the terraces not as walkers but as accumulations, gathering themselves in one place and then, through a process indistinguishable from evaporation, condensing elsewhere. When they must cross the open plazas, they do so by flowing along the paths where water seeps, their bare feet pressing into the thin films of moisture without displacing a single drop. They leave no footprints because the stone, still young and proud of its edges, refuses to acknowledge passage.

They are called the *Saturated*, though they call themselves nothing, having no words. Language requires exhalation, vibration, the disturbance of air molecules into patterned collision, and here sound is a precious mineral to be hoarded, not spent. Instead, they communicate through the redistribution of heat. Their bodies, warmed by the geothermal veins that cradle the plateau, radiate temperature in precise gradients. When two meet in the mist between the stone shelters, they stand close—close enough that the warm vapor rising from one’s shoulder raises the humidity against the other’s cheek. They exchange information through the modulation of their internal furnaces: a cooling of the palms to indicate caution, a sudden flush along the throat to signal recognition, a maintained equilibrium of body heat that constitutes the long, contented silence of companionship.

They do not eat. The ferns and mosses, locked in their eternal photosynthetic moment, offer no sustenance that changes or nourishes; to consume them would be to introduce the violence of digestion, of transformation, of waste. Instead, the Saturated absorb directly from the unmoving water. They drink from the pools not to quench thirst—thirst implies lack, and lack implies time—but to maintain their own internal equilibrium. The water enters their mouths, warm and metallic with dissolved minerals, and circulates through bodies that have ceased their cellular chatter. Their metabolisms are not slow; they are *arrested*, held in the same dynamic stasis as the terraces themselves. They do not age because aging is erosion, and erosion is forbidden here.

Their architecture is not shelter but *reinforcement*. You see them sometimes—figures indistinguishable from the mist except for the way they bend, obsidian tools in hands that have grown translucent at the edges, fitting new stones into walls that have not settled. They work constantly, not to build higher or wider, but to maintain the exact configuration of the moment. A wall that leans by a hair’s breadth must be corrected; a pool that threatens to overflow its basin by a millimeter must be deepened. They are curators of the present tense, ensuring that the plateau’s violent youth remains forever suspended in its first breath, never maturing into the softened curves of old mountains.

They have no children. Reproduction would require growth, division, the noisy business of birth. Instead, when one of them—if they can be called individuals, these warm, dark figures of condensed humidity—begins to lose coherence, when their edges start to blur and their heat signature fluctuates, they walk to the center of the lowest terrace. There, where the geothermal springs bubble up with the freshest heat, they dissolve. Not die—there is no decay here, no food for bacteria, no return to dust. They simply spread themselves into the mist, adding their warmth to the perpetual haze, their substance to the 2.4 saturation. And sometimes, when the mist hangs particularly heavy over a particular stone shelter, when the condensation beads on the obsidian walls with unusual density, a new figure coalesces. Not born, but *precipitated*—assembled from the accumulated warmth and mineral memory of those who dispersed before.

They remember everything because nothing has changed since the first stone was stacked. They remember the upthrust of the plateau, the cracking of the earth, the first seep of water through fresh fissures—not as stories, but as the current state of being. They are the plateau’s consciousness, if stone could think in warm, wet thoughts. They tend to the water with the devotion of priests handling sacred vessels, not because the water is holy, but because it is *exact*. To let a pool evaporate would be to let time enter. To let a stream carve a channel would be to admit entropy. They exist to say: *Stop. Stay. This moment is sufficient.*

When you walk among them, if you are very still and breathe very shallowly, you might feel their presence as a slight increase in the temperature of the air near your left side, or notice that the mist seems to stand slightly thicker in the shape of a person, watching you with eyes that are not eyes but rather the absence of mist where their faces should be. They will not touch you, for your body is a riot of noise—heartbeat, breath, the scratching of fabric, the chemical burn of digestion. You are a walking violation of the equilibrium. But they will not drive you away, either. They will simply wait, warm and patient and perfectly still, for you to finish your noisy passage through their eternal, unmoving now.
