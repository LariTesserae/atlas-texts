First come **the lacquerers**—not quite plants, not quite mineral skin. They are phototrophic films, cyanobacteria and golden algae that have colonized the gunmetal patina of the basalt, creating a living varnish that is slick, photosensitive, and warm to the touch. In the perpetual twilight of the spray-clouds, they perform a slow, geological photosynthesis, digesting the stone itself, excreting a mucus that accelerates the polishing. They are the Spillway’s outermost organ, the interface between water and rock, and they remember the heat of the lava flows that preceded the deluge.

Then, the **bryo-architects**. These are not mere mosses but load-bearing organisms, six-foot curtains of *Sphagnum hydroferox* that have evolved tensile strength rivaling hemp. They root not in soil—there is none—but in the pitted bronze of the penstocks, in the rivet-holes of the fractured aqueducts. Their rhizoids have adapted to conduct electricity; they drink from the stray current of the spinning turbines, using the energy to calcify their base-structures into something approaching masonry. They are the only reason the industrial fossils remain standing, wrapping the iron in vascular cushions that prevent the catastrophic rust. They grow in spirals, mimicking the empty gear housings, as if the memory of machinery has been encoded in their DNA. Beneath their canopies, entire microclimates brew: pockets of still, warm air where condensate drips in rhythmic, prehensile threads.

The air itself is harvested by **the columnar billions**—midges of the genus *Culicoides altus*, who have abandoned the soil entirely. They exist as vertical rivers of chitin and wing, their bodies iridescent with hydrophobic oils that turn each droplet into a lens. They do not swarm randomly; they form standing columns, twenty meters high, that act as living barometers, shifting and compressing as air pressure fluctuates. Birds navigate by them, fish time their spawning by the shadows they cast. When the pressure drops and a new blowhole opens to drain a temporary pool, the columns collapse and reform instantly, a biological liquid crystal responding to the geology’s every hiccup.

In the convulsing cataracts live **the compressed**, high-altitude smelt that have forgotten the shape of rivers. Their bodies are cylindrical, almost eel-like, with collapsible ribcages and swim bladders that can withstand the crushing weight of sudden depth changes. They do not migrate downstream; they migrate *up*, following the temporary waterfalls that flow upward during pressure inversions, their mouths fused into suction discs that clamp onto the slick stone. During the thunderclap drainings, when pools empty through blowholes in seconds, they compress themselves into the microscopic fissures of the basalt, becoming flat, calcified splinters of fish, waiting in suspended animation until the water returns to rehydrate them.

Most peculiar are **the gear-dwellers**, amphibians of no known genus who have completed the transition from animal to mechanism. They live exclusively within the hollows of the water wheels, breeding in the perpetual rotation. Their tadpoles are spiral-shaped, adapted to the centrifuge; their adult forms are pale, translucent, with broad, sucker-like feet that adhere to the spinning bronze. They never touch earth. Their skin has evolved to extract oxygen directly from turbulent water, and they feed on the algae that grows on the axle-grease of ten-million-year-old bearings. Their croaks are synchronized to the rpm of the turbines; they call not to each other, but to the machine, and the machine, unknowing, answers with a groan of metal.

Above them navigate **the shriek-hawks**, birds with ears sealed shut by evolution, eyes nearly vestigial. They navigate by sonar, but of a particular kind: they emit cries pitched precisely to harmonize with the white noise of the Spillway, using the interference patterns to map the shifting architecture of spray. Their nests are woven from midge-silk and rust flakes, wedged into the fracture-points of the aqueducts. They have no concept of mammals as threat; to them, the world is composed of water, stone, and the geometry of falling. They dive not for prey but for the acoustic shadows cast by the largest droplets, snatching insects from the air with beaks that close like circuit breakers.

And finally, **the Smooth**—the *who* that answers the prompt’s open question.

They are the descendants of the hydraulic civilization, or perhaps its final evolution. They are rarely seen, for their bodies have achieved the same finish as the stone: skin like polished gunmetal basalt, without pores or hair to catch the wind shear. They live in the geothermal oases, where warm fog rises from the vents, and they are the only beings who can walk barefoot upon the surfaces here without being abraded away, for they *are* the abrasion, the terminal point of erosion given humanoid form.

They communicate not with words but with *displacement*—moving through the spray in patterns that create temporary silences in the roar, negative spaces of sound that carry meaning. They tend the turbines not with tools, but with their bodies, pressing their smooth backs against the bronze to adjust the vibration, tuning the resonance so that the static discharges crawl in specific patterns across the rocks, fertilizing the moss in precise, mandala-like designs. They eat the mineral deposits, licking the stone, and they drink the mist without condensing it, absorbing droplets through their skin in a state of permanent osmosis.

They are solitary, each occupying a single spray-basin, a single fractured turbine. They are the inheritors of the final form, the smooth ones who have accepted the violence of the water as a permanent condition, and in doing so, have become indistinguishable from the Spillway’s ancient, polished, screaming self.
