They do not sleep, because sleep is a luxury of the satiated, and here hunger is the permanent weather. They do not dream, because dreaming requires a self distinct from the substrate, and they have dissolved into the substrate centuries ago. What lives here is not a *who* but a *when*—a continuous present tense, grammatically singular, biologically pluripotent.

At first, you perceive them as texture. The floor breathes, not metaphorically, but with the tidal respiratory motion of a billion booklice—*Liposcelididae*, perhaps, though the taxonomy has long since liquefied into the gene pool—rising and falling in a carpet thirty centimeters deep. They are swollen, these primary consumers, distended into pale sausages of chitin the color of old candle wax and subcutaneous vein. Their abdomens pulse with the bacterial soup they harvest from the walls, a fermentation of sulfur and historic sorrow. They have no eyes; the sockets have smoothed over, sealed by generations that never needed to navigate toward or away from anything. Instead, their antennae have elongated into feathery, hypersensitive filaments that read the sixty-cycle hum of the bulbs as a compass reads magnetism. To them, electricity is the sun, and it is everywhere.

Among them move the aristocracy: pseudoscorpions the size of rice grains, their pincers (pedipalps, you remember, pedipalps) tipped with sensilla that detect the heartbeat vibrations of prey. They are the governors of this density, though governance here is purely kinetic. When a pseudoscorpion seizes a springtail, the struggle creates a ripple—a momentary clearing in the biomass that fills in with the same inevitability as water closing over a stone. They are old designs, these hunters, unchanged since the Carboniferous, yet here they have become ascetic, bleached to the color of cave shrimp, their venom sacs reduced to atrophied ornaments because there is no space for struggle, only for consumption.

The silverfish—*Lepisma saccharina* or its descendant—thread through the strata like sharks, but sharks with the patience of monks. They are the conservators, eating what the others cannot: the chitin of the dead, the glazes on the walls, the rubber insulation of the ancient wiring that still brings the light. Their bodies gleam with a metallic luster, silicon and copper accumulating in their cuticles from decades of sipping condensation from electrical conduits. They are becoming minerals. They are becoming the slag.

And then there are the mites. The *Hypoaspis*, the *Macrocheles*, the unnamed gamasids—predators, scavengers, parasites of parasites. They exist in the interstices, the pressure points where booklice press against booklice. They are the true residents, perhaps, because they have achieved what the others have not: stillness. A mite will grip the glazed wall for weeks, a living gemstone, waiting for the statistical inevitability of prey brushing past. They are the contemplatives of the Hum, meditating on gradients of heat, on the subtle infrared signature of a dying springtail. They do not move; they *decide*, and the decision is a contraction of eight legs.

What lives here is a *continuity*. Individuality failed in the third millennium, when the last genetic bottleneck passed and the population became a superorganism—a diffuse digestive tract with the room as its peristaltic tube. The creatures are cells in a body that is eating the Anthropocene, converting industrial waste into motion, motion into biology, biology into dust, dust into more motion. 

They have a culture, though it is chemical. When a pseudoscorpion dies, it is not mourned, but *noticed*—a shift in the pheromone cloud, a transient absence of predation pressure that triggers a bloom in the springtail population three generations later. This is their history: not stories, but population waves, boom and crash written in protein. They have philosophy: the certainty that the walls are infinite, that the light is eternal, that the heat rising from the slag is the love of a god that does not know they exist.

They recognize you not as a threat, but as a *novel topology*. When you reach into the wardrobe-sized cosmos, they flow over your skin as if you were merely an unstable section of floor. They sample you. The booklice test your dead skin cells, find them wanting in sulfur, move on. The mites investigate your fingerprints, mapping the ridges as potential harborage. The silverfish taste the salt of your sweat and retreat, disappointed by the lack of copper. You are being read, metabolized by their attention, filed away as "temporary landscape."

If you listen, truly listen, you can hear that they are singing. The sound is not vocal— they lack the apparatus—but the cumulative friction of a billion mandibles grooming, a billion tarsi gripping glass, a billion abdomens dragging across stone. It is the sixty-cycle hum made biological, the drone of the bulbs modulated by the mass of life into something like a chord. A B-flat of digestion. A sustained note that has been held for centuries.

They are waiting for the lights to go out. They do not fear this; they do not anticipate it. But in the genetic memory of their mitochondria, there is a record of darkness, of the time before the grid remembered this pocket. When the bulbs finally fail, they will not die. They will slow. The bacterial film will thicken, becoming a stasis, a pause. The booklice will shrink, generation by generation, reverting to leaner forms. The mites will enter diapause, eight-legged seeds of patience. And they will wait, in the dark, in the cool, for the geology to shift, for the slag to crack, for the outside to finally, inevitably, come in.

They are the inheritors of the sealed world. They are the Hum. And they have all the time that chemistry allows.
