To meet an Ashkari is to first recognize a source of heat in a world of stone and rime. They emerge from crevices—slender when seen from the side, for they have grown up in gaps barely wider than their skulls, but broad when seen from front or back, with chests that seem too large for their frames, ribcages expanded by generations of lunging for breath in the thin, glassy air. Their bodies are dense, compact, carrying the specific gravity of those who live under stone rather than beneath sky. Their skin, where it is visible through the layers of oiled leather and mammoth hair, has a bluish pallor, not from cold—their blood runs hot, nearly feverish—but from the capillary richness required to bind oxygen at these altitudes. They have more blood than lowland peoples, thicker with it, and when they cut themselves—chipping basalt or butchering ice-oxen—the blood dries quickly to a black crust that smells of copper and the iron taste of the young, unweathered peaks.

They speak, when they speak, with their hands first and throats second. The acoustics of Ashkar swallow vowels; sound falls upward into the ice-heavy fog or downward into the sweating stone. So their language evolved in the spaces between breath, a choreography of fingers pressed against palms, coded in the angle of wrists and the tension of knuckles. When they must speak aloud, they do so with lips almost touching the listener's ear, a sub-vocal rumble that vibrates through the mastoid bone, a technology of intimacy forced by the quiet. They know each other's heart rhythms the way lowlanders know faces—by lying in adjoining crevices, separated only by taut membranes of frost-lichen leather, they feel the pulse of their neighbors through the rock itself, conducted through the bedstone.

They are not tall. Selection here favored those who could curl into the womb-like fractures of the plateau's settling crust, who could sleep with knees against chins in galleries carved by grandfathers and expanded by sons. Their spines are more flexible than lowland spines, their shoulders narrower, their hips given to dislocation so they may squeeze through fresh fault lines to reach new ice-cisterns or guano deposits. An Ashkari adult can fit through an aperture that would trap a lowland child's skull. This compression gives them a physical permanence, a sense of being *set* rather than *standing*—they settle into spaces the way the mountains settle into the earth, with patience and a subtle grinding of bone on stone.

Their eyes are the color of the rime-terraces—pale grey, sometimes translucent blue—and they see in contrasts extreme enough to blind outsiders. The surgical clarity of the light at this altitude has shaped their retinas; they navigate by the absolute edges of shadow, reading the texture of cliffs not by color but by the spectrum of light the clear ice refracts. They paint their faces not with pigment but with grease, smearing ice-oxen tallow across cheekbones to prevent frostbite, so their visages gleam in the perpetual half-light like wet basalt. In darkness, they navigate by echolocation—clicking their tongues against the roofs of their mouths, listening for the return from ice walls or the soft absorption of leather curtains.

They live in families compressed into vertical stacks, not horizontal expanses. A household occupies a *rinek*—a natural fault expanded over three generations, twenty levels of habitation connected by bone ladders and bridges of slate sheathed in ice. The grandmother sleeps deepest, where the stone is warmest from the earth's settling flex; the infants sleep highest, near the breathing holes, where the stone-swifts' guano is harvested and the air tastes of static. Between them, the middle generations tend the ice-oxen in the lower galleries, massive shaggy beasts whose body heat rises through floor-grates carved from mammoth scapulae, warming the sleeping lofts in a convection of mammalian breath. The Ashkari do not herd these animals so much as inhabit them thermally; they are parasites of warmth, architects of heat exchange, moving their living spaces seasonally as the oxen migrate from one gallery to another, following the slow drift of the frost-line.

Their days are measured not in hours but in the accumulation of rime on the northern faces of their crevices. They keep time by the growth of ice—watching how the supercooled droplets from the perpetual drizzle build lace upon lace of crystal, noting when a particular stalactite of clear ice bridges the gap between two gallery levels, declaring it safe to cross only when the frost has woven sufficient thickness. A "day" for them might last three lowland days, or seven; they wake when the breath-fog in their crevice begins to settle, sleep when the temperature drops enough to crystallize the moisture on their eyelashes. They are asynchronous with the sun, living in the geological now, synchronized to the *creak* of the plateau as it settles another micrometer into the earth's crust.

Their material culture is one of midwifery. They do not quarry stone so much as assist in its breaking. When a new fault opens in the range's growing pains—a distant, muffled thunder felt through the soles of their feet—they send down the *crack-widows*, elders who have lost their families to shearing walls, those who have nothing left to lose to the mountain's appetite. These figures enter the fresh fractures with bone levers and obsidian chisels, not to widen the rock violently but to nurse it open, to chip away the sharpest edges, to coax the gap into a habitable gallery before the mountain decides to squeeze it shut again. They work in the understanding that they are not building against the rock but negotiating a temporary truce with it, carving spaces that will be reclaimed in decades or centuries by the plateau's slow flexing.

Their children are born into darkness and damp, emerging into crevices lined with mammoth hair and the shed down of stone-swifts. From birth, they are taught the *pressure of adjacency*—how to sleep touching three neighbors, how to defecate in ice-funnels that freeze instantly to pellet form, how to wean on the paste of glacier-fleas mashed with lichen. They grow up knowing the specific texture of their family's leather membrane against their cheek, the smell of their neighbor's sweat mixing with their own in the stagnant air, the sound of a parent's heartbeat conducted through the bedrock. Privacy is unknown; solitude is a death sentence in the cold. Instead, they cultivate an intense interiority, a capacity to be utterly alone in their minds while pressed against the warmth of others—a psychological architecture as compressed as their physical one.

Their dead are not buried—permafrost and bedrock deny them that—but are placed in the upper galleries, in the coldest crevices, where the rime accumulates fastest. The bodies freeze solid within hours, becoming pillars of ice and flesh, and over months, the stone-swifts pick them clean, carrying the phosphate-rich remains to nests higher still. The bones are then collected, ground with basalt pestles, and mixed into the ice-mortar that seals the gallery walls. To be dead in Ashkar is to become architecture, to hold up the next generation's ceiling, to literally support the weight of the Commonwealth. The ancestors do not watch from above; they bear the load from below.

There are those who tend the *breath-ice*—specialists who manage the architecture of condensation. They carve the ice-cisterns not as simple storage, but as organs of the city, understanding how the warm, wet exhalation of ten thousand lungs rises through the vertical maze, how it condenses against the freezing outer stone, how the droplets must be guided by subtle variations in temperature to collect in specific pools. They read the humidity with their skin, sensing when the air is too dry (dangerous—static electricity builds, skin cracks, the ice-oxen sicken) or too wet (dangerous—ice builds too fast, galleries seal shut). They are meteorologists of the micro-climate, maintaining the delicate balance where water exists as suspension, as potential, never quite flowing, never quite frozen.

And there are the *fault-listeners*, the youngest children and the oldest elders, those whose hearing remains sensitive enough to detect the infrasound of the plateau's settling. They press their ears against the bedrock, listening for the grind of the continental shelf as it adjusts, predicting which galleries will shear, which ice-bridges will hold, when to evacuate a crevice that the mountain has decided to close. They speak of the rock as a sleeper turning in its dreams, and they wake the city with warnings when the sleeper stirs too violently.

They are a people of the in-between: between water and ice, between stone and air, between the geological violence that created their home and the biological warmth that makes it habitable. They do not conquer the plateau; they infiltrate it, sliding into its cracks like the rime ice itself, growing slowly, shearing when sheared, filling the voids between the world's tectonic adjustments. To see them is to see life choosing the hardest way—compressed, breathless, suspended in the cold, patient as frost, and utterly, densely alive in the damp stoneheavy quiet of the Vertical Commonwealth.
