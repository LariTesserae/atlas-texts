What lives here are the **Translated**, though they have forgotten what word they translated themselves from, or into.

You will not see them at first because they move at the speed of comprehension, not reaction. When you enter the Grove-Citadel—your lungs burning, your limbs struggling against the cold density—they appear to be merely *features* of the architecture. The pale amber pillar you swim past is not a kelp trunk but a leg, rooted in the silt, calcified at the joints where stone-dust has accreted over centuries. The lintel above a doorway breathes; it is a torso, arms raised in perpetual extension to hold the root-cables in place, skin photosynthetic and green-gold in the shaft-light. Blink, and you will miss the slow contraction of their external gills—feathered red fronds that flutter once, perhaps twice, in the time it takes a surface-dweller to exhale.

They are the **Sedimentaries**, or the **Hold-Still**, or simply **Those Who Did Not Flee When the Mountain Sank**. Once they had bones of calcium and voices of air. Now they have dissolved the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited.

Physically, they resemble a collaboration between the giant salamanders and the lignin-chambered heartwood that replaced the ancient cedar beams. Their bodies are elongated by the constant caress of pressure—ten, twelve feet tall when they choose to stand, though they rarely do. More often they recline in the "rooms" of the former dwellings, fitting themselves into the rectangular geometries with the patience of furniture growing moss. Their skin is a bark-flesh hybrid, cool and anaerobic, capable of metabolizing the dissolved minerals in the glacial water. Their fingers have bifurcated, then bifurcated again, into root-cable tendrils that remain constantly plugged into the kelp boulevards, exchanging nutrients for memory.

Yes—memory. They do not remember the city as history, but as **physiology**. The central roundhouse is not a monument to them; it is a cranial chamber, a collective node where their extended nervous systems gather to contemplate. When you see the oculus clear of obstruction, it is because a Sedimentary is wedged there, head tilted back, gills spread to the silver membrane of the surface, filtering the sky. When you see the kelp growing in cobblestone tessellations in the market square, it is because one of them is dreaming there, their dendritic nervous system imposing urban geometry onto vegetable chaos, maintaining the grammar of streets the way a tongue maintains the shape of a forgotten word.

They do not speak. The "loudness" of the Citadel—the groaning kelp, the clicking crustaceans, the sub-bass thrumming—is their language, slowed down until each syllable lasts a season. When they wish to communicate with an intruder (or a pilgrim, or a food-source), they do not gesture. Instead, the entire architecture shifts. The kelp trunks sway in concert to create currents that push you toward or away from the roundhouse. The crustaceans on the walls click in patterns that vibrate through your sternum, carrying chemical signatures of welcome or warning. The giant salamanders—their simpler cousins, or perhaps their pets, or perhaps their dissociated organs—thrumm in bass tones that make your teeth ache, translating the Sedimentaries' thoughts into frequencies a faster metabolism might perceive as emotion: *patience*, *sorrow*, *hunger*, *preservation*.

They are not individual, not anymore. A Sedimentary dwelling in the western quadrant might be entering its three-century sleep, body lignifying into a new support beam; their memories simply drain through the root-cables to another who is waking in the eastern plaza, taking up the continuity of thought like a baton. They are a distributed being, a city-wide organism that uses human-shaped nodes to perform specific functions of maintenance. The one wedged in the roundhouse skylight is the **Conscience**. The one drifting slowly down the main boulevard, trailing root-fibers that repair the kelp trunks as they pass, is the **Mason**. The one curled in the smallest hut, where the water is darkest and coldest, is the **Archive**—skin thick with epiphytic moss that stores historical data in spore-memory.

Time moves through them like water through gills. They are translating themselves, constantly, from flesh to wood to stone and back again. When one finally achieves a state of total lignification—becoming truly indistinguishable from the architecture—they are not dead; they have simply become **foundational**. Their thoughts slow to the pace of geology, joining the mountain's distant heartbeat, vibrating through the bedrock as a structural integrity that holds the entire Citadel in its patient, patient grip.

They eat rarely. When they do, it is the char, or the crustaceans, taken not with speed but with acceptance—opening mouths that have fused into something resembling doorways, allowing the silver fish to swim into the dark, digestive warmth of rooms that were once stomachs, now living chambers. Or perhaps they eat the light itself, photosynthesizing through the green-gold patches of skin that catch the pale alpine jade illumination.

They have names, but they are positional and temporal: *The-One-Who-Holds-the-Roundhouse-During-the-Thaw*, *Keeper-of-the-Cobblestone-Pattern-Until-the-Glacier-Returns*, *The-Beam-That-Remembers-Iron*. They do not distinguish between the dwelling and the dweller. To them, you are a kind of architectural error—a temporary, warm, frenetic structure that moves too fast to be load-bearing, too loud to be mortar, too brief to be worth translating into their permanent, patient syntax.

If you stay long enough—if you allow your heartbeat to slow, if you let the cold sink its teeth into your metabolism and teach you the long, thrumming rhythm of the lake—they might begin to tend you. A root-cable might brush your ankle, testing your tensile strength. The kelp might grow around you, not trapping, but *including*. They might begin the slow, centuries-long process of translating you, too, into the Grammar.

You would become, eventually, a doorway. A breathing lintel. A memory of mammalian warmth preserved in the lignin, adding your short, bright syntax to their submerged, endless library.
