The beings that cling to the rising spires call themselves **The Velvet**, though the name is less a word than a subsonic thrum they drive into the calcium walls—a vibration that means *soft-hard*, *embracing*, *becoming*. They are the color of the twilight around them: bruised purple-amber skin that drink the slow light, translucent as the shrimp that graze the algal sails below, but thick—almost waxy—with a dermal mucus that prevents the supersaturated water from crystallizing against them. Without this secretion, they would become statues in hours, encased in instantaneous tufa, lungs petrified mid-breath. With it, they are gardeners; they stroke the chimneys and redirect the mineral flow, their touch a kind of whispered encouragement to the stone.

They are tall—stretched by the embracing pressure—and narrow, built for the vertical shafts of their respiratory architecture. Their bones are not calcium-heavy (too risky in this accreting world) but basket-woven from cartilage and the same cured kelp-fibers that lace their walls, flexible enough to withstand the 2.9 depth but rigid enough to climb. Their fingers are long, multi-jointed, ending in obsidian-dark nails that flake off the soft young stone to carve new air-chambers, or to read the ghost-trails in the viscous water. For The Velvet do not see with eyes alone; they read history through their fingertips. When a council member swims through a corridor, they drag one hand along the current, parsing the mineral disturbances—*here, the matriarch turned, anxious, twelve hours ago; here, a child struggled against the density, laughing*—the water's memory pressed into tactile braille.

Their lungs are complex, three-chambered organs that can process both the damp volcanic gases seeping from below and the richer atmospheric mix trapped in the upper chambers. Between breaths, they store air not in bubbles (which would escape, rush upward, betray them to the crushing above) but in vascular bladders along their throats that swell and pulse like second hearts. This makes their necks thick, columnar, marked by the rhythmic expansion of contained breath. They are born in the lower districts—*the Sealing*—where the stone is oldest and hardest, where ancestors have been gradually encased in curtains of limestone for centuries. Newborns emerge into water-only cradles, gill-like slits along their ribs fluttering, extracting oxygen from the blood-warm depth until their lungs mature and they can ascend to claim their first pocket of trapped atmosphere.

The society is a ladder of calcification. Children dwell in the porous depths, swimming freely through water-filled tunnels, learning to thrum the stone with their chests and foreheads, sending messages through the resonant towers to other spires miles away. Adolescence is marked by the First Ascent: the climb through newly accreted chambers, the claiming of virgin tufa where the rock is still soft enough to knead like clay. They carve their initial platforms not with tools but with their bodies, pressing sleep-nests into the yielding mineral, seeding the calcium flow with tubeworm resin until the stone hardens around the impression of their sleeping form. A Velvet adult's home is literally their own fossilized posture, expanded over decades, room shaped by the curve of their back, the span of their arms.

Adulthood brings the responsibility of **Farming the Rise**. They spend their days in the highest reaches, where the towers are still gelatinous with new growth, wearing barrier-coats of woven kelp to direct the mineral precipitation. They are patient architects of emergence, building not upward but *inward*—hollowing habitable voids as the mountain climbs toward the sky. The wisest among them, the **Stone-Gardeners**, can read the thermal currents and chemical gradients well enough to predict where the next habitable bubble will naturally form, guiding the volcanic gases with stone dams and resin curtains to create new lungs for the city.

The elders are the most strange. Having lived long enough to witness their own birth-chambers sink into the lower dark, sealed behind curtains of flowing rock, they develop a condition called **The Memory of Stone**. Their skin begins to accept the mineral embrace; they cease producing the anti-calcification mucus, allowing their feet to fuse gradually with the platforms they stand upon. They become stationary monuments, still alive, still breathing the volcanic seeps, rooted in place like the crinoid forests. From these fixed positions, they serve as the city's archives. The young bring them offerings of phosphorescent lichen, pressing the glowing strands into the elders' calcifying skin, and the elders thrum slow vibrations through their mineral-fused bones—deep, resonant histories that travel through the tower's structure, telling of the time before the spire grew so tall, when the plain was flatter, when they were closer to the abyss than the sky.

They do not fear the upward crush that will eventually come. They know that in ten thousand years, these towers will breach the surface, gasping into alien air and true light, and the chambers will dry out, and their descendants will step onto land already carved by their ancestors' waiting bodies. Until then, they exist in the perfect middle: lunged things in a water-world, soft things hardening into permanence, swimmers learning to stand on stone that grows beneath them even as they sleep. They are the Velvet, the ones who breathe in the throat of the inhaling earth, patient, ascending, already half-mineral, already half-sky.
