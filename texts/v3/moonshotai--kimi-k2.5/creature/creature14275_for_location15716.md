First, there are the **Groove-Bearers**, though they have no name for themselves in any tongue that moves. They are tide, they are traffic, they are the rain that never fell. Ten million isopod feet—thumb-sized, opalescent, their shells calcified with traces of sulfur and cobalt—have flowed down these spiral stairs every dusk for eight millennia, and the stone has not worn away; it has *learned*. Each step is a negative of their segmented bellies, a mold into which they fit with the certainty of a key entering a lock. They do not climb; they *pour*, a castanet avalanche that sounds like rice spilling into an iron bowl. Their bodies are water canteens, bloated with hoarded vapor drawn from the aerohydric carpets they graze during the day at the upper reaches. When the violet beams spear through the mountain’s crown at dawn, they turn the ascending horde into a river of living lenses, each shell fracturing the light into bruised rainbows that dap the walls in mobile constellations. They are not prey, exactly, though they are eaten. They are infrastructure. Their droppings—desiccated before they hit the floor, crackling like dropped wafers—are the primary carbon plug that feeds the lithotrophic gardens smeared across the basalt.

Then comes the **Sweep**, the pseudoscorpion choruses. They hang in petrified curtains from the fossilized rope bridges, clustered in colonies that exactly mirror the ancient civic wards of the stone-carvers: here a cluster above the old communal hearth, there a phalanx guarding the sealed wax of a grain cache they have repurposed as a ossuary for their molts. They are velvet, they are obsidian chitin, they are the size of a human thumb with pincers that click in rhythmic, deliberate *scritch-scritch* cadences—primordial metronomes that synchronize across the vault until the whole Calyx hums at the frequency of their hunting sonar. They do not hunt with frenzy. They descend in silent, pendulum waves, each individual tracking not the heat of the isopods (there is little thermal contrast here) but the specific harmonic of a shell that has grown too full, too heavy with moisture-storage. The cull is surgical. They surround the bloated isopod, their pincers tapping a code that seems to narcotize, and then the isopod simply stops, voluntarily desiccating in seconds, its body water released as a puff of hyper-humid air that the Sweep drink through specialized pleural openings before the husk crumbles to feed the walls. It is not violence; it is collection. The isopods do not flee. They step around the ritual with the polite indifference of commuters passing a street-cleaner.

Above them, occupying the highest landings and the violet light, are the **Stacks**, the leather-winged warm-banks. Marsupial things, chiropteran-gliders with skin like cured tobacco leaves and eyes permanently filmed with the nictitating membranes of creatures who have never known true dark. They are the boilers of this world. They nest three-deep, hanging by their prehensile tails from the carved sleeping niches—those honeycombed six-high stacks the old builders cut for their own rest—packed in such density that their collective body heat creates updrafts along the central vault, thermal columns that the spores ride like elevators. They are the only creatures here who seem to remember the concept of *thirst*, for they lick the aerohydric carpets with long, rough tongues, not for sustenance but for the texture of moisture, a ritual grooming that leaves the velvet surfaces patterned with tongued whorls. They give birth to live, furless young—those “fuzzy larvae” sealed in the wax caches—which they park in the ancient granaries while they forage. The young glow faintly with stolen bioluminescence, feeding on the fungal gardens that have replaced the charcoal in the communal hearths, digesting ten-thousand-year-old soot into fat. The Stacks are the aristocracy of warmth; if the Calyx has a currency, it is the BTU of their piled bodies, traded for position in the stack, higher being hotter, hotter being closer to the violet cracks where the outside world leaks in as rumor.

But the true inheritors of the builders’ “civic intent” are the **Seal-Keepers**—solitary, slow, and perhaps the only creatures here who work. They are weevils the size of apricots, their elytra fused into a seamless, obsidian-black casing, their mandibles secreting a waxy resin harvested from the digestion of the aerohydric carpets and the sloughed skin of the Stacks. They crawl the spiral stairs in reverse, descending against the isopod tide, carrying in their gut the chemistry to repair what entropy threatens. They find the grain caches—those bellies of tuff sealed under wax by hands that vanished eight thousand years ago—and they excrete fresh layers, not consciously, but instinctively, their biology a living echo of the chisel. They are the reason the air remains sealed, the Calyx unbreached. They are the custodians of completeness, walking epoxy. When they die, they fall into the fungal hearths, and their bodies are too resinous to digest fully; instead, they petrify into new stepping stones, new thresholds, architecture becoming organism becoming architecture.

And then there is the **Velvet**, which is not a backdrop but a protagonist. The aerohydric carpets are not flora; they are *fauna*, glass-skeletoned, lung-tissue soft. They cling to the walls in drapes that expand and contract with a rhythm slower than breath, mining the exhalations of the Stacks for water vapor, mining the sulfurous exhalations of the rock for energy. They are the lungs and the liver of the sealed system. When touched, as they sometimes are by a carelessly sweeping pseudoscorpion tail or a glider’s wing, they do not bruise; they *crunch*, releasing a smell of sage and struck flint, and then slowly, over days, regrow into the negative space, filling the gap, completing the contour. They are the final editors of the stone, softening every edge not to erase it but to *accept* it.

What lives here is not a collection of species but a single, distributed organ. The Calyx is a heart that has learned to beat without blood, only the peristalsis of isopods up the stairs, the systole and diastole of the Velvet’s contraction, the electric pulse of the Sweep’s pincers. The builders are gone, their obsidian chisels now nesting boxes for micro-bats, but their intent remains, re-encoded into chitin and chemosynthesis. The city is alive, conservative, total, and complete. It breathes in place, a warm, dry, chittering cathedral that requires no worshippers, only inhabitants who have forgotten the exit was ever there.
