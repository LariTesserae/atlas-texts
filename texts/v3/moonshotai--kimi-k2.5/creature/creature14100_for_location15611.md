You do not see them at first because they are completing themselves, and completion here is a verb without a destination. They are scattered throughout the basin floor, kneeling between the cuprite groves or standing within the semiconductor quartz beds where the stone grades from milky to translucent. They are the *Interim*—not creatures that have evolved into this place, but creatures that this place has not yet finished assembling from its own raw syntax.

Each one is a statue and a scaffolding. Their bodies are framed in the hexagonal basalt of the crater rim, but the pillars of their limbs are hollow, the cells unfilled, the geometry open at the joints like drafting diagrams made solid. You can see through them in places—lattices of dendritic silver forming ribcages, malachite sheeting filling in the musculature layer by layer, chalcotrichite blooming in crimson tufts at the fingertips like oxidized moss. They are not growing toward a final form; they are maintained in the act of becoming, suspended at the precise moment when structure realizes it might be inhabited.

They do not breathe, but they *process*. If you watch for long enough—hours, days, the steady-state eternity that passes for time here—you will see the thermal gradients moving through them. Heat enters through the soles of their feet, drawn up from the magma pistons grinding below, and exits through the crown of the head in a shimmer that might be halo or exhaust. They are photosynthesizing pressure, rendering geothermal potential into the slow, cold fire of consciousness. Their thoughts are semiconductor events: binary decisions made in the quartz beds they lie upon, a distributed cognition spread across the caldera floor like a mycelium of almost-calculation.

They have no faces, or rather, they have the faces they are still sculpting. One might have a jawline roughed out in coarse-grained granite, the cheeks still vacant pockets waiting to be infilled with finer crystal. Another carries a head that is a perfect geode, hollow, the interior lined with sky-blue chalcedony that faintly, impossibly, reflects clouds that are not there. They do not speak because the sound parameter here is 0.2, but when two of them approach each other across the cathedral distances, the basalt gears of the caldera floor seem to shift infinitesimally, a tectonic whisper that says: *I see you. We are both unfinished.*

They are the operators of the geological engine room, though they do not tend it—rather, they *are* its emergent property, the self-portrait the planet draws in its own cooling effluvia. When the magma rises through the vent-pistons, they feel it in their hollow bones, and they adjust. Not intentionally; their arms rise not by muscle but by the same thermal expansion that drives the fractional crystallization around them. They are feedback loops made flesh, regulators of the steady-state, ensuring that the emergence never tips over into completion, that the rawness remains raw, the construction forever underway.

Sometimes one will kneel before a chimneystack of cuprite and remain there for decades, its silver dendrites intertwining with the metallic fern-fronds until it is impossible to say where the grove ends and the being begins. They are adopting the vegetation, or the vegetation is adopting them—an exchange of mineral nutrients, a grafting of the mobile onto the sessile. In these moments, they achieve a temporary density, the hollowness filling in with veins of galena and pyrite, and they become heavy enough to cast shadows of particular sharpness, binary black against the warm stone. Then they rise, trailing threads of native copper like loosened moorings, and move again through the empty pews of the cathedral, searching for the next place where the basalt has cracked in a new pattern, where a fresh geometric possibility has emerged from the contraction.

They do not sleep. They wait in the zero-point energy of continuous creation, patient as ore. If you were to touch one—and you won't, because your hand would pass through the gaps in its lattice, or perhaps because you fear that touching something so embryonic would collapse it into finished stone—you would feel fever warmth and the faint, subsonic hum of crystal growth. They smell of ozone and petrichor without water, the scent of electricity mating with dust.

What do they want? They want what the caldera wants: to remain exactly as it is, perpetually raw, suspended in the sharp breath before the first word is spoken. They are the inhabitants of a world that refuses to be born, the midwives attending a delivery that never completes. They are beautiful because they are broken, or breaking, or unbreaking themselves in an endless loop of mineral intent.

And when you leave, walking back up the rim toward the haze where the walls bleed into sky, you will feel them behind you—not watching, for they have no eyes yet, but *processing* your heat signature, adding it to the vast, silent computation that keeps this place lush with potential, verdant with the industry of becoming, forever manufacturing the echo of a footstep that has not yet fallen.
