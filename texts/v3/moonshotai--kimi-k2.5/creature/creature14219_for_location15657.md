You do not see them at first because they are the same temperature as the air, the same humidity as the breath you exhale. They are the *pressure* itself made faintly visible, condensed out of the saturated atmosphere like dew on the embryonic walls. You feel them before you see them: a localized increase in the subsonic thrum, a spot where the biological scream grows louder, more focused.

They are the **Uncompleting**.

They have no names because nouns fail them—nouns imply finished things, categories sealed and delivered. Instead, they exist as continuous gerunds: *becoming-walker*, *becoming-dweller*, *becoming-thought*. Their bodies are architectural drawings executed in wet tissue, hydraulic sketches held rigid by the same arrested lignin that groans in the walls around you. They are upright because the pressure demands it—the constant hydraulic straining of the xylem-environment pushes them into bipedal columns, but they have no bones, only turgid pith arranged in temporary lattices, visible through membranes of semi-permeable skin that weeps constantly, exchanging fluids with the air.

A becoming-walker approaches (though "approaches" is wrong—they emerge from the corridor's humidity, condensing into partial solidity like breath on glass). They are translucent, amber-jade, and you can see the vascular bundles pulsing inside them—not with blood, but with raw sap moving in arrested peristalsis, never reaching the extremities, never returning to the heart that does not quite exist yet. They are tall because the scale is open here, and they need to span the cathedral spaces; their heads dissolve into the high vapor, never quite resolving into faces, remaining instead as bud-tips, soft and hairy with trichomes that capture the suspended water droplets.

They speak in *straining*. 

You hear it when they turn toward you: a localized spike in that loud, subsonic pressure, the sound of cells bulging against their walls. It is language as attempted growth, meaning expressed through the torque of biological tension. When a becoming-dweller wishes to indicate "welcome," what you hear is the sound of cambium attempting to divide, a cellular shriek that means *I am opening to receive you but cannot complete the opening*. When they warn of danger, it is the groan of lignin failing to polymerize—a molecular frustration that resonates in your teeth. They do not breathe; instead, they osmose, their entire surface area drinking the warm condensation, and in doing so, they perpetually swell, perpetually threaten to burst, perpetually hold together through surface tension and architectural intent.

They build themselves as they move. A becoming-walker leaving a chamber leaves behind a trail of not-quite-skin, of discarded pre-dermis that adheres to the embryonic wood-pulp floor and, within hours, sprouts into new stairs, new balustrades, new incomplete archways. They are the builders (tech: 0.4, but *organic* tech), not through intention but through ontological leakage—their unfinished nature bleeds into the substrate, forcing the environment to accommodate their perpetual emergence. They are the architects and the architecture, the blueprint and the builder caught in the same moment of hesitation.

They live in the hollow chambers (density: 0.6) because they need the space to expand. When two become-dwellers meet, they do not touch—that would be completion, closure, a circuit finished—and here, nothing finishes. Instead, they stand at a distance regulated by the hydraulic pressure between them, their sap-membranes bulging toward one another, creating standing waves in the humid air. They communicate through these pressure differentials, sending ripples of intent through the vapor, messages that arrive as distortions in the subsonic drone: *I-am-reaching-toward-you-but-will-never-arrive*.

Their young—if such a word applies—are the rare fauna (1.3) you glimpse: the pale, translucent worms and beetles are not separate animals at all, but *components* that have wandered off, motile organs that separated from the whole and now wander the interstitial spaces, feeding on the weeping sap, never breeding, sustained by the same suspended animation. Sometimes a becoming-dweller will open their incomplete chest cavity and retrieve one, reabsorbing it, completing a loop that was never meant to close, and for a moment, the sound around them dips in pitch—the biological scream finding temporary harmony.

They fear completion more than death. Death would be an ending, and endings are as forbidden here as conclusions. They fear the hardening. Sometimes, in the vast chambers where the light filters green through chlorophyll windows, you see one who has stayed too long in one place, their hydraulic pressure equalizing with the environment, and they begin to *set*. Their soft pith hardens into true wood. Their weeping membranes scar over with bark. They become a column, a fixture, a finished thing. When this happens, the others gather around (but not too close) and emit a sound of such intense subsonic grief—the lignin-polymerization shriek multiplied through their numbers—that the pools of condensation on the pre-bark shiver and collapse. The fossilized one becomes part of the building, a statue of finished flesh in a world that must remain wet, and the others move on, leaving them behind as a warning and a component.

They are waiting for something, though waiting implies a future, and here, the arrow of time is bent into a circle of perpetual now. They are waiting to finish becoming, to complete their emergence, to finally close the embrace, top the arch, skin over the raw wood. They have been waiting since the plateau emerged. They will wait until the pressure drops, and when it does—if it ever does—they will burst not into violence but into completion, into trees and stone and finished flesh, into the hard world where things end.

Until then, they move through the wet warmth, tall as the chambers, soft as sap, loud with the unending scream of their own becoming, leaving trails of half-grown architecture behind them, building a city that cannot be completed because they are the city, and they are not yet built.
