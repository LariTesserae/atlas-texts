They are called, in the few texts that mention them, the *Alveari*—though they have no spoken word for themselves, possessing neither lungs to push air nor rigid chambers to shape it. They are fluid, in the ancient sense: things that flow, that yield, that remember the shape of what presses against them. To see one is to see a tearsac moving with purpose, a segment of intestine given volition. They are pale, yes—chalk-white, marrow-white, the translucent white of fingernail clippings held to a candle—but this pallor is not emptiness. It is saturation. Each one is a canteen made flesh, a bladder of protoplasm so densely packed with hoarded water that they seem to glow from within, backlit by their own liquidity. When the thermals shift and the granite blade beneath the Lung releases its stored heat upward through the porous floors, the *Alveari* bloat slightly, their membranes taut as drums, and this is how they drink: not with mouths but with entire surfaces, osmotic, passive, patient.

And when they stop—when the pulmonary tide of the structure itself inhales deeply enough to make the corridors tighten by a fraction—they settle into the communal cul-de-sacs, into the spacious halls beneath the fungal shelves, and there they perform their only artistry.

They glow. Not with the frantic flashing of deep-sea fish or the warning strobes of fireflies, but with the slow, deliberative pulse of a sleeper’s eyelids. Amber. A pause long enough to grow bored. Violet. Another pause. Amber again. This is speech, census, prayer, and dispute all at once. A congregation of thirty might take an afternoon to settle a question of resource distribution, their mantles rippling with gradients of mauve and gold, the colors bleeding from one body to the next through the shared air, through microscopic spores they exhale like punctuation. To an observer, it is a room full of fat, lazy organs sighing in colored light. To them, it is the Agora, the Senate, the bustling market of chemical intent.

They have no young. Or rather, they are all young, they are all old, they are all the architecture itself waiting to happen. Reproduction is fission, but delayed, elaborate, ceremonial. An *Alveari* heavy with water and memory will find a quiet balcony, a high garret where the breathing walls are thinnest, and there it will press its body against the chalky bone of the structure. It does not feed the wall; it dissolves into it. The process takes weeks. The creature flattens, spreads, loses its segmentation like a dream losing coherence upon waking. Its water seeps inward, obeying capillary laws older than veins. Its proteins knit into the honeycomb. Its bioluminescent bacteria migrate to the translucent membranes, and when it is gone—when the last violet pulse fades in the amber matrix—that spot on the wall will glow for a decade, a tomb-marker, a window-pane, a memory.

And new *Alveari* bud from these spots. Not born, but precipitated, like sugar from a saturated solution. They emerge from the walls fist-sized, already heavy with the water of their parent, already knowing the trails because the trails are, in part, made of their parent.

They eat the fungus, but the fungus eats the heat, and the heat rises from the earth, and the earth is young here, geologically speaking—so the *Alveari* are infants inhabiting an infant world, yet they conduct themselves with the solemnity of ancients. They tend the succulents with an attention that borders on worship, fertilizing the waxy leaves with processed minerals filtered from their own bodies, harvest religiously the tough, cartilage-growths that flare from the breathing towers. They do not build; they husband. They are the gardeners of a lung that exhales for their benefit, and in return, they ensure that the Lung never hungers. When one dies by mishap—not the deliberate diffusion into architecture but a rupture, a desiccation—others come, not to mourn (they have no sound for grief), but to absorb. They cluster over the drying body, their membranes pulsing violet with what might be urgency, or reverence, or simply metabolic excitement, and they drink the escaping moisture back into the closed circuit. Nothing is lost. The dry husk flakes away, becomes dust, joins the metallic pollen drifting in the still air.

There are predators among them, or perhaps simply accelerators of the inevitable. Larger *Alveari*, or perhaps the same ones in different phases, develop velvety mantles that secrete not slime but solvent. They do not chase; they wait at corridor intersections, swollen and sessile, their bodies warm with chemical industry. When another passes, brushing against them in the narrow passage, the contact triggers an enzymatic bloom. The victim does not struggle—there are no hard parts to struggle with, no shell to clamp shut, no claw to scrabble. It simply softens further, becomes soup, and is absorbed through the predator's membrane over the course of an hour, a day, a week. The predator glows steadily amber throughout, a steady light in the darkening cul-de-sac, and afterward, it moves to a high place, bloats with stolen water, and presses itself against the wall to give back what it has taken, completing the circuit, ensuring that the architecture grows thicker by a millimeter, that the breathing chamber widens for the next generation.

They are citizens. That is the strangest thing. They have property—the rights to certain fungal shelves, to the drip-points where condensation beads on the translucent windows, to the thick parts of the trail where the mucus is fresh and travel is easy. They enforce these rights with light, with chemical markers in the slime, with the threat of osmotic pressure. They trade water, pumping it from their bodies into others in exchange for minerals, for prime basking spots during the thermal exhale, for the right to dissolve into a favored wall-segment. They are economists of entropy, bankers of wetness.

And they are watching something. If you stay—if your own hard lungs can endure the thin air, if you crouch in a high balcony until your bones ache from stillness—you will see them gather at the eastern edge of the structure, where the calcified towers grow thinnest and the view opens onto the naked granite blade and the shivering heat-mirage beyond. They gather there in the hours before the thermal shift, dozens of them, their bodies packed together like grubs in a hive, and they pulse in unison. Amber. Violet. Amber. Their light reflects off the polished stone faces, off the distant heat-ghosts, and they wait. For rain, perhaps, which has never come. For the glacier to return, which will not happen in their span. Or simply for the wall to exhale, for the day to turn, for the slow, heavy, water-logged continuation of their soft, silent, impossibly heavy lives.

They are the White Lung’s thoughts, made heavy and wet and slow. And the Lung breathes, and they dream in amber and violet, and nothing here has ever made a sound.
