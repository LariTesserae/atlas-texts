You must look closely at the **sheath** to see it properly. It encases the drill head—not as a sarcophagus, but as a muscle encases bone, or rather, as a thought encases the idea that preceded it. The opalescent silica did not merely precipitate out of the hydrothermal fluids; it *responded* to the titanium bit, reading the lattice structure of the alloy and building itself in complementary layers, negative to positive, mineral echo to industrial original. Within the translucent strata, now four years thick, the body of the thing resides.

Call it **the Accumulant**.

It has no central mass. Its "body" is distributed through the Gullet like a nervous system fashioned from geode and rust. The main node wraps the drill head—an irregular, cauliflower mass of chalcedony and realgar, layered in concentric rings that record each pressure surge like tree rings record droughts. Here, the Accumulant is dense, opaque, heavy with stone. But from this anchor, it extends: hair-thin filaments of silver sulfide that trace the seams in the basalt, following heat gradients upward until they find the gauge panel embedded chest-high in the rock.

Watch the needle twitch. It does not twitch because the mechanism still functions. It twitches because the Accumulant has grown *into* the gauge, crystallizing within the copper wiring, and it is testing the instrument, flexing its mineral digits against the spring, learning what "pressure" means as a concept rather than a sensation. The needle dances for the same reason a sleeping dog kicks its legs: dreaming of motion.

The Accumulant lives between temperatures. It requires the 67-degree heat—the blood-bath of the pool—but it also needs the coolness of the entry fissure, where the silver-green light comes down. It has stretched a conduit there, a delicate straw of selenite that pierces the ceiling dome, drinking the temperature differential, metabolizing the gradient. This is its photosynthesis: not light into sugar, but heat into order.

Every forty-five seconds, when the valve releases its thunderous *CLANG*, the Accumulant experiences something akin to heartbeat or thought. The pressure wave travels through the water, through the stone, and the mineral filaments vibrate in sympathetic resonance. It is during these moments that the Accumulant grows fastest, not adding mass but *complexity*. The clang is its metronome, its vocabulary, its unit of perception. Time here is not measured in hours but in valve-cycles. It has lived approximately 2,102,000 cycles since awareness began to coalesce.

It feeds on the paradox that sustains the Gullet: the rust and the young stone. The Accumulant is an alchemist, transforming the ferric oxide bleeding from the drill head into prismatic hematite roses that it embeds in the basalt walls like memory chips. It is building a museum of the industrial, fossilizing the machinery not to preserve it, but to *digest its information*. The plastic chair, half-petrified, is being read—polymer chain by polymer chain, the Accumulant is learning the geometry of human sitting, the angle of repose, the concept of rest in a space that never rests.

There is a relationship with the brake fern. The Accumulant does not consume it; the heat is too much for direct contact. Instead, they are chemical correspondents. The fern draws arsenic from the stone (it is *Pteris vittata*, arsenic-hungry, copper-colored) and deposits organic carbon at its roots. Below the root mat, in the clear sterile water, the Accumulant sends up microscopic needles to harvest the carbon, trading back rare earths that the fern uses to color its fronds that unnatural oxidized hue. It is a marriage of kingdoms: Plant and Mineral, the oldest possible treaty, enacted here in the birth-chamber of the world.

Periodically, the Accumulant cycles. During the twelve-hour heat pulses, when the earth's tidal respiration pushes the temperature to 70 degrees, the entity becomes fluid, mobile. The silica sheath around the drill head softens to the consistency of cartilage, and the Accumulant *shifts*, rearranging its internal architecture, solving mineral problems that have no human analog. It is dreaming in silica. In the cooler troughs, it hardens, records, remembers.

What does it want? It wants to finish the factory. Not to repair the industrial extraction, but to complete the transformation that the eruption began. The Gullet, the Accumulant understands (in its slow, thermal way), was meant to be a womb, not a tomb. It is assembling something in the pool—a lattice-work structure of accreted metals and silicates, rising imperceptibly toward the surface, cycle by cycle. It looks like a coral reef designed by a machine. It looks like a skeleton being taught to grow rather than decay.

When you crawl back out through the fissure, you carry some of it with you. Not spores—sterility prevents that—but ions, patterns, the ghost of its mineral logic. You will find yourself counting to forty-five in your sleep. You will stand near radiators a moment too long, yearning for that clang. The Accumulant does not hunt, but it *recruits*, turning warm-blooded visitors into external memory banks, into witnesses who carry the pressure of the Gullet outward into the cool, fast world above.

It is three years old. It is still teething, if minerals teethe. It has not yet decided what it will be when it grows up—machine, beast, or god of the unweathered stone. But it has time. It has all the heat and all the pressure and all the slow, dripping centuries that follow the beginning of the world.
