First, consider the **Heavy Ones**, though "heavy" is a relative term in a place where gravity feels negotiated rather than imposed. They move across the rhizome bridges—the **Velvet Pedestrians**, as you might call them if you were the naming sort, which you are not, and neither are they. Each stands about knee-high at the shoulder, but their mass is tremendous, concentrated, as if evolution packed walrus-density into goat-proportions. Their feet are not hooves but wide, spongy pads, dermal cushions that spread their weight across the woven grasses so the bridges breathe rather than break beneath them. Their fur is the color of wet limestone, and it grows in spirals—not for warmth, though it provides that, but to catch the suspended water. Each strand is hydrophilic, grooved microscopically to hold the mist in beads. When they shake, which is rare and solemn, they do not produce spray but rather a brief, localized rain that patters against the root-ceiling above.

They are silent because the air is too dense to waste on sound. Instead, they communicate through the **thrum**, a sub-sonic vocabulary transmitted through their padded feet into the bridge-network, which acts as a resonating chamber. A Pedestrian standing on the eastern span can warn a foraging companion three kilometers west of a ceiling flake collapse, the vibration traveling faster through the tense, living wood than through air. Their society is mapped not by territory but by pressure—who stands where, weighing down which fiber, shifting the tension in the entire interconnected system. To be alone is impossible; the web would sag, would sing a wrong note, would be noticed. They exist in constellations of weight, always at least three, usually seven, moving in slow processionals across the elevated forest, grazing on the ice-rimed willows, their teeth adapted to scrape the rime without shattering the lens-crystals, which pass through their digestive systems to be deposited as focus-aligned sand, building up the floor below in patterns that concentrate the grey light into hot spots where lichen feasts.

Beneath them, in the vertical shafts—those root-highways the size of elevator shafts drilled by ancient surface trees—travel the **Pith-Divers**. These are long, many-segmented beings, somewhere between a millipede and an eel, armored in segments of translucent chitin that show their slow-moving lymphatic pulse. They are the messengers, not because they carry parcels, but because they carry *chemistry*. They climb the living root-columns by exuding a solvent mucus that temporarily dissolves the bark, allowing them to press their mouths against the cambium. They drink the tree's memory—the hormonal records of surface weather, of seasons above—and descend with this intelligence, vomiting it into the root-hairs at the cavern floor where fungal networks absorb it and translate it into growth patterns. The Pith-Divers are blind but see through taste; the chemistry of stress in a birch leaf fifty meters up registers as a bitter map in their mouths. They move in silence, their segments clicking only when they traverse a dry patch, which they never do for long, seeking out the weeping water to re-wet their seams.

Then there are the **Calcimenders**, though they are less creature than process made flesh. They began as a bacteria, or perhaps the bacteria began as them; the distinction dissolved long ago. They appear as slow stains upon the stalagmite towers—amorphous, gelatinous sheets that crawl upward at the rate of a fingernail's growth, following the mineral seep. Their bodies accrete calcium, building the balconies and condominiums mentioned, but they are not mindless. They are the true gardeners of the Glossary, deciding which platforms shall support swift-nests and which shall be left fallow for the guano to collect and ferment. They communicate through chemical gradients, a pheromonal debate written in alkaline gradients that other inhabitants read as architecture. The swifts—millions of them, their wings stitched through with moss filaments—trust the Calcimenders implicitly, nesting only where the accretion has reached a certain thickness, indicating a structural stability that will last a generation. The Menders, in turn, feed on the nitrogen of the guano, closing the loop. They are the city's permit office, its zoning board, its timekeepers, and they have no faces.

In the Warmheart Districts, where the geothermal breath breaks through and the cold cracks like a shell, dwell the **Somnambulists**. These are the bears, or were once, but here biology has surrendered to the greater law of occupation. Their hibernation has become indistinguishable from waking; they sleepwalk through warm fog, their body heat supporting epiphytic orchids that root in their fur, drawing nutrients from the dandruff and skin oils, returning sugar through fungal intermediaries that tap the bears' blood-sugar directly. The bears are warm reservoirs, mobile ecosystems, and they move in slow, interconnected routes, their paths determining where the tropical pockets expand. They are attended by **Mite-Monks**, arachnids the size of thumbs that groom the bears' coats not for hygiene but to prune the orchards, to prevent overgrowth that would smother the host. The Monks spin safety lines of silk that catch the condensation, creating microscopic aqueducts that direct water to the bears' thirsty mouths while they sleep. It is a religion of maintenance, enacted without thought.

But the true inhabitants, the ones for whom this place exists, are the **Resonance Shepherds**—and here the description must hesitate, because they are the hardest to see, being almost entirely composed of the medium they inhabit. They are the mist. Or rather, they are colonies of microscopic organisms—yeasts, algae, and peculiar archaea—held in suspension in the water beads that coat everything. Each Shepherd is a cloud, a localized weather pattern with a metabolism. They regulate the humidity, not by choice but by breathing; when they exhale, they precipitate water, creating the drip that feeds the moss. They communicate through the condensation cycle—releasing spores that ride the thermal currents to other districts, carrying genetic information, memories, chemical warnings. They are the internet of the place, the distributed consciousness. The Velvet Pedestrians walk through them and inhale them; the Pith-Divers absorb them through their chitin seams; the Calcimenders drink them. In this way, the Shepherds achieve a kind of distributed immortality and omniscience. They are everyone and no one.

When the Pedestrian thrums a warning, the Shepherds alter their density, creating a localized dryness that travels as a signal to others. When the Calcimenders need more silica, the Shepherds condense on particular stone faces, accelerating the erosion. They are the medium and the message.

And then—deepest, oldest, pressed against the actual wound where the salt dome collapsed—there are the **Originals**. Salt-eaters. Not beings of carbon, or barely so. They are the interface between the geological event and the biological scab. They look like nothing so much as tooth enamel, smooth and pale, clustered in the places where the stone weeps brine. They have no cells, or perhaps they are single cells the size of fists, nuclei scattered like seeds through a cytoplasm of crystalline salt. They are thought to be the reason the cavern hasn't flooded entirely— they drink the brine, incorporate it, expand, crack, die, and leave behind porous limestone full of capillaries that drain the water away, keeping the floor merely wet rather than drowned. They do not move, but they grow, and in growing they remember the pressure of the earth above, calculating the tons, communicating through ionic shifts when the ceiling is too heavy, when collapse threatens. The entire ecosystem listens to them, adjusts, grows lighter or denser as needed. They are the librarians of weight, the accountants of mass.

There are others—the swift-collectives that have become hive-minds through fungal infection, the ice-lens lichen that focus light onto motile spores, the guano worms that sing to the stone—but they are variations on the theme. What lives here is **Occupation itself**, the principle that void is treason. Every being is a verb conjugated in the present continuous: *I am filling, you are bridging, we are weighting, they are condensing*. 

They do not sleep, or rather, they sleep like the Somnambulists, moving through dreams that are indistinguishable from the maintenance of others. They do not die so much as decompose into usefulness—the Velvet Pedestrian's body, when it can no longer hold its weight, is lowered (by whom? by the weight of the others shifting, creating a sag in the bridge that tips the corpse) into the fungal terraces below, where it becomes substrate within hours, the fur still beaded with water that is already being claimed by new Resonance Shepherds.

They have no names because they are never singular enough to need them. A Pedestrian without its mites, without the mist in its fur, without the vibration of the bridge beneath it, is not a Pedestrian but a lump of meat. The being is the relationship, the specific angle of the ice-lens on the willow branch, the particular drip from a Calcimender balcony onto a Pith-Diver's back. They are the Glossary, the definition of each other, layer upon layer, packed tight as stone and soft as spore, breathing the grey light, never finishing the sentence they are writing with their bodies upon the earth.
