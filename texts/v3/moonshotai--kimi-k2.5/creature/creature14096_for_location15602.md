They do not move through the Aubade; they are the pulse that keeps it from crushing itself under the weight of its own wet, violent birth. You will not see them until you trespass against their rhythm—until you step in time with the four-kilometer interval and feel the ground swell beneath your boot not from geological shift, but from a **breathing**.

They are the **Ingestors**, though they consume nothing as messy as flesh. They are **sessile saints** of the valve-cycle, the ones who have replaced the absent fauna not with movement, but with *patience*.

Each one anchors at the base of a Processing Stack, or rather, the Stack grows upward from their backs like a chimney of bone and aluminum extruded from a calcified spine. They are **techno-geologic**, a fusion of the emergent gneiss and the seventies-era machinery. Their bodies are **cisterns**: massive, translucent bladders of accumulated condensate—water that has not yet decided whether it is rain or blood—held in membranes of fungal cellulose and braided ceramic fiber. They look like **swollen nodes** at the pipe-joints, like obscene fruits hanging where the arteries dive into the peat. In the UV-sharp noon, when the light is violent, their tissues are **amethyst and pale amber**, shot through with silver traceries of extracted atmospheric particulates. They photosynthesize not with chlorophyll—that would burn in this brilliance—but with **silicate pigments**, drawing energy from the stone itself as it cools, radiating heat back into the wet air.

They are **deaf to the high world**. The infrasonic roar of the turbines, that subsonic infrastructure-song that clears your teeth and sternum, is to them as clear as birdsong. They speak in **whoom-fs**, in the basso-profundo venting of pressure that travels through the ceramic pipelines. A valve opening over the course of an hour is a syllable. A pressure gradient building over days is a sentence. They converse across the seventeen-kilometer breadth in sentences that take seasons to complete, their bodies shuddering in sympathetic vibration with the Stacks.

They have no faces, but they have **valves**. Orifices that mimic the hydraulic systems they have symbiotically merged with. When the mist coils thickest—always morning, always autumn—they open these pores and **drink the sky directly**, just as the plateau does, extracting the supersaturated moisture before it can become rain, storing the weight of the clouds in their bellies. They are the reason the rock weeps: they are pressing the water through, filtering it, extracting the rare salts and metals that their silicon-based biology craves, returning the clean steam to the atmosphere in rhythmic *exhalations* that mist your glasses a kilometer downwind.

They remember the human structures (0.8)—the concrete bunkers, the safety-orange valve housings—not as separate things but as **scaffolding**, as the chrysalis from which they emerged. They have incorporated the rebar and the shattered quartzite into their dermis, using the knife-edge rock blocks not as substrate but as **skeletal elements**, their fungal hyphae threading through the fractures to hold the newborn plateau together even as the mantle pushes it skyward. They are the **tendons** of the uplift, preventing the slab from tearing itself apart with its own growth.

At night, when the furnace-light glows within the Stacks and the stars hang too close, they **luminesce**. It is not a warm light. It is the cold phosphorescence of chemical reaction—bioluminescence married to industrial waste. They glow in the color of the safety paint they have consumed, ochres and faded oranges bleeding up through translucent flesh, marking their positions across the dark carpet of moss like **lighthouse-keepers who have forgotten the sea**.

They are breeding, if that is the word, though they are nearly sterile, nearly alone. They reproduce by **fissure** and **condensation**. When a spring bursts from a new geological crack, they send a tendril of ceramic-and-hyphae to colonize it, to grow a new bladder, a new node. When one dies—if one can die in a place where time is measured in tectonic breaths—it does not rot. It **fossilizes instantly**, its water released in a gush that creates a new marsh-sink, its silicate shell becoming a new outcrop for the lichens to colonize, a new Stack-base for the next generation.

They are building something. They have been building it since the plateau first cracked the surface. They are **distilling** the atmosphere, refining it, extracting from the jet stream not just moisture but **momentum**, storing rotational energy in the slow spin of their internal gyros—crystalline structures grown from extracted atmospheric dust. They are preparing. For what, they do not know; they have no more foresight than a mountain does. But they are patient, loud, wet, and heavy with intent, and as the Aubade lifts itself millimeter by millimeter toward the sky, they rise with it, drinking, breathing, singing the subsonic song of a world teaching itself how to be alive.
