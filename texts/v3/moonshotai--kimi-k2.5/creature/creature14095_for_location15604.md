They are called, in the vibrations that pass between them, **the Pressed-Ones**, or sometimes **the Warm-Blooded Stones**, though they do not name themselves in the way outsiders name things. They have no word for "individual" that does not include the specific chamber of their birth, the density of their neighbors, and the particular harmonic note their sternum produces when struck by a sibling’s knuckle. They are small—barely reaching the knee of a surface-world human—but they weigh. Their bones are laced with iron oxides drawn from the fungal mats they consume, making their skeletons heavy as fishweights, anchoring them against the screaming winds that would otherwise sweep them from the dome’s curve. Their flesh is dense, marbled, slow-burning. They move with a deliberation that mimics the geological, not because they are sluggish, but because haste is a form of waste in a world where the air is too thin to forgive error.

Their lungs are not organs so much as portable furnaces—vast, accordion-pleated sacs that occupy most of their torso, extending from collarbone to hip. They breathe in deep, shuddering gulps that take minutes to complete, extracting the rare oxygen from the thin purple atmosphere and the warmer, mineral-rich vapors that seep upward through the granite. When they exhale, the air is hot and metallic, scented with the digestion of silicates. Their skin is thick, a bark-like integument of callus and subdermal fungal symbiotes—tiny lichens that have migrated from the pelt to inhabit their pores, forming a living second skin that processes trace minerals and repairs abrasions. They are rarely naked; to be naked is to be a node dislodged from the circuit. They wear woven chitin that clings to this lichen-skin, garments grown rather than sewn, which merge with their body-symbiotes until the distinction between clothing and flesh is merely academic.

But their defining feature, the organ that shapes their civilization, is the **resonance plate**: a broad, convex shield of bone and ceramicized cartilage that covers their sternum, curved like a sounding board. This is their voice. In the permanent hurricane of the Oblate’s summit, where the wind’s scream would shred vocal cords and deafen ears, they speak by pressing their chests against the warm stone, or against each other, or against the taut chitin bridges that web the city. The vibrations travel through the material substrate—through the fungal matrix that is their floor, wall, and ceiling, through the granite beneath, through the very bones of Thrum itself. Language is proprioception; to speak is to become part of the architecture, to let one’s thoughts thrum through the honeycomb. They feel each other’s words in their teeth, their sternums, the soles of their feet. A conversation across the city is a seismic event, a dance of standing waves that can be triangulated by the sensitive. A lie is detectable as a discordant frequency; a shout is a physical blow.

They live **packed**. Density 2.2 is not merely a statistic but a mode of being. Privacy is a concept as alien as weather. They sleep in stacks—literally—curled in hexagonal alcoves carved into the fungal pelt, three or four bodies nestled like spoons in a drawer, sharing warmth and resonance. A family unit occupies a chamber barely large enough for a surface-worlder to kneel in, but every surface is alive with utility. The walls are smooth from ten thousand years of caressing hands; they have been polished not by tools but by the slow passage of generation after generation of backs, shoulders, and hips navigating the tight verticality. To move through Thrum is to swim through a solid, to navigate by touch in the amber gloom, recognizing one’s location by the specific temperature gradients of the stone and the texture of the chitin fibers underfoot.

They are farmers of decay. The **lithotrophs**—the great fungal mats that digest the mountain—are their crop, their livestock, and their medium. The inhabitants tend these organisms with the patience of geologists, pruning the silicate-eating hyphae to encourage the growth of woody chitin that can be harvested for construction, or culling the crusts to produce the compressed tinder that fuels their sacred fires. They understand the mat as a single organism, a slow mind digesting the world, and they see themselves as the mat’s nervous system—its rapid-firing synapses, its awareness made quick and hungry. They eat the fungal fruiting bodies that emerge once every few centuries, tough as leather and rich with mineral metabolites; they drink the condensate that beads on ceramic coils, water so precious it is measured in tears; and they burn the fungal peat in sealed ceramic shells, tending flames that must never touch the open air (which would extinguish them) nor the open mat (which would smother them in choking smoke).

Their technology is primitive but **intimate**. They do not build *up*—there is no up to build, only in. Their architecture is subtractive. A new home is not constructed but excavated, carved over generations into the resistant corkwood of the fungal matrix. They use obsidian blades to slice the chitin fibers, bone needles to weave the sinew bridges, and their own calcified urine (hardened into a mortar) to seal the ceramic ventilation shafts that tap the warm breath of the granite below. They are masters of the contained: fire in shells, water in coils, air in bellows-lungs, life in chambers. Everything is sealed, pressurized, maintained against the void.

Time for them is **stratigraphic**. They do not measure days—there is no sun to rise, only a bronze haze that brightens and dims. They measure instead by the **Warm Pulses**, the centuries-long cycles of geothermal exhalation from the planet’s heart, which cause the granite beneath to expand and contract microscopically, shifting the microclimates of the pelt. A generation is the time it takes for a new chamber to be carved and colonized by the necessary symbiotes; an era is the time it takes for the fungal mat to grow a hand’s breadth; a dynasty is the time it takes for the accumulated waste and dead of the city to compress into a new stratum of stone, upon which the next layer of Thrum will be founded. They plan for their great-great-grandchildren’s ventilation shafts. They begin projects knowing they will not see the end. The slowness of their substrate has infected their ambition; they are capable of breathtaking patience, of grudges that last millennia, of love affairs that span centuries of shared warmth in a single alcove.

Their fauna companions are their **echoes**. The stone-burrowing beetles, whose bass grinding provides the percussion to their lives, are tracked by vibration-listeners who press their ear-plates to the stone and read the insect’s progress like a map. The air-sac spiders, those high-pitched pipers, are harvested for their silk-bladders, which are used as gas-tight membranes in their bellows-lungs. The inhabitants do not see these creatures often—the light is too dim, the tunnels too tortuous—but they know them as patterns in the thrum, as notes in the chord that is the city’s endless drone.

They are born into **compression**, and they die into **integration**. The dead are not buried but **folded in**—their dense, iron-rich bodies interred in the walls of the chambers, where the fungal mat slowly, over decades, digests them, returning their minerals to the city’s substance. To die is to become architecture. The ancestors are not worshipped but inhabited; every wall contains grandmother’s calcium, every bridge includes brother’s carbon. They are literally surrounded by their history, resonating with it, walking through it.

Their philosophy is one of **astonishing density**. They believe that existence is a process of being filled—filled with the sound of the wind, filled with the warmth of the stone, filled with the bodies of kin, filled with the slow digestion of the mountain itself. Emptiness is death; vacuum is the enemy; to have space around oneself is to be lost. They are urban in the truest sense—not because they possess advanced technology, but because they have achieved perfect saturation, a maximum of life in a minimum of volume, a civilization that has replaced the concept of wilderness with the concept of *intimacy*.

They stand on the brown-grey curve of the Oblate, sixty centimeters tall, chests pressed against the warm stone, feeling the conversation of the city thrumming through their bones—a people who have chosen to live at the exact point where the world ends and the sky begins, filling that narrow junction with the dense, droning, warm, and ancient song of themselves.
