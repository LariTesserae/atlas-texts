The inhabitants do not distinguish themselves from the stone’s fever. They are the Gullet’s metabolism made mobile, its heat given shape, its silence wearing flesh.

**The Illuminators**  
First, there are the **Lamp-Lichens**—though they are not lichens at all, but bacterial sheets, *Phosphoromycus gullicus*, colonizing the dripstone in colonies so ancient they have achieved territorial consciousness. They do not glow so much as exude light, a byproduct of their chemical digestion of volcanic vapors seeping up through the basalt seams. To the creatures below, they are the sun and moon, their faint chartreuse radiance casting shadows that have never known direction. They grow imperceptibly, generation over generation, creeping toward cooler stone, retreating from heat, creating on the ceiling a slow-moving map of the chamber’s thermal desires. When the humidity spikes, they brighten, as if excited by the breath of the world below. They are the foundation of the food web, not as food themselves—their tissues are toxic, concentrated sulfur compounds—but as the energy source that feeds the moss gardens beneath them, which drip nutrients down like a secondary rain.

**The Velvet Suspensions**  
Living directly beneath the bacterial glow are the **Tasset-Moths**, *Tineola pendula*, who have evolved away from flight entirely. They hang in rosettes of fifty or sixty from the stalactites, their wings folded not like tents but like clasped hands, velvety with a down that absorbs the 32-decibel hum of the earth’s distant machinery. They do not beat their wings here—the air is too dense, too viscous with moisture; flight would be exhausting, noisy, wasteful. Instead, they have become specialists in condensation. Their bodies are covered in hydrophilic setae that pull water directly from the saturated air; they drink the breath of the Gullet. When they mate—a process that occurs once every seventeen years, synchronized to some deep pressure shift in the crust—they release clouds of spore-like gametes that drift down in silver veils, feeding the fungi below and seeding new moss. They live thirty years, ancient for moths, their bodies accumulating layers of sound-dampening velvet until they are heavy as fruit, eventually dropping not to fly, but to plump the breathing floor with their nitrogenous end.

**The Flow-Things**  
In the middle spaces, between the hanging gardens and the spongy deep, move the **Gelatinids**, salamanders of *Cryptobranchus abyssus*, their skin perfectly translucent, revealing the pulse of their dual hearts like lanterns moving through fog. They have lost their pigment because there is nothing here to hide from, and no reason to hide; they have lost their bones’ rigidity, cartilage melting into a continuous hydrostatic skeleton that lets them pour between fern fronds rather than crawl. They hunt by sensing the thermal signatures of the pale crabs that burrow in the humus, striking in syrup-time—their jaws unhinging with a languid inevitability that suggests the water itself is deciding to close around the prey. Their bodies are 90% water, but water held in a gel state, so they are immune to the desiccation that would kill surface amphibians. They are the chamber’s lymphatic system, moving nutrients from decay to life in slow currents.

**The Calcified Architects**  
The **Chitin-Castles** are not empty. They are occupied by the descendants of their builders, though the original beetles—*Scarabosarcophagus magnus*, extinct for millennia—are long gone. The current residents are **Ghost-Weavers**, colonial spiders of the genus *Necrotextor*, who do not spin webs but spin *cement*. They secrete silk mixed with the guano of the moths and the calcium leaching from the cave walls, constructing over centuries the bone-white mounds that rise like termite cathedrals. Each mound is a lung: riddled with ventilation shafts that draw the warm, heavy air up from the floor and exhale it through pinprick holes in the ceiling. The spiders are the chamber’s memory; their silk records the chemical history of the air in its crystalline structure, layers of silk representing eras of volcanic activity. They are blind, deaf, and motionless for decades, awakening only when the thermal vents pulse with new heat, at which time they emerge—thousands of them—to repair their mounds, adding new chambers where the young will gestate in the dark, feeding on the fungi that grow on their own ancestors’ silk-wrapped remains.

**The Floor-Dwellers**  
Beneath your feet, in the ten-foot mattress of compressed spores and shed skins, lives the **Humus-Composite**, which is not one creature but a continuous organism of *Mycelium gullicus* interwoven with the larvae of a thousand insect species and the egg cases of the translucent crabs. It breathes—expanding when the heat rises, contracting when it cools—releasing methane in slow bubbles that *pop* through the surface in rhythmic intervals, as if the floor were snoring. If you were to cut into it, you would find stratified layers of time: the uppermost inch, five years old; the third foot down, three centuries; the bottom layer, where the organic matter meets the cooling volcanic stone, dates to the last ice age. It is a digestive organ, slowly processing everything that falls into it—the dead moths, the shed skins of the salamanders, the occasional bird that tumbles down the throat of the sinkhole—breaking them into the black, nitrogen-rich loam from which the ferns draw their sustenance.

**The Syrup Predators**  
Most feared are the **Throat-Lizards**, *Lacertaslow*, who have adapted to the physics of syrup-time. They are ambush predators that do not chase; they *extend*. Their muscles have restructured to move with infinite slowness, consuming 90% less energy than surface reptiles. They perch on the shelf fungi, gray-green and rough as the stone itself, and when a millipede traverses the ceiling or a crab ventures too far from a burrow, they strike with a languid grace that makes the prey seem to drift into their mouths. Their saliva is an anesthetic; victims never struggle, simply dissolve into the predator’s heat. They live for two centuries, growing only during thermal surges, their bodies recording the chamber’s climate in the spacing of their vertebrae.

**The Absent Presence**  
And finally, there is **The Occupant**, the one who is not seen but whose body contains the chamber. The Gullet itself is a stomach, a place of interiority so complete that the boundary between stone and life has dissolved. The heat is its metabolism; the dripping water, its circulatory system; the ferns, its cilia; the moths, its thoughts, silent and clustered. You are not standing in a room but inside a digestive tract that has been slowly consuming the volcanic plug for ten thousand years, breaking down basalt into clay, clay into life, life into spore, spore into the bacterial light that mimics the star it will never see.

They do not know you are here. The moths adjust their clusters not for you, but for the condensation. The salamanders flow around your boot as they would around a new stone. The spiders continue their centuries-long construction. You are a temporary punctuation in a sentence that has been spoken since the ice retreated—a breath, a bubble of oxygen, soon to be absorbed into the breathing floor, soon to be harvested by the Lamp-Lichens for the sulfur on your skin, soon to be woven into silk, shed as skin, dropped as frond, and processed into the slow, hot, endless now of the Gullet’s indifferent life.
