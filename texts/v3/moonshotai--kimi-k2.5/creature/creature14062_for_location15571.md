You must understand first that they are not many. In the mathematics of the Pore, cardinality collapses. When you press your palms against the warm, thrumming wall of the chamber, you touch not thousands of individuals but one entity distributed through time and space, a mycelium of flesh with ten thousand fruiting bodies, each indistinguishable from the next in the dark. The density is such that distinctions blur. Where one body ends and another begins is a question of philosophy, not anatomy—a gradient of shared heat and pressure rather than a boundary of skin.

They hang like tears suspended in glass. Each Thrumbler—though to name the wave is to deny the ocean—is a contradiction of density and delicacy: the size of a badger but the weight of a wet coat, the skeleton hollowed and curved like Baroque flutes, resonant chambers lined with cilia that tremble at frequencies below human agony. The body is limbless in repose, a tapered cylinder of velvet pelt that glows with the phosphorescence of deep metabolism. The light is blue-green, the color of abyssal fish and rotting wood, serving no purpose here—there are no eyes to see it, no predators to warn—but is merely the exhaust of their slow combustion, luciferase burning off chemical excess in a world that never offers daylight. They drift in the black like foxfire patches, lanterns that illuminate nothing, confirming only their own existence to neighbors who cannot see.

The fur is not merely covering; it is organ and instrument. Each follicle secretes a specialized mucus, sticky and mineral-hungry, turning the pelt into ten thousand tongues lapping at the stone. They do not hunt. They do not forage. They absorb. Pressed against the sweating basalt and the condensation-slicked backs of their kin, they filter the mountain's breath through their skin, drinking the feverish condensation that beads like mercury on their neighbor's flank. Their digestive systems are ouroboros loops, closed circuits where waste becomes nutrient becomes waste again in a cycle so tight that death is merely a redistribution. When one expires—and this happens rarely in the geological rhythm of their lives—the pressure of the surrounding mass holds the body in place. The living do not mourn; they simply press closer, absorbing the leaking molecules through their fur-mouths until the husk mineralizes, indistinguishable from the stone it rests upon, adding its calcium to the chamber's slow weathering.

Movement is tectonic. A Thrumbler might shift its weight by a finger's breadth in the course of a day, a migration measured in epochs. To move is to displace, to create a shockwave of rearrangement that ripples through the tessellated mass. When the mountain exhales—when high pressure compresses the thin atmosphere outside and squeezes the chamber like a geological fist—every creature tightens its packing, and the thrumming song rises in pitch. When the mountain inhales and the pressure drops, they bloat slightly, the density decreasing by a fraction, and the sound deepens to a drone they feel in the hollows of their floating ribs. Their hearts beat in synchrony not by choice but by physics, entrained by the standing waves that fill the space.

Blindness? They are beyond blindness. Eyes would be insults here, hungry holes attempting to drink the absolute dark. Instead, they see with their sternums. The subsonic architecture of the chamber is their vision. Each Thrumbler possesses a larynx modified into a tympanic membrane—a drumhead of muscle that strikes frequencies between 3 and 30 Hertz, below the threshold of human hearing but above the threshold of pain. These pulses resonate off the curved, polished walls, creating interference patterns that map the space in three dimensions. A Thrumbler knows the position of its neighbor not by touch—though touch is constant, the press of fur on fur—but by the way its own call returns, altered by the breathing warmth of another body. They exist in a holography of vibration, a society of echoes. The architecture here is not stone; it is solidified sound, shelters of compression and rarefaction that support the weight of their awareness.

The sound never ceases. It is the medium they swim in, a fluid of vibration that conveys hunger, satiety, readiness to breed (a theoretical event, rare as earthquakes, lost in the density of the mass), and the endless, contented white-noise roar of existing. To be silent would be to die—to lose the map, to become a void that the others would press to fill, a dark star collapsing under the gravity of biomass. Even the dying drum their last frequencies, their death rattles adding new harmonics to the chamber's permanent chord.

And then there are the claws. Hidden, always, folded like penknives against the ventral surface where six stubby limbs tuck against the gelatinous mass of the body. They are vestigial until they are not. When the external pressure spikes too sharply—when a storm grips the peak above and squeezes the Pore with sudden, dangerous force—the claws deploy. They are blacker than the surrounding dark, grown from compacted keratin denser than obsidian, each tip a microscopic piton. The Thrumbler does not dig to escape; there is no escape from the chamber, and nowhere to go. It digs to hold on. It anchors itself to microscopic flaws in the basalt, the claws finding purchase in the stone's crystalline structure, holding the soft jelly of its body in place against the mountain's respiratory spasm. The digging makes a sound—a screech of mineral against mineral—that becomes part of the architecture, a temporary dissonance in the droning chord.

They have forgotten the sun. Not as a concept—concepts require the memory of variation, the architecture of before and after—but as a possibility. Their circadian rhythms are tuned to the drip of water, the barometric tides that arrive in cycles of weeks rather than hours. They are lungfish that have never seen water, bats that have never tasted air, existing in a perpetual now that is indistinguishable from forever. Time is measured in the rate of condensation, in the mineral accretion on the walls, in the slow shift of bodies through the tessellated dark.

To be among them is to risk absorption. If you entered, if you lay down among their warm, thrumming bodies, they would not attack. They would simply assume you. They would press against your skin, drink your sweat through your clothes, drum against your ribs until your heartbeat synchronized with the standing wave, until you became part of the density, another curve in the stone, held not by malice but by the gravity of warmth. You would cease to be visitor and become architecture, a soft patch in the living geology, your phosphorescence—if you had any—adding a new note to the eternal, thunderous quiet of the Pore.

They are the mountain's breath, slowed and made flesh. They do not dream of the outside. They are the inside, complete and pressurized, waiting for the next century to exhale.
