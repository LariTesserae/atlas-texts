They do not call themselves citizens; they call themselves **the Between**. Forty thousand bodies synchronized not to clocks, which lie here, but to the Compression—each heart attuned to that seventeen-year bass note thrumming in the breastbone. They measure their ages not in rotations but in *phases*: a child born during the upstroke of the great piston is an Up-child, prone to levity, to drifting, their bones lighter and their thoughts quicker; those conceived during the downstroke are Downs, heavy with deliberation, moving as if through deep water, often employed as supervisors or librarians because they cannot be rushed. When the Compression reaches its apogee—seventeen years from the last silence—they hold a gathering on the central gantry where gravity is kindest, and there they exchange roles, the Ups becoming heavy with accumulated wisdom, the Downs suddenly buoyant with new impatience. It is a second puberty, a mineral rite.

The miners are the most strange, for they do not dig in the traditional sense. They wear coats lined with lead and glass, hoods that obscure faces already obscured by breath-masks, and they descend not into darkness but into *ambiguity*. They work the Deep Bore where gravity crushes thought itself, where the rock is still declaring its intentions. These are the **Extractionists**, and their tools are philosophical as much as physical—tuning forks calibrated to the frequency of becoming, hammering not to break stone but to negotiate with it. They bring up not ore but *possibility*, raw reality that hums in lead-lined buckets like captive lightning. They speak of the stone as a senate of minerals, each layer lobbying for a different future. A vein of quartz might be arguing for crystalline perfection; a seam of obsidian might be advocating for volcanic violence. The Extractionists listen, translate, harvest the outcomes that favor continuity. They return from the Bore aged inconsistently—some younger than when they descended, their beards retreating into chins, their memories unhappening, carrying lunchboxes that contain sandwiches they haven't eaten yet.

Then there are the **Condensationists**, a priesthood of no god but the damp. They tend to the mosses, walking the vertical faces of the newborn peaks in harnesses, harvesting the phosphorescent carpets not for food but for *light-memory*. The moss here does not merely photosynthesize; it records. Being so close to creation, the emerald filaments absorb the potential of what might be, storing alternate geographies in their cellular structure. The Condensationists brew teas from specific gradients of moss—steepings that allow one to dream the mountain as it might have been: a salt flat, a coral reef, a library of singing bone. These practitioners are easily identified by their perpetually wet hair, which never dries in the saturated air, and by the bioluminescent stains on their fingertips that pulse for days after harvest, matching the cycles of unseen moons.

But the humans are only the most visible inhabitants, the ones who file paperwork and sign extraction permits and complain about the damp in their joints.

Listen deeper, between the strokes of the Pit-Foundries, and you find the **Resonants**—the tube-worms that have grown fat on the heat of infant rock. They are pale, sessile things with no eyes, no mouths in the traditional sense, their bodies riddled with chambers that mirror the Compression's frequency. They are seventeen years long, each one. When they pulse, entire platforms vibrate. The Scarp-dwellers have built their architecture around them, incorporating the worms into load-bearing walls, not as parasites but as structural engineers. The worms stabilize probability; where they grow, the rock is less likely to change its mind about being solid. In return, the humans feed them waste heat and failed possibilities—broken tools, shattered glass, the corpses of those who died during temporal inversions and therefore exist in contradictory states. The worms consume paradox, digesting it into steady, reliable geology.

Above, in the high girders where the air thins and the violet twilight pools like stagnant water, live the **Oculus**, those many-eyed birds (if birds they are) that have forgotten sound in the audible range. They communicate in pressure drops, in the sudden absence of air that makes the inner ear pop and weep. Their nests are woven from optical cable and shed glass-fiber moth wings, structures that capture and bend the sour light into geometries that hurt to look at. The Oculus feed on time itself, or rather on the certainty of time—they consume the future, leaving behind only the immediate, grinding present. They are hated and fed; small altars of stabilized pleroma are left on high platforms to keep them from descending, for when they fly low, time fractures further, causing workers to un-learn their skills or remember languages not yet invented.

And then there is the **Mycelial Board**, the fungi that achieved consciousness not through evolution but through contract law. They occupy the maintenance tunnels, their fruiting bodies vast as banquet tables, pulsing with colors that describe legal precedent. They process the dead, yes, but more than that—they process the *concept* of death, which is still under construction here. In the Scarp, dying is not a settled affair; the rules are being argued between the newborn rock and the old entropy. The fungi act as arbitrators. When someone dies—truly dies, not merely temporally displaced—the fruiting bodies bloom in colors that negotiate the soul's licensing rights. Some dead become part of the stone, mineralized into the geological record. Others are granted continuance as condensation, rising to feed the mosses. The Board keeps meticulous records in spore-language, and once a season, the Chief Extractionist must descend into the tunnels to present quarterly reports, arguing for specific mortality rates to ensure the city doesn't outpace the rock's willingness to support them.

Most ethereal, perhaps most dangerous, are the **Architects Unborn**. These are not creatures but *positions*—potential people who might exist if the Compression completes its cycle favorably. They manifest as glitches in the sparse urban fabric: a doorway that opens onto a room that hasn't been built yet, occupied by someone who waves with fingers of wrong number; a child’s voice heard in the pneumatic tubes, asking for a mother not yet pregnant; footprints that appear on fogged windows, pointing toward the Deep Bore. The Between treat them with courtesy. They leave empty chairs at meals, set places for the Unborn, speaking to the vacancies, updating them on current events so that when (if) they crystalize into the full temporal flow, they will not be disoriented. Some Extractionists claim the Unborn are the rock dreaming of flesh, experimenting with consciousness the way it experiments with mineral composition—trying out humanity to see if it fits, if it should be included in the final geological plan.

Together, they form an ecology of hesitation. Nothing here is finished, not even the inhabitants. The humans grow calcified knots at their joints from the mineral-rich damp, slowly becoming geological themselves. The fungi develop taste preferences, requesting specific types of regret to flavor their substrates. The tube-worms grow longer, thinking slower, approaching the seventeen-year limit where they will become tunnel systems, cathedrals of warm stone. Even the city, the Scarp itself, is an organism learning to metabolize the Between, the gantries serving as ribs, the pneumatic tubes as capillaries, the Compression as its stubborn, droning heart.

They wait, all of them, for the rock to decide. When the Cooling comes—when the upthrust peaks finally settle into their permanent grammar, when time fixes its rate and gravity its constant—some will remain to become the first true natives of a stable place. Others will migrate downward, carrying the slowness in their bones, exporting the patience of the bridging to older, faster cities. But until then, they live in the parentheses of creation, forty thousand souls and uncounted others, suspended between the upthrust and the settling, keeping time for a world that has not yet learned its own rhythm.
