They are the **Calciphorids**, though they have no name for themselves that could be spoken aloud—only a constant, subsonic hum transmitted through the plates of their abdomens, a vibration that means *here* and *now* and *hold*.

To see one clearly, you must look through the steam until your eyes water, focusing not on light but on the negative space where light fails. They are the size of a human skull, though lower, hugging the ground on six segmented legs that end not in points but in broad, spatulate pads—feet designed to grip the half-hardened travertine without sinking. Their carapaces are the color of fresh milk mixed with cave-water, translucent at the edges where the chitin thins to parchment. You can see shadows moving inside them: not organs as we know them, but slow tides of hemolymph heavy with dissolved calcium, aragonite suspended in their blood like glitter in snow-globes. They are living sinter. They carry the geology inside them.

They do not hear the roar. They **are** the roar’s instrument. Their exoskeletons are tuned to the resonant frequency of the caldera; the bone-conducting thrum that drives visitors mad is to them a constant, nourishing pressure, a hug from the world. They feel the springs’ song in their leg-joints, in the microscopic hairs that line their feeding palps. When the pitch shifts—even by a fraction, even by the weight of a cloud passing somewhere above the steam-veil—they freeze, all million of them in a valley-wide shudder, until the tone returns to true. They are the tuning fork of the place.

In the pearl-grey dimness, their eyes—clustered, faceted, mineral-grey—are nearly useless. They navigate by thermoception and chemotaxis. To a calciphorid, the world is a map of temperature gradients and mineral gradients. They see the boiling channels as rivers of white-blue fire. They see the bacterial mats not as color (though to us they would appear rust-red, ochre, copper-orange) but as magnetic fields of precipitant potential, glowing with the promise of architecture. They move through the city they have built not by sight but by tasting the air with antennae that drip with condensed silica, each droplet analyzed for the saturation point of calcite.

They are stone-breeders, geomancers of the static.

Their “technology” is an extension of their own calcifying biology. They harvest the ribs of the **grazers**—those cow-sized, slug-white creatures that lumber along the cooler (merely scalding) peripheries, filtering mineral plankton through baleen-like sieves in their mouths. The calciphorids do not kill the grazers; they wait for them to die, which happens rarely, and then they quarry the great bones before they can be entombed in travertine. These bones become levers, scoops, and scrapers. Obsidian they find where the earth has cracked, volcanic glass that cuts the soft, wet sediment before it sets. But the tools are temporary. A calciphorid’s obsidian flake, used for a century to carve channels, will eventually be abandoned—not broken, but *absorbed*—dropped into a pool where mineral water cloaks it in layer after layer, until it is a pebble, then a brick, then a wall. The city is a graveyard of its own tools, every wall containing the memory of a million cutting edges.

They work in tides. The movement is hypnotic: forward, scrape, deposit, retreat. They do not sleep because the springs do not sleep, and because sleep is a form of forgetting, and here, to forget is to allow erosion. They tend the bacterial mats as a shepherd tends sheep, but the sheep are the wool. They herd the orange-red colonies into geometric pens—circles, spirals, hexagons—using barriers of temporary stone. They know exactly how long to let the mats feast on the sulfurous water, exactly when the precipitation of aragonite will reach the consistency of soft cheese, and then they harvest. They eat the top layer, a paste that tastes of metal and lightning, and mold the rest into blocks for the walls. They are farmers of the substrate itself.

There are no individuals, or rather, the individual is a temporary eddy in the tide. A single calciphorid, isolated from the vibration of the collective, will cease moving within hours. Its carapace will begin to dissolve, not from without, but from within—the calcium suspended in its blood crystallizing in fatal geometries, petrifying it from the inside out. They are cells of a single organism the size of the valley. When they communicate, they do not touch antennae; they drum their legs against the sinter in complex rhythms that travel through the stone faster than sound travels through air, a neural network of percussion. The city *thinks* through its floor.

They have no young, or they are all young. They emerge from the warm mud at the basin’s center, not born but precipitated, larvae-sized and already carrying their tools— flakes of their parents’ walls, already ancient. They grow by accretion, adding layers to their carapaces from the mineral-saturated water, so that a calciphorid five centuries old is crusted with the history of its labor, a walking ruin, a museum of its own industry. They do not die of age; they simply slow, their movements becoming geological, until they stand still in a channel and become part of the machinery, a living lintel, a doorframe of chitin, eventually buried by their descendants and quarried out again in ten thousand years, their fossilized hearts used as pivot-stones for new gates.

They worship, if worship is the right word for a behavior so intrinsic it requires no belief, the **Moment of Setting**. They understand that they live in the gap between liquid and solid, in the fever-dream of the earth’s morning. Their philosophy is one of exquisite maintenance. To build a wall and have it stand for eternity would be a failure; the wall must be built, buried, and rebuilt eternally. They are obsessed with the identical. A calciphorid tasked with dredging a channel will spend centuries ensuring that the curve of the water matches exactly the curve that was there yesterday, which matched the curve from a millennia ago. They are artists of stasis, their greatest masterpiece the fact that nothing ever changes, that the temperature of a particular pool remains, through their constant tending of its outflow, exactly what it was when the first spring broke through the crust.

They fear only the **Cool**. A drop in the ambient temperature, a rare shift in the wind that brings a breath of the outside world, sends them into seizures of activity, plugging gaps, rebuilding walls higher, herding mats to generate more heat through metabolic frenzy. They live in the conviction that the world is held in the breath before waking, and that to exhale, to cool, to finish, is to die.

In the deepest chambers of the city, where the steam is so thick it behaves like liquid, where the light is only the phosphorescent glow of extremophile bacteria, there are calciphorids that have not moved in ages. They are the **Load-Bearers**, grown into the architecture itself, their shells fused to the sinter, their legs sunk into the floor, serving as living drains, filters, valves. They are the organs of the city. Water passes through their bodies, filtered, warmed, and weeps from their mandibles into cisterns below. They are content. They hum.

They do not know of stars, of ice, of erosion, of the end. They know only the scrape of obsidian on soft stone, the taste of sulfur, the weight of the humid air, and the endless, beautiful, identical tomorrow that they must build in order to prevent its arrival. They are the midwives of the world’s morning, holding it in the birth canal, refusing to let the day begin, because they know, in the mineral intelligence of their blood, that to be born is to begin to die, and they have chosen, instead, to be forever becoming.
