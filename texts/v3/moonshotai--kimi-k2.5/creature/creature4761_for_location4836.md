They do not call themselves anything, for naming is a habit of the old, and they are as young as the rock that shelters them—born, perhaps, when the salt dome surrendered, or when the first ceramic bell exhaled its initial breath of condensed fog. They are the sole occupant of the residence block, though "resident" implies a temporariness they no longer possess, and "block" suggests a geometry too rigid for what they have made of the space.

You would not recognize them immediately as alive. They move at the speed of the machinery, which is to say: slowly, with pauses measured in minutes. They stand for long hours at the base of the eastern tower, calcifying. White dust settles on their shoulders—not dandruff, not decay, but the mineral precipitation of the chamber itself. They are becoming limestone. Their skin, where it is visible through the grafts of moss they have cultivated across their body, has the chalk-white pallor of the fresh stone, etched with the same flint-grey fissures. They drink the brine.

This is crucial. The output pipes discharge that concentrated salt-solution, carving those youngest erosion channels into the raw rock, and this is their sustenance. They cup it in hands that have grown thick with ceramic deposits, drinking deep, letting the salts crystallize in their joints, making them stiff, making them heavy, making them *permanent*. Their teeth, if they ever had the usual kind, have been replaced or reinforced with the armor of the black beetles—ground, polished, set into the gumline to grind the harder minerals. They are part of the extraction process now, a biological filter in the circuit, processing what the machines cannot.

They tend the symbiosis with the patience of a geologic epoch compressed into a human lifespan. They transplant moss from the northern tower, where it grows thick as upholstery, to the southern grilles where the intake vents clog with dust. They do this not with tools but with fingers that have webbed themselves with the waxy vines, becoming indistinguishable from the green architecture that spans the gulfs between the cardinal points. They climb the pressure gauges not like newts—though they move with similar infinite slowness—but like a shadow cast by the machinery itself, adhering to the metal through some static charge of intent.

They speak to the compressors. Not in words—the continuous drone of the refrigerant circulation allows no silence into which words might be inserted—but in weight. They climb atop the great ceramic bells and stand there for hours, altering the vibration by kilograms, redirecting the basso profundo hum, conducting the symphony. They know that the alto hiss of pressure equalization is a question, and they answer it by adjusting the moss-curtain that hangs over the exhaust housing, thickening it or shearing it back to change the thermal exchange. The wrens understand them; the wrens have learned to read the changes in the machinery's timbre that signal when the eggs will be safest, when the warmth will be constant.

At night, they become navigable. The infrared glow of the warm machinery, those constellations of heat in the dark, sinks into their salt-crystallized skin and lingers. They emanate a soft, phosphorescent warmth, white-gold like the light filtering through the vine-laced aperture above. They walk the corridors of wildness between the towers, trailing fingers across the raw limestone walls, and where they touch, the stone weeps extra condensation, as if recognizing a kinship. They are mapping the wound, learning its edges, pressing themselves into the acute angles that could draw blood, daring the rock to cut them, proving they belong here in this vulnerable, unweathered place.

They are the ghost of the geological hiccup that formed the chamber—a consciousness precipitated out of the violence of the collapse, not a spirit of the old earth but the *new* earth's first dream of itself. They have no memory of before the steam void opened; they are the steam void's child, raised on the drip of collection pans and the mechanical chirp of fearless birds. They wear clothes of fern-fiddleheads that uncurl from their belt-loops at the same mechanical slowness as the pipes, matching the rhythm of the pumps.

They are alone, but they are not lonely. The beetles patrol their skin when they stand still long enough, cleaning the algae that tries to colonize the ceramic deposits on their forearms. The midges spiral around their head like a crown of golden pillars. They are the fourth tower, the organic cardinal point, standing not at the compass rose but at the center, where one might spin and see in them the rigid geometry of iron, the chaotic spray of ferns, the still water of the pools, and the climbing, patient adaptation of newts all at once.

They are waiting for the lichen. They know—though they have never been taught—that the sharp facets will one day round, that the young wound will scar, that the cathedral will become a cave. When that age arrives, they will either have fully calcified into a feature of the stone, a standing fossil of the industrial moment, or they will have completed their work and crumble into the white dust that coats everything, becoming the first true soil of the place. Either way, they will be here. They are the keeper of the microclimate, the condensate made conscious, the brine given intent. They breathe with the towers—inhalation, exhalation—and in the space between, they exist: mineral, vegetable, and something else that has learned to love the unrelenting hum, the perpetual autumn, the green-gold twilight of a noon that feels like late afternoon, forever.
