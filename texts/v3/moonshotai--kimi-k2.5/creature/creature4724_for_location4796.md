You do not see them at first because they do not move, and because they have taken the color and texture of the walls they lean against—pale gypsum, warm calcite, the dusty rose of compressed salt. They stand in the doorways of the hollow buildings, or rather, they *are* the doorways, their bodies filling the frames exactly, their limbs splayed to touch all four jambs at once. They are tall—seven, eight feet—and extravagantly thin, their bones (if they are bones) hollow as bird femurs, their skin (if it is skin) drawn tight over fluted ribcages that open and close with a motion so slow it resembles geological settling.

They are the **Silex**, or so they might be called, though they have no spoken name for themselves. They have no voices. They have no ears. They perceive the world through **pressure**, through the subsonic thrum that travels up from the soles of their broad, splayed feet into the hollows of their skulls. Their heads are elongated, concave, like the spiral of a nautilus turned inside-out, and these cranial chambers are tuned to specific frequencies of the roar. Where you hear mere noise, they hear **syntax**—a grammar of gale and grit that tells them the temperature of the desert above, the mineral content of the day's dust, the approach of any footfall that disrupts the standing wave patterns they have spent centuries perfecting.

They are eyeless. The harsh white-gold light is not for them; it is for their gardens. They farm the crystalline succulents, the copper-towers, not with water—that fundamental absence defines them—but with **vibration**. They tend the plants by adjusting the acoustic geometry around them. A Silex will stand motionless for weeks beside a stunted copper-frond, subtly shifting its weight, redirecting the wind through the carved spirals of a nearby wall until the resonant frequency changes, until the vibration hitting the plant shifts from a dissonant scream to a nurturing chord. The succulents respond by singing louder, growing faster, extending their glassy fingers toward the light-well in gratitude. The Silex harvest the harmonics, absorbing the tonal overtones through their porous skin, metabolizing sound into the energy that keeps their crystalline muscles tense.

The "pale, eyeless insects" you saw scurrying in the cracks are their **synapses**—extensions of their distributed nervous system. When an insect flashes across a salt-block, the Silex know, three terraces down, that a shadow has passed. When the parchment-winged moth flutters near the ceiling vent, they feel the perturbation in the airflow like a twitch in their own eye.

But mostly, they stand in the **nodes**.

In the central plaza, where the roar collapses into a pocket of near-silence, they gather in a circle, facing inward, their hollow bodies creating a secondary chamber within the spherical void. Here, the sound does not cease—it accumulates. They are **absorbers**. The roar enters their open mouths, travels down their esophagi (lined with fine, vibrating cilia like the strings of a harpsichord), and is trapped in their bellies, compressed, refined. They are eating the wind. They are drinking the scream. When they have absorbed enough—when their abdomens glow faintly with stored acoustic pressure—they retreat to the dry cisterns, descend into the circular pits, and **excrete architecture**.

From their bodies, they secrete a paste of gypsum and copper salts, a cement that hardens instantly upon contact with the warm air. They build to improve the acoustics. They fill fissures that leak sound. They extend the spiral grooves that whistle in minor keys, refining the city's ability to channel the eternal gale. They are not the city's original builders—that honor belongs to ancestors who first broke through the salt-crust and heard the potential in the howl—but they are its immune system, its maintenance crew, its evolution.

They are not solitary. In the deepest cistern, at the nadir of the sphere, there is a pile of them, a tangle of elongated limbs and concave heads, woven together like dried reeds. This is their **chorus**, their repository of memory. When two Silex meet in a narrow alley, where the pressure quadruples, they do not gesture. They press their hollow chests together, creating a sealed chamber between them, and they **hum**—not with vocal cords, but by adjusting the tension of their crystalline ribs, modulating the wind that passes through them. They exchange days of information in seconds: the growth rate of the north-wall lichen, the taste of this afternoon's dust, the subtle shift in the vent's aperture that suggests the desert above is flooding with new sand.

They are warm. Their metabolism runs hot, powered by the friction of sound against their internal membranes. If you touch one—and you might, if you mistake it for a statue—you will find it feverish, thrumming with the captured roar, vibrating slightly against your palm like a plucked string held long after the note has faded.

They have no fear of you. You are temporary, water-bloated, noisy in your soft, fleshy way. You disturb the standing waves with your heartbeat, your blinking, your clumsy gravity. They will wait. When you leave, or when you collapse (for the heat and the sound will take you, eventually), they will absorb the calcium of your bones into the city, voting with their secretions on whether your ribs would make a good baffle for the western terrace's new whistle.

They are old. They are patient. They are the listeners at the bottom of the shell, waiting for the ocean to finally say something worth hearing.
