They call themselves **the Attendants**, though they do not speak the word often enough for it to echo. They are twenty, no more, no less—a quorum set by the acoustics of the dome. Any more, and their heartbeats would muddy the Song; any fewer, and there would be no one left to hear the stone’s dreaming.

You will not see them at first. They have learned the color of wet basalt, the texture of moss in shadow. Their skin is the grey of condensation on warm stone, thick and faintly translucent, allowing the perpetual damp to seep through rather than bead upon them. They are tall, attenuated—stretched by the thin air at the lip of the world—yet they move with the deliberation of mineral growth. Their feet are broad and prehensile, toes splayed to grip the emerald velvet of the floor without crushing it, soles secreting a resin that prevents their slipping on the weeping walls where they climb to listen.

For they are listeners first. Their ears—if ears they are, wide cups of cartilage and sensitive filament—have evolved to cradle the 2.3 resonance of the Hollow. They can hear a fissure opening in the floor three days before it splits, interpreting the subsonic torque of the crystal lattices. They hear the water ascending the capillary channels, a sound like silk being drawn through a needle’s eye, and they move to greet it, channeling the upward flow into new ceramic vessels fired in the ge vents. These vessels are their only true technology, hard and resonant, shaped to ping like bells when the condensation strikes them, adding percussion to the Song.

They live in the circular cells carved into the warm walls, but they do not sleep as we do. They enter **resonance**—a state where their bodies slump against the basalt, skin flushing dark as they drain their body heat into the still-cooling stone, slowing their hearts to match the mountain’s geologic pulse. In these hours, they are indistinguishable from the rock itself; you might mistake them for odd protrusions, calcified pilgrims fossilized mid-gesture.

Their society is organized around the **Center**—the shifting pool that breathes. The oldest, or perhaps the most hollowed-out by listening, live in the innermost ring of terraces, close enough to the water to mark its thermal rise and fall with their own submerged bodies. They are the **Auscultants**, deciphering the mountain’s intentions from the pool’s tides. When the water rises, they know new rooms are pushing outward in the depths; they take up their obsidian wedges not to carve, but to clear, prying away moss that would choke the emerging staircases, levering fallen fern fronds from cracks that the stone needs to expand into.

The great amphitheater at the north end is their cathedral, though they never fill it. It seats five hundred, but the twenty Attendants sit scattered in its tiers, never side by side, each positioned at a nodal point where the wall-ticking converges into harmony. Here, they perform the **Maintenance of Earthing**—a ritual of absence. They sit in the empty seats and listen to the wind tend the space, ensuring the acoustics remain pure for the day when the mountain finally exhales. They believe they are preparing a nursery not for their own young, but for the mountain’s offspring: the architecture itself, which is slowly extruding teeth and halls and lungs from the basalt, will one day wake and need a place to gather.

They eat little. The blind crabs and parchment moths are sparingly harvested, thanked in subsonic vibrations that make the steam shiver. Mostly, they drink—the condensation that runs in silver threads down the walls, the weeping curtains that defy gravity. They are become half-stone themselves, their bones dense with minerals leached from the water, their blood slow as sap.

They do not name each other with sounds. They have positions in the concentric rings—the **First Terrace**, the **Seventh**, the **Keeper of the North Weep**, the **Warden of the Warm Wall**. When one dies, their body is not burned nor buried, but placed in the path of an emerging fissure, allowed to be crushed and incorporated into the new stone, so that their mass may feed the growth. A new Attendant is chosen from the lower altitudes, brought up to replace the silence, taught to walk the three hundred steps from wall to wall without leaving a footprint in the moss, taught to hear the loud patience of the dome.

To watch them is to watch a prayer in slow motion. A figure stands in a clearing of wet stone, head tilted, listening to a single droplet form on the ceiling above. The droplet swells, pregnant with sky-light. It falls. The Attendant’s mouth opens, not to catch it, but to shape the note it strikes upon the floor—a bell-tone, pure and brief. They hold that note in their chest until the next droplet falls, hours later, beating out the rhythm of the Hollow’s breath.

They are the midwives of lithic birth, the lubricators of geological emergence. They tend the lung that has not yet decided to exhale, keeping the passages clear, the water flowing, the Song uninterrupted. They believe that one day, the stone will finish cooling, the ticking will stop, and the Hollow will sigh—and on that day, the five hundred seats will fill not with people, but with the mountain’s own voice, amplified by the ceramics they have so carefully fired, ringing out the first word of a new world.

Until then, they wait, twenty echoes in a room built for a god’s first cry.
