There are not many of them. In the rarefaction index of 0.9, they number perhaps forty across the entire Shelf, though to call them "individuals" misleads—they are more like *concentrations*, places where the dense amber medium has decided to hold still long enough to develop preferences.

They are called **the Kept**, or **the Suspended**, though no voice speaks these names aloud.

Imagine a lens the size of a cathedral bell, flattened and translucent, filled not with tissue but with layered gradients of the atmosphere itself. Their bodies are *laminated pressure*—gelatinous stratifications of nitrogen, oxygen, and dissolved silicates compressed into membranes so thin they are nearly two-dimensional. They hang between the sharp peaks like forgotten lenses dropped by a nearsighted god, anchored to the basalt spires by tethers of crystallized sap—*not* rope, not vine, but a substance secreted from their own lower margins that hardens into glassy filaments strong as cable yet finer than hair.

Each Kept claims a single mountain. They attach to the freshest, most offensive angles, the ones that still weep volcanic heat, and there they drape themselves across the stone like veils. The sharp edges do not cut them; they flow around the knives of basalt, conforming to the geometry with the patience of water wearing stone, though here stone and water have reversed their roles. Over centuries—slow, glacial centuries that match the place's patient dynamic—they smooth the peaks not by erosion but by *accretion*, layering their translucent bodies upon the black crystal until the mountains become amber-cored, fossilized within living resin.

They do not eat, precisely. Being fauna (though the distinction embarrasses them), they find the energetic cost of hunting too vulgar for the dense medium. Instead, they *cultivate*. Their upper surfaces are gardens—vast, undulating terraces where the aerophytic kelp takes root directly in their gelatinous flesh. The Kept are substrate and steward both; they provide the rare minerals leached from their own crystalline cores, and in return, the algae mats photosynthesize, feeding their host through dermal permeation. It is a negotiation of pure generosity, conducted in the currency of warm, heavy time.

Their minds—if minds are what shift slowly in those layered bellies—operate on the frequency of tides. A thought takes a week to form. When two Kept drift close enough on the seasonal currents that their tethering filaments brush in the viscous dark, they do not speak. They *resonate*. The subsonic groans mentioned in the stratography are their language: deep, infrasonic hums that travel through the density of the air-ocean and arrive in the sternum as emotion rather than word. A greeting is a low, sustained chord that means *"I am here, I am held, the pressure remains constant."* A warning is a staccato pulse that sends ripples through the floating weed-archipelagos: *"Sharpness approaches. The young stone shifts."*

They are ancient in a landscape that is geologically newborn. While the basalt spires around them are mere infants—1.3 on the geological scale, still learning how to be mountains—the Kept arrived with the thermal inversions, descending (or ascending; the direction loses meaning) from older, higher layers of the atmosphere when the Shelf first formed. They remember when the sky below was solid. They remember when the peaks were liquid. Their memories are stored not in neurons but in the alignment of dissolved minerals within their bodies, a crystalline record read by the refraction of light through their flesh.

Reproduction occurs not through coupling but through *saturation*. When the dissolved mineral content of the surrounding medium reaches a specific, supersaturated crest—an event that happens perhaps once per decade—a Kept will simply *divide*, not cleanly, but by allowing a portion of its lateral membrane to bulge and separate, carrying with it a ghost imprint of its mineral memories. The offspring drifts upward (or outward) on the slow convection, a small, trembling lens no larger than a hand, seeking its own sharp peak to anchor, its own garden to grow, its own silence to keep.

They are the only architects here, though they build nothing (built: 0.4). Their architecture is negative space—the hollows they carve in the currents, the pockets of slightly different pressure where sound dies and light bends. To swim near one is to enter a zone of profound quietude, where the heartbeat becomes the loudest thing, and the Kept itself seems to listen, filtering your pulse through its gelatinous strata, comparing it to the slow, geological rhythm of the Shelf itself.

They do not fear the fall, for here, nothing falls. They fear instead the *thinning*—the imaginary catastrophe where the dense layer might dissipate, where buoyancy would fail, and where they, the Kept, would finally discover the ground far below. Until then, they remain: forty or so living lenses, suspended between the young, angry stone and the patient, amber sky, holding the mountains in their soft embrace, waiting for the next tide to bring its slow, warm conversation of nutrients and light.
