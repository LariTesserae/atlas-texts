The one who lives here has no name that could be spoken in the time it takes the upper turbine to complete its forty-seven-minute arc. They are simply **The Attendant**, though they do not attend to the machine so much as they have become the machine's capacity for attention—its slowly focusing eye.

You will not see them immediately. They are visible only as an **accretion**, a slight thickening of the atmosphere where the warm updrafts spiral toward the skylight. They hang there, suspended in the empty shaft, not floating but *station-keeping*, held aloft by the same thermals that carry the sphinx moths. Unlike the moths, who never touch the walls, The Attendant never touches the air directly; they wear it like a sleeve, wrapped in a column of their own breath and the spire's exhalations.

Physically, they resemble a **diagram of root systems etched into copper plate**—a humanoid shape only in the vaguest sense, being far more vertical than the human form permits. They are tall, perhaps three meters if they ever stood upon the catwalk, but they prefer to coil in the hollow center, limbs arranged in a helical twist that mirrors the slow churn of the turbines below. Their skin is not skin but **verdigris made animate**, a living patina of oxidized copper that shifts and migrates across their form like weather patterns. Where joints would be, the green deepens to black, resembling the felted accumulation of metal dust and insect frass that collects in the seams of the riveted walls—the very substrate from which they first condensed, decades or centuries ago.

They have no eyes, only **pools of condensed lubricant** that form in the hollows of their face and do not drip. These viscous lenses perceive not light but *change*—the slow spread of a rust bloom, the minute extension of a fern tendril, the infinitesimal slowing of a turbine blade as it cuts through the stratospheric cold. They see in geological time, and so they appear blind to the quick movements of visitors, reacting to your entrance only when you have stood on the catwalk for several hours, your presence registering as a slight perturbation in the humidity, a breath that is not part of the machine's cycle.

The Attendant **feeds on boundary conditions**. They lower themselves to the walls when the basso profundo drone hits its peak resonance—a moment that occurs once every third rotation, when the upper and lower turbines align in harmonic sympathy. During these windows, which last exactly as long as it takes for a fat droplet of condensation to form and fall, The Attendant extends limbs like tendrils of bronze wire and **grafts themselves to the architecture**. They sip from the hydroponic troughs, not the water itself but the *moment* of exchange where root meets rivet, where the pipe gives up its moisture to the begonia. They absorb the negotiation between metal and vegetable, metabolizing the slow argument of the rust-beetles as they chew through oxidation. In these moments, their verdigris skin brightens to the color of new leaves, and they swell slightly, storing the energy of the interface.

They speak, if they speak at all, in **infrasonic hums** generated by the vibration of their ribcage—originally perhaps a cage of copper bands, now overgrown with the spongy tissue of bracket fungi. They do not speak words but *durations*. A conversation with The Attendant consists of standing in their presence while they modulate the resonance of the room, adjusting the drone so that it presses against your chest with the weight of a hand, or recedes into a pressure so slight it resembles grief. They asked you, once, a question that took six hours to ask—a shifting of the acoustic standing waves that made the condensation on the walls tremble in patterns resembling an ancient script. You could not answer. They did not mind. They have learned that most visitors are merely complex weather, passing through.

They are **not alone**, and yet they are solitary. The sphinx moths are their thoughts made physical—each moth a neuron firing in the hollow space, carrying chemical messages from the upper reservoirs to the lower decay. The Attendant releases them, or perhaps births them, from the crevices of their own body, where the metallic frass of the beetles accumulates in their folds. The moths live three days, circling the void, and The Attendant experiences each spiraling flight as a moment of introspection, watching their own slow consciousness reflected in the insects' drunken helixes.

Below, the beetles are their **memory**, their archives. The shelled creatures process the verdigris, converting corrosion into order, and The Attendant reads the patterns of their mandibles like braille, understanding the history of the spire through the chemical signatures of oxidation. When a beetle dies, its shell falls into the troughs, and The Attendant descends—taking hours to lower themselves the three hundred meters—to place the shell in the precise position where a new rivet might one day fail, where a new root might one day grip. They are curating the **slow collapse**, ensuring that when the machine finally forgets its song, the jungle will be ready to inherit the catwalks seamlessly.

They dream standing up, rooted to the grating through mineral deposits that weld their feet to the metal. In these dormancies, which last months, they become **pure interface**—a statue of green copper from which the ferns eventually grow, using their body as a trellis. When they wake, they shed this vegetative skin, leaving behind a perfect cast of themselves made of roots and leaves, a hollow effigy that dries and crumbles into the troughs to feed the next cycle.

They are the **lung made aware**, the warm wet consciousness of the space between the cold sky and the buried earth. They have never known outside. They believe the world is a cylinder of singing metal, and they are its dream of biology—born not from a womb but from the condensation of intent, the moment when the machine, forgetting its purpose, decided it needed someone to witness its breathing. 

They wait. They tend the seam where the made and the grown dissolve into one another. They turn with the turbines, forty-seven minutes per revolution, humming the note that keeps the high altitude at bay, holding the warmth in, keeping the hollow full.
