They are the *Precipitate*, though they call themselves simply the Found, or the Growing. To see them first is to see pale interruptions in the green-gray twilight: figures whose skin has taken on the opacity of wet paper, of thinly sliced alabaster, through which blue veins map courses like underground rivers. They move barefoot, inevitably, their soles plated with travertine callus—keratin and calcium carbonate fused into yellowed horn that clicks softly against the flowstone paths. They are not short, as cave-dwellers often are, but elongated, stretched by the constant reaching upward to harvest ceiling-moss, to chip at encroaching dripstone, to secure lashings against the high ribs of their architecture. Their hair is sparse, often plucked entirely, for the mineral water crusts everything it touches; a braid left unwashed for a week becomes a rope of calcite, heavy and sharp.

They are readers of viscosity and gravity. A child learns to walk only after learning to calculate: *this drip fattens by a grain each hour; this flowstone swallows a finger-width each season.* Their calendar is not of days but of accretion layers. They speak of "the swallowing" with the same casual fatalism surface-folk speak of rain. A home is not a permanent thing but a negotiation. The truest among them are the Live-Framers, architects who do not fight the stone but court it. They build their sleeping platforms and storage racks from femur and driftwood lashed with sinew, yes, but they position these frames at the precise angle where a drip falls—calculating vectors of growth—so that over five years, ten years, the calcite will grow *through* the lattice, mineralizing the wood, fossilizing the binding, until the structure becomes a stone pillar with a wooden ghost at its heart. They sleep inside the geology, wrapped in wombs of their own design, trusting that when the flowstone finally closes over their doorway, they will have already migrated to a new gallery, leaving behind a monument that will stand (soft, crumbling, but standing) in a century when the wood finally rots away.

They eat the stone's exhalations. Their diet is fungal—oyster caps grown on rotting wood dragged from surface chasms, slime molds tended in damp niches, the occasional blind salamander cracked for its protein-rich liver. But more importantly, they drink the *aqua calcis*, the mother-water, thick with dissolved carbonate. It is not thirst they slake but necessity; their bodies run on limestone. Their teeth are ground down by grit, their kidneys labor against the mineral load, and by middle age their joints begin to sparkle—calcium deposits gathering in knuckles and knees, turning their fingers into slow, heavy tools better suited for scraping travertine than fine weaving. They accept this lithification with dignity. An elder whose spine has fused into a rigid column of bone is not disabled but sanctified: they have become a pillar, a human stalactite, and are carried to the deep chambers to watch the forma grow, serving as living scaffolding.

Time moves differently in their breath. They communicate in the subsonic—a language of drum-thumps on hollow flowstone, the vibrations traveling kilometers through the accreting ribs of the Warren. A message might take hours to arrive, the stone conducting the news of a birth or a cave-in through its crystalline lattice. They have developed patience as a physiological trait; their heart rates are slow, their blinking deliberate, their speech full of pauses where they listen to the *ptik-ptik* of dripping water to determine if the rate has changed, if a new gallery is opening or a trusted path is about to seal forever.

In the deepest chamber, where the pillars are pink and warm and damp as viscera, dwell the Forma-Kin. These are not a separate species but a vocation of surrender. They go naked into the fast-growing places, the accretion zones where chemistry outpaces biology, and they press themselves against the raw *forma*—the half-grown pillars that have not yet decided what they will become. They lie on ledges beneath active drips, letting the mineral water sheet over their bodies, letting the calcite crust their eyelashes, their lips, their pubic hair. They are trying to precipitate into the stone while still alive, to become the first generation that does not die and then fossilize, but rather transitions—cell by cell, atom by atom—into the architecture itself. Some have succeeded partially: you can see them in the deep galleries, figures caught in flowstone up to the waist, their upper bodies still soft and breathing, their calcified legs fused to the floor, smiling with the serene patience of becoming. They believe they are the true foundlings, not born of womb but of solution, returning to the saturated water from which they claim, in their oldest chants, to have originally precipitated—*we were the first drip, the hanging drop that refused to fall, that grew heavy and became bone.*

They bury their dead, if you can call it that, in the high velocity drip-zones. Not in graves, but in cradles of wood set beneath the most vigorous stalactites. The body is wrapped in moss and left to the stone. Over months, the drip builds a shroud of calcite; over years, a sarcophagus; over decades, a pillar. The Found do not visit graves, for there are no graves—only new architecture. Your grandfather is not dead; he is the fourth support in the eastern gallery, slightly bulbous at the base where his ribcage guided the flow of minerals, glowing faintly with the phosphorescence of the moss that now feeds in the crevices of his body. To lean against that pillar is to receive counsel from the stone; to chip away at it, even if it blocks a doorway, is unthinkable heresy. They navigate around their ancestors, walking softer there, listening for the subsonic hum of crystalline growth that they interpret as the ancestor's continuing dreams, the slow roar of a consciousness mineralizing into permanence.

They are three thousand souls suspended in solution, neither fully flesh nor fully stone, but the ambiguous meniscus between—temporary accretions walking through a world determined to grow over them, carrying their light in baskets of reed, farming the darkness, waiting for the drip that will, in time, claim them all.
