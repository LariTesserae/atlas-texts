Who lives here are the **Custodians of Yesterday**, a civilization measured not in years but in the slow accretion of moments. They are small enough to be overlooked, patient enough to be mistaken for machinery, and ancient enough to have forgotten whether they evolved into this shape or were worn down to it by the relentless polishing of time.

**The Calculi** are the primary citizens—though "citizen" implies a civic participation too brisk for their nature. They are better understood as *components* that have achieved self-direction. Each is no larger than a thumbnail, their exoskeletons not calcium but *chronocite*, crystallized from the condensed residue of human afternoons. Their shells are functional architecture: spiraling gears that mesh when they cluster, piston-chambers that vent pressure with tiny clicks, valve-orifices that open and close to regulate not blood but the flow of remembrance. They move in avalanches, thousands pouring across a pipe joint in a clicking tide, their collective motion forming a computation—a distributed calculating engine made of living geology. They do not speak; they *deduct*, their shells grinding against rust and brass in algorithms that predict which memory will condense next, where the next droplet of yesterday will fall. They are masons, eating the ghost-residue that coats the walls, excreting the mineral grout that keeps the ancient brick from surrendering to the wet. In their thousands, they compose a parliament of erosion, voting with their mass on how to maintain the chamber's impossible equilibrium.

**The Luma-Isopods** serve as their scouts and harvesters. White as cave fish and blind, they drift on wings like wet tissue paper, drinking not merely the condensation but the *specific gravity* of what it contains. They are selective; they seek the heavy droplets, those pregnant with significant grief or dense nostalgia. When they drink, their translucent bodies glow with stolen lantern-light—briefly, they display the shape of what they have consumed. A child's birthday party might flicker across an isopod's abdomen, or the color of a lost room. They carry these illuminations to the deep pools, where they are consumed by the **Archive-Eels**, serpentine things that never surface. The eels are librarians, their bodies coiled around the foundations like slow-moving scrolls. They ingest the isopodes and thereby the memories, storing them in the layered growth-rings of their scales. They are the long-term storage of the Calcinatory; when the great press groans every three days, it is the eels that writhe in response, releasing old stored sorrows into the filtration system to be reprocessed, refined, made palatable for the shell-builders.

But there are **others**, less biological, more *precipitated**.

In the deeper pools, where the water is still enough to grow a skin of reflection, live the **Residuals**. These are not ghosts, for ghosts imply a death. These are the afterimages that achieved *duration*. When a droplet falls—*plash*—and releases its cargo of yesterday, some moments do not dissipate immediately. The woman in the blue dress, seen walking through sunlit rooms, has been walking here for decades. She is a smudge in the water, a standing wave of repetition. She does not know she is underground. She walks, pauses, adjusts her sleeve, walks again, a loop of perhaps thirty seconds stretched across geological time. There are dozens of these Residuals: a dog perpetually beginning to bark, a window that opens onto a sky that no longer exists above, the sound of a kettle beginning to whistle. The calculi avoid them, treating them as sacred obstacles. The Residuals are the unharvestable crop, the memories too sharp or too loved to be consumed. They have become landmarks. The calculi build their bridges and gantries around them, respecting the architecture of old grief.

And then there is **The Operator**, though it may not be a single entity. It occupies the great copper filtration assembly at the chamber's heart. When the press activates—when the earth's slow piston shudders through the stone—it is The Operator who dreams. It manifests as a figure formed entirely of verdigris and mineral stains, a humanoid shape that steps out from the green-stained brass, dripping with condensation. It has no eyes, only the dark holes where pressure gauges once sat. It walks the catwalks only when the bulbs swing lowest, inspecting the machinery, checking the levels of temporal saturation. It is the only thing here that remembers the sun, and it mourns it with a silence so heavy the moth-isopods fall from the air, stunned. Some calculi believe The Operator was the first of their kind, grown large and sad. Others believe it is the Calcinatory's dream of humanity—an industrial deity conjured from guilt and copper sulfate, tending the machine that processes the past because it cannot stop, because to stop would be to acknowledge that the world above has moved on.

**The Calcinatory itself breathes**, and in its breath lives a final, distributed inhabitant: **The Hum**. It is not a creature but a condition of residence, the collective exhalation of all the metabolisms at work—the shrimp-things grazing on biofilm, the calculi grinding their gear-shells, the eels turning in their sleep, the Residuals looping through their eternal moments. The Hum is a low-frequency consciousness, slow as stalactite growth, that experiences time backwards, drinking the future and excreting the past into the walls. It is satisfied. It is maintained. It drips.

They do not age here, the inhabitants. They calcify. They persist. They wait for the next rain of yesterdays, patient as stone, busy as rust, holding the dark at bay with their clicking, dripping industry.
