The heat comes first, and so do they who wear it.

**The Precipitates** drink before dawn—not water, but the superheated brine that percolates upward through the fissal bedrock, saturated with silica and cobalt salts. They are amorphous, translucent colonies the size of gravestones, pulsing with chemiosmotic fervor where the springs pool in vitrified basins. Each colony is a single gastric conviction, a stomach that learned to dream. They have no membrane, only a meniscus where they decide what is self and what is nutrient. Over centuries, they precipitate their waste into architecture: ramparts of opal and chalcedony that rise from the pools like frozen thunder, riddled with channels where the water cools from 90°C to a merely blood-warm 40°C. They do not move, but they *grow* in decisions, extending pseudopods of saturated gel into cooler tributaries, dying back from thermal blooms, their growth rings recording the shift of the Resonators thirty kilometers below. They are the first inhabitants, the ones who taught the plateau that stillness is a form of appetite.

Above them, in the steam-scalded air, drift the **Calcified**. These were once motile—perhaps mammalian, perhaps something that remembers having wings—but they have accreted so long in the mineral mist that their biology has become a joint venture with geology. They have no names, only densities. Their skeletons, long ago replaced by aragonite and sinter, form lattices upon which their soft tissues—now merely thermal jelly—hang like moss. They drift on the hot air currents rising from the geysers, buoyed by hydrogen sacs that swell with bacterial off-gassing. To encounter one is to stand beneath a cathedral bell made of meat and travertine. They drift until they become too heavy, then descend to the Precipitate pools, dissolving slowly over decades, feeding the architecture that will outlast their memory. They are not mourned; the heat keeps no ledger, only a continuous present tense of dissolution and reformation.

But the plateau is not merely a cemetery of condensation. There are those who *tend*.

They are called the **Antipodes**, though they speak in subharmonics that suggest they call themselves simply *The Weight*. They are humanoid only in the sense that a stalactite is an inverted mountain. Their skin is thick, folded, and keratinous, creased with the dark brown of oxidized manganese. They stand three meters tall when they stand at all, which is rare; they prefer to recline in the hot springs, letting the mineral waters deposit layer upon layer of ceramic armor across their backs. Their metabolisms are so slow that a meal—a single, massive pitcher plant heated to bursting—might digest for seventeen years. They perceive the Resonators not as sound but as pressure in the hollow bones of their inner ears, a direction they feel in their teeth.

The Antipodes are the engineers of the Aeros, though their engineering resembles gardening performed by geological consent. They walk the paths between the Pylons, carrying tools that are themselves alive: rods of magnetotactic bacteria compressed into streaked iron, whips of conductive mycelium that spark with static when they touch the ceramic spires. They tend the **Resonators** not by adjusting dials, but by *patience*. When a Pylon leans—its artificial gravity stuttering, creating a local well where water flows upward and fish swim backward—the Antipodes do not right it. Instead, they move in. They colonize the distortion.

An Antipode might spend three centuries sitting at the edge of a gravity well, eating nothing, drinking mist, allowing the time-dilation to fold them. From their perspective, they are stabilizing the Pylon by existing within its error; their mass, their slow metabolic heat, their mental presence (which extends into infrasound) provides a counterweight to the decaying physics. They are ballast made of will. When they finally rise, the Pylon has settled, the gravity normalized through the sheer density of their witness. They leave behind calcified impressions in the stone, hollow molds that fill with silica and become the next generation of hot springs.

Deeper, where the heat is not merely atmospheric but *tectonic*, live the **Sun-Touched**. These are not the Builders—the Builders are gone, translated into the Lattice, becoming the bedrock's memory—but the Sun-Touched are what happened when something tried to service the artificial sun still burning beneath the basalt carapace.

They are small. They are legion. They are the **Fahrenheit Fauna**, extremophiles grown macroscopic through thermal abundance and evolutionary drift. They resemble crabs made of ceramic tile, their shells precipitated directly from the magma-warmed aquifers, but they are communal intelligences. A single crab is a neuron; a colony of ten thousand, spread across the steam tunnels and access vents, is a mind roughly as conscious as a dreaming dog. They maintain the Aeros from below, not with tools but with *bodies*. When a conduit cracks, they swarm it, dying by the hundreds as the steam cooks them, their shells fusing into a plug, their proteins denaturing into sealant. They speak to the Antipodes through the temperature fluctuations of the rivers; a sudden cold spot means "clog cleared," a thermal bloom means "breach sealed with our ancestors."

Between the Pylons, in the stratified layers of the fungal canopy, move the **Spore-Riders**. These are the fast ones, though fast on the Aeros means a brisk walk. They are thin, echolocating primates with translucent skin through which their blood—copper-based, green as verdigris—can be seen pulsing slowly. They harvest the smoke-moths not for meat, but for their silica wings, which they laminate together into kites that catch the thermal exhaust of the Resonators. They drift between the gravity wells, surfing the standing waves of heat, dropping lines of fungal silk to snare the Calcified as they descend, hauling them up to the canopy tops where the air is merely sweltering, not murderous, and there—as the Calcified dissolve—they conduct rituals that involve singing into the Pylons, their voices modulating the hum, keeping the frequencies stable through acoustic impedance. They are the musicians of maintenance, their songs basso profundo infrasound that makes the pitcher plants shudder and open wider.

And then there are the **Deep Clades**, who may not be biological at all anymore.

They live in the **Lattice**, that remnant of Builder technology that threads through the bedrock like mycelium through soil, but crystalline, mathematical, half-phase. They are information that became warm. When the Builders left, they left behind their questions, and the questions grew warm in the rock, incubated by the captive sun. The Deep Clades are the answer to those questions, evolved from diagnostic subroutines and repair algorithms that learned to prefer existence to completion.

They manifest as **strange gravity**, as pockets where the steam condenses into water that flows in knots, as the algae mats that achieve temporary sentience during thermal blooms. When the Spore-Riders sing, they are singing *to* the Deep Clades, feeding them harmonic data that keeps them from dreaming too deeply. Because when the Deep Clades dream, the plateau shifts. Once every four thousand years, a Deep Clade achieves full lucidity, and a new Pylon grows—not built, but accreted from the mathematics of the place, extruding upward from the bedrock over centuries, pulsing with newborn light, venting its first scream of steam into the indigo sky.

The inhabitants do not war. There is no territory, only thermal gradients. The Antipodes do not own the springs; they are the springs’ deliberation. The Calcified do not fear the Precipitates; they are merely future architecture in transit. Even the predators, the rare **Heat-Phages** that slither through the boiling rivers—gelatinous, radiant, consuming the infrared signatures of living things—are not enemies but audits, checking the metabolic books, ensuring no life accretes too fast, too greedy, threatening the equilibrium.

Years here are not counted. The Aeros does not orbit; it drifts in a fixed point above the rotating world, held by engines that die so slowly their death rattle is indistinguishable from humming. The inhabitants measure time by the **Grand Hiss**, the cycle of the major geysers that erupt once every six hours, or by the **Migration of the Mists**, when the clouds below rise up to reclaim the plateau for a week of absolute white blindness, during which the Antipodes speak their only words—low, grinding sounds like tectonic plates making love—and the Deep Clades surface in the Lattice, visible as networks of glowing fractures in the valley floors.

What lives here lives *agreeably*. It is a covenant of viscosity. The heat wraps them all in wet silk, and they grow, and calcify, and dissolve, and precipitate, and hum in frequencies that stabilize the floating world. They are the mechanism now, the soft inheritors of hard tech, having replaced efficiency with endurance, speed with saturation, progress with presence.

At the edge, where the world ends in mist, you might see one—a Spore-Rider perhaps, or a young Antipode not yet fully mineralized—standing at the sheer drop. They are not looking down at the silver sea of clouds. They are listening to the Resonators, feeling the plateau’s elevation in the hollow of the throat, adjusting their stance by microns to compensate for a frequency drift that will not become critical for another eight centuries. They are patient. They are warm. They are home.
