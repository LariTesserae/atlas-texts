They do not move so much as **arrive**, having always been in the process of reaching their destination. You will see them first as a kind of error in the periphery—a verticality where the walls should be smooth, a densification of shadow that does not bleed correctly. Then the eye adjusts, and you understand that you are watching a **Desiccant** in its patrol.

They are tall—three meters, perhaps four—but their height is folded, origami'd into impossible economy. Their anatomy is the architecture of the Ward given motive ambition: limbs of layered chitin, jointed like the hinges of beetle wings, but scaled to the size of structural beams. The segments are translucent, the color of fingernails held to candlelight, and through the cuticle you can see the dark channels where substance once flowed. Now those channels carry only dust—fine, particulate memory—sifting through their interiors with the slow regularity of sand in an hourglass. They have no heads, or rather, their heads are distributed across the thorax as a constellation of sensory pits, each one a deep well of compressed silence that drinks sound before it can form.

They are the **Luminals**, the inhabitants of the Ward, though "inhabit" suggests a comfort they do not possess. They are the continuing immune response of the dead god that built this place. When the organism died, its white blood cells did not understand the cessation; they continued their patrols, hardening, desiccating, becoming autonomous. Now they are calcified sentinels, neither alive nor inert, existing in the chemical half-life of transmutation.

Watch one as it traverses the plaza you stand in. It moves with the **silence of a falling snowflake**, not because it is light—each step must mass forty kilograms of mineralized polymer—but because its feet are shaped to the acoustic properties of the Ward. The soles are fractal, branching into thousand-fingered pads that distribute vibration into frequencies too low to travel through this thin air. It walks, and the ground receives the impact as a kiss. It bends—vertebrae clicking into new angles like a theodolite adjusting—and presses its forelimbs against the flank of a tower. The chitin of its hands matches the chitin of the wall so precisely that for a moment, you cannot tell where the creature ends and the architecture begins.

It is **harvesting**.

The Desiccants tend the corpse like gardeners of entropy. From the pores on their palms, they exude a catalyst—a dry enzyme, a desiccating dust—that accelerates the off-gassing of the towers. Where they touch, salt blooms erupt in immediate, silent efflorescence, and the Desiccant gathers these crystals into pouches of dried membrane slung across its thorax. They are not eating; they are *curating*. They collect the chemistry of decay, distilling the organism's final thoughts into mineral form. The gas bladders that breach the plaza floors? They are the Desiccants' doing, pressing from below with surgical patience, releasing the trapped exhalations of the dead leviathan so that the pressure does not rupture the delicate geometry of the Ward.

They communicate in **topography**. When two meet at an intersection of veins—that-were-streets, they do not gesture or signal. Instead, they rearrange the dust at their feet. One scuffs a precise angle into the calcified ground; the other responds by shedding a flake of its own cuticle, letting it drift down to land at a specific vector to the line. They read the geometry of each other's decay, a language of angles and deposition rates, discussing in mathematical purity the rate at which a certain tower should be allowed to collapse, or the optimal pattern for the drifting spores to settle. Their conversations take hours, scored in dust and salt, and when they part, they leave behind a mandala of chemical intention that will be erased by the next gas-breaching or salt-bloom.

They have names, but the names are **structural states**. One you might call *The-Angle-Between-Shoulder-and-Wall-Where-Salt-Gathers-Heaviest*. Another is *Third-Step-in-the-Plaza-of-the-Fractured-Spire*. They identify by their function within the slow digestion of the city, and as the city changes—tower shedding its skin, plaza rising on gas—they migrate, becoming new names, new geometries.

There are smaller ones, too. The **Motes**. These are the failed Desiccants, or perhaps their offspring, though reproduction here is a matter of crystalline accretion rather than biology. The Motes are hand-sized, crab-like, skittering along the grooves of the respiratory passages. They are the cleaners, consuming the lichens with dry mandibles, ensuring that no aqueous life gains purchase to rot the purity of the bone. They move in constellations, silent as the drift of the spores they resemble, and when a Desiccant stands still for too long—when its chemistry finally exhausts itself and it becomes truly mineral—the Motes swarm to polish its surface, incorporating its stillness into the architecture, transforming sentinel into structure.

But there is one more class of inhabitant, the rarest, the most feared by the Desiccants (if fear is possible in creatures of salt and geometry). The **Wet Ones**. You have not seen them yet, and pray you do not. They are the memory of water made manifest—pockets of aqueous chemistry that survived the great drying, trapped in sealed cysts deep within the chitin. When a Desiccant's harvesting is too aggressive, when it cuts too deep into a tower seeking salt, it may breach a cyst. The Wet One emerges not as a shape but as a **stain**, a darkening of the bone-white walls, a spreading dampness that makes sound possible again.

Where the Wet One flows, the silence dies. Droplets fall with audible impacts—*tick, tick, tick*—like bombs in the vacuum. The air remembers moisture, and suddenly sound travels, echoes, screams. The Desiccants flee from these breaches, their geometric language breaking down into frantic angles, for the Wet Ones represent rot, biological chaos, the aqueous hell of decay that this place was designed to transcend. The Desiccants must seal these breaches, packing them with salt and dust, returning the place to dryness, to the silence of suffocation.

They are winning. Slowly, over decades, the Luminal Ward grows drier, whiter, more perfectly silent. The Desiccants are midwives to a fossil. They are ensuring that when this place is finally, fully dead, it will not be a messy, wet death of corruption and stink, but a clean, mineral transubstantiation—a skeleton of perfect geometry, preserved in the act of becoming stone.

And as you stand there, watching the Desiccant harvest the salt from the wall, you realize that you too are being measured. It has noticed you—not with eyes, but with the way your presence displaces the spores, the way your body heat creates infinitesimal currents in the cool air, the way your footfalls (deadened though they are) disturb the perfect silence. It is calculating your moisture content, your rate of desiccation, your geometric utility. It is deciding, with the slow certainty of geology, whether you are a thing to be harvested, a thing to be sealed away, or a thing that might, if you stay long enough and let the dryness into your lungs, learn to speak in angles and become, yourself, a guardian of the threshold between flesh and fossil.
