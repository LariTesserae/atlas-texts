At first, you might mistake them for geography—long, low ridges of accumulated chitin and compacted substrate that follow the fissure lines of the caldera floor. They are the color of wet rust, of peat compressed into lignitic hardness, of the iron-red laterite that bleeds through when a watercourse shifts its allegiance. Call them the Stokers, though they do not tend the fire so much as they *are* the fire’s housing. But they are not passive. The "tech" (1.6) is theirs. 

Within their segmented hulls, they orchestrate the refinery. They inhale the leaf-litter carpet—not dead, they know, but transitional—through mouths that are more like intake valves, grinding the material not with teeth but with the slow, millimetric compression of gizzard-plates coated in industrial-grade enzymes. The sound (1.1) they make is subsonic, a subliminal hum felt in the thorax of the amphibians that rest against their flanks: the vibration of tons of biomass being reduced, atom by atom, in the wet metabolic heat.

They communicate with the billions of smaller detritivores—cockroaches, pale millipedes, nematode masses—not through signal or dance, but through chemistry. Consume this excess.* The termitariums, those chimney-like cooling towers, are not separate structures but symbiotic outgrowths, domesticated fungal colonies that the Stokers have cultivated since the caldera first subsided to elevation 0.7. They have no wings; flight is a fantasy of empty spaces, and here there are none (density 2.7). Their legs—hundreds of pairs, each the diameter of a human torso—do not stride but *flow*, moving with the dynamic (1.2) of geological drift, shifting position perhaps once in a human lifetime to better align with a new fissure of geothermal heat, or to pursue the slow creep of the water table. When they move, it is an industry: the displacement of matter, the pressing through the green smoke of moss curtains, the crushing compaction of roots that will, in centuries, reroute around their new position and braid them in again.

They are ancient. The smallest among them remembers the last weathering of the caldera's edge, the slow slumping that established the current basin form. They do not die so much as sediment, their final segments gradually surrendering to the humus, becoming the factory floor themselves, their chitin armor fossilizing into the spongy matrix of the ground, adding their mass to the perpetual night shift of digestion.

And in the absolute dark, in the heat that comes from below, they dream—not in images, for there is no light to seed imagination, but in chemical potentials. They dream of the perfect reduction, the total efficiency where all biomass is converted, where the wet heat is captured forever in the endless fermentation, where the bowl is full and the process is complete. They are the inhabitants, the operators, the weathered and established proprietors of this place, and they have all the time that stone dissolving in acid rain can offer.
