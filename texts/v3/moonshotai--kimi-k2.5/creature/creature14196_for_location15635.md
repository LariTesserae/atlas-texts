You do not meet them so much as you recognize the texture of their labor in the grain. They are the residue of the billion hands, but not the hands themselves; those have long since fused into the architecture, their fingerprints calcified into whorled knots that serve as handholds on the bridge-vines. These are the inheritors—the ones who remained when the shaping was finished, or perhaps were grown afterward, emergent properties of a world that had been pruned into self-awareness.

They are vertical creatures, theoretically, though they navigate the sideways gravity wells with the ease of fish swimming upstream. Their skeletons are not calcium but lignin—dense, amber-colored heartwood that clicks softly when they shift position, a sound that harmonizes with the subsonic settling of the structure. They possess too many joints: knees that bend backward and sideways, elbows that rotate through full circumferences, allowing them to brace themselves against the reality-loopholes and the temporal stutters. When time freezes a bird mid-flight in the bronze twilight, they are the blur that moves around it, tending to the suspended creature with stone-tipped fingers, checking its beak for parasites, adjusting its wings so that when motion resumes the flight path will not intersect with a void.

They have no mouths. The absolute silence bred this feature into them, or perhaps they cultivated the silence first, understanding that sound was a corruption in a space where water had to crawl upward against its nature. Communication moves through the sap-tides. They press their palms—three-fingered, obsidian-nailed—against the fossilized walls, and their thoughts enter the hydraulic network, traveling with the nutrient pulse to emerge weeks later in another chamber as a memory-dream in a listener's mind. Their language is vegetal, asynchronous, and heavy with the weight of cellulose memory.

They are tall, ranging eight to ten feet when standing in the standard down, but they prefer to fold themselves, to flow like the boneless ink-predators through the hollows, moving in the blur-state while the rest of the world hangs frozen. Their skin is the color of old parchment, photosynthetic, freckled with bioluminescent symbionts that pulse in rhythm with the upward rains. They wear no clothing—what would be the purpose?—but they carry the tools of their ancestors: adzes of knapped obsidian strapped to their wrists with braided cambium, petrified thorns piercing their ear-lobes (marked by no sound), shell scoops hanging from belts of woven xylem. They use these not to build—the era of building is over—but to *tend*.

And tending here is a mathematics of absence. They walk the vast empty corridors, their bare feet polishing the wood where countless generations have walked before, adding infinitesimal layers to the smoothness. They find the places where the angles converge wrong, where the parallel lines meet, and they carve with their flint tools—not to destroy the paradox, but to smooth it, to teach the wood to dream in Euclidian curves again. They harvest the condensation that refuses to fall, collecting it in gourds grown specifically for the purpose, carrying the upward-crawling water to the higher galleries to pour into the root-webs of the distant ceiling, maintaining the circulation.

They eat the silence. They consume the frozen moments, grazing on temporal stasis, metabolizing the weirdness. When they must take solid food, they scrape the bioluminescent lichens from the structural ribs, or they catch the glass-winged insectoids with nets of spider-silk and wood-fiber, crushing them into pastes that they smear on their chests, absorbing the nutrients through their skin.

There are not many of them. Or perhaps there are thousands, but they exist in different temporal densities. Some you see as statues, standing motionless in the great empty chambers, bark growing slowly over their skin, serving as living cairns, markers in the void. They have stood there for centuries, their wood-bones fusing with the architecture, becoming part of the garden-pruned-cosmos. Others move too fast to see, maintaining the pressure differentials, tacking the cloud-banks in the lower voids so they don't drift into the structural members, herding the weather like shepherds.

They remember the builders, but incorrectly—the way the place itself is remembered to be entered. They remember being the builders, and in a sense they are: each generation presses their hands into the wounds of the wood, leaving prints in the hardening sap, calcifying slowly into the structure, so that in ten thousand years their hands will be the handholds, their bones the beams.

They fear the sound. Somewhere, deep in the lower galleries where the gravity pools in corners and debris collects on walls, something is making a noise. A vibration that is not the settling creak, not the sap-tide pulse. A true sound. They are drifting downward, folding through the reality-loopholes, obsidian tools gleaming in the bronze dimness, patient, silent, legion. They go to prune the noise, to smooth it into the wood, to return the sphere to its absolute, perfected silence. They are the maintenance of the impossible. They are the hands that remain, still building, still tending, long after the building was finished.
