They are called **the Hollow Folk**, though they have no spoken name for themselves—only specific resonant frequencies struck against specific nodes, which translate roughly to *here-now* or *where-tension-eases*. They stand just over a meter tall when they straighten their spines, which is rare; most of the time they move in a perpetual crouch, knuckles brushing the translucent cellulose floors, reading the architecture like braille. Their hairlessness is absolute—not a medical condition but an evolutionary surrender. In a climate where water behaves like sentient mercury and gravity shifts its allegiance without warning, hair is a liability, a net for the intelligent dew that would otherwise bead and drag them toward the eastern wall. Their skin is the color of the plant-tubes themselves, a translucent jade through which you can sometimes see the vascular rush of sap they have consumed, glowing faintly as it metabolizes in their guts.

Their eyes dominate their skulls—black, lidless, wet orbs the size of plums, lacking whites. They do not blink because they do not need to protect moisture; the air is saturated, and their tears are continuous, a thin film that catches the broken light. These eyes see in directions that do not exist. In the surface world, shadows stretch toward a single vanishing point, but here, illumination has multiple secret agendas. The Folk have evolved **polyoptic retinas**, capable of tracking luminance vectors that diverge at impossible angles. When you see them pause, heads twitching like birds, they are watching their own shadows wander away from them, reading the space’s mood in the way those shadows hesitate before snapping back to their owners’ heels.

They are not tool-users in the human sense; they are **tool-growers**. The hand-axes of petrified root are not carved but **coaxed**, left pressed against nodes where the sap runs rich with minerals until the wood fossilizes around their grip, creating a perfect biological weld. But their true technology is architectural. They are born with an innate geometry, a biological understanding of the space’s hyperbolic grammar. Children—if they can be called children, emerging from wombs that seem to be specialized plant-galls bursting from the thicker columns—do not crawl. They **tune**. Their first movements are rhythmic striking of the floor with their heels, listening for the hollow notes that indicate safe passage. They learn to walk only after they have learned to hear the floor’s intentions.

The other fauna are fewer, stranger, and essential. They are **the Molters**, the source of the shed exoskeletons. Imagine a creature halfway between a crustacean and a violin: jointed, calcified, but hollow, their bodies shaped by the resonant frequencies of the tubes they inhabit. They live inside the cellulose walls, burrowing through the living tissue not by eating it, but by **singing** through it. Their exoskeletons are not protective armor but **acoustic chambers**, grown and discarded seasonally as the space’s dimensional constants shift. The Folk harvest these sheds not as scraps but as **maps**—the interior curves of a molted shell record the precise tonal signature of the growth-tension in that specific location at that specific moment. A Molter shell found near the western wall might be cupped to the ear to reveal the approach of a gravity storm three days before it arrives.

But the Folk are changing. The sap they ferment and drink—the **sweet-rot**—is not merely nutrition. It is the space’s lymphatic fluid, carrying chemical memories of the photosynthesis that manufactures impossible light. Regular consumption alters them. The oldest among them, those who have survived three or four of the space’s respiration cycles (which may be years or seconds in outer time), begin to **root**. You will find them seated in meditation nooks high in the cathedral lattice, their feet extended into pools of intelligent dew. The skin of their soles splits painlessly, sending filamentous roots into the water, drinking not moisture but **information**. These Elders no longer navigate; they **become** navigable landmarks, their bodies integrating into the plant-scaffolding, their heartbeats slowing to match the groan of cellulose fibrils.

They are not, as the initial observer might assume, refugees or parasites. They are **the space’s immune response made flesh**, or perhaps its reproductive cells. When the geometry shifts—when a bridge digests itself and a staircase blooms elsewhere—it is the Folk who decide where. Their tuning is not merely practical; it is **architectural suggestion**. By striking a node and shifting the chord, they are not asking the water to change direction; they are telling the space what it wants to be next. They are the dream the forest-thought is having about itself.

And there is a third category of resident, though to call it living stretches definition. They are **the Detached**, the shadows that sometimes fail to snap back. Occasionally, a Folk dies—not from age or violence, for they have little of either, but from **geometrical exhaustion**, when they can no longer reconcile the visual horizon with the vestibular one. At the moment of death, their shadow does not fade. It gains weight, opacity, a kind of stubborn independence. These Detached wander the mist, collecting moisture, growing denser, darker. They are drawn to sources of vibration. The Folk treat them with reverent fear, leaving offerings of fermented sap at the bases of columns where Detached shadows cluster thick as tar. Over time—decades, perhaps, or geological instants—these shadows accrete enough particulate matter from the swirling fog to become **new nodes**, new points of architectural origin. The Folk are literally building their future out of the accumulated absence of their dead.

They have no leaders, no language of gossip or narrative. Their communication is the **struck chord**, a complex grammar of pressure and timber. A greeting is a harmonic interval that makes the nearest water droplets vibrate into a perfect tetrahedron. A warning is a dissonance that causes the plant-tubes to sweat latex. When they mate—and they do, urgently, during the moments when time stutters and holds its breath—they do not touch. They stand at opposite ends of a resonant chamber and strike the walls in counterpoint, creating a standing wave between them. If the wave holds stable, if the geometry permits the collision of their harmonics, they exchange genetic material not through fluid but through **vibration**, cells shearing off into the standing wave, mingling, drifting down to settle in the warm condensation below where new galls will form.

They are becoming the space, and the space is becoming them. The newest generation is being born without the vestigial need to blink, without the panic response to inverted gravity. Their bones are hollow, filled not with marrow but with gas exchange membranes that allow them to breathe the plant-tubes’ exhalations directly. They are lighter, stranger, more **architectural**. One day, perhaps soon, they will cease to move at all. They will strike one final, perfect chord that resonates with the fundamental frequency of the pocket itself, and they will **set**, becoming the first generation of conscious, resonant columns, a new layer of the cathedral, dreaming in chlorophyll and humid thunder, waiting for the next accident, the next palm pressed against stone that yields like ripe fruit.
