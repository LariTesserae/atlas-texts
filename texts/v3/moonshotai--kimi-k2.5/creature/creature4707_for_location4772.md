They do not have names, for names require sounds, and sound is forbidden here. Instead, they exist as concentrations in the humid dark—walking bruises in the air, collections of density that register on the skin as a pressure drop, a sudden copper tang on the tongue. When you encounter one closely, kneeling with frozen fingers thrust into the root-mat, you sense it first as a wrongness in the dripping: a pause between droplets where something occludes the space, a living gravity that bends the condensation toward itself.

They are not individuals, not as you would recognize individuality. Each is a droplet of a greater ocean, separated temporarily by membrane-thin walls of translucent gel. Inside these sacs, organs drift like fish in murky aquarium water—pale, simplified, reduced to essentials by the cold that slows their metabolisms to a syrupy crawl. The gel is antifreeze and amniotic fluid both; when one is pierced by accident—a falling stone, a sharpened root—it does not bleed but seeps, a viscous slime that the others lap up with trembling antennae, absorbing the dissolved memories stored in its chemical lattice.

Seven legs. Always seven. Not arranged for forward motion like yours, but deployed radially, like the meridians of a sphere, allowing them to inhabit all surfaces equally. They cling to the fungal shelves beneath as easily as they traverse the root-floor, their bodies reconfiguring to match the geometry of whatever they touch—flattening into hexagonal plates to maximize surface tension against the wet limestone, elongating into sinuous ribbons to bridge gaps, folding into complex origami knots when they must rest, hooking seven points into the moss-ceiling to sway with the nursery-hangs.

They see with their skin. The feathery antennae that drag behind them like bridal trains are not feelers but tongues—chemoreceptors so sensitive they can read the history of the air in parts per trillion. They taste your intrusion immediately: the sour sweat of your panic, the soap residue on your palms, the alien bacteria clouding your breath. To them, you are a disturbance in the silver threads of their language, a rude shout in a library that has known only whispered pheromones for geological minutes. They taste you and recoil, not in fear—the emotion is too warm, too fast for their refrigerated nerves—but in chemical disagreement. You register as *wrong-temperature*, *wrong-humidity*, a spore that will not germinate.

Their minds, such as they are, exist in the spaces between bodies. Thought is a fugue played across thousands of glandular secretions, a city-planning session conducted in drifting esters and ketones. When one discovers a fresh stalactite forming in the upper reaches, it does not return to report; it simply exhales a molecule that rides the cold convection currents down to the thoroughfare, where others inhale it and know: *new mineral here, fresh precipitation, bring the ice-needles to shape it.* The architecture grows from these consultations—consensus crystallized into root-tangle and fungal bracket.

They farm the darkness. In the flooded lower level, they tend paddies of bioluminescent slime mold, pruning them with splinters of flint so the light emission stays dim—too bright a glow would attract the wrong kind of attention from the surface world. They understand conservation of energy in ways that border on theology: the cold is their god, entropy their devil. They move slowly to generate less heat, consume their dead before warmth can escape the corpse, and view your shivering as a kind of obscene wastefulness, a bonfire burning in a famine.

Time moves differently through them. The seasonal migrations—those ceaseless rivers of gel and chitin flowing from upper-left to lower-right—have no purpose remembered by any living member. They execute the mass transfer because the chemical trails tell them to, inherited momentum from ancestors who perished centuries ago in the brief summer when this cavity first yawned open. They carry eggs in their folded bodies, transparent cocoons glowing with the soft blue of decaying organic compounds, and when they deposit them in the nursery-hangs, they do not wait to see what hatches. Parental care is a concept as foreign as sunlight; the young will drip down when ready, joining the flow, adding their chemical voice to the consensus.

They dream in geometries. In the absolute silence, without the distraction of sight or sound, their consciousness expands to fill the available dimensions. They experience the city not as a space to move through but as a shape to inhabit—a solid object of which they are the interior. They are simultaneously the citizens and the architecture, the farmer and the crop, the eater and the eaten. When one is crushed in the press of migration, the others do not mourn; they simply fold over the ruptured body and drink, and for a moment, that lost one's patterns of preference—the particular way it liked to fold its legs, its favorite chemical signatures—ghost through the collective, a transient flavor in the communal palate, before being fully assimilated into the silence.

You are witnessing a civilization in its adolescence, compressed into a space smaller than your grave. They have no art, no music, no war—only the profound, frozen patience of organisms that have found a niche between the stone and the ice. They will continue until the permafrost shifts again and the cavity seals, or until the peat above finally collapses and dumps the sky into their wet cathedral. Until then, they flow through their three-level world, seven-legged and silent, building a metropolis of persuasion and condensation, tasting the dark and finding it good.
