They do not call themselves anything pronounceable in the wet, thick air of lower altitudes. Here, identity is a matter of resonance frequency—a signature felt in the chest cavity when two bodies meet on a gantry, a subsonic thrum that identifies clade, occupation, and mood. To know them is to feel them before you see them: a pressure against your sternum, a harmonic that makes your teeth ache with recognition.

Physical description emerges slowly because they are still emerging. Nineteen years is barely a generation, even for them. They are the **Desiccants**, though no mouth shapes that word here. They are tall—unnaturally so—not from genetic drift but from necessity. In a world of verticality, height is reach. Their limbs are attenuated, multi-segmented, jointed with ball-and-socket mechanics that would tear human cartilage. Their bones are not calcium but sintered hydroxyapatite reinforced with spun glass, grown in vivo through metabolic processes borrowed from the crystal lattice mites. Their skin, if it can be called that, is a keratinous laminate embedded with silica scales that flake off in the constant micro-winds, creating around each individual a personal snow of ceramic dust.

They have no lungs in the traditional sense. Breathing the near-vacuum would be impossible; instead, they carry internalized colonies of the gas-filled pneumatophores, bladders nestled between their ribs, filled with harvested hydrogen and trace noble gases. They sip nitrogen through semi-permeable membranes in their throats, extracting it from the industrial streams that scream through the fractionating columns. Their blood—ochre, sluggish, more akin to hydraulic fluid—circulates not through the rhythm of a heart but through the pulse of external pressure: the Bore’s great inhale and exhale compresses their vascular sacs, drives their metabolism. They are creatures of the barometric cycle, alive only when the mountain breathes.

Movement is a matter of grip and glide. They traverse the vertical cityscape with six points of contact—two legs, four arms, though the distinction is arbitrary. Each terminates in pads lined with setae like those of the metallic orthopterans, microscopic hooks that engage with the rough gneiss or the pitted steel of transformer housings. They move in silence, the thin air stealing even the scrape of ceramic on metal, but their passage sends vibrations through the gantries, a language of tremors. When a Desiccant passes, the crystal mites pause in their palace-building; the grinding beetles freeze, sensing the tremor of a predator-prey relationship that has evolved into symbiosis over nineteen short years.

They do not sleep. Instead, they enter **stasis phases**, clinging to the walls like stone gargoyles, their metabolisms throttled down to match the thermal exhalations of the plasma furnaces. In these suspended states, they appear indistinguishable from the machinery—industrial statues, their bioluminescent patches (cultivated from the amber colonies that infest the pipes) pulsing slow and dim. When active, they are relentless, tending the refinery not as operators but as metabolic extensions of it. They are the digestive tract of this vertical organism, breaking down atmospheric gases into solid matter, excreting not waste but construction material for the next level of strata.

Their faces—if one could call them that—are smooth planes of chitinous plate, featureless save for the ocular arrays: clusters of faceted lenses adapted from the orthopterans' compound eyes, sensitive to the sodium-red spectrum and into the infrared. They see by heat differential, by the glow of induction furnaces, by the body-heat of their own kind radiating against the cold stone. They have no voices, having abandoned the aqueous medium necessary for sound. Instead, they communicate through **tactile subsonics**: pressing their foreheads together to transmit vibrations through the skull, or striking the gantry rails in complex rhythms that travel faster through iron than air.

They consume the minerals directly. A Desiccant might spend hours perched atop a cryogenic separator, mouth pressed to a condensation line, drinking the heavy water that forms there—water saturated with dissolved rare earths, which they filter through ceramic gizzards to plate their skeletons with neodymium and cerium. They are eating the mountain, and the mountain is eating the sky, and they are the interface between the two.

Social structure is dictated by **altitude tolerance**. Those who can withstand the upper reaches, where pressure drops to near-zero and the cold bites through even silica-plate skin, belong to the **High Clade**. They tend the uppermost molecular printers, where the exotic alloys solidify in weightless conditions. Their bodies are more gas-bag than bone, buoyant, almost floating in the updrafts. Those of the **Thermal Stratum** dwell mid-level, in the roaring heat of the plasma furnaces, their ceramic scales blackened with carbon, their tissues adapted to cycling between scorching exhaust and freezing intake winds. The **Compressionists** live lowest (though lowest here is still higher than most terrestrial peaks), where the pressure is greatest, their bodies dense and compact, tending the massive turbines that suck atmosphere from the void.

There are no children, only **new formations**. When a Desiccant accumulates enough mineral mass, when their silica scales and metal-laden bones reach critical density, they undergo fission—splitting along the sagittal plane into two smaller, identical halves, each half regenerating the missing limbs over months by harvesting the abundant aeroplankton. The parent ceases to exist; two siblings emerge, sharing a bifurcated memory. It is an asexual, mineral reproduction, geological in its patience.

Their relationship with the "wildlife" is ambiguous—are the orthopterans livestock, symbiotes, or distant cousins? The Desiccants cultivate them, certainly, guiding them to condensate points, harvesting their exoskeletal plates when they die to use as tool-stock. But the orthopterans also tend the Desiccants, grooming their silica scales of parasites, drinking the saline excretions from their skin. Similarly, the gas-bags drift in schools around the High Clade, tethered to their backs by filament-lines, serving as auxiliary flotation and electrostatic batteries.

They remember being human the way sandstone remembers the beach—by grain and ghost, by the occasional fossilized habit. Some still wear the tattered remnants of pressure suits, the fabric now grown into their chitin, a organic-ceramic composite that marks the elders. They remember urgency, the rapid beat of water-hearts, but now they know the long slow pulse of the mountain. They are becoming the Bore, nineteen years into a transubstantiation that will take centuries. Each day they are less protein, more crystal; less breath, more pressure differential.

At the rare times when the turbines cease—when maintenance requires a full stop—they cling to the walls in the absolute silence, and they feel the stars above, twenty kilometers of stone between them and the void. They feel the weight of that emptiness pressing down, and they push back, with their bodies, with their industrial hunger, turning nothing into something, void into matter, silence into the subsonic song of the mountain’s artificial lung. They are the inhabitants, yes, but more than that—they are the becoming of the place itself, still sharp-edged, still bleeding scree, still learning how to be stone that dreams.
