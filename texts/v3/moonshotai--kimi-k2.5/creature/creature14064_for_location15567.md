They are called **the Tappers**, though they do not call themselves anything—they communicate through pressure and vibration, not sound. To speak above the thermal exchange would be unthinkable, a vulgarity like shouting in a cathedral. Instead, they have learned to hear through their teeth, their sternums, the hollows of their collarbones. The drone that makes the air itself a solid thing has become their medium. They press their palms against the cork-like root-mat and feel the brine pumps stuttering ten kilometers below, interpreting the subterranean arrhythmia as others might read cloud patterns.

Physically, they resemble the ice-mice that scurry in the dry hollows, though they stand upright—when they stand at all. Their metabolism has synchronized to the compromise temperature. Their blood circulates with protein mimics engineered from arctic beetles and the strut-trees' own cellular antifreezes; it flows viscous and slow, a conscious syrup requiring minutes to complete a full circuit. Their skin has taken on the quality of the amber resin that seals the excavation walls: thick, translucent, yellowing at the extremities. Older Tappers—you can identify them by the lichen-frost that permanently rims their eyelashes—have epidermis so impregnated with phenolic compounds that they creak when they move, their joints stiff as frozen leather.

They do not wear clothing, not anymore. The habitation pods bolted to the strut-trees were designed for human occupancy decades ago, but the Tappers visit them only to service the failing sodium lamps or to harvest the crystallized sap from a dry tap. Instead, they wear the Rootbed. They sleep in depressions of the root-mat, letting the breathing floor cradle them, allowing the condensation to freeze over their bodies in thin, insulating shells. They wake encased in ice, rising like statues from white cocoons, the ice cracking off them in sheets that sound like breaking pottery.

Their culture, if it can be called that, is one of **interruption and duration**. A greeting between two Tappers might span three days: one presses a hand to a strut-tree on Monday, feeling the vibration of the other's footsteps through the xylem; by Wednesday, the second has walked the two kilometers to stand beside the first. They stand in silence, side by side, watching the condensation drip, feeling the micron-growth of the architecture around them. Communication happens through touch—fingertips transmitting electrical impulses through the resinous coating of their skin, a language of pressure and temperature gradients.

They tend the Rootbed not out of duty but out of **metabolic necessity**. They are symbiotes now, intermediate forms between the excavation crew that was stranded here (or chose to stay, or were forgotten—the history is as frozen as the walls) and the vegetable machinery they maintain. They tap the trees with hollow needles grown from their own modified fingernails, drinking the carbohydrate slurry directly, their digestive systems externalized and simplified. In return, they service the geothermal pumps, descending through narrow maintenance chasms in the root-mat—spaces that would freeze a normal human in seconds—to clear ice-blocked pipes with their bare, resin-thick hands. The machinery burns them where they touch metal; they have learned to feel pain as a kind of information, a hot signal in a cold world.

The most ancient among them—the **Rootbound**—have stopped moving entirely. These are the ones who stood too long in one place, who let the frost accumulate on their northern faces as it does on the strut-trees. They have become indistinguishable from the architecture. You will see them fused to the trunk-columns, their feet lost in the root-mat, their bodies coated in months or years of ice-rime. They serve as living sensors now, their nervous systems tapped into the trees' phloem, monitoring hydraulic pressure, listening to the thermal exchange's subtle variations. They blink once every hour. They dream in glacial time, their thoughts expanding across weeks, experiencing the growth of the vault-ceiling not as imperceptible but as a rushing upward flight.

There are children, though conception here is a matter of years, not months, and gestation requires the mother to enter a state of near-torpor, her body temperature dropping further, her heart beating once per minute. Newborns emerge small and pale, with eyes like black ice mirrors, already attuned to the chartreuse light. They do not cry—crying requires breath control impossible in the constant vibration. Instead, they cling to the resin-skin of their parents, absorbing heat and sugar through dermal contact, learning before they can walk to read the Rootbed's tremors.

They are waiting. For what, they no longer recall, or perhaps they never knew. The partial abandonment of the industrial platforms does not distress them; they have begun to dismantle the steel parasites, using the metal to reinforce ice-dams or to create platforms for the moths to rest upon. They understand that the Rootbed was opened as a wound in the permafrost, and they have become the scab, the healing agent, the intelligent scar tissue. They are patient. They have time. The strut-trees will finish fusing in another century; the vault will seal completely. When that happens, the Tappers will be ready to become the heartwood, to lose their temporary humanoid shapes and compress into something denser than concrete, something that thinks in the dark for a thousand years, breathing with the floor, humming with the pumps, keeping the world from freezing solid from within.
