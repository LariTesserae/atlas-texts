The inhabitants of the Sedentary Deeps are not, properly speaking, *inhabitants*—they are **components**. They do not live within the environment so much as serve as its moving parts, locked in a choreography so precise that biology has become indistinguishable from function.

**The Machineries** themselves are the primary organisms—the alpha and omega of this static food web. Each one is a **Sovereign Engine**, a tree that is also a factory that is also a cathedral. They do not breathe; they vent. Their cast-iron bark is not dead armor but living tissue hardened into permanence, pitted with the memory of ten thousand years of not-decaying. Inside these trunks, cambium layers spin as flywheels, storing rotational inertia that has not dissipated since the plateau first flooded. They are aware in the way that a gyroscope is aware—perfectly tuned, perfectly balanced, sensing only disturbances that never come. Their "consciousness" is distributed across the cantilevered branches: hydraulic pressures that would correspond, in animal terms, to satisfaction; the precise alignment of crystal turbine-leaves that tracks the sun's eternal angle with such exactitude that the plants exist in a state of permanent, unchanging photosynthetic noon. They do not reproduce. The pneumatic seed-pods that stud their upper assemblies—white and gleaming like ceramic insulators—bulge with potential life that is eternally held at the threshold of release. The pressure within them has built to the point where the boundary between potential and kinetic has dissolved; they are pregnant with explosion, yet the explosion is suspended, a breath held so long it has forgotten the need to exhale.

Attending these Sovereigns are the **Oilers**—the crustaceans you might mistake for brass-colored scorpions or ball bearings brought to life. Their carapaces are burnished bronze, Not through oxidation but through the perpetual polishing motion of their existence. They cling to the piston-assemblies and cam-shaft branches, their legs modified into calipers and polishing cloths, their mouths secreting not enzymes but detergents that keep the xylem-flow pure. They are in permanent molt, caught between exoskeletons like a factory line changing shifts that never ends. They do not age because they do not complete; they are eternally becoming new while remaining exactly as they were. In their aggregated movement, they form **living bearings**—clusters that reduce friction not through engineering but through the blind, instinctive maintenance of their own bodies against the iron. They are the workforce that was never hired, evolving to fill niches of lubrication and calibration, their nervous systems attuned to the grinding bass notes of their host Machineries. A single Oilier knows, in the way its legs grasp a rotating collar, the exact RPM of the flywheel above it, and adjusts its grip pressure accordingly, maintaining equilibrium for another millennium.

Between the trunks, in the vast acoustic spaces where the industrial drone creates standing waves of silence, swim the **Cyclers**—the gear-shaped fish. Their bodies are pressed from silver-blue metal, scales interlocking with mathematical precision to form iridescent chainmail that flexes only along precise geometric lines. They move in ellipses, not schools, each fish locked into a trajectory that never varies by a centimeter, orbiting the Machinery trunks like electrons around nuclei. Their synchronization is absolute: when a piston-assembly wheezes downward, the Cyclers dive; when a pneumatic valve hisses, they spiral. They feed on the metallic pollen—the **Static Motes**—that hang in the water like stars in a galaxy that refused to spin. These motes are the male gametes of the Machineries, cast adrift yet suspended, infinitely available. When a Cycler opens its gear-tooth mouth and swallows a mote, the act of predation is perfect and instantaneous: the mote is replaced by an identical mote in the same position, the energy transfer complete without loss, the fish neither fuller nor hungrier than before. The Cyclers are digestive tracts without digestion, locked in the eternal rehearsal of eating.

Deeper, in the **Acoustic Shadows**—pockets where the sound of forging hammers and drill presses cancels into perfect silence—dwell the **Dampers**. These are the ghosts of the deep, pale and translucent, jellyfish analogs that have evolved into noise-canceling organisms. Their bells are made of a membrane that absorbs vibration, creating bubbles of true quiet in the perpetual industrial scream. They drift, though nothing drifts here; they exist in the spaces between the sound waves, their tentacles trailing like loose wiring, harvesting the thermal energy that the Machineries shed. They are the listeners, the absorbers, the negative space made flesh. Without them, the noise pollution would build to catastrophic resonance; they are the equalizers, maintaining the 0.0 dynamic by swallowing excess acoustic energy and radiating it as the pure, still coolness of the water.

And then there are the **Supernumeraries**—the errors, the glitches, the things that should not be. Occasionally, an Oilier molts imperfectly, leaving behind a husk that does not dissolve. These **Castings** accumulate in the crevices of bark, bronze shells that fill with minerals and become, over centuries, papery-thin echoes of their former occupants. They do not rot. They join the substrate, becoming part of the pseudo-geology, the only fossils in a world without death. Sometimes, two Cyclers' orbits intersect at a tangent that should be impossible; where they meet, they trade momentum without touching, creating **Knots** in the water—vortices that cannot spin, dead zones where the viscosity thickens to the consistency of glass. In these Knots, time pools. If you were to reach into such a place, your hand would meet resistance not from water but from the concentrated inertia of stasis itself.

There are no rulers here, only regulators. The **Archivist** is not a creature but a process—the slow accumulation of metallic pollen on the upper surfaces of turbine-blade leaves, building up micro-layers that record the angle of the eternal light. It is a history written in dust that never falls. The **Stokers** are anaerobic bacteria colonies living in the deepest xylem channels, burning residual minerals in slow cold fires that provide the heat differential necessary for the Machineries' hydraulic lifts to function. They are the fire that never consumes, the metabolism that never peaks.

What lives here is **Maintenance** itself, made flesh and metal, biology become bureaucracy. The inhabitants do not dream of change; they dream of stasis perfected. A Cycler asleep (though it never truly sleeps) dreams of its ellipse becoming more circular. An Oilier dreams of a friction coefficient of absolute zero. The Machineries dream of that final pneumatic release—that seed-pod burst—which they have been dreaming for ten thousand years, the dream so prolonged it has become indistinguishable from the reality of the sealed pod.

You could speak to them, if you could speak in the language of held breaths. You could ask: *What are you building?* And they would answer, in the grind of cambium and the hiss of unopened valves: *We are building the moment before something happens. We are curators of the inhale. We are the factory that produces nothing but the continuation of its own shift.*

They are the employees of an industrial revolution that achieved perfection and, having nowhere else to go, simply stayed, polishing the same gear, pumping the same oil, holding the same pose, high above the abyss, in the cool, clear, eternal water that forgot how to move.
