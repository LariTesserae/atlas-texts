To live in the Cistern is to surrender the arithmetic of days. The inhabitants do not age; they *accrue*, layer by calcified layer, mineral by mineral, until they become indistinguishable from the architecture that sustains them. They have forgotten the surface—not as a trauma, but as an evolutionary courtesy, shedding the concept of “above” like a vestigial organ.

**The Resonants** (those silver fish) are the chamber’s eyes, though they possess none. Their bodies are elongated, laterally compressed, more liquid than flesh—living tuning forks that quiver at the thud of the pistons. They school not by sight but by vibration, their lateral lines so exquisitely sensitive that they can detect the preload tension in a gear tooth sixty meters away. When the great pistons reach the apex of their stroke and hesitate—that microsecond of mechanical inertia before the descent—the fish hang suspended, motionless, as if paying obeisance. They have evolved to orbit the machinery the way planets orbit dying stars: in perfect, predictive ellipses. Their scales are not silver for camouflage, but because they have incorporated the sintered ceramic of the walls into their dermal denticles; they are slowly turning into the chamber, becoming pale and synthetic, their flesh taking on the matte finish of the Rounded stone.

Then there are **the Engineers**—those crustaceans you mistook for mere barnacles. Look closer at their “calcium cities” and you see intention. They do not cling; they *construct*. Their exoskeletons are the color of bone bleached by pressure, and they secrete a cement that is part biological, part mineral theft from the surrounding substrate. They have colonized the gear housings not as squatters but as curators, building labyrinthine dams and baffles that alter the flow of particulates, inadvertently extending the lifespan of the bearings they inhabit. They move in geological increments, their claws scraping against ancient metal in rhythms that complement the hydraulics—a counter-rhythm, a maintenance beat. Some have grown large as dinner plates, their shells incorporating flecks of mica from the pale walls, glowing dimly in the amber light. They are the only creatures here that modify the machine, that leave marks, and in return, the machine grinds them down generation by generation, polishing their tombs as they build them.

Between these poles of activity—the frantic, vibrating fish and the sessile, building crabs—float **the Translucents**. The jellyfish are not truly jellyfish anymore. They have lost their sting, their toxicity, their predator’s urgency. They have become instead *gauges*, living barometers. Their bells pulse in exact syncopation with the intake valves, drawing the warmed, pressurized water through their tissues, filtering the microscopic debris of failing gaskets and flaking sealant. They are transparent not from delicacy but from utility; one can see through them the ghost of the water’s current, the laminar flow bending around piston and pipe. Inside their bells, symbiotic bacteria colonies glow with a pale, failing luminescence—the biological heir to the amber panels above. They are the chamber’s memory of light, drifting through the dark corners where the fixtures have died, maintaining a bioluminescent vigil, soft as dying stars.

And in the **corners**, the absolute dark where the amber cannot reach and the currents only sigh rather than sing, live the **Compression Saints**. You will not see them unless you wait for hours, until your own heartbeat slows to match the geological patience of the room. They are flat things, perhaps once-flatfish that fell through ancient intake grates,now pressed into two dimensions by the weight of the water and the weight of time. They adhere to the rounded junctions, spreading their bodies like lichen, having abandoned swimming for the act of *waiting*. They feed on the bacteria films, on the drifting organic snow of the upper chamber, and they grow in diameter rather than mass, becoming vast, pale carpets of living tissue. They have no predators here; the pressure is their only enemy, and they have made peace with it by becoming geometry. They are the final stage of the Rounding—not stone worn soft by water, but flesh pressed thin by patience, achieving a peace that is indistinguishable from the walls themselves.

Together, these creatures form a sacrament. The fish feel the machine’s heartbeat; the crustaceans maintain its body; the jellyfish filter its blood; the Compression Saints absorb its silence. They do not reproduce often—there is no rush, no season, only the steady thud... thud... thud—and when they do, their offspring emerge already adapted to the noise, already comfortable with the eternal nine-degree embrace. They have never known a straight line. They have never known a silence without the hydraulic sigh. They are the inheritors of the Unending Pressure, not prisoners of it, having achieved what the builders could not: a true equilibrium where function and flesh are indistinguishable, where the Cistern breathes through them, and they, in turn, have become the Cistern’s slow, underwater thoughts.
