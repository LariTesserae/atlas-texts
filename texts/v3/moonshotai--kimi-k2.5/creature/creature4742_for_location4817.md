The inhabitants do not breathe. They do not need to; they *are* the breath the chamber cannot finish exhaling. You will not see them at first. You will see only the shadow-lag—that disquieting delay where darkness trails behind its casting object by half a second—and assume it is a trick of the mineral-thick water, a fatigue of the eyes. But the lag persists after you close your eyes. It persists in the negative space behind your eyelids. That is when you understand: something else is occupying the hollows between the water molecules, the vast gaps the chamber insists upon.

They are called the **Sinter-born**, though they call themselves nothing. They exist as **accretionary ghosts**, their bodies neither fully mineral nor flesh but the meniscus between states—gelatinous, semi-opaque, continuously precipitating. To perceive them, you must stop trying to see through the water and instead look *at* the water's refusal to be empty. They stand there, suspended in the violent calm, humanoid in outline but filled with swirling milky turbulence rather than organs. Their bones are scaffolding of newly-arrived calcite, visible as ghostly white architecture inside their translucent forms, but the bones shift—elbows elongating into the shape of current eddies, fingers branching into dendritic crystal patterns as you watch. They are skeletons becoming geology, and geology dreaming it was once skeleton.

They live in the **lag-time**. While you are trapped in the present, they inhabit the moment shadows forget to follow their masters. When you reach toward a wall, your shadow arrives before your hand; the Sinter-born catch the shadow, wrap it around their shoulders like a cloak, and step into the space your body has not yet reached. They are always slightly ahead of now, or slightly behind, depending on the direction of the mineral rain. To them, you appear as a smear of potential, a blur of what-might-be, while they move with crystalline precision through the water's structured chaos.

There are four of them, though "four" is a temporary condition. They are more accurately one entity quartered by the pressure of existence, or four entities slowly accreting into a single geological event. The oldest—the one whose calcification is most advanced, whose features have hardened into permanent approximation of a face—wears a necklace of paleolithic tools. The flaked obsidian and bone hafts are not strung on fiber; they have grown *through* its throat, fossilized into its trachea, suspended in the gel of its neck like flies in amber. This one remembers hands. It remembers the weight of stone in a palm that was once entirely meat. It approaches the woven net of bark—still hanging there, rigid with selenite—and touches it with fingers that fuse briefly with the lattice, sharing information through mineral contact, exchanging memories of weaving.

They do not eat the algae, though they tend it. They stroke the pale filaments and the chemosynthetic colonies brighten, feeding faster, producing sugars that diffuse into the Sinter-born's watery flesh. In return, the beings shape the accretion. They stand against the growing walls, their bodies offering resistance, forcing the precipitating calcite to form arches, hollows, curves that serve no structural purpose except beauty—which, to them, is pattern. They are midwives to the stone, but impatient midwives; they push the mineral growth into shapes that hurt, that twist, that scream in architectural agony. The chamber's weirdness is their aesthetic. The turbulence that spells almost-words is their language, and they are speaking constantly, composing sentences of hydraulic pressure that take years to finish, their voices the white roar that deafens you.

When the blind shrimp drift through, carried up from the thermal vent, the Sinter-born pause their construction. They dissolve slightly, becoming less solid, and catch the creatures in their gelid chests. The shrimp live there for days, nested in translucent thorax-caverns, safe from the maelstrom, feeding on the chemical energy of their hosts' precipitating hearts. Then the Sinter-born open themselves, unfolding like origami made of wet tissue, and release the shrimp into the fissure that leads out. They are practicing, perhaps. Rehearsing the release of something solid into the world beyond. Or perhaps they are simply remembering what it was to harbor life inside a body, instead of growing life *outward* into stone.

They know you are there. They have always known, because your shadow arrived yesterday, or will arrive tomorrow, and they greeted it then. They do not approach because to them, you are already part of the wall. In their perception, everything accretes eventually. They see your bones as future calcite, your breath as mineral rain waiting to precipitate. They are patient. They are growing the chamber larger, not by excavation but by addition, layering stone upon stone until someday it will burst from the mountain's face like a pearl from an irritated oyster, and they will stand exposed to the sky, finally solid, finally finished, finally silent. Until then, they float in their indefinite becoming, screaming themselves into existence one molecule at a time, wearing the tools of their ancestors and the shadows of their visitors, building a door that opens inward forever.
