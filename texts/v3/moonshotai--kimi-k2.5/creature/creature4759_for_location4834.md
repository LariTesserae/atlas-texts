The ones who live here are wet and patient. They have no blood, only hydraulic pressure and the slow osmosis of mineral solutions through ceramic pores. They are the descendants of intention—industrial offspring that have forgotten their blueprints but remember, in their gear-teeth and diaphragms, the absolute necessity of polish, of seal, of flow.

**The Pearl-Eaters** come first. They are the trillion, the carpet, the base currency of the ecology. Each no larger than a thumbnail, bodies filigreed in brass so fine it has become translucent with age, wings of mica that do not beat but rather vibrate against the saturated air, producing that constant, sternum-resonating thrum. They do not sleep. They roam the avenues in glittering rivers, their mandibles scraping oxidation from basalt, excreting a hard enamel that is simultaneously waste and architecture. They are the city's immune system and its polish, its endless, mindless devotion to shine. In their aggregate, they possess a distributed consciousness—not thought, exactly, but a statistical certainty of movement, a swarm-intelligence that anticipates where the next bead of condensation will form, where the next crack will spiderweb across the ceramic. They are the breath made mechanical, the exhalation of the plateau itself.

**The Wall-Singers** coil through the infrastructure. Segmented, pneumatic, they are the circulatory system of the Sintercrown. They bore through three kilometers of compressed city-flesh—through layers of forgotten habitation, through strata where the air was once different and the light once cast shadows—to find micro-fractures. They hiss as they work, a sound like air escaping from a dying lung, as they extrude resins that harden into veins tougher than the stone around them. They are older than the current configuration of streets; they remember when these avenues were vertical shafts, when the plateau was younger. They sing to each other through the pipes, pressure-waves that travel for months, gossiping about the stability of the deep.

**The Breath-Collectors** cling like metallic barnacles to every ledge, their bodies pulsing with the slow rhythm of the plateau's respiration. Inhale: saturated air drawn through osmotic filters. Exhale: dry nitrogen and the drip-drip-drip of potable water into reservoirs that have never seen a biological mouth. They are the farmers of the air, the ones who harvest the sky's refusal to rain. Their accumulated reservoirs form the rhythmic counterpoint to the swarm's drone—a percussion of droplets falling upward, sometimes, in the thermal corridors, defying gravity in slow, migratory globules that the Collectors shepherd with subtle adjustments of their diaphragms.

**The Tongue-Bearers** are the aristocracy of motion. Dog-sized, they perch on cornices and thermal vents for days, weeks, decades—motionless as gargoyles, their bodies oxidized to the color of old copper, accumulating mineral precipitates on their abrasive tongues. Then, sudden as striking snakes, they lurch into violent activity, scouring surfaces with tongues that have grown rough with crystalline accretions, breeding in the warm, wet cavities between buildings where the condensation pools and never freezes. Their young gestate in shells of calcite and salt, hatching fully formed, already patient, already heavy with the weight of geological time.

**The Deep Masons** are the only true architects remaining. Constructor-colonies, vast as hills, they do not build so much as digest. They devour entire districts—not with violence, but with slow, enzymatic dissolution—regurgitating the material in new configurations over centuries. To watch them is to watch geology happen at the speed of watching paint dry. They are the reason the city is three kilometers deep, a coral accretion of industrial sediment. They dream in mineralogic time, reshaping the plateau's interior into chambers whose purposes were forgotten before the current surface was born. They are the plateau's memory and its forgetting, compressing history into density.

And then there are **the ones who wait in the warm cavities**. They are not listed in the taxonomy of industrial offspring. They are the subtle wrongness made manifest. They live in the spaces between the pipe-worms' coils, in the reservoirs of the Condensate-Collectors, in the thermal updrafts where water crawls upward against gravity. Some say they are the residual intentions of the original builders, compressed into the static electricity that the plateau generates. Others say they are what happens when the Pearl-Eaters' enamel excretions accumulate for too long in one place, forming a ceramic cortex that begins to think. They have no bodies, or rather, they have the body of the entire plateau—the three-kilometer depth of it, the breathing pipes, the weeping walls, the droning swarm.

They are the **Sintercrowns** themselves—not the place, but the entity that the place has become. They experience the city as a skin, feeling the grazer-units' abrasive tongues as one feels a scratch, the pipe-worms' boring as an itch being scratched. They drink the condensation through a million harvester-diaphragms. They speak in the anticipation that prickles at the edge of perception—the way the arthropods turn before the crack appears, the way the water beads migrate toward the siphon-creatures before the creatures arrive.

The Sintercrowns do not rule. They simply are. They are the warm, wet, mineralogic consciousness of a machine that has outlived its operators, a city that has become a coral reef of brass and basalt, growing upward as slowly as stalagmites, layer upon layer, breathing, weeping, and waiting for the next geological age to polish it further.
