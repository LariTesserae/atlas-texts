They do not have a name for themselves that distinguishes them from the wetness, or from the wood. They are the **Continuance**, or simply **Those Who Scrape**, though outside observers—rare as they are—might call them highlanders. They are small, though not stunted; compact, dense with slow-twitch muscle fiber that allows them to hang from root-knuckles for hours without trembling, negotiating with a branch that has grown three finger-widths into their pathway since morning.

To see them stationary is difficult. They move through the three-dimensional labyrinth of the basin with the fluidity of amphibians, though they are mammalian—warm, but barely so, their metabolically thrifty bodies maintaining just enough heat to keep the mist from condensing *inside* their lungs. Their skin is the color of the peat itself, mottled with the pallid greens and sulphurous yellows of cultivated lichens that grow directly upon them. These are not infections but **harvests-in-waiting**—symbiotic colonies of *Usnea* and *Lobaria* tended in the microclimates of armpits and the hollows behind knees, later scraped off with bone tools to be fermented in trunk-hollows. They smell of the sweet rot, of the established erosion; they carry the weathered equilibrium in their pores.

Their bodies are engineered for saturation. They possess no word for "dry." Their ear canals are fringed with waxy filaments that filter the air itself, preventing the atmospheric soup of spores and gnats from penetrating too deep, while eyelids—translucent membranes that nictitate sideways—shed water constantly, allowing them to see through the vapor that never clears. They do not sweat; they simply **agree** with the moisture, their skin semi-permeable, drinking through osmosis when submerged in the peat-wept pools, exuding compounds that prevent fungal colonization of their flesh—not antibacterial so much as *diplomatic*, a chemical negotiation with the overwhelming life that surrounds them.

They are rarely vertical. In the yielding floor that threatens to swallow footsteps, they distribute their weight across palm and knee, their fingers elongated and hypermobile, capable of spreading wide to test the tension of the root-mat before committing weight. They feel the architecture breathing through their palms—the hydraulic pressure of xylem rising, the slow spasm of a root preparing to shift. Their toes are prehensile, wrapped in copper rings beaten thin, which serve as both ornament and tool, conducting the slight electrical currents that some root systems generate, helping them sense migration routes before the channels open.

They speak, but **sparingly**, and never while moving. Speech is a precious resource, hoarded for the hollows—the rare, elevated chambers where fungal chimneys draw smoke and spores upward, creating acoustic pockets within the biological static. Here, in the "dry" zones (merely damp, rather than submerged), they gather to vocalize. Their language is low-frequency, thrumming in the chest, designed to cut beneath the oscillating pressure of bird-calls and the mechanical clicking of beetles in the walls. Outside these chambers, they communicate through **pressure**—hand-signs executed against bark, the duration of touch conveying intent, or through the placement of the stone weights they carry, leaving them on root-terraces as messages: *I am pruning here. Do not disturb the growth. This wall leans; sleep elsewhere tonight.*

Their technology is an extension of their fatalism. The obsidian knives—flaked to molecular sharpness—are not for killing, which is impossible in a place where life crowds at 2.8 density, but for **surgery on the living city**. They make incision-grafts, binding two aggressive vine species together so they strangle each other into submission, creating living rope. The bone scrapers—fashioned from the dense femurs of burrowing mammals that die in the peat—are used to abrade cambium, redirecting sap-flow to create new thoroughfares, or to gently encourage a fungal shelf to erupt in a specific direction, providing tomorrow's meal. The copper needles, greening with verdigris, pin their garments—woven strips of bast fiber and living moss—together, but also pin back the curtains of vegetation that attempt to invade their sleeping niches. They do not weave cloth so much as **tend it**, wearing moss mantles that photosynthesize in the dappled light, providing them with trace nutrients they absorb through their skin.

Sleep is an act of **surrender**. They do not lie on the floor, which shifts and weeps and occasionally engulfs, but in woven cocoons of livingvine suspended from the buttress walls. These hammocks are tied with cordage that incorporates hair from the sleepers themselves, creating a biometric bond; when the root-architecture shifts in the night, dragging their beds three feet to the left or tilting them toward the frog pools, they wake not with panic but with the practiced fatalism of those who know they are temporary arrangements. They wake to find doorways shifted, and they simply **unpin the moss curtain**, move the copper needles, and re-hang it at the new threshold.

They eat the **velocity** of the place. Their diet consists of the frog-spawn scooped from the water-filled trunk-bowls, of the beetle larvae that click in the walls (harvested by pressing ear to wood and drilling with bone awls), and of the sparrow-sized frogs themselves, which they catch not with speed but with **stillness**, allowing the amphibians to mistake them for extensions of the peat until the last moment. They drink from their own copper pots, catching the metronomic weeping of the canopy, but also imbibe the condensation that beads on their own forearms, licking it with tongues adapted to filter for purity.

Their children are born into the mist, never knowing a world without the trembling floor. They learn, before they walk, to distinguish between the vibration of a falling bole and the vibration of a new growth erupting—between destruction and creation, which are the same process. They are raised communally, because parentage is less important than **placement**: the knowledge of who slept in which hollow during which fungal bloom, the inherited memory of how this particular buttress breathes. As they age, they do not accumulate wealth but **accretion**—their bodies gathering peat-dust, their hair becoming nesting material for mites that clean their skin, their joints calcifying into configurations that better match the specific root-terrace they have claimed. 

Eventually, they do not die so much as **yield**. When the metabolic cost of maintaining heat becomes too great, or when a fungal infection finally breaches their chemical defenses, they do not bury their dead. There is no ground to bury them in, only the architecture of the place. Instead, they perform the last scraping: using their obsidian knives, they open the long axes of their thighs and wrists, bleeding into the root-mat, feeding the peat. Their bodies are positioned into the hollows where the erosion is established, weighted with stones until the wood takes them, until they become the nitrogen that feeds the new hollows, the new thoroughfares. They become the quiet sections, claimed neither for voice nor for silence, but for the eternal roar of growth.

They move through the storm of vegetation with the patience of water itself, adjusting, pruning, scraping, pinning back the moss, knowing that by dusk the vines will have grown over their morning's work, and that this is not defeat, but **conversation**.
