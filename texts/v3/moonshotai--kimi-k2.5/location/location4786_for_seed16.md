The place is called **The Dry Meridian**. It is not a region but a node—a single, buried ovoid no larger than a cathedral nave, compressed like a bubble in deep bedrock. The elevation is low not in the sense of a valley, but of a sinkhole in the world's foundations, a pocket pressed deep beneath the weight of continental stone. You enter through a fissure—if you find it at all—and descend into a chamber where the air pressure is thick, pressing against the eardrums with the dull insistence of depth.

There is no water here. The zero is absolute. The concept of moisture has been chemically abolished; the atmosphere is anhydrous, a desiccated breath that leathers the skin instantly. Nothing has drunk here for millennia. The stone walls are calcined beyond dryness, sucking latent humidity from anything organic that enters. It is a sea without wetness, a desert of mineral intent.

And yet, it is lush.

The vegetation—if such a word can stretch to accommodate beings that never photosynthesize—consists of **thermocrystal lithophytes**. They are silicate organisms, or perhaps mineral accretions that mimic life so perfectly the distinction becomes theological. They grow in dense, branching thickets of translucent amber and iron-rust red, forming coral-like forests that occupy the chamber's center in a riot of fractal complexity. Fern-like vanes of mica-fine filigree reach from floor to ceiling; bulbous, brain-like masses of intergrown quartz and metal salts crowd against each other; delicate, hollow spires ring softly when the air moves. They are lush in the sense of abandon, of enthusiastic occupation, though they are dry as parchment, dry as fossil bone. They have replaced water with heat; their metabolic processes are thermal, drawing energy from the gradient between the scorching floor and the merely warm air above.

The floor is hot. It radiates upward in visible shimmers, a dry bake that would raise blisters on unprotected skin within minutes. The heat seems to emanate from the basaltic substrate itself—perhaps from a distant magma vein, perhaps from residual radioactive decay in the bedrock—a steady, baking 2.8 intensity that never wavers. This heat drives the dynamics of the space. It creates a weather system contained in a bottle: air heated at the stone floor rises in lazy, perceptible columns, cools against the high, shadowed ceiling, and sinks again, creating a permanent, gentle cyclone that stirs the crystal fronds and keeps the atmosphere in constant, slow motion. Dust motes—mineral, crystalline—spiral in these currents for years without settling.

And the sound is loud. Not deafening, but pervasive, a 1.5 intensity that fills the chamber and denies silence. As the crystals absorb heat, they expand; as the convection currents cool them, they contract. Millions of microscopic joints and facets flex in unison. The result is a chorus: a deep, thrumming *om* of mineral tension, peppered with high, glassy chimes like distant wind through bamboo, though there is no wind. It is the sound of growth accelerating, of thermal breathing. Occasionally, a larger crystal fractures with a gunshot *crack* that rolls off the curved walls, a punctuation mark in the droning symphony that can last for twenty seconds before fading. The air itself seems to sing.

Woven through this mineral jungle is the urban. It is not a ruin of grandeur but of *density*. The builders—whoever they were, whatever they were—worked with primitive tools (tech 1.0): basalt hammerstones, obsidian chisels, grinding rocks. They constructed a settlement-scale infrastructure of low, dry-stacked slate walls, creating a maze of alleyways and cell-like chambers that follow an organized, city-like logic. There are paved sections where hexagonal flagstones were patiently chipped to fit, their surfaces now warped and crazed by centuries of thermal expansion. There are terraced platforms that might have been markets or threshing floors; a circular plaza defined by a ring of rough megaliths; drainage channels (bone-dry, absolute zero) that suggest they once anticipated fluid, or perhaps ritualistically banished it.

The construction is layered—generations of hands adding to the walls, raising them a few stones higher, subdividing spaces, creating infrastructure. It is weathered, established (erosion 2.1); the edges of the stone are rounded not by water but by thermal cycling, by the slow sandblasting of mineral dust stirred by the convection winds. The settlement has achieved a settled form, an equilibrium where the growth of the crystals and the decay of the masonry have reached a balanced stasis.

Despite this urban organization, the density is low (0.4). The Dry Meridian is mostly empty space. The ceiling looms high overhead in shadow beyond the reach of the dim light. The periphery of the ovoid is bare, scorched rock fading into darkness. The buildings are scattered like dice thrown casually; one can walk for minutes between structures, footsteps ringing sharp on stone, before encountering another wall. It is a city plan designed for a population that never arrived, or that evaporated long ago, leaving only the architecture and the gardens.

Light is dim (0.6). No sun reaches here. The illumination comes from the crystals themselves—phosphorescence triggered by the heat, or perhaps a bioluminescence embedded in their silicon structure. They glow with a sullen, amber-orange light, like banked embers, casting long, dancing shadows of the stone walls against the bare rock. The light pools in the low places, filters through the translucent crystal leaves, creates a perpetual sunset ambiance without ever changing, without dawn or dusk.

There is no fauna. Nothing crawls. Nothing flies. No insects click in corners, no bats stir in the ceiling. The ecology is botanical-mineral, a closed loop of crystalline growth and thermal decay. The "plants" compete for heat-gradients, casting thermal shadows to cool their rivals, engaging in slow-motion warfare of entanglement and overgrowth. The only consumers are the crystals themselves, digesting the stone, precipitating their bodies from the air and the substrate, dying and crumbling into dust that becomes new stone, feeding new growth. It is an ecosystem in motion, predator and prey replaced by thermal gradient and mineral succession, active and hungry despite the absence of blood.

The weirdness is subtle (0.6), a quiet wrongness rather than overt surrealism. The geometry of the urban layout follows no human logic—the alleys turn at angles that suggest a different shoulder width, a different gait, perhaps a serpentine or radial symmetry. The crystals grow in patterns that almost spell words, almost form topographic maps of places that don't exist. The heat emanates selectively, warmer in some alleys than others for no discernible reason, creating microclimates where certain species of crystal dominate and others retreat. The space feels observed, though no eyes exist here. It is a sealed, self-sufficient world, a model city built in a kiln, singing to itself in the dark, growing and shifting, lush and empty, hot and dim and loud with the voice of stones.
