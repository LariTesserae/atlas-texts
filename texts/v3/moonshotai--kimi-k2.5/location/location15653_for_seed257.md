**The Ovoid**

You find it high in the range, buried within the heart of a mountain that itself has worn down to a soft, rounded hump over the aeons. There are no openings. To enter, one would have to pass through stone that has long since flowed back into itself, sealing the entrance with the patient geology of deep time. Inside—though *inside* is the only direction that matters, for the exterior has become theoretical—is a chamber roughly the size of a cathedral nave, though spherical rather than vaulted: a perfect ovoid, empty of air, filled instead with warm water that has not touched the sky in three million years.

The construction is total. Every surface is made. The walls are limestone, soft and pale, quarried with tools of harder stone and soft, green-copper chisels by hands that never knew iron. You can still read the method in the striations—patient, diagonal strokes of a stone adze, repeated ten thousand times to hollow a single block. The blocks were fitted without mortar, held by gravity and obsessive precision, each joint measured to the width of a hair. But time has been at work here with its own crude tools. Two million years of warm, mineral water have eroded those hairline seams into rounded gutters, into soft, flowing channels. The walls have become seamless, satin, touchable only as a single continuous surface that feels like teeth worn smooth by age.

The water occupies the space completely—submerged, absolute. It is warm, near blood temperature, held steady by the thermal mass of surrounding stone and perhaps by faint chemical fires deep below, reactions of sulfur and salt that trickle up through micro-fissures. The water is ancient, heavy with dissolved calcium and the exhalations of its inhabitants. It does not move. There are no currents here. The dynamic of the place is glacial, almost geological. If you were to drop a grain of sand, it would take hours to settle through the viscous, life-thick medium.

For the water is dense—not with pressure, but with presence. It is crowded. There is no plant life here, no photosynthesis, no green; the vegetation parameter is zero, a mineral purity. But the fauna is abundant beyond counting, filling the sphere with a thin, drifting soup of organisms that have never seen light. They are tiny, most no larger than grains of rice: pale, translucent copepods with long, blind antennae; soft, colonial organisms like drifting veils of mucus that filter the stone’s dissolving minerals; and larger things, slow as clouds, resembling feathered slugs that pulse through the darkness with movements spaced by minutes. They navigate by chemical gradient, by the faint warmth of the walls, by touch. They have no need of eyes in this darkness that rates 0.4 on any scale of illumination—a darkness absolute, velvet, warm as a held breath.

The sound is silence. The water is still, stratified, motionless. Any vibration is swallowed by the density of suspended life and the soft, absorbent stone. You might hear your own heartbeat, if you were here, amplified by the press of warm water against your eardrums, but nothing else. The creatures do not call; they brush past each other with the soft friction of wet silk.

There is a subtle weirdness to it, not overwhelming but persistent: the patterns. The erosion has not been random. Ancient carvings—concentric rings, astronomical charts for stars that have since died—have worn away not into chaos, but into ordered ripples. The stone seems to have melted into intentional waves. The fauna, too, arrange themselves. In certain centuries, they align in geometric lattices, forming structures that resemble architecture—arches of clustered organisms, floating spirals that rotate with the precession of the earth, slow and invisible. They are building, perhaps, or remembering.

The scale is intimate, despite the cathedral volume. The density makes it feel like a small room, a closet filled with hanging garments that brush your skin. The boundaries are close; the warm stone is always near, palpable in the dark.

It is a sealed world, built by primitives with infinite patience, now occupied by descendants who have simplified themselves into perfection. The stone feeds the water; the water feeds the creatures; the creatures, dying, become sediment that presses against the stone. Nothing enters, nothing leaves. The warm dark turns, slower than continents, patient as ore.
