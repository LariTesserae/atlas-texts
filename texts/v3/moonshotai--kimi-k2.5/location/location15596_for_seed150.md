You enter through a breach where the cloud-bank has torn against the ridge, and the cold hits first: not the bite of wind, but the steady, humid chill of a place that has never known sun. Zero-point-one degrees above freezing. Your breath does not cloud—it settles, droplets joining the universal damp that varnishes every surface. This is not rain, exactly; the water hangs in suspension, condensing on the pale fuzz of things that might be walls, weeping from their lower edges in a rhythm too slow to hear but constant enough to soak your boots in the first sixty steps.

The scale deceives. From outside, the cirque appeared small, a cathedral-hollow carved by the glacier that retreated only forty years ago—an eye-blink, a fresh wound in the mountain. But inside, the density consumes perspective. You are not walking through a grove; you are walking through a lung.

Everything touches everything. Above, a canopy of bracket-fungi interlocks, shelf over shelf, dripping with clear lymph. To your sides, structures rise that resemble architecture only in their organization: towers of pale mycelium clustered in tight municipal rings, avenues between them compressed to shoulder-width by the pressure of competing growths. The “streets” are not paved; they are channels worn by capillary action, where water runs in thin glass threads toward the center. The ground is a mattress of moss so thick it bears your weight like a waterbed, but beneath that give, you feel the sharpness of young erosion—basalt shards still unrounded by time, their edges finding upward through the biomass like teeth in a gum.

There is no stone here, and no tool has ever touched this place, yet it is unmistakably urban. Districts announce themselves by texture. To your left, a neighborhood of cup-lichens, their thalli forming bowl-shaped tenements that harvest fog, each dwelling no larger than a cupboard but packed with spore-chambers. To your right, the “industrial” zone: a forest of stinkhorn ascocarps, tall as lampposts, their caps weeping the same slick fluid that runs in the streets, creating a hydraulic network that pulses slowly—sap moving against gravity in dead-of-night rhythms. The layout is too organized for chaos, too biological for plan. Alleyways turn at angles that suggest intent, yet the corners are soft, rounded by cellular growth. The weirdness lies here: in the mimicry of city planning by organisms that possess no nervous system, as though the mathematics of settlement have been independently discovered by slime molds and frost.

Light filters through as a dim green-grey, the color of pond ice. It comes from above, where the cloud-ceiling is itself a living filter—an entangled mat of usnea lichen suspended between the cirque walls, straining the daylight into something subaqueous. What illumination survives is captured by the architecture. Some towers are sheathed in jewelweed glassiness; others hoard the gloom in velvet pores that seem to drink it. Bioluminescence is absent—the cold forbids such extravagance—but there is a faint phosphorescence to the water itself, as if the condensation has learned to glow.

Sound is swallowed by the saturation. Your footsteps produce no crunch, only a soft, fungal *squelch* immediately filled by viscous growth. Occasionally, a *tick* or *pop*—the thermal contraction of ice crystals forming in the upper cortex of a lichen, or the release of spores in a sound like distant rice falling on glass. Mostly, the quiet presses: the heavy, expectant silence of things growing so densely they have run out of room to speak.

Fauna is nearly absent. A mite, perhaps, translucent and slow-moving in a water-bead on a moss-leaf. A tardigrade, tun-hunched in its cryptobiotic sleep, waiting centuries. Once, the blur of a wing—some high-altitude moth drawn to the moisture, its passage leaving a tunnel in the mist that closes behind it like a wound. The dynamic energy here is entirely vegetative, fungal, microbial. You can see it if you wait long enough: a sporangium extending, millimeter by millimeter, its tip probing toward a gap in the canopy; a slime mold calculating the shortest path between two “plazas” of liverwort; water being wicked up through hollow rhizines, defying gravity in columns no wider than a hair.

This is a city in the process of becoming its own geography. The erosion is young—the underlying stone still holds the striations of the glacier’s passage, sharp north-south grooves that now serve as conduits for root-hairs. But the biological weathering is already aggressive. Acids leach from the lichen-thalli, etching the basalt into new shapes, creating the sharp negative spaces that will become tomorrow’s “streets,” tomorrow’s “windows.” It is a settlement eating its own foundation, replacing mineral with living mass, converting elevation into architecture.

You find the heart of it—or what passes for a center in this packed organism—a chamber where the density achieves its maximum. Here, the growths have reached a stalemate, a climax of compression. Fungal hyphae have enmeshed with moss rhizoids, creating a composite material with the tensile strength of rope and the insulation of wool. This is the “town hall,” though no one convenes here; it is simply where the biomass has decided, through chemical election, to thicken. Water drips from its apex in a steady metronome, feeding a pool below that never overflows, drunk as fast as it falls by the thirsty architecture.

To stay here is to feel the cold as a form of time. Growth happens in the cold, but slowly—centuries compressed into the span of a single frost. The place is active, breathing, trading gases and nutrients through interfaces so dense they resemble crowd barriers, immune barriers. It is a pre-technological metropolis, a civilization of cellulose and chitin, built by committee of spore and hypha, wet to the bone, sharp beneath the softness, and utterly, hummingly alive.
