You find it by following the warmth. Down past the sub-basements, through a maintenance hatch whose label has worn off, into the skin of the building itself—a gap that should not exist according to the blueprints, a thermal contraction that tore open only recently, geologically speaking, though in building-years it is already middle-aged.

**The Cavity**

It is the size of a modest bedroom, but compressed. Not vertical but horizontal, a lung-cavity between the inner furnace-wall and the outer concrete skin of the foundry. The erosion here is *young*: sharp, fresh surfaces that have barely had time to round. When the heat-exchangers cycled one too many times, the thermal shock sheared the insulation bolts clean through, exposing virgin metal edges that gleam like fresh-cut flint. Crystalline mineral deposits—chlorides and sulfates leached from fifty years of brick—have begun to grow in sharp, aggressive needles, not the rounded flowstone of ancient caves but the jagged teeth of a wound still healing wrong.

**The Density**

Every cubic centimeter is tenanted. The density is suffocating—not claustrophobic in the sense of walls closing in, but in the sense that *occupancy* has reached saturation. The space is a three-dimensional lattice of half-dissolved phenolic foam, its honeycomb cells now supporting a metropolis of life. Chitin and silk and calcium carbonate interweave with rust and aluminum. Silverfish have constructed vertical galleries in the foam, their passages so densely packed that the walls appear to breathe with their collective motion. Spiderwebs—dozens of species, from coin-sized orb-weavers to long-legged cellar spiders—form a gauze that catches the condensation, weighted down with droplets until they quiver and fall in slow, silver arcs.

The fauna is abundant but invisible unless you look closely. Mites move in herds across the warm inner wall, grazing on metal-oxidizing bacteria that paint the steel in iridescent purples and greens. A colony of dampwood termites has built a castle the size of a suitcase against a heating pipe, their paper-thin galleries interlaced with copper wire they have chewed from abandoned conduits—somehow incorporating the metal into their constructions without dying, their gut biomes adapted to the industrial age.

**The Atmosphere**

It is warm—perpetually, enzymatically warm. The inner wall radiates heat from the foundry beyond, maintaining a tropical 28°C even when winter rages outside. The outer wall is cool concrete, slick with *damp*. Not flowing water, but the persistent cling of condensation, the breath of the building. Water here exists as potential: droplets that form on the ceiling, fatten, and fall in a rhythmic tattoo—one every four seconds, precisely—into a shallow depression that never quite fills, the concrete drinking as fast as the sky weeps. The air is heavy, carrying the mineral tang of hot stone and the sweet rot of ancient glue.

Light filters through a crack in the outer wall—a thin blade of daylight that slices through the humidity, catching on the mineral crystals and refracting into unlikely rainbows. Otherwise, the illumination comes from a single industrial fixture, its casing cracked, hanging by three wires, buzzing at 40 hertz with a flicker too fast to see but slow enough to make the shadows hesitate.

**The Strange**

This is where the parameters bend. The weirdness here is not dramatic, not tentacles or floating stones, but a subtle wrongness in the metabolism of the place. The metal is *growing*. The young erosion works backward here: the sharp edges of sheared steel are not dulling but sharpening, crystallizing into whiskers of pure iron that extend millimeter by millimeter into the space, fed by the electric potential in the hanging wires and the chemical banquet of the damp. The termites do not eat the metal; they *farm* it, cultivating the bacterial films that precipitate ferrous oxide into architectural spires.

Time moves differently in the packed density. A clock left here by a maintenance worker (a scattering of *built* artifacts: a broken caliper, a hard hat slowly being digested by the foam, a name-tag reading M. CHEN) lost eleven minutes in six hours. The active dynamic of the place is metabolic, not mechanical—everything is eating, growing, accreting, crystallizing, but in a silence so profound you can hear the spiders' footfalls. The sound parameter holds: it is mostly quiet, the foam absorbing echoes, only the *plink* of water and the subsonic hum of the heating pipe drilling into your sternum.

**The Rules**

This is a pocket ecosystem founded on *enthalpy*. The primary producers are not plants—the vegetation remains sparse, limited to a few algae mats that Photosynthesize the feeble light—but chemolithoautotrophs, bacteria that eat metal and excrete architecture. The rules here state that nothing is discarded, everything is incorporated. The scattered built structures do not decay so much as they are *assimilated*, becoming indistinguishable from the biology within decades rather than centuries.

To enter is to become part of the calculation. The density allows no empty witnesses. Stand still for ten minutes and mites will sample your boots. Twenty minutes, and the silverfish will investigate your cuffs. The warmth welcomes, the damp claims, and the sharp young edges of this hidden place wait, patient and mineral, to incorporate your body heat into their slow, strange geology.
