The **Singing Basins** rest on a plateau so high that the sky above them is not blue but a bleached, silvery white, the sun a burning coin that reflects off every surface until the eyes ache. Here, at the edge of the upper atmosphere where the air is thin and cool—never freezing, but carrying the persistent chill of altitude—there is no soil, no rooting earth, no tree line to cross. The ground itself ends in a sudden geological punctuation, and upon this raw, young stone, humanity (or something like it) has enacted a total replacement.

Every inch is constructed. Not with iron, not with fire-forged tools, but with the patient accumulation of primitive craft: stones split by thermal shock and frost-wedging, surfaces smoothed by grinding with harder minerals, fitted together without mortar into a three-dimensional lattice so dense that emptiness has become theoretical. The density is suffocating in its completeness—there is no "ground" separate from the structure, only the structure itself, rising and falling in terraces, channels, cisterns, and spires that extend across a valley-sized expanse. It is open in scale—you can see the arrangement sprawling to every horizon—but packed in substance; there is no bare rock face, no unworked surface. Everywhere, the stone has been persuaded into bowls, runnels, flutes, and catches.

And it is saturated. Water is the breath of this place. Being high and cool, the plateau intercepts the moisture-laden winds that scrape across its elevations. The architecture serves as a vast, non-biological lung: condensation forms instantly on the pale, heat-reflective stone (brightness rating: searing), gathering into droplets that obey a slow, patient physics. Water emerges from the atmosphere itself, beading on overhanging ledges, weeping from the undersides of stone bridges, filling the countless basins to their brims. It does not rush. The dynamic here is glacial, meditative. A drop may take an hour to traverse a carved groove, moving through a maze of engineered channels that descend by micrometers, never spilling, always contained. Some basins appear to fill from below, water percolating upward through capillary networks in the lithic sponge, defying expectation in that subtle, low-grade weirdness that permeates the environment.

There is no vegetation because there is no medium for it—no humus, no trapped dirt, only mineral surfaces washed constantly by moving water. What grows here grows in the manner of crystals. In the shadows of the deeper channels, where the light is merely bright rather than blinding, mineral accretions build up in forms that mimic coral or bone: aragonite flowers, calcite feathers, gypsum rosettes that expand across the stonework with the slowness of geology. They are the only ecology, a pre-biological or post-biological kingdom of saturated salts.

No animals navigate these channels. The silence—mostly silence, rating just above absolute null—is broken only by the occasional *plink* of a droplet striking a full pool, or the subterranean gurgle of water moving through enclosed pipes carved through the stone. The sound has a peculiar quality here; the dense arrangement of hard surfaces creates acoustic shadows and unexpected clarities. A drop falling three terraces down rings with a bell-like purity, the stone itself humming at frequencies felt in the teeth rather than heard.

The construction is young, in the geological sense. The edges of the fitted stones are sharp, unrounded by time, as if the plateau was uncovered from retreating ice only centuries ago and immediately, obsessively, patterned. Or perhaps the stone itself is nascent, still extruding from some slow mineral upwelling, and the builders (if there were builders) merely guided the natural accretion, teaching the rock how to grow into channels and cups.

To move through the Singing Basins is to navigate a world without nature and without wilderness, yet not dead. It is a wet desert, a cool furnace, a packed void. The water cycles endlessly from vapor to liquid to mineral and back, never leaving, caught in the bright, airless embrace of the high place. There are no inhabitants, only the architecture, which seems to be waiting—collecting, dripping, calcifying—through slow centuries, for purposes that erode as slowly as the stone itself refuses to wear.
