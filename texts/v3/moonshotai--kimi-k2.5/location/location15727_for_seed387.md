The sound arrives before anything else—a constant, bone-conducting **roar** that compresses the skull and makes thought stutter. It is not the rhythmic pulse of waves nor the white noise of rain, but the screaming of water forced through tortuous channels under impossible pressure, a sound that has matured over decades into a solid, unchanging fact. This is **The Pocket**, and it is drowning in noise.

You are inside a sphere roughly sixty meters in diameter, buried beneath two kilometers of sediment and stone, itself submerged in the lightless belly of an ocean. The scale is intimate enough that you could swim from one curving wall to the other in minutes, yet the density of construction makes it feel like a city compressed into a jar. 

The chamber is **empty**—not of water, but of presence. The center is a vast, turbulence-churned void of warm saline (temperature 2.0), running at a feverish 34 degrees Celsius, heated by geothermal seepage through the floor and the residual decay of the machinery. Suspended within this aqueous cavity, however, is a three-dimensional lattice of **urban infrastructure**: catwalks that spiral like DNA helices, pipework bundled into arteries thick as redwoods, and platforms layered like the contents of a sunken drawer. It is industrial (tech 2.1)—all riveted steel, ceramic conduits, and manganese-caked switching boxes—yet organized with the density of a settlement. Staircases lead nowhere, terminating in blunt rust; balconies overlook only water. The construction has achieved a state of **weathered equilibrium** (erosion 1.9): the metal has oxidized to a stable ochre crust, the concrete has been polished smooth by decades of abrasive particulate, and the whole assembly has settled into its shape like a shipwreck accepting the sea.

Light here is scarce—0.7 on any scale—filtering down through fissures in the ceiling as a faint, phosphorescent blue, or emitting from the occasional surviving porthole of bioluminescent bacteria farms that have long since gone feral. The illumination reveals the **dynamics** of the place: the water is never still. It corkscrews through the chamber in a permanent **turbulent** state, driven by thermal differentials and the intake vents that still suck from the ocean above. Currents collide mid-chamber, creating standing vortices that spin for weeks, shearing rust flakes into spiraling galaxies. The movement is chaotic but **established**, a storm that has raged so long it has become architecture.

There is no plant life—nothing so complex as kelp or algae could survive the toxicity and motion. Instead, the **vegetation** consists of primitive chemosynthetic mats, orange and sulfur-yellow, that cling to the warm pipes like fungal lichen, feeding on the chemical sweat of the metal. They are the only softness in a mineral world of scabs and glass-smooth erosion.

**Fauna** is rare (0.7)—perhaps three or four creatures at any given time. They are translucent, gelatinous things that have found their way through the intake grates: amphipods with eyes like black pearls, or the occasional ghostly squid that drifts through, disoriented by the noise, hunting the bacterial films. They appear as afterimages, brief disturbances in the particulate haze.

The **weirdness** of the place lies in its acoustic properties. The deafening sound (2.9) does not echo; instead, it *concentrates*. Certain nodes in the chamber—specific junctions of pipe and platform—act as silent spots where the roar cancels itself out, creating pockets of absolute, pressure-heavy silence that feel like violence compared to the surrounding scream. In these spots, you can hear the secondary sounds: the ticking of cooling metal, the growth of crystals in the pipes, the slow, grinding migration of the entire structure as it settles deeper into the silt.

This is a world in its own state, a **hidden** room where an industrial town has been dissolved into warm, rushing water. It is a lung that breathes sea, a pocket of organized chaos that has accepted its submersion and now simply persists—deafening, warm, and complete.
