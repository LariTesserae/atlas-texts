The sound arrives before any other sense—a chord of seventeen discrete frequencies that has not altered in amplitude, timbre, or resonance for ten thousand years. It is not music, nor is it merely noise; it is a structural property of the space, loud enough to blur the vision and set the teeth singing against one another, yet so immutable that the ear quickly edits it into silence, accepting the pressure as atmosphere itself. This is the Hum, and within the Hum, nothing changes.

You descend through recent fracturing—sharp, unweathered basalt teeth that have not yet had time to round or oxidize, the rock still bleeding powder-white where excavation or seismic violence split it open. The cavity reveals itself not as a cavern but as a **geode**, roughly cathedralic in scale, perhaps sixty meters in its longest axis, yet so densely packed that traversing it requires climbing as much as walking. The air is warm—consistently, unpleasantly so, the temperature of a fever that never breaks—and utterly desiccated. Water exists here only as a theoretical concept, chemically locked into anhydrous mineral salts that glitter in the dull, arterial glow emanating from the machinery. There is no moisture to rot, no solvent to erode.

The industrial presence is scattered, fragmentary, yet total. One passes a flywheel the height of a man, still spinning on bearings that have not been greased in geological time; its motion is frictionless, perfect, part of the frozen equilibrium. Valves open and close in sequences that spell out mathematical constants, eternally compressing and releasing gases that have already reached their final chemical state. Pipes burst from the walls in braids, meet in midair, support nesting colonies of copper-thread and shed chitin. The technology is heavy, Victorian in its brute materiality—cast iron, brass, ceramic insulators—but arranged without plan, as if a factory exploded and was flash-preserved in the moment of dispersal.

Yet this is not abandonment. The fauna—**abundant**, impossibly so—have claimed the debris not as shelter but as **anatomy**. Thousands of creatures occupy every cubic meter, layered from floor to ceiling. They are the **maintainers**, the **operators**. Sessile forms clamp to pipe-joints: flattened, trilobite-like things with shells of piezoelectric crystal that drink the vibration of the Hum and transmute it into metabolic stasis. They do not grow; they simply *are*, their shells etched with patterns that match the thermal vents they hug. Mobile fauna navigate the gear-trains—slender, many-jointed beings with ceramic articulation, perfectly evolved to fit the gaps between spinning cogs. They do not wear the metal, for the metal does not wear. They do not reproduce, for the population remains in perfect equilibrium, constant in number, position, and intent.

Sparse vegetation—lichenous, metallic, the color of verdigris and dried blood—clings to heat sources: radiator fins, transformer housings, the warm underbellies of dormant engines. These organism-mineral hybrids photosynthesize not light but infrared waste, and they do not spread. Each patch occupies its precise allotted space, having reached the limit of its nutrient gradient millennia ago.

The dim light sources—failing indicator lamps, phosphorescent mold that never blooms nor fades, the barest cherry-red glow from eternally heating elements—cast shadows that do not shift. In the Hum-Locked Cavern, time is not a river but a standing wave. The dynamic is zero. There are no gradients left to exploit, no succession to occur, no weathering to soften the young, sharp edges of the rock. The ecosystem is a photograph of industrial climax, still wet with the chemistry of its creation yet preserved forever in the amber of entropic stasis.

Here, a piston is caught mid-stroke and will complete it never, yet remains in motion. There, a creature extends a jaw toward a morsel of mineralized grease, suspended in the gesture, neither hungry nor sated, existing in the eternal present of the Hum. The place is strange because it defies the imperative of decay. It is a room where entropy has been negotiated into a draw, where the second law sleeps, and the machines—scattered, broken-looking, yet functional—continue their purposeless work forever, attended by beasts that have become geometry, in a warmth that never cools, under a noise that never ceases, in the dry, dense dark beneath the world.
