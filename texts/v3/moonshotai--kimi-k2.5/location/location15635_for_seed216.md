You do not arrive at this place so much as you remember it wrong and find yourself inside. It is a sphere—or perhaps a continent adrift in cloud—where the boundary between grown and built has dissolved entirely. Every surface, from foundation to zenith, has been shaped by patient hands using tools of knapped flint, obsidian, and petrified thorn. There is no stone here, no metal, no bedrock. Only the vast, warm, breathing architecture of cellulose and lignin, carved and cajoled over epochs into something that resembles a world.

The elevation is dizzying. You exist at a height where atmosphere should thin to ice, yet the air is thick, warm, and heavy with moisture that refuses to fall. Water behaves according to older, forgotten rules. It beads along the grain of cathedral-sized beams, crawling upward against gravity in slow, deliberate rivulets, collecting in pools that hang suspended in midair like lenses of mercury. Condensation is the weather here; warm rain falls upward from the lower galleries, rising to feed the canopy above, which is not a sky but another floor, another root-webbed ceiling miles distant, filtering what light exists down to a perpetual bronze twilight. The illumination—dim, sourceless, diffuse—seeps from the living wood itself, bioluminescence bred or carved into the grain over millennia.

The scale is planetary, yet the density is that of a held breath. For all the total vegetation coverage—the absolute subjugation of every surface by plant matter—there is almost nothing here. The architecture is hollow. You walk through corridors between structural ribs that could contain oceans; you stand in chambers where the walls are distant horizons of bark; you look across voids where the only landmarks are the occasional bridge-vines, braided by hands long calcified into the wood itself, spanning distances that would swallow cities. The builders made space. Immense, resonant, empty space. The erosion here is settled, finished—the wood has fossilized into its final form, neither growing nor decaying but existing in a state of perfected equilibrium, smooth as river stone where countless palms have polished it over generations.

Because the technology is primitive. Obsessively, beautifully so. You can see the tool marks: the scoop-gouges of shell scoops, the parallel striations of stone adzes, the whorled fingerprints left in sap that hardened like amber. There are no machines, no smelting, no wheels. Only the evidence of hands—billions of them, working in succession over timescales that make geology seem hurried—shaping the living wood while it grew, bending it, hollowing it, pruning the heartwood to create these impossible vaults. The "built" parameter here does not mean manufactured; it means *tended*. The entire planet is a garden that has been pruned into the shape of a cosmos.

And it is breaking down. The weirdness bleeds through in the angles. Corridors that should parallel each other converge in the distance. You can walk for days toward a specific archway and find yourself approaching it from behind. Gravity is a suggestion; in some chambers it pulls sideways, in others it pools like water in the corners, creating wells where debris collects on the "walls." Time stutters. You might see a bird—one of the abundant fauna, for life teems in the thick, wet air—frozen mid-flight, suspended not in space but in temporal amber, while twenty feet away its flock moves in a blur too fast to track. The fauna themselves are silent, despite their numbers. Insectoid things with glass wings drift through the humid twilight; soft, boneless predators flow like spilled ink around the structural columns; all of them hunt and eat and reproduce without a whisper. The silence is absolute, broken only by the subsonic creak of the structure settling—a sound felt in the teeth rather than heard—and the persistent, maddening drip of upward-falling condensation.

The dynamics are biological and geological at once. The vast space breathes. On cycles measured in weeks, the warm air pressure shifts, creating weather systems within the hollows: cloud banks forming in the lower voids, rising to clot the upper reaches, releasing their moisture in warm, upward rains that feed the distant ceiling-gardens. Sap tides move through the structural members like slow blood, nutrients migrating from the outer shell to the inner chambers and back. Predator-prey relationships exist in the dim hollows, but they are abstract, silent dances of presence and absence, creatures folding through the reality-loopholes to hunt in dimensions adjacent to their prey.

It is a place that should not exist: a high-altitude, hot-house void; a handcrafted emptiness; a silent, teeming monument to the patience of primitive hands who decided that nature itself was not sufficient, and carved it into something more impossible. You walk for days through the warm, wet, dim silence, and the wood shows you where they worked, and where they stopped, and how the place has continued building itself—slowly, silently, wrongly—ever since.
