The place sits in a cold pocket of the valley floor, where the elevation dips just enough to trap the damp exhalations of the river half a mile away. Here, the air carries a perpetual weight of imminent rain, though the skies often hold back, releasing only condensation—a fine, clinging dew that beads on every surface and drips from the eaves of things that are not quite leaves.

They call it the Lattice Quarter, though it is scarcely a quarter of anything, being only hamlet-sized in its extent. It is urban without being crowded: organized according to the ruthless logic of city planning—zoning, infrastructure, arterial paths—but executed in a language of such profound sparseness that the eye travels for meters through empty air before finding the next wall, the next pillar, the next junction box. The architecture is total vegetation, yet it is not a forest. It is industry grown woody.

Imagine a factory built by patient, alien hands who understood only cellulose and lignin. The structures are skeletal—cathedral frames of pale, smooth wood that has weathered for decades into a silver-grey finish, the erosion of time having sanded every corner to a blunt, accepting curve. These are not dead timbers but living masses, the substrate itself grown into habitable form. The walls breathe: vast planes of heartwood riddled with capillary channels, pulsing slowly with the pressure of hydraulic sap, acting as pistons and pipes simultaneously. The "floors" are taut membranes of cambium layered with compressed sphagnum, springy and damp, elevated above the true ground by networks of buttress roots that serve as load-bearing columns.

It is cool here, always. The temperature hovers at the threshold of discomfort, preserved by the thick, thermal insulation of the living walls and the constant evaporation of moisture from the surfaces. Light enters through translucent panels—perhaps modified leaf-scales or thin sections of fungal chitin—diffusing the outside day into a soft, even glow that illuminates the space without casting harsh shadows. Normal light. Sufficient to read by, insufficient to warm.

The Quarter is sparse. You can stand in the center of the main processing chamber—cathedral-tall, room-wide—and hear your own footsteps fade into the distance. The machinery, if one can call it that, is scattered with intentional spaciousness. A distillation vat here: a hollowed burl the size of a cottage, its interior lined with glass-smooth resin, slowly filling with condensate from the breathing walls. A conveyor there: a belt of tightly woven vine that creeps forward at the pace of geological drift, carrying unprocessed seed-pods toward a cracking station where heavy, seed-vice "jaws" of Ironwood (genetically persuaded, perhaps, or simply ancient) open and close with wet, pneumatic sighs. The soundscape is mostly quiet—just these soft percussions, the occasional *thunk* of a valve (a sphincter-like occlusion in a tuber) shutting, the *pat-pat-pat* of condensed fog dripping from the ceiling onto the floor and being absorbed almost instantly by the thirsty flooring-moss.

There is life here, but fauna is rare. You might see one of the Maintenance Beetles—if they are beetles, or if they are something engineered, or something that simply evolved to fill the niche—a slow, glossy black shape the size of a fist, trundling along a root-highway to clear a blockage in a phloem-channel. Perhaps a solitary human in a waxed coat, checking pressure gauges that are actually swollen nodes on a stem, watching the color change from pale yellow to amber to indicate readiness. But mostly, the Quarter operates without animal intervention. It is an ecosystem in active motion, predator and prey replaced by process and product, the dynamic churn of biological industry: starches being converted, waters being filtered, heavy compounds being moved from growth-chamber to growth-chamber via peristaltic tunnels that pulse visibly beneath the bark-surface.

The erosion is established. This place has settled into its final form, weathered into its own state. The sharp edges of its initial growth have long since been worn by the internal friction of its functions and the external caress of the damp wind. It is in balance, neither expanding nor decaying, a steady state of productive metabolism. The wetness keeps it from drying out; the coolness keeps it from rotting too fast; the biological pace keeps it from burning out.

It is unusual, yes. The weirdness is subtle, like a dream of a factory half-remembered. You expect the clang of metal, the scream of turbines, but instead find only the wet, rhythmic creaking of expanding wood, the factory whistle that is actually a seed-pod splitting open at a pre-calculated pressure, releasing a puff of spores into the damp air like steam. It is a city of one organism, or a symbiosis so complete it might as well be one, living and working in the quiet hollow, dripping, breathing, and processing, forever.
