This is the sound of the **Wall**, a meteorological impossibility: a stationary storm that has been discharging into this high basin for millennia, a vertical river of rain that strikes the surface with such relentless force that the boundary between air and liquid has long since dissolved. Without fauna to graze them, the plants have become the predators, the scavengers, the pollinators—exchanging genetic material through explosive spore-clouds that bloom in the dark like silent artillery.

The **scale** is open (1.6), regional. The basin extends beyond sight, a network of drowned valleys and submerged peaks creating a topography of underwater cliffs and trenches. Between these features, the **density** is sparse (1.2)—vast stretches of open, dark water where the only solids are the drifting, sleeping seeds of the great kelp, each the size of a melon, glowing faintly with stored phosphorescence. These structures are primitive (tech: 1.1): dry-stone chambers constructed by hands that understood the weight of rock but not the lever. Air bells. Cairns marking navigable channels through the kelp. Woven nets of *Arborhydra* fiber, now ossified and skeletal, left by people who attempted to harvest this place and found themselves instead harvested by the cold. The huts are empty, have been for generations, but they are maintained. The plants do this. The **weirdness** (2.0) is here: the vegetation exhibits behaviors that suggest distributed cognition. Root systems infiltrate the air bells not to destroy, but to support, creating buttresses of living wood that keep the primitive shelters from collapsing. It is as if the ecosystem remembers the builders, or studies them, or waits.

The rules of this world are simple but strange: water flows upward here, sometimes, in thermal spirals caused by the temperature differential between the icy depths and the slightly less frigid surface. Sound travels in channels, the hollow stalks acting as aeolian pipes, so that a stone dropped in the eastern trench can be heard as a bell-tone in the western shallows. Nothing moves quickly except the plants. The dynamic (1.7) is geological in pace but biological in intent—the constant, writhing growth, the steady rain-hammer, the slow grinding of root against rock.

There is no night or day, only the varying intensity of the Wall's fury. There is no animal cry, only the creaking of stalks and the drum of rain. It is a place that has settled into its own state, weathered, self-regulating, an established (1.6) equilibrium where the boundary between what grows and what is built has become as permeable as the boundary between air and water. To enter is to become part of the garden—not as a predator, for there are none, but as a potential nutrient, a curiosity, or perhaps, if the plants find you interesting, a tenant in one of the dry stone rooms, kept empty for reasons the vegetation does not explain.
