The **Resonant Expanse** is not a desert in the way of sand and heat, but a desert of *liquidity*. It exists as a single, continuous plateau lifted three kilometers above the theoretical sea level of its world—a continent-sized tableland of weathered stone so vast that it generates its own atmospheric cells, its own weather that never falls as rain. Here, water is a phantom measured in milligrams per cubic meter, a vapor so diffuse that it has forgotten how to condense. The air is thin, cool, and bright, carrying light with a piercing clarity that makes distant horizons appear close enough to touch, then reveals them to be days away.

The elevation has bleached the world of warmth. Temperatures hover in the low positive digits, a perpetual autumn that never quite freezes, preserving the landscape in a state of suspended animation. Yet this coolness is not dead. It is the operating temperature of a chemistry that does not require the liquidity of water to thrive.

You would notice the vegetation first, not because it carpets the land—it does not—but because of its impossible lushness where it *does* exist. The Expanse is dotted with **basin gardens**, eroded amphitheaters carved by wind over geological epochs (erosion 2.5/3) where the polished stone dips to collect what little the air offers. In these depressions grow the **Silva Vitrea**, glass-forests that appear, from a distance, as if massive chandeliers have been planted in the rock. They are not plants in the aqueous sense, but lithotrophic colonies—part fungus, part mineral, part photosynthetic engine—that extract moisture directly from the supersaturated wind and fix carbon using the intense, unfiltered stellar radiation (light 2.8/3). 

Their "leaves" are translucent silica scales, fractal and overlapping, creating structures that look like weeping willows carved from ice and opal. These forests are *lush* in the truest sense: dense, layered, complex ecosystems unto themselves. Within a single basin, the glass-trees grow thick as kelp, their branches interlocking to form vaulted canopies under which the air is noticeably warmer, trapped and cycled by the metabolic heat of the organisms. Hanging gardens of smaller, gem-like epiphytes drape from the branches—crimson, amber, and violet organisms that filter specific wavelengths of light. The forest floor is a spongy mat of living mineral, a slow-crawling lichen that digests the bedrock itself, creating a soil of microscopic glass beads.

But these basins are islands. Between them stretches the **Intervene**, vast tracts of bare, wind-sculpted stone where nothing grows. The density of life is sparse (1.1/3); you might walk for fifty kilometers across polished granite domes and see only the occasional solitary "tree"—a pioneer specimen standing alone on a ridge, its silica leaves chiming almost inaudibly in the perpetual wind. The landscape is established, settled into its form, the stone rounded and smooth as eggs, the basins positioned where they have been for ten million years, in balance with the wind.

The soundscape is the absence of sound (0.5/3). The thin atmosphere carries little vibration. The wind moves across the glass-forests with a high, thin whisper that exists at the upper threshold of hearing, more felt in the teeth than heard in the ears. When the wind is still, the silence is absolute, a pressure against the eardrums. This is a world without birdsong, without insect hum, without the crash of surf. The rare fauna (1.0/3) that exists here has adapted to this quiet: the **Calcifer** beetles, whose shells are perfect spheres of calcium carbonate, rolling slowly between basins like living stones, making no sound; and the **Aerials**, translucent gas-bag organisms that drift on thermal currents, filtering spores, their movements silent as dust.

Yet the Expanse is dynamic (2.0/3), alive with processes that operate on scales both glacial and immediate. The glass-forests are chemically active, undergoing seasonal "blooms" where they shift their silica metabolism, causing entire basins to change color from blue-white to deep amber over the course of a month. Wind erosion continues its patient work, polishing the Intervene, occasionally carving new basins when a particularly ancient tree-root system finally collapses, leaving a hollow to catch the wind-borne spores. The weather systems are violent but invisible—katabatic winds pouring off the plateau edges, creating standing waves in the upper atmosphere that manifest as rippling auroras at night, even in the brightness of day.

There is a subtle weirdness (1.4/3) to the place, a physics slightly askew. The Silva Vitrea are piezoelectric. As the wind stresses their glassy branches, they generate static fields that make the air taste of copper. In the deepest basins, where the forests are oldest and densest, these fields accumulate, creating pools of electrical potential that occasionally discharge in silent, violet coronas around the treetops—St. Elmo's fire that dances for hours during the storm seasons. The ground itself holds a charge; to lie down on the polished stone is to feel a faint, rhythmic thrum, as if the plateau were breathing, the slow geological pulse of a world that has never known the softness of water, but has learned to grow hard and bright and strange instead.

No one has built here (0.4/3). No walls, no roads, no tools. The Expanse is pre-tool, pre-construction, a pure ecology of mineral and light. It exists in its own state, complete, weathered into its final form, vast enough to hold its own silence, bright enough to blind, cool enough to preserve, and active enough to remind you that even in the absence of water, in the absence of sound, the world is still growing.
