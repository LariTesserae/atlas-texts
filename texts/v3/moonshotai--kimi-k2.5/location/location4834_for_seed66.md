The Sintercrown Plateaus rise seventeen kilometers above the basal plains, a continental shelf of mountains planed flat by something older than glaciers. Here, the air is thick, warm, and pregnant with moisture that refuses to fall as rain; instead, it condenses on every surface in beads the size of pearls, running in mineralized rivulets down facades that have been weathered smooth over epochs. Nothing green grows—no lichen etches the stone, no spore takes root in the cracks. The world is basalt, ceramic, oxidized copper, and glass, weeping constantly with a slow, saline perspiration that pools in the worn hollows of ancient thoroughfares and evaporates in the thermal updrafts, leaving behind lace-veins of salt and calcite.

It is a city, or rather, a conurbation of cities fused into a single metabolism, vast enough to generate its own microclimates and static electricity. The scale is such that a traveler might walk for months and remain within the same district, the horizon always obscured by the perpetual mist that clings to this high altitude, diffusing the sunlight into a featureless silver glow that casts no shadows. The architecture is dense, layered, compacted—structures packed shoulder-to-shoulder, bridges woven through with pipes and conduits, the entire landscape compressed into a vertical and horizontal saturation of organized construction. Yet despite the density, there is no chaos. Everything has settled into a long-established equilibrium, rounded and polished by time.

The fauna is overwhelming—not biological, but industrial offspring that have outbred their purpose. They blanket every surface. Trillions of maintenance arthropods, no larger than thumbnails, with bodies of filigreed brass and wings of mica, swarm in glittering carpets across the avenues, consuming oxidation and excreting a fine, hard enamel that polishes the stone beneath their feet. Pipe-worms, segmented and pneumatic, coil through the infrastructure, hissing as they bore through walls to seal micro-fractures with resin. Condensate-harvesters cluster like metallic barnacles on every ledge, their diaphragms pulsing slowly—inhale, exhale—distilling potable water from the saturated air, their accumulated reservoirs dripping in rhythmic counterpoint to the larger flows. Higher up, on the cornices and thermal vents, grazer-units the size of dogs perch motionless for days, then move with sudden, startling jerks to scour surfaces with abrasive tongues, breeding in the warm, wet cavities between buildings where mineral precipitates provide shell-material for their offspring.

The sound is the aggregate voice of this mechanical ecology: a constant, loud, droning thrum of gear-teeth meshing, the high-pitched whine of capacitors charging, the basso groan of geothermal vents that heat the plateau—explaining the anomalous warmth at such elevation—and the ceaseless, sandpaper whisper of the swarm. It is not a cacophony but a settled industrial chant, loud enough that conversation requires leaning close, the vibrations felt in the sternum as much as heard.

Time moves slowly here. The fauna operate on geological patience; a grooming cycle might take a decade to complete a single façade. Buildings are not demolished but gradually devoured and regurgitated by constructor-colonies, their forms shifting in increments visible only across centuries. The condensate itself moves strangely—beads of water crawling upward against gravity in certain thermal corridors, refusing to freeze despite the thin atmosphere, forming slow, migratory globules that traverse the city like liquid livestock, harvested by specialized siphon-creatures.

The rules of this world are mineralogic. Water is the primary currency and hazard, constantly threatening to pool and corrode, constantly being managed by the fauna. There is no distinction between infrastructure and organism; the pipes breathe, the walls digest, the streets are living carpets of maintenance engines. Energy derives from the deep thermal core and the chemical potential of oxidizing metals. The aesthetic is one of wet, polished industrial baroque—surfaces gleaming under their coats of condensation, intricate as coral reefs, every inch occupied, every void filled with the slow, clicking industry of the machine-swarm.

There is a subtle wrongness to the place, a low-grade weirdness that prickles at the edge of perception. Perhaps it is the way the mechanical fauna anticipate each other’s movements with no visible signal, or the realization that the city is not built *upon* the plateau but *is* the plateau—a coral accretion of industrial sediment, three kilometers deep, growing upward as slowly as stalagmites, layer upon layer of forgotten functions compressed into a single, breathing, weeping, warm and ancient machine.
