You find it at the edge of the breathable world, where the atmosphere thins to a silver wire and even lichen grows tired. A fresh wound in the mountainside: a sinkhole punched through by a recent ice-calf or tectonic shrug, revealing a chamber no larger than a village church. The stone here has not had time to learn patience. Everything is edges—spars of basalt and flint-sharp quartz that would slice an ungloved palm, surfaces so freshly exposed they still smell of the deep earth, of sulfur and cold iron. Young rock, virginal and vicious.

You descend into darkness. The light dies within three body-lengths of the entrance, extinguished not by distance alone but by the density of what lives here. The air is cool, perpetually damp, heavy with condensation that beads on your hair and drips from the ceiling in a slow, metronomic percussion. It is never silent. The sound hits you first in the sternum—a low, resonant thunder that is felt before it is heard, a subsonic booming that climbs into the audible range as a constant, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack*, like boulders grinding in a mill, or the world's slowest applause. Loud. Unrelenting. Yet the tempo is glacial; each percussive peak arrives only after your lungs have filled and emptied twenty times, a heartbeat measured in epochs.

Then your eyes adjust, and you understand that you have not entered a cave, but a *body*.

The Chamber of the Stone-Eaters is packed so densely with fauna that the architecture has become biological. They hang from every surface, packed shoulder-to-shoulder—or where shoulders would be—layered three and four deep against the walls, dangling from the ceiling in clustering rosettes, carpeting the floor in mats that rise to your knees. There is no empty space; even the air feels occupied by their presence. They are overwhelming not in size—the largest is no bigger than a sleeping dog—but in absolute, suffocating number. Ten thousand? Twenty? They defy counting, a single organism fractured into ten thousand slow-moving mouths.

They are *weird*. They resemble nothing so much as the intersection of a tardigrade, a fungus, and a nightmare of mathematics. Their bodies are translucent, pale as cave fish, through which you can see viscous fluids pulsing in lazy circuits, carrying nutrients on journeys that take days to complete. Each possesses too many limbs—six, eight, twelve—jointed like grasshopper legs but moving with the terrible patience of trees growing. They are lithotrophs, stone-eaters, fastened to the raw rock by sucker-feet or by fusion; their mouths—radial, toothless, dripping with acid—are pressed against the young stone, dissolving it slowly, recycling the mountain into flesh. This is why the erosion remains young: they consume the smooth, groping for the sharp, maintaining the chamber in a state of perpetual, hungry freshness.

They move, but you must watch for an hour to see it. A limb lifts, pendulous, through syrupy time, selecting a specific flake of obsidian from a scatter of debris on the floor. It holds the stone against its belly, using it to scrape minerals from the wall—a behavior that sits at the threshold of instinct and technology. Primitive. Simple tools selected, used for a season, discarded. The floor is littered with these implements: water-worn cobbles shaped by use rather than knapping, sticks blackened by acidic secretions, arranged in no pattern, heaped in accidental cairns. No built structures mar the space—no walls, no roofs, no hearths—only these scattered objects and the creatures themselves, which serve as the only architecture, the only furnishing, the only inhabitants.

They make the sound. They click. Each creature possesses calcified nodules on their ventral plates, and they strike these together, or against the stone, or against the nodules of their neighbors. The synchronization is imperfect but persistent, a statistical rhythm emerging from ten thousand individual slow choices, creating that thunderous, patient drone that fills the chamber and makes the fresh rock vibrate. It is a communication, or a digestion, or a prayer to the mountain they are slowly becoming.

The ecology is closed, tight as a fist. Sparse filaments of algae line the entrance, converting the thin high-altitude light into sugars that dust the air; the creatures catch this dust on their mucous skins. Everything else they mine from the stone itself—iron, sulfur, silica—transmuting geology into biology at a rate visible only to geological time. They die where they hang, calcifying into the walls, becoming the stone they ate, maintaining the density even in death. 

To disturb them is to violate a slow process. They would not scatter—they move too slowly for that—but they would turn, gradually, like planets shifting orbit, to fix you with eyeless faces, considering your heat, your moisture, your usefulness as a temporary surface. The rules here are simple: motion is currency, and you are spending too fast. The air is damp and cool and tastes of stone milk. The darkness is complete except for the bioluminescent tracery in their guts, a dim, networked glow that maps the chamber in constellations of pale green. 

You are the alien here, the fast thing, the temporary. They were here before the last ice, and they will be here when the mountain wears down, grinding their primitive stones, clicking their slow rhythm, overwhelming the dark with the sheer fact of their patient, chewing existence.
