The sound arrives first, always—a subsonic pressure that lives in the sternum rather than the ear, a basso drone that has been ongoing for seventeen years and will continue for seventeen more before it completes one full cycle. This is the *Compression*, the great engine at the heart of the ExtractionTerraces, and it sets the tempo for everything in the Scarp.

You are standing at 6,000 meters above the conceptual sea level, though "above" behaves oddly here. The air is cool—perpetually autumn, the breath turning to mist before the face—but damp, saturated with mineral-rich condensation that beads on every surface and never quite freezes. Condensation drips from the undersides of iron girders, collects in the hollows of raw stone, and runs in thin, singing channels down faces of rock that are, geologically speaking, newborn. The erosion parameter here is embryonic. These peaks did not exist a century ago; they thrust themselves up through the skin of the world in a tectonic hiccup, and the rock is still cooling, still off-gassing, still deciding what kind of mineral it wants to be. It steams.

The Scarp is urban, technically—a settlement of 40,000 souls sprawled across a valley-system of extreme elevation—but the density is scattered, broken, sparse. The town does not cluster; it *bridges*. Structures hang suspended between jagged points of raw geology like droplets on a spider’s web, connected by cantilevered walkways, pneumatic tubes, and cable-car lines that sag under their own weight. Buildings here are layered vertically, carved into the flanks of mountains that have not yet been worn smooth by wind or water. Instead of streets, there are gantries. Instead of squares, there are platforms bolted directly into strata that still weep magma. The construction is industrial, electrified, but the electricity behaves strangely—arcing in slow, viscous blue ropes between transformers, filling the dimness with a buzzing that contributes to the omnipresent loudness.

For it is loud here, though slowly loud. The noise does not attack; it accumulates. The great Pit-Foundries operate on geological time, their pistons rising and falling with the patience of continental drift, each impact a thunderclap separated by hours of grinding, settling, hissing. Steam vents scream in cycles that match the tidal pulls of moons you cannot see. The abundant fauna has adapted to this slowness—there are creatures here that look like moths made of glass fiber, fluttering in place for days at a time, harvesting the condensation, their wings beating so slowly they seem to float. There are tube-worms that grow up through the raw stone, their pink, fleshy bodies pulsing in time with the Compression, feeding on the heat of infant rock. Birds, or things like birds, with too many eyes, nest in the high girders, their calls subsonic, felt as pressure drops in the inner ear rather than heard.

The light is dim, a perpetual crepuscular haze. The extreme elevation should bring brilliant clarity, starlight above and cloud-seas below, but the reality breakdown—oh, the weirdness is strong here—bends photons into tired arcs. The sky is not a dome but a bowl, pressing down, filtering sunlight into a sour, violet twilight that makes shadows point in conflicting directions. Sometimes the sun rises in the north and sets in the west on the same day. Sometimes it hangs in place for weeks, a bruised disk behind layers of manufacturing smog and natural cloud that cling to these peaks like wet wool. The urban lighting—sodium vapor and arc lamps—creates pockets of harsh orange visibility, but between the structures, in the wide gaps where the sparse density leaves room for the valley to breathe, the darkness is absolute and *active*, a presence that seems to be waiting for the rock to finish forming so it can claim it.

The vegetation is lush, impossibly so for this altitude and temperature, thriving on the damp and the mineral seep. Mosses carpet the raw stone in carpets of phosphorescent emerald, fed by the condensation and the strange nutrients upwelling from the world’s unformed depths. Fungi grow in the maintenance tunnels, fruiting bodies the size of bannisters that pulse with bioluminescence in colors that have no names, feeding on the reality-breakdown as much as on organic matter. They say the mycelium here has achieved a kind of industrial consciousness, negotiating with the factory owners for the rights to process the dead.

The rules of the world are under construction here, literally. Gravity varies by location—lighter on the eastern platforms, crushingly heavy near the Deep Bore. Time runs at different speeds depending on proximity to the Compression; workers on the night shift sometimes return younger than their children. The rock itself is not entirely solid; in the quiet hours, when the great engines pause between strokes, you can hear the stone *reorganizing*, mineral lattices shifting like sleepers turning in their beds. The city is built on a foundation that is still dreaming itself into existence.

And so the inhabitants live in the interstices of creation. They are miners, yes, but they are mining the *potential* of the rock, harvesting raw reality before it sets into fixed laws. The factories process this protean material into stabilized pleroma, shipping it down-mountain to the lower cities where the world is older and more predictable. They wear heavy coats against the cool, breath masks against the damp mineral air, and they move slowly, matching the dynamic of the place, their lives paced to the grinding of the world’s slow manufacture. They know that they do not live in a place so much as in a *process*—a moment of urbanization caught between the upthrust of raw geology and the eventual entropy of erosion. For now, the rock is young, the city is sparse and loud and dim and strange, and the great engines turn, turn, turn, keeping time for a world that has not yet decided what it wants to become.
