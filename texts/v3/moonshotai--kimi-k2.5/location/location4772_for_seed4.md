It is called the Creche, though it has no name in any human tongue. You find it by accident, on hands and knees in the peat, when the cold has numbed your fingers and the dark has made your eyes useless. Your palm breaks through a crust of frozen moss into emptiness, and you tumble forward into a space that should not exist—a cavity no larger than a merchant’s wagon, yet containing a world.

The erosion that formed it happened yesterday, geologically speaking. A shelf of limestone, still sharp enough to cut, sheared away when the permafrost belched, exposing this pocket to the freezing wet air. The water is immediate: not flowing, but condensing, weeping from every surface. The cold here is ancient, stored in the stone, but the wet is new, breath-fogging, the air supersaturated with moisture that beads on every thread and surface.

And every surface is plant. Total vegetative colonization has occurred in the span it takes lichen to blink. The floor is a woven mat of hair-fine roots, white as bone, pulsing with capillary action. The walls are stacked shelves of bracket fungi, rigid as timber, arranged in fractal geometries. The ceiling—a dense braid of moss stems and frost-resistant creepers—hangs low enough to brush your forehead, heavy with condensation that drips with metronomic patience into pools below. The architecture is not built; it is grown, but grown *wrong*, grown with intent. This is a city carved by persuasion rather than axe, trained over mere seasons into avenues and chambers, into three distinct levels bridged by living vines braided so tight they creak like rope.

The builders are here. They are the fauna—overwhelming, absolute, a biomass that outstrips the vegetable substrate despite the total plant-conquest of the space. They are silent. Utterly, eerily silent. Thousands upon thousands of them, each no larger than your thumb, moving in rivers and eddies through the hollow. They have no eyes—the darkness here is permanent, a 0.1 lux void where photons go to die—but they possess long, feathery antennae that sway in currents you cannot feel, tasting the humidity gradients, reading the ripples in the dripping water.

They are strange, marked by the 1.6 weirdness coefficient of this place. Their bodies are not quite chitin, not quite flesh: translucent, gel-filled sacs that shift between geometries, now hexagonal, now serpentine, now folding into origami knots when they rest. They have too many legs—seven, always seven—arranged not in pairs but in a radial pattern that lets them cling to any surface. When they move, they do not scuttle; they flow, their legs moving in waves like millipede legs but without the whisper of exoskeleton on stone. They are mute. Their communication is chemical, pheromonal trails laid down in the air itself, visible to sensitive noses as silver threads in the blackness.

The city is theirs, and it is urban—layered, organized, infrastructurally dense despite the pocket scale. The lower level is flooded, a bath of meltwater where fungal paddies are tended. The middle level, where you crouch, is the thoroughfare: broad avenues of compressed spore-dust between root-columns, sparse in population now but capable of channeling thousands. The upper level, suspended from the moss-ceiling, is where the nursery-hangs sway—gelatinous pods that incubate the next generation, glowing faintly with bioluminescence that does not illuminate the darkness so much as texture it.

Dynamic processes surround you. This place is active, young, still forming. The limestone walls weep new minerals that the fungi precipitate into sharp, fresh stalactites. The root-floor creeps outward, inching toward the entrance you breached, hungry to seal the wound. The fauna are in migration, a seasonal shift (though seasons mean little here) that sees them carrying eggs from the upper left quadrant to the lower right, a biological tide that never stops, never sleeps. They tend their living architecture with primitive tools—needles of ice, scrapers of slate, wedges of frozen peat—pruning the roots to keep avenues open, milking the fungi for resin that they use to seal leaks in their waterworks.

The sound is mostly quiet. Your own heartbeat is loud. The drip... drip... of water provides the only percussion. Occasionally, a soft *thud* as a ripe spore-pod drops into the wet lower level, or the creak of a root bridge adjusting to new weight. The fauna make no noise. Even when they die—when one is crushed in the press of migration, or when the cold finally claims an elder—the others consume it instantly, silently, recycling the biomass with terrifying efficiency.

You are an intruder in a microcosm where the rules are simple but alien: silence is law, darkness is home, and the boundary between the built environment and the living organism has dissolved entirely. The air tastes of copper and petrichor. The cold keeps everything slowed, preserved, extending the moments between heartbeats of this hidden metropolis. It is a city, yes, but one that fits in a coffin; a civilization in a teacup; a freezing, weeping, silent rush of life overwhelming the dark.
