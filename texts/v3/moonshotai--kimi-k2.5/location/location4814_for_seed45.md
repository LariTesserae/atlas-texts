You stand at the edge of a world in its first hour. The air—or what passes for it here—is thin, dry, and carries the taste of exhausted fire. No moisture has ever condensed upon this ground; it is an anhydrous expanse, a desert not of sand but of *potential*, where hydrogen remains locked in the crystal lattices of pyroxenes and olivines that have never known weathering. The temperature presses against your skin with a subterranean insistence, a warmth that radiates upward from the stone itself rather than down from any sun. Above, the sky is not blue but a depthless, starless umbral vault—darkness that has weight, that seems to pool in the hollows of your clavicles and behind your knees. Light, here, is a rumor. Pale, sickly phosphorescence seeps from veins of skutterudite and rare-earth phosphates threading through the bedrock, casting everything in the greenish gloom of deep water, though not a drop of water exists for miles in any direction.

You are high. Extraordinarily high. The plateau extends beneath your feet at an altitude where atmospheres typically thin to vacuum, yet here the pressure remains stubborn, heated from below by the planet’s bleeding heart. This is the **Syntectic Rise**, a continental shelf being born in violent real-time, thrust upward through the crust faster than erosion can imagine it. The elevation is so extreme that looking toward the horizon gives vertigo—not because of the drop, but because the curvature of the world seems to fold upward, the edge of the plateau reaching toward itself like a mouth closing.

The terrain is *embryonic*. Nothing here is rounded; nothing has been caressed by wind or worn by rain. The geology is raw, premature, still bearing the fingerprints of its manufacture. Basalt emerges in hexagonal columns that haven't had millennia to settle into regularity—these are jagged, elbowed, crystallizing even as you watch, sweating mineral salts that frost the black glass in intricate, fungal-like dendrites. These mineral accretions spread across the stone at perceptible speeds, pure chemistry miming life, consuming thermal gradients instead of sunlight. They are not alive, but they *grow*, depositing layer after layer of cubic pyrite and octahedral magnetite in formations that look disturbingly cultivated—gardens of perfect geometry blooming in the warm dark.

Scattered across this nascent expanse are structures that appear, at first, to be ruins. A colonnade of obsidian pillars stands alone in a valley of fresh pumice, too regular to be accidental, too sharp to be ancient. A ziggurat of layered gabbro rises from a plain, its steps unmarred by dust, its angles meeting in Platonic precision. But there is no mortar, no tool-mark. These are the **Autogenesis Cairns**, mineral formations created by the turbulence itself—the dynamic here is chaotic, hyperactive, a geologic storm-state. The crust does not drift; it *convulses*. Magma injects itself into fractures that open and freeze within hours. Gas bubbles the size of cathedrals inflate beneath the surface and collapse, leaving behind vaults and arches that look designed for worship. The ground breathes. Every few minutes, the silence—profound, cotton-thick, mostly quiet—is shattered by a sound like the world tearing open: a new mountain thrusting up thirty meters to the west, shedding scoria in a clatter that echoes for minutes across the empty vastness.

Between these violent births, the quiet returns, so absolute you can hear the mineral veins ticking as they cool. The scale is overwhelming; you could walk for days across cooling flows that shift and crack underfoot, navigating around newborn cinder cones that belch dry steam into the dark, starless sky. The density of features is sparse—you might travel hours between the mineral-cathedrals, crossing plains of fresh, un-oxidized iron that shine like fresh blood in the dim phosphorescence, your solitude absolute. There are no animals. No bacteria even, not yet. The chemistry is too raw, too changeable; life requires stability, and this place is defined by its refusal to stabilize.

The weirdness seeps in slowly. You notice that the shadows cast by the phosphorescent veins do not fall predictably—they seem to predate the objects casting them, or lag behind, as if darkness here has inertia. The warm stone under your palms thrums with a frequency that resonates in your teeth, a subsonic recitation of the planet’s mantle. Time moves strangely in the Autogenesis Cairns; a formation that appears solid as you approach might be viscous, moving in a slow surge, when you touch it. The plateau is not merely being built; it is *learning*, experimenting with architectures, discarding them. Yesterday’s spire lies collapsed into tomorrow’s canyon. The scattered "built" structures are the landscape's own sketches, its attempts to remember shapes it saw in dreams of the deep earth.

This is a world before weather, after fire, in the permanent gestational dark. It is warm, empty, violent, and new—a mineral nursery where stone practices being stone, breaking its own rules, refusing to settle, waiting for water that will never come.
