The **Resounding Plateau** sits at the lip of what cartographers once called the Endless Steppe, though "steppe" suggests grass, and here nothing soft grows. At thirteen hundred meters above the coastal basins, the air should by rights be thin and alpine, but the Temperature holds at a thick, glandular **warmth** (2.0)—not the dry heat of lowlands, but a clammy, metallic warmth that seems to rise from the ground itself, as if the bedrock were running a fever. This is the first clue that you are standing on something that is not quite geology anymore.

The **scale** (2.1) is disorienting. You can see for twenty kilometers in every direction, a regional sprawl that extends past the immediate horizon in bowls and saddles of fractured limestone. Yet the **density** (1.2) is so low that you might walk for hours without encountering another breathing thing. The landscape is **urban** (2.5) in the way a fossil is biological: it retains the shape of intent, but the life has mineralized into something else. What you see are **Engines**.

They are everywhere, and they are enormous. 

These are not machines in the human sense of discrete factories with walls and roofs. The **built** environment here is *excavated* as much as constructed—layered constructions (2.5) of pitted brass and vitrified ceramic that have sunk into the karst, weathered (2.5) until the distinction between structure and stone has dissolved. The **erosion** is established, ancient, in-balance; the machines have been grinding against the plateau for so long that they have achieved a kind of geological equilibrium. Their surfaces are scored with runnels where acid rain and condensing vapor have worked for centuries, creating smooth, anatomical curves in the metal that resemble the whorls of a sea shell or the grain of wood.

Because the **water** (1.8) is everywhere, but it is not alive. It is *processing*. 

The **wetness** here is atmospheric, surgical. Warm fronts collide with the cold underbellies of the engines, which never stop bleeding heat from their internal mechanisms. The result is a constant, ghostly *weeping*. Water condenses on the flanks of the machinery in trembling sheets, gathers in the grooves of gear-teeth the size of amphitheaters, and drips in slow, mineral-heavy beads onto the calcified ground below. There are no rivers, only *trickles*—thin, rust-colored threads that find the paths of least resistance across the brass topography and pool in shallows where the water is supersaturated with copper salts and oxide. The air tastes like a battery. It is humid, but nothing green thrives. The **vegetation** (0.6) is reduced to a few desperate, translucent lichens that subsist on the trace chemicals in the condensation, and fungal crusts that bloom in the shadow of exhaust vents, feeding not on photosynthesis but on the sulfur compounds in the steam.

And then there is the **sound** (2.6). 

It is **deafening**, but it is not loud in the way of a blast or a storm. It is a *pressure*. A subsonic drone that lives in the marrow of your bones and the fluid of your eyes. It is the sound of continent-sized flywheels turning once per day. It is the groan of tectonic bearings settling by micrometers per hour. The **dynamic** (0.9) of the world here is so slowed, so patient, that the movement of the machines is nearly imperceptible—you must return to the same gear-tooth at dawn and dusk to see that it has rotated a hand’s width—but the *friction* of that movement generates a constant, overwhelming hum. It blurs vision at the edges. It makes standing water vibrate into complex, hypnotic patterns, standing waves that never settle. Communication is nearly impossible without mechanical amplification; voices are eaten by the drone. The fauna (0.8), what little there is, has adapted by abandoning hearing entirely. Things move here by feel, by vibration through the metal substrate. You might see a pale, eyeless crawler the size of a cat, its exoskeleton grown from the calcium deposits, tapping its way across a piston housing with antennae that read the harmonic frequencies of the deep structure.

The **weirdness** (1.6) is subtle but pervasive. It is in the way the water behaves. Because of the constant low-frequency vibration, droplets sometimes cling to vertical surfaces longer than gravity should allow, or roll *uphill* along inclines where the resonance creates acoustic nodes of lower pressure. It is in the **light** (1.8), which is normal daylight—neither twilit nor overbright—but which behaves strangely when filtered through the thermal haze and the vibrating air. Objects at a distance shimmer not with heat, but with *sound*, their edges fuzzing into the audible spectrum. At certain times of day, when the humidity peaks, you can see the sound: concentric rings of condensation suspended in the air, marking the paths of pressure waves like ripples in a pond frozen mid-spread.

The rules of this world are mechanical but ancient. Time is measured not in hours but in *cycles*—the compression stroke of the Great Eastern Cylinder, the refill rate of the Condensation Vats, the slow erosion of a gear tooth against the limestone bed. The plateau is a machine that is also a landscape, or a landscape that has been digested by a machine so slowly that the digestion has become indistinguishable from nature. There are settlements here—sparse, scattered (1.2), built into the shelter of valve housings and inside the hollows of rusted condenser towers. The people, if they are people, maintain the wetness, harvest the mineral precipitates, and live in the shadow of the deafening hum, communicating in touch and gesture, marking their calendars by the gradual, patient turn of the eternal engines.
