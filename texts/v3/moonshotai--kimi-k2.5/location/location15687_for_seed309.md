You descend through the calcite throat of an abandoned elevator shaft—rust-eaten cables long since replaced by braided ropes of aerial root—until the air thickens and the temperature stabilizes at exactly the point where skin forgets it is separate from atmosphere. Here, beneath the weight of above-ground geology, you enter the **Pulmonary Ward**: a single, bounded cavern roughly the size of a cathedral nave, though it breathes with a rhythm that no church ever possessed.

The space is **sparse** in the manner of a factory floor designed for the movement of heavy loads. Massive pillars rise at irregular intervals—twenty, perhaps thirty meters of vertical distance between each—toweringtrunks of lignified tissue that have replaced whatever steel support columns once held this excavation open. These are not trees in any surface sense; they are hardwood heartwood grown under impossible pressure, grain dens than iron, fluted with the architectural ambition of Gothic pillars but articulated with the bulbous joints of tuberous growth. Between them, the floor stretches in vast, open precincts paved with rubbery, living moss that compacts underfoot like industrial matting. The ceiling is lost in dimness, but you sense it lowers in vaults and groins, dripping not with stalactites but with the pendant root-tendrils of something vast and vegetative that has claimed the rock entirely.

This is a **built** environment, undeniably **urban**, though its masonry is cellulose and its mortar is sap. The layout follows a grid—you can trace the ghost of city planning in the alignment of the pillars, the avenues between them, the layered platforms that spiral up some trunks like apartment blocks. But the **total vegetation** has subsumed every distinction between infrastructure and organism. What appear to be smokestacks are hollow stems venting vapor; aqueducts are open channels of xylem lined with smooth amber resin; power cables hang in bunches, braided vines that pulse with phosphorescent lymph, conducting not electricity exactly but a bio-electrochemical potential that lights the dim space with a feverish, submarine green.

The **damp** is pervasive—condensation beading on every surface, occasional heavy drops falling from the ceiling to strike the moss with the sound of distant machinery. It is **warm**, humid as a greenhouse, the air saturated with spores and volatile organic compounds. The moisture collects in occasional, shallow pools cupped by bracket fungi the size of dinner tables, where it stagnates briefly before being absorbed or evaporated back into the respiratory cycle of the chamber.

But the defining feature—the overwhelming presence that makes the scale feel **small** despite its cathedral height—is the **fauna**.

They are everywhere. The air is thick with them, a living blizzard of chitin and wing. **Loud** does not begin to describe the acoustics: the ultrasonic whine of communication, the pneumatic hiss of spiracles, the machine-gun rattle of mandibles processing cellulose, the bass thrum of larger organisms beating against the woody pillars. These are not surface insects scaled up, but something evolved (or engineered) for this specific industrial niche—beetles with carapaces like burnished copper gears, wasps with abdomens that glow like welding torches, centipede-like maintenance crews that thread through the hollow pipes of the vascular plant-structures, clearing blockages, pruning dead growth, harvesting sap to feed the greater colony.

The **density** is sparse in architecture but overwhelming in biology. Between the great pillars, you can see clear across the chamber to the far wall, yet the air itself feels crowded. Swarms move in coordinated patterns like smoke, like factory sound-waves visualized, obscuring the dim light with their bodies. On the ground, the moss is not empty; it teems with smaller creatures, a carpet of biomechanical fauna that processes the leaf-litter, the shed chitin, the dead that fall from above. They are the **industrial** workforce, the **engines** of this place. The "factories" are their bodies—their digestive tracts processing raw biomass into refined nutrients, their secretions building the resinous walls, their very existence creating the **active** dynamic that keeps the space from stagnation.

There is a weather here, beneath the earth. Every few hours, the great pillars **piston**—expanding slightly, contracting, pumping fluids through the vascular network with a sound like distant thunder. This is the tide of the place, the heartbeat. When it happens, the swarms rise in agitation, the temperature climbs a fraction, and a fine rain of distilled water mist falls from the ceiling, collecting on the moss, feeding the cycle. The **erosion** here is not of stone wearing down, but of biological equilibrium, a weathering of form into function, established over centuries until the distinction between what was built and what grew has completely dissolved.

It is **strange** beyond the mere juxtaposition of organic and mechanical. Time moves differently here—you can see it in the way a "gear" of wood rotates slowly where a beetle colony has eaten a perfect circle in a trunk, the way windows of translucent cellulose membrane flex and dilate like eyes, adjusting to the dim phosphorescence. The rules are those of symbiosis pushed to totality: everything eats everything else, but nothing is consumed entirely. The light is dim, but it is sufficient, coming from the bioluminescent trails the insects paint in the air, from the glowing sap-lines, from the faint chemical luminescence of decomposition.

You stand in a clearing between two pillars, and you realize that this is not a ruin, not an abandoned factory reclaimed by nature, but something else entirely: a settlement that has achieved its final form. The **urban** plan remains, but it is now a **pure-plant ecosystem** where the "citizens" are the swarms, the "industry" is their metabolism, and the "architecture" is the frozen gesture of growth responding to their millionfold need. It is loud, warm, damp, and teeming—a pocket of subterranean existence where life has become indistinguishable from manufacture, and the small, bounded world breathes on, patient and overwhelming.
