The **Resonance Plain** is not old in the way forests or mountains are old. It is old in the way continental shields are old: *finished*. Four billion years of weathering have wrought here a surface so thoroughly planed that the horizon does not dip or rise; it simply draws a straight line between warm humid air and warm humid air, the curvature of the planet visible only as a faint theoretical shimmer where the sky bleeds into stone.

Elevation here is a forgotten concept. The bedrock—a dark, banded gneiss older than photosynthesis—lies exposed across ninety percent of the terrain, worn to a nub so low that water does not rush but *lounges*. What rivers existed here vanished into geological deep time; now, water exists as a sheet, a skin, a resigned omnipresence. During the late afternoon, when the temperature peaks its subtropical warmth, the atmosphere itself seems to precipitate. Moisture beads on every surface—granite weeps, gneiss sweats, and the shallow depressions between rock swirls hold puddles that have persisted for millennia, never deep enough to become lakes, never drying enough to become soil. The water table is a palpable presence, a ghost pressing up against the soles of anything that walks here, visible as a silver sheen that migrates slowly across the stone during the day, retreating into microscopic fractures at night.

Vegetation is a courtesy, not a conquest. Across this vast, open scale—regional, town-sized in its empty breadth—plants exist as isolated punctuation. Stunted cycads with trunks like stone fingers grip cracks in the gneiss, their fronds sparse and leathery, coated in algal film. Ancient whisk ferns cluster in the lee of boulders, and lichens paint the rock in maps of orange and grey, so slowly growing that their expansion is measurable only across centuries. Trees, when they appear, are solitary monarchs—perhaps a single Ghost Gum or a primitive conifer—standing sentinel in the emptiness, their root systems shallow and broad, drinking from the humidity rather than any deep water source. The density of life is low; walk for an hour and you might pass three such trees, each separated by километры of gleaming stone.

But if the plants are sparse, the fauna compensates with a ferocious, orchestral abundance. This is an amphibian planet, a world of lungs half-turned to gills and skins that drink the air. The sound here is the landscape's dominant feature—not the crash of erosion (that work ended eons ago) but the *drone*. By mid-morning, the chorus begins: marsh frogs, their bodies emerald and leopard-spotted, inflate their throats in unison, creating a bass note so constant it becomes a physical pressure against the eardrums. Trilling tree frogs add treble from the scattered cycads. Swarms of dragonflies—ancient, large as sparrows—hum in metallic clouds above the water-sheets, their wings catching the normal, diffused light that filters through the perpetual haze of evaporation. Lungfish stir in the deeper pools, rising to gulp air with wet, prehistoric sounds. The noise is loud, pervasive, and *slow*; it does not pulse or spike but maintains a steady-state thrum, shifting only with the gradual thermal tides of the day. There are no sudden storms here, no violent winds—the dynamic of the place is glacial, patient. A cloud might take three days to cross the sky. A puddle might take a decade to deepen into a pond.

The rules of this world are simple and severe: **stability is virtue, change is suspect.** Evolution here has selected for constancy. The animals that thrive are those that can sing for weeks without ceasing, that can breathe through their skin when the oxygen in the water grows thin, that can wait in the open for the rare shadow of a passing tree. Predation is lazy; predators are outnumbered by prey ten thousand to one, and hunt with the desperation of things that know food is everywhere, so why hurry? Insects buzz from cycad to cycad, following chemical trails laid down by their grandmothers. The water sheets move across the plain at the speed of continental drift, drawn by imperceptible gradients toward the distant, lower-low of some final vanishing point.

At dusk, the temperature drops fractionally, and the condensation intensifies. Fog rises from the warm rock, creating layers of visibility: the immediate ground clear, the middle distance veiled, the horizon nonexistent. The sound changes pitch—night frogs take the bass line, and the drone drops an octave, becoming a subterranean vibration felt in the chest. The ancient gneiss, still warm from the day, continues to weep moisture into the dark.

There is nothing built here, nothing made. Not because the place is virgin, but because it is *satiated*. It has achieved a form so final that to add a structure would be redundant, and to subtract from it would require energy the world no longer possesses. It is the last landscape, the one that remains when all mountains have been worn away and all seas have soaked into the stone. It breathes slowly, sings loudly, and waits, as it has waited since the crust first cooled, for nothing in particular.
