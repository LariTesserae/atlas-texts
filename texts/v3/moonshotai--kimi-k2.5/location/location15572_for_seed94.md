The **Resin Highlands** are not mountains in the traditional sense, though they sit two kilometers above the surrounding basins, where atmospheric pressure drops to a thin whisper. Nor are they empty, windswept places. They are, instead, a single, continental organism of packed matter—three hundred thousand square kilometers of surface where the gap between "soil" and "sky" has been so thoroughly colonized that the distinction has become academic.

To stand here is to stand inside a lung.

The elevation (2.4) keeps the temperature pinned between four and eight degrees Celsius (0.6), a perpetual coolness that should, by rights, slow biological ambition. Yet the density (2.6) defies this chill. There is no horizon of bare rock, no meadows, no gaps. The terrain rises in steps, each step a terrace of compressed peat and root-mat thirty meters thick, and each terrace is stacked upon the last like the layers of a compressed sponge. You cannot see the geological skeleton beneath; it has been wrapped, padded, and entirely hidden by a living insulation that has achieved industrial efficiency (1.6) without ever having been built.

The "industry" here is entirely grown. For three million years, the vegetation has evolved to mine. Specialized xylem structures—hollow, lignin-reinforced tubes that look distressingly like factory piping—extract rare earths from the granitic bedrock below. These minerals are electroplated along the inner walls of the tubes by bacterial colonies, building conductive veins of near-pure copper and cadmium that run from root to canopy. The trees do not grow wood so much as they grow circuitry. When lightning strikes the high points—and it does, frequently, in this charged altitude—the current does not split bark but travels down these mineral pathways, powering chemical factories in the root nodules. Nitrogen is fixed at industrial scales. Silicates are smelted into glassy nodules that serve as structural support. The forest hums with low-voltage current, a galvanic heartbeat, but it does so in absolute silence (0.5).

The sound is swallowed by the water (2.1). Not rivers—there are few open channels—but a system of atmospheric distillation. The Highlands exist in a permanent cloud cap. Moist air from the lower basins hits the escarpment and condenses, but instead of falling as rain, the water adheres to the nanoscopic trichomes of the moss-carpet. It weeps from leaf to leaf, following capillary paths down through the strata, never falling more than a centimeter at a time. The result is a landscape of weeping stone where the stone is buried under meters of absorbent flesh. Everything is wet: not flooded, but *saturated*. The air itself is a suspension of micro-droplets, a cool, dense fog that blurs vision beyond twenty meters and conducts the electrical whispers of the great vegetable machine.

Fauna (2.0) is abundant but invisible to the ear. The density allows for vertical specialization. In the lower root-mat, blind lithovores the size of badgers mine the mineral veins, their teeth adapted to scrape pure copper, digesting the metal to plate their own exoskeletons. They move with slow, hydraulic grace, their joints cushioned by gel sacs that muffle all sound. Higher up, in the hollows of the "factory-trees," colonies of bat-like creatures roost, but they do not echolocate—they sense the electric fields of the vegetation, navigating by the voltage gradients. They are silent, their wings membranous and soft as wet leather. Predators exist: large, low-slung mustelids with fur that acts as electrical shielding, hunting by detecting the galvanic signatures of their prey. But they kill with swift, hydraulic precision—no cries, no struggle, just the soft wet snap of a neck and the absorption of the body into the mulch.

The scale (2.7) generates its own weather patterns. Storm cells born over the Highlands do not drift away; they circulate, caught in the thermal weirdness of this elevated, breathing mass. Fog banks roll across the terraces with the speed of ocean currents, carrying spores and electrostatic charge. The erosion (2.0) is in perfect balance with this activity. Material is worn down by acid secretions from the vegetation at exactly the rate it is built up by biomineralization. The landscape is ancient, settled into a steady-state that has persisted since the last ice age retreated. It does not change, yet it is dynamic (1.7)—a continuous, silent churn of processing, refining, growing, dying, and being chemically digested on the spot.

There is an uncanny quality to it (weirdness 1.2)—the sense that one is walking through the intestines of something vast. The tubular roots carry not sap but slurries of dissolved granite. The leaves, broad and dark, are not green but a bruised, metallic violet, optimized for capturing infrared as much as light (2.0). When wind does manage to thread through the upper canopy, it produces no rustle; the leaves are thick and waxy, designed to funnel water downward, and they move against each other with the slippery, silent motion of wet rubber.

The light (2.0) is diffuse, gray-green, filtered through the mist and the metabolic fog. Shadows do not fall sharply here; they bleed into the moisture. Days are long twilights. Nights are absolute black, broken only by the faint, bioluminescent glow of warning patches on poisonous fungi—soft blue points in the murk, like indicator lights on dormant machinery.

To traverse the Highlands is to become part of the processing. There are no trails; the fauna do not need them, moving through tunnels in the root-mat or gliding between trees. A human attempting to walk here would find the vegetation packed too tight to penetrate, yielding like rubber, springing back, absorbing footsteps. The only way through is to follow the "waste channels"—dry (relatively) arroyos where the vegetation excretes pure silica sand, the indigestible dross of its geological mining. These white ribbons wind through the violet-green mass, silent conveyor belts of glass dust, leading nowhere in particular, simply moving excess to the edges.

Nothing here has been made. Everything has been grown, optimized, tuned to a productive hum that requires no voice. It is a civilization of chemistry and voltage, vast enough to be a continent, dense enough to have no inside or outside, cool and wet and infinitely, silently busy.
