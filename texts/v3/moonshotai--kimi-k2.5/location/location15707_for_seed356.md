**The Lignin Works**

You cannot see it. The darkness here is absolute—not the darkness of closed eyes, but of vast depths, of ink suspended in ink, of a lightless continent under a lightless sky. Yet it is warm. The heat presses against your skin like a held breath, humid and dense, a greenhouse effect generated not by any sun but by the metabolic fever of the matter itself. This is a place of thermal equilibrium, where the temperature has stabilized at precisely blood-warmth across an area the size of a small moon, and has remained so, unchanged, since the moment of emergence.

You would know, if you moved through it, that the ground is not ground. There is no mineral substrate here, no stone, no soil. The surface beneath your feet—and it extends downward for miles—is entirely vegetable: compressed, stratified, living and dead root-matter knitted into a fabric dense as meat. It gives slightly under pressure, exhaling moisture. The *total vegetation* parameter does not mean trees in the conventional sense. It means that everything, from the microscopic to the continental, is plant. Architecture is leaf. Infrastructure is stem. The substrate breathes, slowly, in a rhythm that has not varied in epochs.

The topography is low, a shallow bowl pressed into the fabric of reality, but the scale is vast enough that curvature is not perceptible. You could walk for years and the elevation would not rise. The horizon, if you could see it, would be a wall of vertical integration.

Despite the darkness, the forms are crisp. The erosion parameter insists on youth—these features are newly birthed, sharp-edged, unrounded by time. Vascular structures cut the air like fresh blade wounds. Tubes of xylem, wide enough to crawl through, rise at perfect ninety-degree angles, their surfaces slick with condensation. They have not been worn smooth; they emerged this way, yesterday in geological terms, and will remain this way forever. The dynamic stasis means nothing weathers. Nothing accretes. The water droplets that bead on every surface—condensing from the supersaturated warm air—grow fat and fall, always at the same rate, in the same places, following trajectories calculated to the micron, a rainfall as predictable as clockwork.

The silence is profound. With no fauna, there are no calls, no skittering, no wingbeats. The vegetation itself makes no sound because it does not move—or rather, it moves only in that it is eternally mid-process. This is the resolution of the industrial paradox. The tech level is high, mechanized, electrified in a biological sense, but there are no *built* structures, no artifacts of hands or minds. Instead, the plants have arranged themselves into factory architecture. Photosynthetic refinery towers loom, their cellular structures organized like assembly lines. Massive pistons—wooden cylinders sheathed in bark hard as ceramic—hang frozen mid-stroke within cylinders of pith. Conveyor systems of braided cambium transport packets of refined sap from vat to vat, the fluid suspended in the act of transit, never progressing, never retreating. It is industry crystallized into a single, eternal moment of operation.

You would feel, if you touched them, that these pistons are warm to the touch, vibrating with potential energy that never translates to motion. The "factories" process something—perhaps light that no longer exists, perhaps heat, perhaps time itself—extracting, refining, packaging the void into dense cubes of cellulose that accumulate in silos of woven root. The machinery is dense, layered, three-dimensional. You cannot walk between the structures; you must climb over and through them, pressing against warm, damp surfaces that feel like skin over machinery. Every cubic meter is saturated with presence. There is no negative space.

The weirdness lies in the implication: this is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is *running*, caught in a temporal stutter, a production line that manufactures the same second over and over. The young erosion means it looks like it was built last night—fresh sap still beading at the cut sites, edges sharp enough to cut, no dust, no decay. Yet it has been here forever, will be here forever, a warm, wet, silent continent of frozen manufacture, breathing heat into the void, dripping water into the eternal night, sharp and new and ancient and unchanged.

If you listen carefully, in the absolute silence, you might hear the plink of water striking water, identical to the drop that fell before it, identical to the one that will fall after, a metronome marking time in a place where time has stopped.
