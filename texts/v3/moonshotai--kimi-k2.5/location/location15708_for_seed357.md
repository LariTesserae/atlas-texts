You arrive at the rim and the wind hits you wet and hot, like the breath of something feverish. Below stretches the Caldera of Unfinished Canton, a high bowl freshly punched through the crust—perhaps last year, perhaps this morning; the rock is that sharp, that unweathered. The basalt knives upward in black serrations, obsidian still glinting in seams, and everything is bleeding water. Not raining, precisely, though the air is saturated to the point of precipitation—the moisture simply exists, condensing on every surface, running in rivulets down the fresh-cut stone, pooling in the cracks before evaporating again in the geothermal heat. Steam rises from ten thousand micro-vents. The elevation makes the sky thin and bruised-purple, but the light is ordinary, terrestrial, mid-morning clarity filtered through shifting curtains of vapor.

This is a city. You must understand that first, or you will mistake it for a forest.

But it is a city without masons, a metropolis of cellulose and lignin secreted by organisms that have no brains, only blueprints written in RNA. The **Built** parameter here is total—every space is constructed, chambered,roofed, and buttressed—yet there is not a single tool, nail, or fired brick. The **Vegetation** is the substrate; the ground itself is a mat of compressed root-hairs and fungal weave, but sparse, so sparse—the density is low, creating vast plazas of acidic hot springs and travertine terraces where nothing stands for hundreds of meters, then sudden, shocking **architectures**.

They rise like coral cathedrals extruded in fast-forward. Young, yes—the erosion is minimal, the features sharp—but the growth is younger still. A tower might begin as a nodule at dawn and by dusk have extended twenty meters skyward, its walls already honeycombed with room-like hollows, its floors already buckling under its own weight as the base lignifies and the top still softens, weeping sap. The dynamic is **turbulent**, **hyperactive**, a **Solaris-like** frenzy of creation and destruction. You can hear it—the **sound** is loud, a constant orchestral shifting: the *crack* of fibers snapping under torsional stress, the *hiss* of steam escaping through porous xylem tubes, the *groan* of massive trunks expanding in the heat, the *slap* of heavy rubbery leaves unfurling and striking their neighbors. It is the noise of a construction site where the scaffolding is alive and complaining.

The structures are white, or pale green, or the color of old bone. They resemble pagodas, apartment blocks, amphitheaters—architectural forms generated by genetic algorithms rather than intent. Hollow tubes cross-girded by flying buttresses of fungal leather. Vaulted ceilings of leaf-mosaic that breathe, expanding and contracting with humidity. Staircases that spiral up into nothing, leading to platforms that crumble before they are ever used. Because there are no **fauna** here. Zero. No insects to pollinate, no birds to nest, no microbes even—the water is too hot, too acidic, or perhaps the vegetation produces natural biocides. The city is sterile, a biological machine building habitation for inhabitants that never arrived, or that died out eons ago and left their architectural preferences encoded in the plant-genome like ghosts.

You walk between the spires across the **sparse** distances. The scale is open, **regional**; you could wander for hours from district to district. One zone is a forest of columns, each a single hollow stem the width of a redwood, spaced fifty meters apart, rising from pools of bubbling silica-water. Another is a labyrinth of walls—cellular barriers extruded by sheet-fungi, creating alleyways that dead-end or loop back on themselves, filled with the sound of dripping and the smell of cinnamon and sulfur.

The **weirdness** is in the behavior. The plants are not merely growing; they are *competing to build*. Species of strangling vine that don’t climb but construct—they weave themselves into tensegrity domes, then die, leaving behind woven skeletal domes that stand for days before rot takes them. Succulents that balloon with pressurized sap until they burst, spraying seeds against the walls of nearby towers, the impact sounding like gunshots. Thermotropic moss that creeps across the basalt in visible waves, advancing and retreating based on the minute-by-minute shifting of steam shadows.

It is **hot**—28, 32 degrees Celsius even at this elevation—and the wetness is total. Your clothes never dry. The rocks weep. The architecture weeps. Condensation runs down the cathedral walls in streams, collecting in basins that overflow, creating temporary waterfalls that vanish when the sun strikes and the plant-walls greedily reabsorb the moisture through their bark.

There are rules here, though no lawgiver. The first rule is **flux**. Nothing you see will exist in an hour. Everything is built around absence—the plants create negative space, chambers, rooms, as if trying to summon something to fill them, some creature that needs shelter, some god that needs a temple. The third rule is **isolation**. Despite the total vegetative coverage, despite the fact that every surface is alive, the density of presence is low. You are alone in a crowded room. The plants do not touch each other gently; they war at the margins, leaving dead zones of acidic dust between territories.

At the center of the caldera, if you dare to walk that far, lies the Growth Nexus—a structure so large it creates its own weather. It is a tower, or a tree, or a castle, or a thermal vent wrapped in woody intention. It pulses. You can feel it through your feet: the throb of hydraulic pressure pushing sap up hundreds of meters, the seismic shift of new buttresses punching out from the main trunk, the subsonic rumble of roots breaking fresh bedrock (young erosion meeting young growth, both violent). It is loud here, deafening—a white noise of biological industry, of a world building itself and eating itself simultaneously in the hot, wet, empty air.

You could scream here. The plants would absorb the sound into their porous chambers. No echo. No answer. Just the wet heat, the sharp young rocks, and the endless, empty architecture of the growing.
