You descend through permanent twilight into a basin that breathes. The air is cool, heavy with mineral damp—never rain, exactly, but a constant exhalation of condensation that beads on every surface, runs in silver threads down walls that have never known sunlight. This is the **Hollowworks**, a regional valley where the distinction between bedrock and architecture has not yet been born. The erosion here runs backward: stone emerges raw from the dark, embryonic, soft as unfired clay, and hardens only as the moisture evaporates into the chill air.

There are no plants. Nothing green has ever rooted here. Instead, the valley floor and walls display a dense saturation of half-formed structures packed so tightly they constitute the only terrain—no ground exists between them, only the constructed matter itself, layered like the cross-section of a termite metropolis magnified to the scale of a town. You navigate through corridors that are simultaneously tunnels and building foundations, chambers that are both quarries and cathedrals. Everything is handmade. The marks are visible everywhere: the broad, shallow scars of antler picks, the precise concussions of stone hammers, the dragged grooves of bone scrapers. Primitive tech, yes—chert, obsidian, green copper—but deployed with such relentless, geological patience that the entire valley has become a single artifact.

The sound is tremendous. In the darkness, the valley is never silent. The soft stone groans as it cures, shrinking microscopically, setting up stresses that crack like distant thunder. Collapse is frequent—temporary scaffolding of woven root and sinew (the only organic material here, imported and desiccated) gives way, sending tons of semi-hardened material avalanching into lower chambers. Water drips from the ceiling, not from aquifers but from the atmospheric moisture condensing on the cool stone, each drop striking resonant bowls carved into the floors, creating a percussive drone that never ceases. And beneath it all, the working.

The fauna is rare—perhaps one creature per acre—but they are the builders. Large, pale, moving with the slowness of glaciers through the dense architecture. You hear them more than see them: the scrape of their tools, the wet exhalations as they press their bodies against the soft stone to shape it by pressure and friction. They are the agents of the dynamic, the ones maintaining the emergence. As fast as the valley hardens, they excavate new chambers, keeping the stone in its vulnerable, fetal state, preventing the world from setting into permanence. They work by touch in the absolute dark, navigating by the loud reverberations of their own tapping, which returns to them as sonar through the damp, dense air.

Weirdness seeps from the geometry. The architecture follows rules of growth rather than engineering—spiraling ramparts that resemble the interior of shells, bulbous chambers connected by throat-like corridors, all clearly carved yet appearing to have accreted like crystals or fungal masses. The stone itself is strange: when wet, it possesses the translucency of alabaster, glowing faintly with any phosphorescence; as it dries, it opaques into basaltic black. The valley is therefore a map of temporal gradients—wet, soft, glowing formations at the heights where the fog is thickest; dry, ringing, obsidian-hard vaults below where the air settles and the weight of the dense over-structure presses the moisture out.

You walk on a floor that is the roof of another chamber, which is the wall of another. The density is absolute—three-dimensional, claustrophobic, yet the scale extends for miles, a regional maze. Occasionally, you break through into "courtyards"—spaces the size of village squares where the construction has paused, leaving vertical wells of darkness above, the walls honeycombed with ten thousand niches, each holding a single drop of water that reflects nothing, gleaming like a manufactured eye.

Here, the world is not eroding; it is being born in reverse, carved from the inside out by primitive hands that have never known metal, working in loud, wet darkness to keep the valley forever in its architectural infancy, a place that is all construction and no completion, dense with the labor of becoming.
