To descend into the **Pool of Ridges** is to enter a mouth that has only recently learned to chew. The crater itself yawned open a scant century ago—some subterranean chamber collapsed, or perhaps the earth simply sighed—and since that day, mineral-charged water has been rising to meet the air, depositing its burden in frantic haste. What results is not soil but architecture: a riot of sharp travertine blades, opaline shelves, and silica curtains that grow visibly year to year, hardening from cream to bone-white as they age. The erosion here is *young*; nothing has been worn smooth. Every surface is knapped, fluted, urgent with newness.

The air tastes of wet stone and electricity. The temperature hovers in the perpetual cool of evaporation—never freezing, but always the chill of a cheek pressed against glass. Water is not a destination here but a matrix: it seeps from every pore, trickles down every face, pools in cupped palms of stone before vanishing again into the calcite lattice below. The ground breathes steam. The rim of the crater is low, barely elevated above the surrounding fen, yet within the bowl the world drops into a dense verticality.

**The Settlement** fills the space with a saturation that defies breathing. There is no “ground” in the traditional sense, only a three-dimensional puzzle of habitation wedged between formations. Using tools of knapped chert, antler, and soft native copper, the inhabitants have not built *over* the terrain but *into* it, exploiting the brief window when the stone is still soft enough to score. They drive pegs of petrified wood into fresh terraces; they stretch gut and woven reed between stalactites that did not exist twenty years ago. Walkways are cantilevered nets; rooms are hollowed behind flowstone drapes; storage jars hang like fruit from mineral branches. Privacy is a fiction—every window faces another window three arm-lengths away. You sleep within earshot of your neighbor’s dreams, your back pressed against a wall that will thicken by a finger’s width before winter.

Vegetation scarcely takes hold. The water is too alkaline, the surfaces too fresh and slick. Only a few pale lithophytic mosses colonize the northern faces, and the occasional stunted reed rises from a catch-basin, its roots already being entombed in accreting silica. Fauna is similarly scarce—a few species of blind moth, some isopods that graze on algal films, and the occasional *Skerren*, a goat-sure mammal with calcified joints that permit it to leap from blade to blade without piercing its pads. But mostly, the ecology is mineral. Sound is swallowed. The porous stone drinks voices. Conversations occur in murmurs, in the soft clink of stone tools against freshening surfaces. The dominant music is the drip—a thousand points of saturation falling onto resonant floors, each with a distinct pitch depending on the hollowness beneath. It is a quiet of accumulation, not absence.

**The Weirdness** lies in the water’s memory. The fluid here is saturated not just with lime but with microscopic silicate structures that align to magnetic fields. A pool left undisturbed for a day will record the shadow of a passerby in its surface tension; if you pour it out, the pattern remains in the film, a ghost-image that persists for hours. The settlers read these films like tea leaves, discerning intentions and futures in the silicate fossils of yesterday’s movements. They have learned to fear still water; mirrors are broken immediately, lest they trap too much of a soul. The stone itself, when freshly deposited, retains a faint phosphorescence—blue-green glows that map the hidden waterways beneath the village, shifting as the plumbing of the earth rearranges itself.

There are rules to living here. You must shave your walls quarterly, scraping away the new growth before it seals your door. You must never obstruct a main seep, for the water will find another path, and that path might be through your chest while you sleep. You must accept that your home will outlast you in a matter of decades, that your corpse will likely be sealed into the architecture—calcified, roofed over, becoming the floor of someone else’s loft in the generation to come. Death is not burial here but *enlargement*. The village grows by digesting its own history, layer by accretive layer, a compact, humid, dripping urban organism thriving in the hollow of a geology that has not yet learned to be old.
