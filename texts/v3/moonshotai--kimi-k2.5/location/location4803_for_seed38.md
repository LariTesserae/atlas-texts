The **Phytoplano** rises three kilometers above the surrounding lithosphere, but it is not a mountain. It is a blister—a continental scab of organic matter that has accumulated, compressed, and lifted itself over four hundred million years, until the bedrock beneath it is no longer stone but indigestible mineral residue, a thin shell floating on cellulose. Here, erosion has completed its work. The surface is not rugged but unsettlingly *planar*, worn smooth by rain that has fallen since before the evolution of flowering plants, leaving a tabletop kingdom the size of a subcontinent, uniformly warm, uniformly dim, uniformly wet.

You do not walk upon soil. You walk upon *depth*. The vegetation is not merely cover; it is the architecture, the geology, the substrate. The ground is a mattress of collapsed forest—generations of canopy upon canopy, compressed into a spongy, fibrous peat that extends two kilometers downward before reaching anything inorganic. It breathes. When you press your weight upon it, clear amber fluid squeezes between your toes, not groundwater exactly but the accumulated lymph of a billion root systems, pressurized by the weight of everything above. The water rating is high not because of rivers or lakes—though those exist, meandering in channels lined with living wood—but because the air itself is saturated, condensing on every surface, weeping from every pore. The plateau sweats. Mist rises from the warmth trapped in the organic mass, creating a perpetual cloud ceiling that diffuses the sun into a gray-green twilight, a dimness that has persisted so long that the plants here have abandoned chlorophyll in favor of chemosynthesis, digesting the heat of the deep biomass and the faint volcanic breath seeping through the ancient bedrock below.

It is dense here, but not claustrophobic. The space is layered like a collapsed cathedral. Above, the canopy is not a single ceiling but a topography of its own—hollows and vaults formed by the interlocking trunks of sequoia-like organisms that grow not up but *across*, knitting the sky shut with lateral branches that fuse upon contact, creating load-bearing arches that span valleys. Between these wooden buttresses hang curtains of aerial roots, dripping with condensation, tangled with flowering parasites that glow with pale bioluminescence to compensate for the lack of direct light. Below, in the understory, the air is thick with spores and pollen that drift like snow, accumulating in drifts that themselves sprout, creating secondary floors, tertiary canopies, a fractal nesting of habitats where every cubic meter contains six distinct ecological niches stacked atop one another.

The fauna has adapted to the quiet. Sound dies here, absorbed by the spongy walls, muffled by the humidity. There is no birdsong, no insect buzz in the human register. Instead, there are the *soft ones*—creatures of gel and membrane, evolved in the silence and the dimness. Translucent slugs the size of dogs glide along branches, leaving trails of phosphorescent mucus. colonies of vibration-sensitive manta-like creatures filter-feed on airborne plankton, their wingspans vast but their mass negligible, moving through the mist with the grace of underwater flight. They communicate through infrasound that rumbles in the chest rather than the ear, or through chemical signatures released in puffs of pheromones. When they die, they fall softly into the absorbent peat, adding to the substrate, recycling themselves into the living floor.

Weirdness permeates the physics of the place. Time moves differently here, or perhaps it is merely that the inhabitants perceive it so. The plateau is in a state of slow, deliberate collapse—its edges crumbling at the rate of a meter per century, the entire mass gradually compacting under its own weight, burning its organic matter in slow, subterranean combustion that keeps the core warm. The plants have evolved to exploit this entropy. They grow *downward*, sending roots not to seek water—water is everywhere—but to probe the heat of compression, tapping into the friction of the settling mass. Some trees grow inverted, their crowns thrusting into the warming depths while their roots dangle into the air like chandeliers. Water flows in spirals here, diverted not by gravity alone but by the capillary action of the living sponge, moving uphill in places, pooling in mid-air where root-nets have formed impromptu aquifers.

Scattered across this vast, soft plain are the *remains*. Not cities, exactly, but calcifications—places where the organic matter, millions of years ago, grew too dense, compressed too tightly, and mineralized into bone-white structures that poke through the peat like broken teeth. They are the skeletons of ancient hyper-forests, their lignin turned to stone, honeycombed with tunnels where creatures once nested. Now they serve as lithic islands in the sea of green, covered in lichens that are themselves migrating ecosystems. On one, a structure stands that might have been a tower, grown rather than built, a hollow tube of petrified heartwood fifty meters across, now home to a colony of silent, eyeless primates that groom each other in the perpetual dusk, their fur long and trailing like moss.

It is old here. Not merely aged, but *settled*. The plateau has reached its final form; it has eroded not into dust but into this maximum density of life, a stable-state ecosystem where decay and growth have achieved perfect equilibrium. Nothing changes quickly. Storms, when they come, are slow-pressure systems that take weeks to pass, releasing warm rain that falls for months without pause, the water table rising to meet the sky until the boundary between air and liquid blurs. The plateau drinks, swells slightly, and exhales mist.

To stand here is to stand inside a lung. The atmosphere is heavy, sweet with rot and nectar, pressing against the skin with the weight of all that growing, dying, compressing, persisting. It is a place where the distinction between landscape and organism has dissolved entirely, where the world is one vast, breathing, weeping plant, ancient beyond reckoning, vast beyond crossing, quietly digesting the light and the dark in equal measure, millimeter by patient millimeter, forever.
