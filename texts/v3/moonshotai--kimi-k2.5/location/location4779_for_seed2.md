The Octant sits at the nadir, four kilometers below the artificial datum plane, where the continental foundation flattens into an absolute basalt of pressed ferrocrete and directionally-solidified alloy. This is the Floor: not stone, not silt, but a single, unbroken construction surface poured ninety-three years ago during the primary habitation phase. The material is still young. Its edges have not yet been planed smooth by current or settlement; the expansion joints remain knife-sharp, the titanium stress-ribs still gleam with the violet iridescence of fresh anodization, and the ceramite tiling shows none of the barnacle freckling or calcium rosettes that will, in a millennium, soften its geometry. When you drag a hand along the substrate, you come away with no dust, only the faint tack of polymer sealants still off-gassing their curing vapors into the cool water.

Above you, the water column rises in a cathedral of impossible scale. This is not an ocean pressed against natural rock; it is a contained and constructed void, a submersed architecture so vast that it generates its own microclimate. The Octant alone covers the surface area of a small continent, yet it is hollow—functionally empty. The density of matter here is vanishingly low. You can swim for hours and encounter only the crystalline coolness of the water itself, maintained at a constant four degrees Celsius by the thermal inertia of the surrounding walls. Visibility extends for kilometers through the sterile, stirred clarity, limited not by turbidity but by the Rayleigh scattering of the medium itself. When the floods activate (and they are always on), the light shafts descend like solid pillars of pale jade, illuminating nothing but water and the distant, hazy suggestion of the opposite wall.

The illumination is prosaic, industrial. Sodium-vapor arc lanterns hang from the ceiling grid—three kilometers overhead—spaced every eight hundred meters in a perfect Cartesian lattice. They burn with a steady, unwavering 2,400-lumen output that banishes the abyssal dark one expects at such depths. Under their glare, the environment reveals itself as a factory floor that happens to be filled with liquid. The light catches the silver flanks of the *residents*.

Fauna here is abundant, adaptive, and fast. Schools of filter-feeding chordates—translucent, ribbed things with mechanical-looking gill-rakers—move in coordinated weather systems, numbering in the billions but occupying less than a percent of the available volume. They feed on the sparse vegetation: engineered algal films that clutch only the north-facing thermal ridges where the substrate sheds its artificial chill. These rust-orange mats cover perhaps one wall in fifty, thin as paint, sustained by the mineral seep of fresh concrete rather than photosynthesis. Higher up the trophic ladder, armored crustaceans pick their way along the expansion trenches, their shells clicking against the unweathered ceramic with a sound like typing. Jellyfish, bloomed from dormant spores in the construction water, drift through the floodlit emptiness like discarded translucent bags, pulsing in time to currents generated not by tide but by machinery.

For this place is loud. The soundscape is a constant, active drone of industrial metabolism. At this depth, below the simulated surface, the hydrostatic pressure is maintained not by passive weight but by active process. Vast reciprocating pumps—cathedral engines in their own right—throb at the Octant’s corners, shunting water through desalination loops and thermal regulation vents. Their bass notes travel through the liquid as physical pressure waves, a 22-hertz thrum that resonates in your sternum. Over this, the higher harmonics: the hiss of pressure-relief valves cycling to compensate for the structure’s diurnal thermal breathing, the clank of inspection submersibles docking at maintenance nodes, the ultrasonic ping of structural integrity sensors mapping the young, sharp features for micro-fractures.

The ecology is in motion, dynamic, establishing itself in real-time. Predatory fish—sleek, bio-engineered mackerel analogues—hunt in the illuminated void, their schooling patterns disrupted only by the scheduled current reversals that sweep the floor every six hours to prevent particulate settlement. The sparse vegetation releases spores on these artificial tides; the abundant fauna swarms to consume them; the machinery grinds on, monitoring, adjusting, ensuring that erosion remains suspended at year zero, keeping the sharp edges sharp and the vast, water-filled emptiness pristine.

And it is empty. Despite the continental scale, despite the thunder of industry, you can swim through the central atrium—a space large enough to swallow mountain ranges—and feel the psychological weight of the low density. The walls are too far apart to see simultaneously. The surface above is a theoretical construct, a geometry rather than a felt presence. You are alone with the clarity of the water, the newness of the materials, the hum of the pumps, and the silver clouds of fish that turn as one body, filling the void with the only movement in a place that is otherwise still, sharp, young, and waiting.
