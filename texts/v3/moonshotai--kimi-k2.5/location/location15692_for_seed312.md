You do not approach the city so much as discover that you have been within it for miles. The **Kharak Plateau**—a continental shelf shoved skyward only ten thousand years ago, still flexing and settling like an animal disturbed from sleep—rises so gradually from the outer scree fields that its urban density creeps upon you. One moment you are navigating a field of frost-shattered gneiss, the next you realize the boulders are not boulders but the outermost ruins of **Ashkar**, the Vertical Commonwealth.

Here, "high" means something different. The plateau floor sits at an altitude where atmosphere thins to a glassy blue, yet the dampness (0.6) persists in the form of **rime ice** and **breath-fog**. Warm, wet air from the distant lowlands pushes up against this tectonic wall, condensing against the young, sharp stone (erosion 1.4). The result is a perpetual, silent drizzle of supercooled droplets that freeze upon contact, coating the jagged peaks in mantles of clear ice that grow outward like fungal blooms. Nothing here is rounded. The mountains are infants, their faces unweathered, fractures still bleeding pale mineral dust when the freeze-thaw cycles lever them apart.

The city fills the voids between these fresh eruptions of rock. Ashkar is not built *atop* the plateau so much as *infused* into its fractures. With technology limited to stone hammers, obsidian chisels, bone levers, and cordage of twisted mammoth hair (tech 0.9), the inhabitants cannot reshape the stone so much as negotiate with it. They have expanded natural faults into **galleries**, stacked them twenty or thirty levels high, bridged them with slabs of ice-reinforced slate. The density (2.0) is absolute: in the inhabited canyons, you could stretch your arms and touch the dwelling walls of three different households simultaneously. Families live in crevices barely wider than their shoulders, separated by membranes of oiled leather cured with frost-lichen. The air smells of dense mammalian warmth—thousands of bodies breathing, the accumulated heat of **abundant fauna** (2.5) packed into stone wombs.

For vegetation is almost absent (1.1). Only the hardiest crustose lichens map the outer cliffs in maps of rust and sulfur. Yet the city teems with life. The Ashkari keep **ice-oxen**, shaggy creatures that graze on the sparse lichens and store fat in humps like camels. They live in the lower galleries, their body heat rising through floor-grates to warm the sleeping lofts above. **Glacier-fleas**—actually a species of wingless midge—are farmed in meltwater pools kept at the freezing point, providing protein. Most numerous are the **stone-swifts**, birds that nest by the millions in the porous young rock, their guano harvested for fertilizer and the ammonia used to tan hides. The city is loud with life but **quiet** (0.9) in its acoustics; the snow absorbs sound, the stone swallows it. You hear your own heartbeat, the wet crunch of ice underfoot, the distant, muffled thunder of a new fault opening in the range's slow (dynamic 1.3) growing pains.

Normal light (2.1) here has a quality of surgical clarity. At this elevation, with this little humidity in the upper air, shadows are absolute and highlights are blinding. The sun, when it breaches the peaks, illuminates the frost-terraces in a way that makes the ice seem to glow from within, each crystal a lantern. But the weather is continental—**vast** (scale 2.9) enough to generate its own systems. Storms born over the plateau can persist for weeks, circling the same thermal updrafts, dropping snow that sublimates before it reaches the ground, leaving only the taste of static electricity and cold metal on the tongue.

The rhythm of the place is glacial, in the true sense. A new gallery takes three generations to carve. A family might spend a year moving a single boulder to create a retaining wall. The young mountains shift constantly; buildings are designed to shear, to flex, to be abandoned when the rock beneath them decides to settle another inch. The Ashkari measure time not in hours but in **frost-cycles**—the slow accumulation of rime on the northern faces, the patient growth of ice-wedged architecture. 

There is no metal here. Tools are knapped from the basalt that erupts fresh and glassy from the new-formed faults. Water is conserved in the form of **ice-cisterns**, carved into cliffs, filled by the condensation that drips eternally from the warmer, breathing interior of the city into the freezing outer air. It is a place of **intermittent moisture**—never quite dry enough to be a desert, never quite wet enough to flow as rivers. Water exists as suspension, as potential, as the breath exhaled from ten thousand lungs condensing into the architecture itself.

To stand on the outermost precipice is to understand the rules: this is a settlement built in the moment of geological trauma, a civilization choosing to occupy the sharp edge of the world’s slowest, most patient violence. The mountains are young and still growing; the city is dense and static; and between the two, life persists in the damp, cold, stone-heavy quiet, waiting for the next crack to appear.
