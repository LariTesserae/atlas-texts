**The Basalt Terrace of Unmoving Water**

You approach from below, climbing through cloud banks that never storm, never dissipate, simply *hang* in their appointed postures like draped gauze. The ascent is steep—this is young country, geology still flexing from its own recent birth. The plateau thrust upward only yesterday, in the measure of stones, and it has kept every jagged edge of that violence. The rock here is black obsidian and rough porous basalt, unsoftened by time’s patience. Edges remain edges. Cracks are hairlines that have not widened. The stone cuts if you touch it carelessly, bright and fresh as a knife just drawn from the forge.

Then you reach the top, and the air opens into a regional vastness—a town-sized expanse of irregular terraces and shallow bowls stretching across the high elevation. The scale is open, yawning. You can see across the entire settlement from one edge to the other, sightlines running unbroken for miles across stone plazas and low-built structures. Yet the density remains sparse; the buildings stand separated by generous intervals of naked rock, as if the architects feared crowding the silence. They are built low to the ground, constructed from the same sharp stone that forms the earth here—dry-stacked walls without mortar, fitted by hand-tools of obsidian and soft copper, primitive but precise. Terraces step down toward the center, following natural fault lines in the uplifted crust.

And everywhere: water.

The wetness is not the violence of rivers but the constancy of seep. Water at 2.4 saturation means the plateau weeps. It bleeds from every fissure, drips from the eaves of stone buildings, forms mirror-slick pools in depressions that never evaporate. The atmosphere itself condenses against the warm black rock—temperature here is warm, unexpectedly so for this height, fed by geothermal veins still cooling from the plateau’s volcanic nativity. Steam rises in thin, unmoving columns from hot springs, joining the mist to create a perpetual, shimmering veil. The light is normal, clear, but filtered through this aqueous haze so that everything appears slightly underwater, edges softened by humidity despite the sharpness of the stone.

Age does not touch this place. Dynamic equilibrium reigns. The water flows, yes—you can see it trickling down the tiered streets, hear the occasional *tap* of a droplet striking a pool—but it erodes nothing. The basalt remains unground. The springs bubble up at the same rate, in the same places, with the same chemical composition, millennium upon millennium. Pools hold their level without rising or falling. Vegetation exists—lush in its way, ferns and moss and hardy succulents occupying the cracks—but they do not grow taller, do not spread, do not die back. They photosynthesize in eternal stasis, perpetually green, perpetually the same size, consuming exactly what the static chemistry of the rock provides. The ecology is frozen in a moment of perfect balance, neither climax nor pioneer, simply *held*.

There are no animals. None. The silence is absolute and architectural. No insect hums between the fronds, no bird cuts through the mist, no rodent scratches in the walls. The sound rating here is 0.3—the quiet of water dripping onto stone in a vacuum of biological noise. When the wind moves (and it does, warm and slow), it makes no sound because there are no leaves to rustle, no grass to whisper, no chimes or voices. The primitive urbanity exists in a hush so profound you can hear your own heartbeat against the drum of your ribs. If people live here—and the construction suggests they do, or did—they move without speech, without footfall, honoring the equilibrium. They harvest nothing because nothing grows beyond its current state; they take water that is replenished but never changed.

You walk the streets—broad avenues of naked rock between the scattered stone shelters—and feel the strange youth of the world. This place should be raw, chaotic, changing. Instead, it is a photograph of a newborn landscape, preserved under glass. The weeping water patters on your shoulder, warm as blood, but leaves no wetness behind. The mist beads on your eyelashes but does not fall. Everything waits. Everything has already arrived. The Basalt Terrace is a landscape that rose up, built itself a city of silence, and then stopped—holding its breath forever in the warm, wet, empty air.
