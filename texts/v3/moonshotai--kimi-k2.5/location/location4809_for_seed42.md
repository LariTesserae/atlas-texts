The water is not blue, here, but a pale alpine jade—cold enough to slow your heartbeat (10°C, perhaps 12) and so clear that distance becomes deceptive. You are high, higher than most clouds dare float, suspended in a mountain lake that has no business being this deep. The surface is a membrane of silver far above, pressing down with a weight that feels like a held breath. This is the **Grove-Citadel of Vorn**, and it is small—small enough to swim from end to end in the time it takes for a single lung to burn, yet arranged with the deliberate density of a town.

It was built once, long ago, by hands that knew stone and cedar but had not yet dreamed of iron. The original architects laid their streets in concentric rings, packed their dwellings shoulder-to-shoulder, raised a central roundhouse the size of a cathedral nave. They built well for their tools—mortarless walls of fitted shale, beams socketed with wooden pegs, everything lashed and notched rather than nailed. Then the water came, or the mountain sank, or the glacier melted; the erosion parameter here speaks not of crumbling but of *settling*, of a long acquiescence. The city has been submerged for so many centuries that its sharp edges have been polished by silt and time into something that looks grown rather than made.

Now the vegetation has achieved totality. This is not a matter of plants growing *on* the ruins, but of plants *becoming* the substrate. Massive stalks of freshwater kelp—*Nereocystis montana*, or something older and unnamed—rise from the old avenues, each trunk thick as a man’s waist, pale amber and fibrous, filled with air bladders that creak and ping as they adjust to the thermocline. They grow in lines too straight to be accidental, following the old thoroughfares, creating boulevards of living tissue between the former dwellings. The buildings themselves are hollows now, scooped out by root systems that have digested the original timber and replaced it with lignin-chambered heartwood, maintaining the rectangular geometries of rooms and hearths. You can swim through a doorway that is no longer stone but a frame of braided root-cables, enter a living chamber where the floor is a mat of moss-carpet and the ceiling a lattice of light-filtering fronds.

The density is sparse despite the urban layout. The streets are wide, the plazas open to the water, creating a sense of architectural generosity, of planned emptiness. Between the vegetated structures, the water is open, allowing for the passage of schools of ghost-white char that move like slow silver clouds. The fauna is abundant and vocal: crustaceans the size of dinner plates clamber over the root-systems, their claws clicking against stone in a constant, rhythmic percussion that echoes through the water. Giant salamanders, blotched gold and black, drift from window to window of the plant-houses, their external gills fluttering like red feathers. They sing—if you can call it that—a low, thrumming drone that resonates in the sternum, utilizing the excellent acoustic conductivity of the high-altitude water to communicate across the entire settlement.

It is loud here (2.3/3). The sound is not chaotic but orchestral in its constancy: the groaning of the kelp trunks as they sway in the minute currents of the deep lake, the *snap-hiss* of air bladders releasing oxygen, the rumbling sub-bass of the mountain’s distant geothermal heart vibrating through the bedrock. It is a slow symphony. Everything moves gradually. The char circle in patterns that take hours to complete. The salamanders blink once a minute. The plants grow at the rate of fingernails, extending their boulevards by centimeters per year.

The weirdness is subtle—just enough to make you doubt your perception. The plants maintain the city *too* well. A fallen roof-beam will be replaced by a bridging root within a season. The central roundhouse remains clear of obstruction, its oculus open to the surface, as if the vegetation understands the concept of a skylight. In the market square, the kelp grows in tessellated patterns that resemble cobblestones. It suggests a symbiosis so old that the distinction between the built and the grown has dissolved into something else: a maintained ruin, or a cultivated wilderness.

Light filters down in pale shafts (normal, 1.7/3), catching on motes of glacial flour that drift like slow snow. The illumination turns the amber kelp to stained glass, casting green-gold rectangles across the silt floor where the shadows of ancient foundations still lie, darker patches where the stone refuses to be overgrown. It is cool, eternal, and patient. Nothing here is decaying; everything is being translated, slowly, from the language of human habitation into the syntax of a submerged forest, with all the street-plans preserved like grammar in a fossil tongue.
