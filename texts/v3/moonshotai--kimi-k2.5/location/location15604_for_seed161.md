The **Oblate** rises from the spine of the continent like a bead of water on a hot stone—smooth, brown-grey, and impossibly high. This is the worn nub of a mountain that existed when continents were young, ground by wind and ice over three billion years until every angle surrendered, every fault line sealed itself shut, and the peak settled into its final, eternal shape: a polished granite dome barely three hundred paces across, a cathedral-scaled world floating in the thin, dark purple sky of the upper mesosphere.

There is no water here. Not a drop of rain has struck the Oblate in geological epochs; the air is too desiccated to hold vapor as liquid. It exists as a sealed mineral environment, a dry womb of warm stone kept at a constant, blood-warm temperature by the radioactive breath of the planet’s heart seeping upward through the ancient granite. 

Yet it is *lush*. 

Covering the dome is a living pelt two meters deep: a dense, spongy matrix of lithotrophic organisms—giant, slow-growing ascomycetes and crystalline lichens that have evolved to metabolize silicate rather than sunlight. They do not photosynthesize; they digest the stone, excreting a tough, woody chitin that forms a stratified carpet of ochre, rust, and deep umber. The texture is somewhere between peat and petrified velvet, warm to the touch, riddled with capillary chambers. From above, it resembles a forest canopy pressed into two dimensions—a dense, matted jungle with no verticality but infinite internal depth.

And here, in this small, bounded space, exists a city.

They call it **Thrum**, though the name is felt as much as spoken—a vibration in the thorax. The inhabitants are small, perhaps sixty centimeters in height, with dense bones and bellows-like lungs adapted to the near-vacuum. Their technology is primitive (Tech 1.4): obsidian blades, bone needles, fire carefully husbanded in ceramic shells fueled by pressed fungal tinder. Yet their architecture is profoundly urban (Built 1.8). 

Thrum is infrastructure-dense, a vertical settlement carved not *onto* but *into* the fungal pelt. The organisms are ancient, their tissues hard as corkwood, and the inhabitants have spent ten thousand years excavating them. The result is a layered agglomeration: hexagonal chambers stacked like honeycomb, connected by woven bridges of chitin-fiber and sinew, ventilation shafts that spiral down to tap the warm stone, and cisterns that condense trace atmospheric moisture on cold ceramic coils. It is a town of tunnels and balconies, all packed tight (Density 2.2), a saturation of presences—dwellings, granaries, nurseries, and fungal farms—occupying every cubic handspan of the dome. Standing on the surface, you would see only the undulating carpet of rust-colored growth, but beneath your feet, a metropolis thrums.

The fauna (Fauna 2.3) is abundant, loud (Sound 1.7), and entirely cryptic. The dome echoes with their industry: the bass drone of fist-sized stone-burrowing beetles grinding through the crust, the high-pitched piping of air-sac spiders that balloon between the fungal towers, the constant, arrhythmic clicking of the inhabitants’ own language, which they speak through chest-plates that resonate against the warm stone. But the dominant sound is the wind. At this extreme elevation, the air moves in a permanent, patient scream, shearing over the Oblate’s curve and thrumming the taut fibers of the bridges until the whole city becomes an instrument—a drone that never ceases, loud enough that conversation is conducted in shouts or through touch-sign. The sound is the landscape’s heartbeat, slow and heavy.

Light (Light 0.6) comes dim and bronze. The sun, though stark white at this altitude, is filtered through a perpetual high-altitude haze of volcanic dust and ice crystals that turns the sky a dusty amber, casting everything in twilight even at noon. Shadows are soft, edges blurred; the fungal canopy absorbs what little light reaches it, glowing internally with phosphorescent spores only in the deepest interior chambers.

Everything here is slow (Dynamic 0.8). The fungal pelt grows at a rate of one millimeter per decade. The inhabitants mark time not by seasons—there are none—but by the gradual, millennial migration of the dome’s microclimates as the granite beneath breathes its heat in centuries-long pulses. Erosion has finished its work; the world has achieved stasis. There is no weather, no storm, no tide. Only the slow digestion of stone by life, the patient carving of new rooms, the accumulation of waste that will one day become the foundation for the next layer of Thrum.

It is a world at the end of its becoming, dense with life and habitation, primitive yet urban, warm and dry and loud, suspended in the thin dark air where the mountain meets the sky, and the stone finally surrenders to the vegetable.
