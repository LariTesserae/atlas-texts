You descend through a narrow shaft carved from packed earth and reinforced with fresh timber—wood that still smells of sap and splitting wedges. The ladder ends not at a floor, but at a threshold where the world becomes entirely liquid. You slip below the surface into warmth.

This is the Cistern. It is new. The sharp edges of its construction prove it: terracotta tiles, each one hand-pressed and sun-baked before firing in a surface kiln, then lowered down and mortared into place with a slurry of the same clay. The mortar is still slightly proud of the seams, not yet worn down by time or motion. Corners meet at perfect right angles that have not been softened. The floor is paved with the same ceramic, slightly rough with the texture of the woven mats that supported the clay while it dried. Nothing here is stone, nothing is raw earth, nothing grew. Everything is made.

The chamber is small—intimate, pocket-sized. Perhaps four meters in diameter, three meters high, a buried ceramic vessel open to the sky through a wide oculus above. That opening blazes with light. The sun sits directly overhead, or perhaps it is simply the accumulation of a white sky, but the illumination is brutal and total, piercing through the warm water to turn every suspended particle into a fleck of gold. There are no shadows, only the bright amber of liquid light.

You are submerged. The water fills the space to within a hand’s breadth of the vaulted ceiling, leaving only a thin breathing space where air trembles with humidity. The temperature holds steady at blood-warm, neither cooling nor heating as you move through it, suggesting geothermal veins in the constructed floor, or perhaps the ceramic itself retains the heat of its making.

Sound dominates. It is loud—not the muffled quiet one expects underwater, but a roaring, percussive drumming. Rain. A torrential, vertical wall of rain is falling through the oculus, striking the water’s surface with such force that the impact creates a standing resonance in the chamber. The sound is physical, a bass thrum in your chest, the high-frequency hiss of impact, and the deep, bell-like tone of the ceramic walls vibrating in sympathy. The water is never still. It heaves and rebounds, rising in sharp little peaks where the drops strike, creating complex interference patterns that race across the surface and bounce back from the tiled walls.

Vegetation exists, but barely. Sparse filaments of aquatic moss cling to the upper corners where light is most intense, their green so pale it is almost yellow. A few drifting strands of elodea hang in the water column, rooted in drainage holes left in the tilework, swaying in the convection currents caused by the warm water meeting the cooler rain. That is all. No fish dart through the brightness. No insects skate the surface. No worms burrow in the mortar. The ecology is reduced to these few plants and the water itself, a sterile nursery waiting to be populated, or perhaps deliberately kept clean.

The dynamics are constant. The rain fills; the overflow drains through carefully constructed sluices near the base—channels cut with copper chisels, the metal still showing the hammer marks of its forging. Water enters; water leaves. The level remains constant, but the substance is always new, always moving, always loud. Bubbles rise in chains from the warm floor, carrying minerals, catching the light as they ascend to burst into the air-pocket above, adding their small pops to the greater roar.

You float in the center, turning slowly. The density of the place is low—there is so much empty water between you and the walls, so much silence between the sounds, despite the smallness of the space. It feels like being inside a drum, or a heart, or a newly fired pot still cooling from the kiln, filled with warm water to test its seal. The world here is entirely artifact, entirely recent, sharp-edged and bright and loud, a handmade womb of warm rain and ceramic, hidden below the surface, singing with the noise of its own filling.
