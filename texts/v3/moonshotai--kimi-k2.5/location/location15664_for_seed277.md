The place is called the Gleaning, though it has no cartographers to name it. 

You arrive at its edge after climbing through leagues of thinning atmosphere, only to discover that elevation here does not bring cold, nor does it bring light. The Gleaning sits beneath a lid of permanent storm—an ocean of cloud so dense and so high that it rests only a thousand meters above the plateau floor, pressing down like a held breath. Day does not penetrate; only a diffuse phosphorescence sometimes bleeds through the nimbostratus, a submarine twilight the color of bruised plums. Yet the air is warm, twenty-five degrees or more, heated from below by the breathing of the land itself and by the vast, slow metabolisms that populate it.

This is a continent-sized tableland, weathered into settled forms over epochs. The erosion here is not the violence of young mountains but the patient negotiation of existence—rounded basins, fluted columns, wind-sculpted arches that have stood in their current postures for millions of years. Nothing is sharp. Everything has been agreed upon by time.

The ground is damp but never wet. Moisture arrives not as rain but as condensation, a persistent mineral sweat that beads on every surface at dawn and dusk. It beads on the calcified hulls of things that are not quite alive and not quite machines. Here is where the contradiction resolves: the Gleaning has achieved industrial complexity without ever having been built.

The fauna—and they are abundant, threading across the vast distances in scattered densities—are the factories. They are lithotrophic megafauna, organisms whose evolutionary niche is metallurgy. A single specimen, viewed from a distance, resembles a combination of coral reef and foundry: a central kiln-stomach of refractory ceramic bone, surrounded by chimney-lungs that vent steam and sulfur compounds, connected by tendon-belts to crushing mandibles that grind ore from the bedrock. They move, but slowly, migrating across the plateau in herds of thirty or forty, leaving behind trails of processed powders: refined oxides, pure silicon, precipitates of copper and zinc. These trails are the rivers of the Gleaning—dry streams of metal dust that occasionally, when the condensation is heavy, become slurries of dark mud.

There are no plants here, not as you understand them. Vegetation is sparse to the point of absence—only desiccated lichens that etch the rocks in gray-green circuitry, feeding on the chemical rain that drips from the creatures' stacks. The food web is based not on photosynthesis but on geology. The fauna consume stone and excrete structure; smaller organisms—tick-creatures the size of dogs, with pneumatic pistons for legs—harvest the heat and the refined minerals from the giants' flanks. Predators exist, but they are mechanical in their precision: ambush hunters that camouflage as boulders, their jaws spring-loaded with cartilage and compressed nitrogen, snapping shut with the finality of a safety valve releasing.

The scale dwarfs comprehension. A herd of the forge-beasts might occupy a valley the size of a European country, their individual bodies like hills that rise and fall with their eighty-year respiration cycles. Between them, empty basins stretch silent for days' travel. The density is sparse—you might walk for weeks and see only the distant venting of steam on the horizon, a cloud manufacturing its own weather—but when you encounter life, it is overwhelming, complex, humming with internal combustion.

For the Gleaning is not quiet, though it is nearly silent. Sound here is subsonic and seismic. You feel the industry as a pressure in your teeth: the grinding of tectonic jaws, the pumping of geological bellows, the low-frequency thrum of mating calls that propagate through basalt for hundreds of miles. What you hear is mostly the settling of stone, the occasional hiss of a vent releasing pent gases, the patter of condensation from biological cooling towers. It is the quiet of a factory floor when the workers have stepped away, but the machines continue their blind, sacred labor.

Rules govern this place that belong to neither biology nor engineering, but to some hybrid covenant. Energy flows from geothermal gradients up through the food chain, converted into motion and mineral purity. The creatures do not breed so much as accrete, adding modular chambers to their bodies until they become too heavy and fissure, splitting like cells. There is no waste; every effluent becomes architecture, the exoskeletons of dead fauna slowly eroding into the established forms of the landscape, becoming the strange, pillared factories that the next generation will inhabit and consume.

In the darkest depths of the twilight, where the cloud ceiling presses lowest, you can see the bioluminescence of process—blue-white arcs of electrical discharge between the chimney-stacks, the glow of furnace-hearts shining through translucent ceramic ribs. It is an industrial aurora, cold and warm at once, illuminating nothing but the immediate fog.

You are alone here. There are no roads, no ruins, no artifacts. The technology is entirely immanent—grown, secreted, calcified. You walk through a vastness that manufactures itself, warm and damp and dark, where life has found that mechanical efficiency is just another evolutionary strategy, and the plateau grinds on, patient as ore becoming steel, steel becoming dust, and dust becoming the Gleaning once again.
