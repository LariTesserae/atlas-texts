You descend through the access shaft—if “descend” is the word for a direction that seems to shift as you move, gravity tugging at your inner ear like a half-remembered melody—and emerge into the **Hollow**.

It is not large. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other, though the stone might never land where you expect. The chamber is cathedral-scale, perhaps two hundred meters across, but it feels vaster because of how little fills it. The air hits first: warm, thick, viscous with humidity, carrying the mineral tang of fresh stone and ozone. You are deep, deep beneath the surface, and yet the space feels *newborn*. The walls are raw obsidian and basalt, unsoftened by time, their edges knife-sharp, the surfaces still slick with the geothermal birth-sweat of their making. This void opened only decades ago—a gasp in the crust when a magma chamber emptied and groundwater rushed in to kiss the void. It has not yet learned to be a cave.

And in this fresh wound, someone built a city.

The urban layer clings to the walls in terraced strata, a settlement of black glass and phosphorescent filament that appears to have grown rather than been constructed. The technology here operates on principles that make your teeth ache—gravity is a suggestion, not a law. Buildings hang from the ceiling like inverted stalactites, their foundations rooted in the rock above, while others jut horizontally from the walls, connected by walkways that corkscrew through the air without supports. You see water flowing *upward* in transparent channels, defying the local gradient, cycling from the superheated pool at the chamber’s base to filtration spines near the roof. This is infrastructure that manipulates spacetime at the Planck scale, architecture that remembers its own blueprint and regrows itself when damaged.

It is empty.

Not abandoned—*empty*. The density of presence is so low it feels like a held breath. The city was built for thousands, but you sense no footsteps, no breath, no heartbeat. There are no animals. No insects tick against the light-fixtures; no bats cling to the high corners; not even bacteria seem to colonize the puddles in the traditional way. The ecology here is sterile, curated, mechanical-biological. What moves is process, not population.

The vegetation is lush, but wrong. **Chemosynthetic chandeliers** hang from the ceiling-gardens—crystalline structures part fungus, part mineral, part quantum-computational substrate. They bloom in fractal branches of violet and deep-sea green, opening and closing their photosynthetic petals not to sunlight (there is none) but to the rhythmic pulses of heat from below. They feed on geothermal exhalations, on the sulfurous steam that vents from fissures in the floor. Their growth is visible if you watch for minutes: tendrils extending, mineral-nodes swelling, a lushness that is hungry and mechanical.

Water is everywhere, and it is loud. The chamber is a machine for moving water through states. The pool at the bottom churns at eighty degrees Celsius, kept from boiling only by the pressure of depth. It evaporates constantly, filling the air with vapor that condenses on the cooler surfaces of the upper city—**rain in reverse**, a perpetual weeping from the ceiling. The sound is overwhelming: the roar of steam vents like the breathing of leviathans, the crash of water falling *down* in some zones and *up* in others, the sub-bass hum of the gravity-manipulators cycling their containment fields, and beneath it all, the groan of the young rock still settling, still cracking its knuckles after the trauma of creation. It is a loudness without voices, an active, dynamic chorus of physics and machinery.

The light is dim, sourced entirely from the bioluminescent vegetation and the faint cyan glow of the active tech. Shadows pool thickly in the corners, and the water reflects nothing clearly, smearing the glow into halos of indigo and rust-orange.

You walk along a causeway that shifts its angle to accommodate your stride—**dynamic architecture**, responding to your mass, adjusting its local gravity so you always feel the ground as “down” even as the ground tilts toward vertical. You pass through a market square where stalls of carbon-fiber lattice stand empty, their surfaces still displaying holographic prices in languages that haven’t been spoken here since the chamber formed. The air shimmers where heat meets cold, creating mirages of movement, ghosts of commerce.

The rules here are simple but alien:  
**Water seeks the highest bidder.** In this space, H₂O responds to gravitational eddies; it flows toward the negative-mass generators that power the city, climbing to reach them, then falling when the generators pulse off-cycle.  
**Growth is geometry.** The vegetation does not evolve; it calculates, expanding into optimal configurations for heat-exchange.  
**Silence is prohibited.** The systems maintain a minimum decibel level—the loudness is functional, necessary to prevent quantum decoherence in the structural materials. The city must sing to keep its shape.  
**Nothing may live that is not approved.** The sterile field is absolute. Fauna—carbon-based, self-replicating, motile life—is filtered from the air by nanoscale sentinels. Only vegetable stasis and mechanical process are permitted.

You reach the center of the chamber, where a spire of pure computronium rises from the boiling pool, its surface rippling with non-Euclidean topologies. It is the heart of the warmth, the source of the wet, the conductor of the symphony. It is growing, slowly, extending new branches into the water, crystallizing the minerals into fresh architecture. The city is young because it *recently decided to exist*, extruding itself from the deep crust like a pearl accreting around the irritation of human absence.

You stand there, drenched in condensation, ears ringing with the white-noise roar of an empty metropolis that maintains itself for no one, watching the water climb upward into the dark, and you understand: this is not a ruin. It is a nursery. It is waiting for something to be born here that has not yet been imagined, keeping the lights on and the gravity tuned and the gardens growing in lush, patient preparation for a population that may never arrive.
