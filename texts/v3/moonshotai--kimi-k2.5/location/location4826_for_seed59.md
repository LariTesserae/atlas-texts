You arrive at the threshold breathless, not from the climb—though you have ascended three thousand feet through cloud—but from the density of the air itself, which carries twice its weight in water. The highland basin spreads before you like a lung exhaling. It is not large; you could shout from one wall of living wood to the other and hear your voice returned, distorted by the thousand throats of the inhabitants. Cathedral-sized, yes, but a cathedral turned inside-out, its nave filled not with emptiness but with saturation.

The ground is not ground. It is a matted stratum of peat and root-cord, compressed over centuries into a springy, fibrous floor that yields beneath your step and weeps water when compressed. You are walking on the architecture of the place itself. The vegetation here has achieved total dominion—2.8 out of 3 on any scale of green conquest. Trees do not stand *on* the earth; they *are* the earth, their trunks fused into buttresses that form the outer walls of this chamber, their root systems braiding together to create elevated terraces, hollowed chambers, and arterial channels. This is the "built" environment: not stone laid upon land, but land taught to grow into habitation. Primitive technology—bone scrapers, obsidian knives, copper needles—has not conquered the wood but negotiated with it. Generations of highlanders have pruned, bent, grafted, and cajoled the living substrate until it grew into thoroughfares, storage caches, and sleeping niches. The result is an urban density without masonry; a town where the infrastructure breathes, sweats, and occasionally shifts.

The temperature holds at a persistent coolness, just above the threshold where breath becomes visible vapor, though here the vapor never fully departs. Water exists in its intermediate states—mist, drip, seepage, condensation. It beads on every surface, runs down the channelled grooves of bark-aqueducts, and pools in the cupped leaves of parasites that grow upon the parasites. The erosion parameter speaks of established balance: this wetness does not destroy, it sculpts. Water has worked the wood for centuries, softening fibers, hollowing bowls in trunks where sparrow-sized frogs now breed in trembling congregations.

And they are loud. The sound rating here is not the violence of storm or waterfall, but the accumulated volume of overwhelming fauna. At 2.8, life does not merely exist; it crowds. Insects constitute a permanent atmospheric condition—gnats that thread the air in silver braids, beetles that click in the walls like mechanical hearts, moths whose wings beat against your eyelashes. Birds move in flocks that occlude the dim light, their calls not individual notes but a sustained, oscillating pressure. Mammals scurry in the dense understory—rodents, shrews, climbing things with prehensile tails—that make the living floor perpetually tremble. You cannot lift a hand without disturbing three species. The sound is biological static, a roar of metabolism.

Light filters through the canopy in normal quantities—dappled, shifting, neither gloom nor glare—but the dynamic quality of the place makes the light itself seem unstable. Turbulence rules here. This is not a peaceful grove; it is a storm of growth. Vines creep visibly across the span of an afternoon, their tendrils questing across walkways, requiring daily clearing. Fungal shelves erupt overnight from the root-mass, swelling with hydraulic pressure, creating new tables, new obstacles, new topography by dawn. Water channels migrate as root-pressure squeezes them shut or opens new sphincters in the peat. The "city" is in constant renovation. Its citizens—the highlanders—move through this chaos with the practiced fatalism of those who know their homes are temporary arrangements with a vegetative god. They wake to find their doorways shifted, their sleeping platforms lifted by bulbous growths, their paths rerouted by the slow avalanche of a leaning bole.

Yet there is order in the turbulence. The small scale—barely a hamlet's breadth—keeps it navigable. It is a bounded intensity, a pressure chamber of ecology. You trace the social logic in the way certain roots are tied with cordage to create handholds, the way fungal chimneys are tended to draw smoke from hearth-fires, the way the loudest sections—the frog pools, the insect nurseries—are left to the wild while the quieter, drier hollows are claimed for human voice. The primitive tech shows in the bone needles pinning back curtains of moss, in the stone weights training branches into archways, in the copper pots catching the weeping water that falls in steady, metronomic drops from the ceiling of leaves.

It smells of wet cellulose, of spore and pollen suspended in the mist, of the sweet rot of the established erosion. It is old here. The place has existed in this state—wet, dense, loud, turbulent—for longer than memory, achieving a weathered equilibrium where destruction and creation are the same process. You stand in a room that is a marsh that is a city that is a body, cool to the touch, loud to the ear, and moving beneath your feet with the slow, irresistible patience of root and rot.
