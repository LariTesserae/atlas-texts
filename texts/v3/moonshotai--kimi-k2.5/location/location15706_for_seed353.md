You stand at the lip of a bowl-shaped cavity that should not exist—an excavation the size of a fallen city, though excavated not by tools but by patient, grinding hunger. The **terrain** is not earth but *eroded architecture*: vast compressed terraces of lignin and suberin that have flowed like cooling lava over eons, settling into their final forms. What were once factory floors have sagged into warm, undulating hills; load-bearing columns have hypertrophied into bulbous, striated buttes; and everywhere the ground breathes—rising and falling by centimeters with the slow, tidal respiration of root-pneuma. This is elevation without altitude: you are high only because the substrate has piled upward through accretion, generation after generation of growth stacking upon itself until the hollow mountain of living matter stands three kilometers above the theoretical bedrock, suspended in a cavernous dark.

There is **no water** here, not as liquid. The humidity reads zero on any gauge, yet the air is *thick*—heavy with volatile resins and sublimating turpentines that carry moisture in chemical bondage. What little water existed in this sealed system was captured ten thousand years ago, locked into hemicellulose matrices and metabolic cycles. The **vegetation**—if that word suffices—has achieved total saturation. It is the substrate, the structure, the atmosphere, and the machine. Everything is **built**, yet nothing was constructed by hands. The distinction between factory and forest dissolved so long ago that the concept of "natural substrate" is geological fiction. You walk on warm heartwood dense as iron. The walls are vertical jungles of carbon-fiber reeds that grow in crystalline lattices, their hollow stems acting as ventilation shafts, capacitor banks, and skeletal scaffolding simultaneously.

The **temperature** is a constant 67°C, originating not from sun or magma but from the friction of metabolic processes. This is a compost pile the size of a prefecture, digesting itself. Ancient industrial heat—perhaps from the geothermal tap that originally powered the facility—provided the initial spark, but now the **dynamic** turbulence is self-sustaining and violent. The landscape is in **storm-state**, though silent. Root systems clash underground in slow-motion warfare, hydraulic pressures burst silo-like seed-pods that spray metallic spores in silent geysers, and whole sections of the "ground" suddenly delaminate as new growth shoves old growth aside in surging waves of biological succession. It is an anthill energy without the ants—pure vegetative restlessness, a bazaar of matter constantly trading places.

Yet it is **mostly quiet**. The density of the growth absorbs sound. You hear your own heartbeat, the crunch of desiccated phloem underfoot, and the occasional *crack* of thermal expansion as a massive lignin wall splits along hidden fault lines. But there is no wind through leaves—there are no leaves, only facets and cubes and spiraling geometries of woody tissue. No drip of water. No buzz of insects. **No fauna** whatsoever; this ecosystem solved the problem of pollination and seed dispersal through explosive dehiscence and tremor-propagation, evolving beyond the need for animal intermediaries millions of years ago.

**Light** is vestigial. A bioluminescent smear here and there—decaying nodes of phosphorescent fungus analogues that feed on the excess heat, emitting sickly chartreuse glows that illuminate nothing, only make the dark deeper. Mostly you navigate by the infrared ghost of the living wood, which glows in your thermal vision like embers, revealing cathedrals of black growth that vanish into absolute shadow.

The **materials** around you are industrial fossils. Amber has replaced circuitry; fossilized sap runs in veins where copper once flowed, carrying chemical signals instead of electrons. Pistons are now lignaceous cylinders grown in place, powered not by steam but by the osmotic pressure of hypertonic sap. Gears mesh silently—perfectly calcified wood gears that turn with glacial inevitability, grinding dead material into mulch for the next cycle. The **weirdness** lies in the precision: trees that grow square, roots that braid themselves into suspension cables, bark that extrudes itself in seamless pipes. It is nature mimicking the factory so perfectly that the factory has become indistinguishable from nature.

This is an **ancient** place, stable only in its instability. The erosion is complete; the original industrial seed has been worn down, digested, and re-expressed until the landscape has reached its heat-death of form—constantly changing, yet forever returning to the same configurations. It is the end-state of a world where industry and organism merged, creating a closed loop of matter that knows no seasons, only the eternal, sweltering, whisper-quiet riot of growth in total darkness.
