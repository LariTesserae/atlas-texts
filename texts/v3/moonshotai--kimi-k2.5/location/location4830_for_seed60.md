At first you think it is a greenhouse, then a cathedral, then a body. You are correct each time, and wrong.

You enter through a mouth of collapsed brickwork, teeth of crumbling mortar softened by the lips of strangler figs. Inside, the air is thick—not with humidity alone, though that is present, gathering in droplets on every surface and falling in a constant, syncopated rhythm that creates the *sound*: a percussive language of drip and splash, of fluid percussion against leaves, metal, and the drums of your own eardrums. It is loud here, but not chaotic. The noise has structure. It is the sound of breathing, if lungs were the size of swimming pools.

The space is small—perhaps fifty paces across, thirty high—but the *density* makes it infinite. There is no floor, only layers of substrate: rotting cellulose compressed into peat, then into something like felt, then into a living mat of roots so interwoven they have achieved the consistency of rubber. Walking requires you to sink slightly, to be accepted. The walls are not walls but trunks, hundreds of them, not standing side by side but *fused*, their vascular systems married through grafts both natural and obscene. The vegetation has achieved maximum saturation; it is not an ecosystem but a single organism pretending to be a forest, or perhaps a forest that has decided to become a single room.

Scattered throughout this vegetative pressure—at knee-height, at chest-height, emerging from the canopy above like islands in a green flood—are the remains of the industrial. A turbine, brass-green with verdigris and lichen, still spins. Its axle has been absorbed by the heartwood of a mahogany tree, which has grown around it, incorporating the rotation into its own growth rings. The tree does not seem to mind. It produces a thick, amber sap that lubricates the ancient bearings. The machine produces electricity, or perhaps the tree does; the distinction has eroded along with the boundary between built and grown.

Copper wires descend from above like lianas, choked every few inches by the trunk-swell of encasing trees. They still carry current. You can feel it in your fillings, a hum that resonates in the wet air, loud enough to be a presence. The light—diffuse, normal, filtered through layers of chlorophyll and stained glass (for there is glass here, panes of it, smothered by moss but intact, wedged between branches like jewels in a setting)—casts everything in a jade-gold haze.

This is the established form, the settled state. The erosion here is not degradation but digestion. The factory—because it was a factory, three generations ago, producing what? rope? refrigerant? the memory is pulp now—has been weathered into compliance. It exists now as architecture for the plant, as scaffolding for ambition. The steam pipes, breached and open, vent warm vapor constantly, maintaining the temperature at a feverish, tropical constant. Condensation beads on every surface, runs in rivulets down the faces of abandoned gearboxes, pools in the cupped hands of bromeliads that grow in the rusted crevices.

There is little fauna. Perhaps a moth, its wings like torn wallpaper, beating against the current of humid air. Perhaps the silver glint of a fish in a cistern that used to be a boiler. The ecology is closed, complete, sufficient unto itself. The plants have become predators of machinery, digesting the industrial age into cellulose, into lignin, into the stubborn, patient architecture of packed density.

Weirdness seeps from the pores of this place. Time moves slowly, on the scale of lignification and mineral accretion. The machines operate, but they operate *wrong*. A conveyor belt advances at the speed of root growth, carrying nothing but pollen. A pressure gauge reads not in PSI but in blooming cycles. The rules are simple: nothing is discarded, only incorporated; nothing is empty, only filled; nothing is silent, only speaking in frequencies too low or too high for casual hearing.

The water—1.6 on any arbitrary scale—comes from everywhere. It weeps from the ceiling of leaves, condenses on the cold metal bones of forgotten engines, rises from the saturated substrate beneath your feet. It is not just water. It is sap, and rust-water, and the effluvia of this slow, mechanical digestion. It collects in the hollows of leaves the size of serving trays, overflows, falls, joins the chorus.

You stand in a grove that is a room that is a stomach. The remnants of human industry are not ruins here; they are organs. The plant has not destroyed the factory. It has *become* it, or it has allowed the factory to become plant. The distinction is academic, and the air is too wet for scholarship. You are breathing the exhalations of this patient, loud, saturated world, where every cubic inch is occupied, where the past has not been erased but enfolded, where the strange logic of symbiosis has achieved its ultimate, breathing, dripping, humming expression.

There is no exit visible, only the suggestion of paths between the pillar-trees, between the sleeping machines. But movement here is slow. The dynamic is glacial, tidal, measured in the opening of stomata. You will leave, eventually, or you will stay, and either way, the space will remain: warm, wet, packed with green intention, spinning its turbines in a dream of chlorophyll.
