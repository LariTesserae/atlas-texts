You do not walk *through* the Groan-Steppe; you walk *between* its breathing.  

From the crusted rim of the world—where the air thins but refuses to cool—you look out upon a continent that is less a land than a process. The temperature sits heavy at the back of the throat, a dry heat of 2.8 magnitude that shimmers off stone and living wood alike. This is the **High Thermal Pan**, an elevated basin vast enough to generate its own gyres of weather, its own pressure systems that spin lazily across the ochre distance. The elevation grants you a view of curvature: the horizon is not a line but a slow, vegetal swell, punctuated by the **Throats**.

There are perhaps three hundred of them across the Pan, though “three hundred” is a number too small for the scale. Each is a single organism, or a colony fused into singularity over geological time—established, weathered, settled into forms that erosion has polished rather than destroyed. They rise like cooling towers of lignin and cork, five kilometers across at the base, their flanks barrel-chested and pocked with dark, irregular apertures. They are the vegetation at 2.8: plant-as-architecture, plant-as-terrain, plant-as-world. Between them, the land is not empty but *sparse*—a dusting of metallic lichens and subcutaneous root-hairs that biologically carpet the stone yet leave it visually naked. You can walk for six days in a straight line and meet only the whisper of this biological substrate, the occasional condensation-runoff pooling in a cupped depression, the rare thermal vent hissing steam that the plants drink.

The water is shy here—rating a mere 1.1. There are no rivers, no lakes. Moisture comes as **condensate**: the hot, mineral exhalations of the Pan’s geothermal belly meeting the cooler air at the Throats’ upper reaches, precipitating into silver sheets that sheet down the woody flanks. The Throats store it in internal cisterns of spongy pith, bleeding it slowly through capillary action to the biocrust below. It is a landscape of intermittent damp—sweating stone, morning mist that burns off by noon, the damp breath of the earth itself.

And the sound.  

The sound is **2.6**—a deafening, physical pressure that you feel in your molars before your ears parse it. It is the **Groan**, the collective exhalation of the Throats’ fauna. For these hollow pillars are not silent monoliths; they are **overwhelmingly alive**. Each Throat houses billions—trillions, if you count the microfauna—of organisms that exist in a liminal space between animal and vegetable. They are the fruit and the flesh of the structure itself. Winged things with chitinous struts for bones emerge from bud-chambers at dawn, their wingspans wide as sails, their bodies translucent and photosynthetic. They spend their days harvesting spores from the lichen-fields, returning at dusk to dissolve against the inner walls, their nutrients weeping back into the woody heartwood, their chitin shed in scaly drifts that carpet the Throats’ bases.

The noise is the friction of their passage, the clicking of mandibles used to carve new chambers, the supersonic whine of mating flights that Doppler-shift overhead, and always, always, the **low-frequency thrum** of the Throats’ hydraulic systems—vast columns of water and sap being lifted against gravity, creaking the cellulose, making the structures shudder at frequencies felt in the gut. In the heat of midday, the wood expands, and the Throats **sing**: a basso profundo groan that rolls across the Pan, bouncing off distant cousins, creating standing waves of sound that can dislodge boulders. To stand at the base is to be inside a bell struck by the sun.

This is the weirdness, the 2.1 strangeness that permeates the place: **the cycle of reabsorption**. Here, fauna is not distinct from flora. The creatures are mobile organs—kidneys, hands, pollen-fingers—loaned temporary autonomy. They breed in the warm, wet dark of the interior, their eggs planted in walls that supply nutrients directly to the yolk. When they die, they do not fall to a separate ecosystem of decay; they are gathered by specialized scarab-forms that are themselves only days from dissolution, carrying the dead up to porous digestion-pits where enzymes await. Nothing is wasted; everything is the Throat, temporarily escaped from itself.

And scattered across this vastness, rating barely 0.8 on the scale of built environments, are the **Scaffolders**. These are the tool-users—small groups of bipeds with simple bronze knives, bone needles, and hands calloused from weaving rope from shed plant fiber. Their technology is primitive, pre-industrial, manual. They do not build cities; they build **nests**.

You find them clinging to the outer bark like moss, their platforms lashed to the apertures, little more than woven hammocks and windbreaks of translucent wing-membrane. Some are solitary hermits occupying a single high hole; others are small bands of twenty, marking the Throat’s surface with faint, ochre handprints—a territorial script that fades in a season. They harvest the fallen: the chitin scales that flake off in sheets large enough to roof a hut, the “meat-fruit” (creatures that died naturally and tumbled from the holes, still warm), and the sweet sap that bleeds from wounds in the wood. They move with the shadow, migrating from Throat to Throat across the sparse plains when the Groan of one particular tower grows too loud, or when the thermal vents beneath shift and a Throat begins to roar in new, unsettling octaves.

The light is normal—1.5—a clear, high-altitude glare that flattens shadows and makes the distances deceptive. At noon, the Throats are pale gold, bleached and smooth. At sunset, they become blood-red, their silhouettes swallowing the sun, the holes glowing from within with the phosphorescent decay of dying symbiotes, a lantern-show of blue-green shifting inside the crimson wood.

The dynamism of the place is biological, not geological. The Throats grow, but so slowly that a generation of Scaffolders will not perceive it; instead, they perceive the **pulse**—the diurnal inflation of the wood as it heats, the seasonal shedding of the outer cortex in sheets that slide down like avalanches, the migration of entire faunal layers from the cool north face to the baking south as the sun’s angle shifts. Weather is local: each Throat creates its own column of rising hot air, spawning dust-devils that wander the plains like herds of ghostly buffalo, or sudden thunderstorms that break against the woody flanks, the rain hissing into steam before it reaches the ground.

This is the world: a continent of perhaps twelve inhabited Throats, separated by weeks of walking across crust and dust, where the horizon is always a massive, curved wall of breathing wood, and the air is a solid thing, hot and loud and alive. To die here is to be carried, if you are lucky, to a Scaffolder’s burial site—a shallow scrape in the lichen where your body will be reabsorbed not by the alien Throats, but by the patient, sparse substrate itself, becoming slowly, over centuries, part of the thin biological film that holds the stone together, waiting for the next Throat to erupt from the ground, ancient and settled, already groaning.
