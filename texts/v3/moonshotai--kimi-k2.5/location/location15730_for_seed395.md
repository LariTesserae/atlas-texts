The **Basin of Accretion** stretches across a low, subsiding continental shield that geography has not yet finished making. It is a place caught in the first hour of its own existence: the bedrock here is fresh basaltic glass, cooling from the rift below, still sighing with the heat of its birth. No rivers have had time to carve it; no winds have worn it smooth. The erosion parameter reads not as subtraction but as hesitant addition—minerals precipitating, chemistry solidifying, the land still deciding what shape to hold.

And it has chosen to become a city.

Not a city of tools—that parameter forbids it—but a metropolis of pure, saturated biology. Over millions of years, a symbiotic superorganism has evolved to fill the dense, packed mandate. Calcium-carbonate secreting trees, siliceous fungi, and pneumatic flowering vines have converged into a single architectural strategy: they grow up, then die, then grow upon their own bones, creating a continuous urban fabric that covers the entire basin from horizon to horizon without a single gap. There are no streets, only capillary channels; no buildings, only hollow chambers in the accumulated coral-like mass; no empty lots because the density parameter insists that every cubic meter be occupied—by root, by nest, by the living and the dead stacked in stratified tenancy.

The scale is planetary. You could walk for months and never leave the shadow of the Biome Metropolis, yet you would never climb higher than a few hundred meters above the original datum, for the elevation remains stubbornly low. The city sprawls outward rather than up, a sprawling mat of organic masonry that has engulfed entire mountain ranges-to-be, smoothing them into the general floor.

Water exists here at 0.4—barely present, never free. There are no lakes, no morning dew, no subterranean aquifers in the traditional sense. Instead, the vegetation has become hydraulic architecture. Each structure is a living cistern: swollen trunks of translucent gel hold bound water in molecular cages; leaves are waxy seals; the entire ecosystem operates on internal circulation, a closed-loop vascular system at continental scale. When a creature dies, its fluids are scavenged within seconds by specialized decomposers that have evolved to look like maintenance crews, sealing moisture back into the walls.

The temperature hovers at a consistent warmth, a greenhouse effect of the living structures themselves, which respire slowly and trap geologic heat from below.

Abundant fauna moves through this urban jungle, but the silence is absolute. Evolution here has favored the noiseless. Gliding membrane-mammals with suction-cupped feet traverse vertical facades without sound; translucent beetles communicate through bioluminescent Morse code rather than chirrup; the apex predators are ambush hunters that detect vibration through the structural roots, so prey have learned to freeze, to move in slow, viscous flows. The only sound is the occasional *tick* of expanding silica as a new spire finishes its growth, or the subsonic groan of the basin settling another millimeter lower.

Dynamic processes are constant but glacial in tempo. Districts rise and fall on the scale of decades as nutrient flows shift. A tower may be abandoned by its living constructors, left to petrify into habitable stone, then recolonized by a new generation that grow through its windows like vines through ruins—except here, the ruin is only thirty years old. Predator-prey cycles occur in the interstitial spaces, silent as chess moves. Weather exists at the continental scale: slow atmospheric rivers of spores that drift for weeks, coating everything in a fine, static-charged dust that feeds the upper canopy.

Light filters through normally—sun, shadow, the golden hour—but it passes through translucent membranes and prismatic crystal-bark, breaking into soft, interior glows. The whole place feels like walking through the bones of a cathedral while the congregation is still growing the walls.

The rules of this world are simple: construction is metabolism; occupancy is mandatory; water is blood, never rain; and the city is never finished. It is urban in the truestsense—a settlement of impossible density, infrastructure without infrastructure, a technology so thoroughly biological that it has forgotten it was ever alive.
