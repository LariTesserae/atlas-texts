**Preparation for the Grove-Citadel of Vorn**

**I. The Body: Acclimating to Altitude and Cold**

You must arrive at least seventy-two hours before your descent to acclimate to the elevation. The lake sits in atmospheric thinness—your blood will thicken, your breath will shorten, and the surface world will already feel like a dream before you even enter the water. Do not rush this. The Citadel does not tolerate haste, and altitude sickness above water becomes panic below it.

The water is *ten degrees Celsius*. Remember this number. It is not frigid enough to kill you instantly, but it is cold enough to steal your dexterity within minutes and your judgment shortly after. You will need a dry suit—neoprene traps nitrogen bubbles that distort at high altitudes and will leave you dangerously buoyant. Choose a compression-resistant membrane suit in matte earth tones; the ghost-char are spooked by bright colors, and the crustaceans interpret shiny objects as rival shells. Fit your hood with acoustic dampeners—the sub-bass frequencies of the mountain's geothermal heart will rattle your inner ear and induce vertigo if unfiltered.

Breathing apparatus must be rebreather-based, not open-circuit. The Citadel's acoustic environment is precise; the *snap-hiss* of exhaust bubbles will shatter the local soundscape like a dropped plate, disrupting the salamanders' thrumming communications and potentially triggering defensive behavior from the root systems. More importantly, the water's clarity creates distance deception. What appears to be twenty meters may be sixty. A limited air supply forces you to acknowledge that you are a temporary visitor in a place that measures time in centuries.

Bring gloves with thermal linings but tactile sensitivity—you will need to feel the difference between original shale masonry and lignin-chambered replacement wood. The distinction matters. Touching the wrong wall might invite a root-cable to test your composition.

**II. The Mind: Adjusting Temporal Perception**

Before entering, practice slowing your metabolic sense of time. Spend three days observing glaciers or growing crystals. The Citadel operates on dendrochronological time. The kelp extends boulevards at the rate of fingernails; the salamanders blink once per minute; the char complete their circling patterns in hours. If you enter with urban urgency, your heart rate will betray you. The cold water will slow your heartbeat physically, but you must slow your expectations metaphysically.

Prepare for the *grammar* of the place. This is not a ruin being reclaimed; it is a text being translated. When you swim through a doorway that is now braided root-cables, do not mourn the lost timber—recognize the preserved syntax. The roundhouse oculus remains open not by chance, but by botanical consent. Maintain a mental state of "attentive patience." You are not exploring; you are being allowed to read.

Carry a visual reference card showing normal freshwater kelp growth patterns. The *Nereocystis* here grows in tessellated cobblestone patterns and follow straight Roman-style avenues. When you see geometry that is too perfect, acknowledge the unease—this is the "weirdness" the location generates. Do not dismiss it as your imagination. The vegetation maintains the city *too* well; it suggests a distributed intelligence older than the original architects. Treat the kelp forest with the courtesy you would afford a host who has meticulously prepared their home for guests who arrive only once every thousand years.

**III. Sensory Equipment**

**Vision:** Bring polarized lenses that enhance the jade spectrum (490-570nm). The pale alpine jade water filters red light quickly; without enhancement, you will miss the subtle emanations of the geothermal vents that warm certain pockets of the settlement. The "ghost-white" char are nearly invisible without contrast enhancement, and you must see them to avoid swimming through their slow processions—they follow ancient street plans and do not deviate for mammals.

**Acoustics:** Wear a dual-frequency audio rig. The crustacean percussion operates in high frequencies (their claws clicking against stone), while the salamander song and kelp trunk groaning occupy infrasound (below 20Hz). You need to hear both to navigate. The infrasound will attempt to sync with your heartbeat; configure your dampeners to maintain your pulse at no lower than 50 BPM. There are stories of travelers who allowed their hearts to match the mountain's sub-bass, sinking into torpor among the root-cables, becoming new doorways before they realized they had stopped swimming.

**Proprioception:** Bring a monofilament line dispenser. The extreme clarity creates vertigo—without particulate matter to judge distance, you may swim into walls you thought were fifty meters away, or hesitate before openings that are within arm's reach. The line gives you tactile scale in a visually infinite medium.

**IV. Biological Protocols**

The crustaceans are the size of dinner plates and territorial. They are not predatory toward humans, but they test the edibility of new objects by rapping them with calcified claws. If one begins drumming on your tank or femur, remain still. The sound will echo through the boulevards, and other crustaceans will approach to "listen." This is not an attack; it is an audit. They are determining if you are new substrate—something to be colonized and replaced with lignin, like the ancient timber. Do not strike them. Instead, emit a low hum (100-150Hz) from your throat; it approximates the salamander "all-clear" tone and confers temporary diplomatic immunity.

Do not touch the salamanders' external gills. They look like red feathers, but they are delicate chromatophore organs that detect chemical changes in the water. Your sunscreen, skin oils, or anxiety-sweat will register as pollution. If a salamander drifts toward you, freeze. Let it window-shop. They are the true residents now, moving from living-room to living-room through windows that are no longer stone but vascular plant tissue.

**V. The Descent**

Enter at the market square if possible. The tessellated kelp patterns there provide the best visual reference for horizontal orientation. Descend slowly enough that your descent rate matches the speed of the rising oxygen bubbles from the kelp bladders—this synchronizes you with the local vertical current and prevents stirring the silt where the stone foundations lie.

Do not bring lights. The illumination is 1.7/3—sufficient, if you let your pupils dilate. Artificial light destroys the stained-glass effect of the sun filtering through amber kelp and glacial flour. It also blinds the char, which navigate by the shadows of the foundations. You are visiting a cathedral, not spelunking a cave. Illuminate only in emergencies, and then with red-spectrum only.

**VI. Emergency Considerations**

If you become disoriented, look for shadows. The stone foundations refuse overgrowth; they appear as darker rectangles on the silt floor. These are your anchors to the original geometry. Swim toward any shadow that forms a right angle—it will be a building, and buildings cluster toward the center roundhouse.

If you begin to feel euphoric or timeless, check your rebreather mix. Nitrogen narcosis at altitude combines with the infrasound to create a sedative effect travelers call "acquiescence"—the same phenomenon that makes the erosion here a matter of settling rather than crumbling. If you feel yourself wanting to stay, to become part of the translation from built to grown, activate your emergency ascent protocol immediately. The Citadel has been maintaining itself for centuries; it does not need your calcium to complete its grammar.

**VII. Departure Ritual**

When you leave, pause at the silver membrane of the surface for three minutes. Let your body warm slightly. Look back down at the geometry of roots and stone. Acknowledge that you have walked through a language that is still being spoken, slowly, by plant and stone and time. Do not take souvenirs. The architecture here is not abandoned; it is merely undergoing a very long revision.

The Grove-Citadel of Vorn will remain, extending its boulevards by centimeters per year, maintaining the oculus, translating human ambition into fibrous amber patience. You are fortunate to have read even a single sentence of its ongoing story. Return to your world of iron and haste slowly. The cold stays in the bones longer than you think, and the thrumming song of the salamanders will echo in your sternum for days, reminding you that some places are not ruins, but merely works in progress, written in wood and water and the grammar of waiting.
