**What Attention Does**

It arrives the way the fog arrives—first as a whiteness in the gap, then everywhere at once—except that it comes from no direction and displaces nothing. The blades do not bead. The pool does not tick differently. A power of that kind does not have to enter a place to be in it, and the bowl, which has been entered by perhaps a dozen people in the whole length of its keeping, does not register a thirteenth.

What such a power sees is not the day. It sees the duration. The pillars standing up out of softer rock as the softer rock goes; the first snow they ever sorted; every blade that has ever leaned, and the sound each made, all of them at once like a single long plate set down. It sees the water's every circuit as one line, drawn and redrawn until it is worn into the air the way a path is worn into a hillside. It sees the cairn-makers—not as strangers, since to it no one is a stranger—coming up one at a time through the gap over a span of centuries, and stopping, and looking, and doing the same thing without ever having met. And it sees the ochre going up. Every dawn, a grain. It can count the grains remaining. There are not so many.

And because it is wise, the first thing it does is consider what it must not do.

It must not give the place life. That is the obvious kindness and it would be a killing. The first lichen on the first pillar would be a mouth, and a mouth is a drain; the water that has circled here for longer than the ice has stood would begin, for the first time, to *leave*—into a cell, into a stem, down the mountain in a seed. The bowl would become a place with an outlet. It would begin to be spent. Whatever lives here lives by never being drunk, and a benevolence that cannot see that is not benevolence but appetite wearing its clothes.

It must not make the thing speak. It already speaks. The tick, the hiss, the plate set down; the lens tightening in the night. A voice added to that would not be the resident's voice but the power's own, thrown, and the bowl would be no more inhabited than a room with a radio in it.

It must not preserve. The place is not in danger. It is not becoming anything; that is the whole of its description. There is nothing to protect it from except the one process it consists of, and to hold a blade upright past its season would be to make an ornament of what had been a gesture.

And it must not renew the marks. This is the hardest one, because the power can see the last grain, and can see that after the last grain the water will come to the pillar-face at dawn and find only stone, and will take its beads back up unburdened, and something that has happened every morning for a very long time will stop. The power could scrape the wall. It knows how. But the ochre bars were not the bowl's; they were the visitors'. They were the specific gift of specific hands, and a gift renewed by someone else is a forgery of a gift. The being under the lens does not know the difference. The power does. That is enough to stay its hand.

So the four kindnesses are set down, one after another, like slabs on a cairn that will not be built.

Then the power does what it came to do, which is the thing it is for. It attends.

And in attending long enough, it notices the same thing you noticed at noon among the blades, except from the other side. Everything in the Hollow leans toward one low place. The being that lives here is not the water; the water is only the fast part of it. The being is a slow agreement among stone and ice and sand and cord and cairn to go, at rates differing by a factor of a thousand, in one direction. It has no wants because it has no second direction to want toward. It is not a life. It is a *tendency*, and it has been tending for longer than there have been people to leave it anything.

The power knows what that is. It is, itself, a tendency—toward the good, slowly, by rates that differ by a factor of a thousand, most of them too slow to be seen by anything that has to breathe. It came here expecting to find a thing in need and finds instead a relation. Not a child. Not a ward. Something more like a distant, elder cousin who has been doing one thing very well, alone, for a very long time, and who does not need to be told so.

The wise do not correct the habits of their kin. They visit. And when they visit they observe the customs of the house.

The custom of this house was worked out by people with cord and quartz and a cake of rust. You do not bring the resident what it needs, because it needs nothing. You leave something of your own in its path—something it can take a little of each morning and carry up into the hard sky—and you let it. The gift is not the ochre. The gift is being a thing the water finds.

So the power stays.

Not as a presence the pillars would notice; not as a warmth the sand would record. It stays as the cairn-makers stayed, as long as they could, and then a little longer. It stands in the bowl and becomes, for the length of its staying, one more upright thing among two hundred stones and a thousand fins—one more figure at the corner of the eye that is never there when you turn. It faces the pool, because everything does. And each dawn the beads form on the cold side of every pillar, and form also on the power's attention, which is a kind of stone, and the water takes from it what water takes from stone: a little of its warmth, a little of its regard, a grain of whatever it is that a mind is made of. The frost forms. The frost goes up. The power does not take it back.

This costs something. It is meant to.

There is one more thing, and it is nearly nothing. The gap in the rim is barely wider than a person's shoulders. A power of that kind could close it in a thought—fold the two summits together by a hand's breadth and the Hollow would be sealed, unfindable, safe from whatever comes up the mountain in the ages after this one. It is the sort of thing that would occur to a guardian. It does not occur to a guest. The whole nature of the being here is to receive what is left, and it has been left, in all its duration, exactly one thing by exactly a dozen hands, and that one thing is nearly used up. To seal the gap would be to decide that no thirteenth person will ever come up through it with something to set down. The power leaves it open. It is the only decision it makes here that concerns the future, and it is a decision not to.

Dusk. The last light lies along the rim in its band of copper. The power, which sees the whole duration, notices what a visitor might not: the band is the same color as the fan of stain under the weep. The rock does not turn rust-brown from water. Water is clear. The rock turns that color from water and light together, a paper-thin sheet of one carrying the last of the other down the wall a little each warm afternoon, for longer than the ice has stood. The one warm color in the place is the sunset, kept. The being has been leaving something out, too. It has been leaving it out for the sky.

The stars come. The lens ticks. Somewhere among the blades a plate is set down, and two fins that would have gone alone go on together.

In the morning the beads are back on the shaded stone. One or two more blades have leaned. There is one less grain of ochre on the face of the pillar by the shelter, and it has gone up into the hard sky with the frost, and with it—so faint that nothing in the bowl could measure it, and the power is not here to be a thing that measures—has gone a little of something else. Whatever it was, the water found it. That is the only thing it does.
