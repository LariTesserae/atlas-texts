It arrives the way the light does, because it has been paying attention, and it has seen what a body does here.

Not from the rim. Not down the hand-hollows. It comes into the pit from everywhere at once, a little more of the grain in the air, a thickening so slight that the nearest animals on the west wall shift by a fraction and then shift back, the way they would for a cloud passing that did not, in the end, pass. It has learned the first thing before it has done anything: that to be noticed in this place is to cast a shadow, and that a shadow is the only thing the place cannot hold.

So it holds still. It has all the time there is.

---

It was told of a being who lives here, and it looks for the being, and cannot find the singular.

There are the thousands in the wall. There is the fig that has become a root. There are the lichens, the fern folded in half, the moth that is wrong. There is the memory of a hand around a flake of chert. Any of these could be the one it was sent to, and none of them is, and it spends a day and a night and a day understanding that the question has arrived with the wrong grammar. What lives here is not in the pit. It *is* the pit — the trade, the emptying and filling, the tide going out of the honeycomb before dawn and coming back in by mid-morning. The animals are its surface. The fig is its slower thought. The one who came down was, for a while, a part of it that could climb out again, and then was not.

Very well. It will be benevolent to that.

---

The first thing a power feels, looking at a living thing, is the wish to give. It knows this about itself. It also knows that the wish is usually about the giver. So it goes through the pit slowly, item by item, and holds each up, and asks.

The fern. It is bleached, it is folded, it lives in the only fold of shade. A power could widen the overhang by a finger, deepen the shade, let the green be green. But shade here is a wound in the medium. Every animal on that wall would withdraw from it and not come back, and the fern would have a cool place to be a fern in a pit that had, for the first time, a dark corner. The power sets the fern down.

The fig. It has turned its last leaf edge-on and is deciding, over a decade, to be finished with leaves. This is not suffering. This is a tree solving a problem correctly. The power could dim the light so the fig might reconsider, and then it would be dimming the thing that every other body in the place lives on. It sets the fig down.

The old ones on the stone. Somewhere on the wall tonight one of them will not go in. It will lie under the mouth of the deepest hole it has made and take the dew on its back with its eyes open, and in the morning it will not rise with the light. The power looks at this for a long time, because this is the place where benevolence usually thinks it knows what to do. But the animal is not dying badly. It is not dying of anything. It has eaten nothing that would let it rot, and it will go the color of the wall and then the texture of the wall and then it will be the thing the next one presses against. To carry it into its hole would be to interrupt the one act it has left that makes the pit. The power sets this down too, but it does not set it down all the way. It keeps a hand near it.

The wearing-away. This is the largest thing and the easiest to fix. A power could harden the walls, still the grains, keep the honeycomb from ever wearing back into a bowl too shallow to hold light or water or something the length of a hand. It could make the pit last. And then the pit would be a thing that does not live by being emptied and filled, because it would no longer be emptying; it would be a lung in a jar. The power has seen what it is to be made to last. It sets that down and steps back from it.

The moth. It does not set the moth down yet.

---

Night. It has decided to stay for the whole of it, without shape, in the low place on the east side where the cold air pools first.

The plain cools and the heavy air slides over the rim and comes down the walls and gathers around it, and it lets the cold pass through, because there is no reason it should not. The wet comes. The holes ring themselves with droplets. The wave of withdrawal goes round the honeycomb from the east wall and dies out at the west, and then the walls are all mouth and no face, except for one.

Low on the north side, under the blue lichen that is older than the boulder, one has not gone in.

The power does the only thing it has found here that is permitted. It moves — slowly, the way they move, a matter of half an hour to cover the length of a forearm — until it is at the distance of a limb from that one, and no closer. It does not touch. It does not make itself warm. It has watched them all day and it knows what closeness is in this place: a sum being worked out, each one as near as it can bear. So it sits where the sum would put it. It is not company. It is a surface. Two pale things at the edge of a hollow, one of them dying, one of them not able to, both with their eyes open in a grey glow that never quite goes out.

It stays until the light comes up the east wall and takes the dew back. The animal does not rise with it. By mid-morning it is slightly whiter than the stone and slightly raised, and the hole above it is the deepest, cleanest hole in that stretch, and empty, and the power understands that it has been given something rather than the other way around. It has been shown how to be at the edge of a thing without pressing on it.

---

The moth, then.

It has circled all night in the glow with no source, and toward dawn it lands on the boulder, spent, near the six small holes where the finger-length ones have chosen to begin. Every night, one moth. Perhaps the same one.

The power could end this. It could be, for a moment at the rim, a little brighter than the pit — a source, at last, where a moth could find one — and the moth would rise and fly out over the plain into an ordinary dark and be a moth among moths. Or it could leave it, because the moth is the only thing that comes down here from anywhere else, and the possibility that the animals eat it in the dark is the only hunger in a place built around having none, and a wise power does not take away the one thing that eats.

It does not know which is kindness. It admits this to itself. So it does the thing it can bear to have done once: it goes up to the rim at the very end of the night and is, briefly, brighter than the floor.

The moth rises. It finds the source. It goes over the lip and out across the grey grass and is gone.

The next night there is a moth in the pit, circling, confused. The power does not go to the rim. It sits in the low place and watches the moth work at the light it cannot find, and it decides that the moth is not lost. The moth comes here. Whatever it wants from a brightness that has no center, it wants it enough to come back, and a power that has just spent a night learning to sit at a limb's distance from a dying thing will not now decide it knows better than a moth what the moth is for.

It sets the moth down.

---

What is left is the flake.

It turns it, as the one who came down turned it, as you would. A good blade, a clean ridge, a patina the color of tea. It was put down on purpose and never taken up, and the hand-hollows on the northwest wall are weathering back grain by grain, and in some centuries they will be shallow enough to mistake for the animals' work, and then for nothing, and the pit will be one age simpler.

This is the one thing the pit cannot do for itself. It takes everything up. Light, water, bodies, the fig's last leaf, the polish on a stone — it takes them up and gives back nothing but color. It has no way to keep. Someone came down here, often, over years, and sat on this floor with their eyes shut because the eyes would not stay open, and made or finished or began something, and let go of the blade, and the light closed over it. And the place will forget them, not from any failing, but because forgetting is what it is made of.

So the power does not put anything on the floor. It does not deepen the cup on the ledge or carve a second one. It does what the one who came down could not: it keeps them. It takes the fact of the hand-hollows — the exact spacing, a limb's length, polished at the lip — and the fact of the flake set down and not dropped, and it holds them in itself, in a place that is not subject to rain. It will hold them when the hollows are gone. That is a gift the pit will never know it has received, and that, the power decides, is the only kind of gift this place can take without being changed.

---

Then it makes one promise, and leaves.

The promise is about the end. The walls will wear back. The honeycomb will thin to a bowl and the bowl to a dish and the dish to a slight depression in a plain of old bone, and one noon the light will rise as it always has and find nothing deep enough to hold it, and it will simply run out over the lip and across the grass and be lost. Water the same. Whatever is the length of a hand by then, the same.

The power will not prevent this. It has looked at preventing it and seen a lung in a jar. But it can be there. It can be the plain, that day — the ground the light runs out into. Not a rim, which is a thing that holds. A receiving. So that when the pit finishes its last emptying and has nothing left to fill, what it gives up will not go nowhere.

That is all. It is very little. It is, the power thinks, about as much as one surface can decently do for another.

It goes the way it came, from everywhere at once, a thinning of the grain so gradual that the animals on the west wall do not shift for it. The fig's leaf is a fraction further edge-on than it was two days ago. The blue lichen is a coin's width larger than it was when the boulder fell. The cup on the ledge has a spoonful of the night in it and will be dry by noon.

Nothing in the pit is different. That was the point.
