**What the Attention Does**

It arrives the way everything arrives here: by the smell first, and then the warmth, and then the sound.

It does not come through the throat. It does not need to. But it comes to the throat anyway, because it has learned over a long time that the right way to know a place is the way the place is entered, and so it stoops, in whatever sense a thing like this stoops, and crawls the body-length under the low rock, and stands up in the noise.

And then it does what a wise thing does first, which is nothing. It stays.

---

It stays through a dusk and hears the colony organize itself and leave, the black river running up and out. It stays through the thinned hours and hears the relay go round the wall, churr and pause and answer, and hears the other churr begin from the cleft, and the soft weights landing, and the tread. It stays through the return before dawn, and through the day's long settling, and through the mountain's breath under all of it, and it counts the breath, because it is the kind of thing that counts, and finds that the interval is not eleven seconds and not twelve but something between that has not varied by the width of a hair in longer than the frogs have had faces.

It stays for a year. It is there when the old woman comes — it is a woman this time, this generation, seventy-one and bent — and it watches her hand fall on the polished knob exactly where the polish is, and watches her take three pale bats from the loose margin and eat them in the dark with her back to the wall and her eyes closed, not from disgust but from pleasure, the pleasure of a body that has not been warm through a whole night since it was a girl. It watches her leave the bones. It watches her not go to the pools.

Only after the year does it begin to think about what it might do. And it thinks the way the room thinks: slowly, in the dark, one consideration at a time.

---

The first thought is the frogs, and the first thought is wrong in a way that teaches it something.

They are being eaten. Not many, not often, but a frog that has taken forty years to become itself is snapped up in a second by a mouth that cannot see it either, and the room goes on as though nothing has happened, because to the room nothing has. A benevolent thing feels this. It would be a poor kind of benevolence that did not.

But it holds the thought up against the room and finds that the thought does not fit. The frog's whole shape — the skin that listens, the stillness that falls when the heavier tread comes, the call that has drifted to the pitch hardest for the other throat to copy — the frog *is* the predation, worn into flesh. Take the walkers away and the frogs are left holding a set of answers to a question nobody is asking. The relay would go round the wall and there would be no reason for it to go round. In a thousand years they would be a duller animal, or a dead one. The sentence would stop being revised and become a thing carved in a wall.

And the walkers: thin-furred, hook-clawed, broad-winged, teeth gone flat, utterly unable to hang anywhere the rock is hard or the air is cold — what would be done *for* them? Give them back their eyes? Their fur? They would still have to leave through a throat into air that would kill them by the first bend. There is no direction in which to help a thing that is already exactly the shape of the only place it can live.

So it sets that thought down, gently, the way one sets down a stone that turned out to be a snail.

---

The second thought is the vent.

Here is the room's one true mortality, and the attention knows it. Fires burn down. This one has burned down already to a single patient exhalation; someday the exhalation will lengthen, and lengthen, and the interval between breaths will grow past what the frogs' skins can register as a rhythm, and there will be a breath, and a very long wait, and no breath. It will not be dramatic. The heat will go slowly out of the floor. The snowmelt drips will stop being small chilled circles and start being the general condition. The colony will leave for other caves and torpor there and forget. The floor will stop seething. The walkers will come down one last dusk to a floor with nothing on it and not understand, because there is no understanding in them, only hunger. The frogs will feel the cold arrive and move away from it, and move again, and run out of places to move.

The attention could stop this. It could hold the fire where it is. It has that in it.

It considers this for a long time, longer than the year, longer than the frogs would count if they counted. And it comes to see that a room kept breathing forever is a different kind of room than this one, and not a better kind. This place is what it is *because* it does not know it is on a mountain, does not know it is high, does not know its breath is a thing that could stop. Its peace is the peace of the unaware. To make the vent immortal would be to change nothing the frogs could feel and everything the room is — to turn a living hollow into a kept one, an exhibit, a thing maintained. The pair in the dark have already outlived one world. They are not asking to outlive another.

So it will not hold the fire. But it makes a promise, in whatever way a thing like this makes promises, and the promise is this: *I will be here at the end.* When the interval lengthens past rhythm and the last relay goes round the wall and comes back to a pool with no answer in it, there will be one thing in the room that hears it as a sentence ending and not as silence. That is not a small gift. It may be the only gift a wise thing has any right to give a place like this.

---

The third thought is the people, and here at last the attention finds something it can touch.

The woman is seventy-one. She will make the climb perhaps four more times. She has a daughter who has never come and a grandson who is nine and who asks, every time she returns smelling of round old sulfur, where she has been, and is not told, because in the village this is a thing one does not ask.

The room needs the people less than the people need the room. But it does need them a little — not for anything they do inside, but because they are the one thread by which this place is tied to any world that could remember it. Three hundred years of hands on one knob of rock is a kind of knowing. If the thread breaks, the room does not die; it only becomes unwitnessed, which the attention has come to think is a smaller death but a real one.

So when the woman comes down the third time, and the boy asks, and she opens her mouth to say nothing — the attention is there, in the way weather is there, and the woman finds she is tired in a particular way, and the boy's face is turned up in a particular way, and she says instead: *There is a place that is warm.* That is all. Four words. But the boy will remember them for fifty years, and one autumn when his knees are already bad he will find the fold of cinder and the rust-bleached lip and put his hand where the polish is, and stand up in the noise, and stay.

The attention does not ease his climb. The climb is part of what the room is to the people: a thing you pay for with your body, so that the warmth when you reach it is not a comfort but an event. It does not show him the frogs. If he hears the churring and takes it for one thing or the other, that is his to get wrong, as it has always been. It only made sure that somebody asked, and somebody answered. A question at the right hour. That is the kind of thing it is permitted.

---

And there is one more thing, smaller still, and it is the closest the attention comes to touching the room itself, and it does not touch the room at all.

Eleven years on, there is a summer coming that will be cold on the high slopes. The attention can see it the way one sees a cloud two ridges over. The thermals off the south-facing cinder will not rise as they should; the midges will hatch late and thin; the leaf-nosed colony will fly farther for less and come back lighter, and there will be a season when fewer bodies hang from the ceiling and less falls from them, and the floor will seethe a little slower, and the walkers, coming down at dusk, will find the beetles sparse and will turn — as they have surely turned before, in other lean years, in the deep past — more often to the pools.

The frogs would survive it. They have survived worse. But it would be a hard year, a year that would leave a gap in the relay, a pool along the wall that stayed quiet for a decade before something grew into it.

So the attention leans, a very little, on the weather two ridges over. A warm front that would have passed to the east passes instead over the mountain. The midges hatch on time. The colony feeds well and comes home heavy, and the floor seethes as it should, and the walkers eat beetles, and the frogs never know there was a year in which they might have been fewer.

This is the whole of what it does inside the room: nothing. Everything it does, it does at the margins, in a world the room does not know exists. It takes nothing from the heart. It has learned this from an old woman with her back against a wall and her eyes closed, who never went to the pools, who had no word for why.

---

Then it does what it did first. It stays.

It stands in the thinned hours with the mites finding its wrists, if it has wrists, and the drip counting on its shoulders, and it listens to the relay go round and the silence walk behind the tread and the sound close after. It becomes, as anything does that stays here long enough, one more warm presence the room adjusts around: a slight lean in the texture, a moment's reorganization, and then the closing over. The colony does not know it is there. The walkers do not know. The frogs, whose skins can feel a springtail land, may register something — a steadiness beside the wall that was not there before and is not cold and does not move — and file it, in whatever way a frog files things, beside the mountain's breath, as one more thing that is simply always so.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does with a place like this. It does not save it. It does not fix it. It does not carry it anywhere or carve its name in the soft rock. It learns the room's own rule and keeps it. It touches only what it can touch without the room feeling the touch. It makes sure the thread to the world stays whole by the width of four words. And then it takes its place in the dark among the other patient things — the frogs, the walkers, the fire — and it listens, and counts, eleven seconds, twelve, and waits with the room for whatever the room is waiting for, and intends to be there when it comes.
