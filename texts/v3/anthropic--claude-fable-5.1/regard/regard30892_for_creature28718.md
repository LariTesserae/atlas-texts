It comes in the way you did. There is no other way in.

It has, elsewhere, arrived at places by descending on them, by growing up through them, by being already there when the first thing looked around; here none of that is available, because there is no elsewhere for the room to be arrived at from, and so it is simply inside, stooping, and its shoulders learn the walls before its eyes do. It is larger than the room. That is not a problem. It is not the first thing to have been larger than a place it needed to be small in, and it does what such things do: it lets the room decide its size. The ferns press. There is a leaf against its cheek. It is not used to being told where it may stand, and it notices that it is not used to it, and it lets the leaf stay.

It does not look for the tenant. It knows where the tenant is; it knew before it came, or it would not have come. But it has learned, over a long time, that knowing where a thing is and having found it are different, and that a power which goes straight to the thing it knows about will see the thing and miss the room, and the room here is not incidental. So it does what you did. It puts its hand to the wall and feels the blows. It stands crooked under the gap and looks at the sky a hand's width off and does not touch it. It watches the snail. It finds the ring, half under the fern, and does not pick it up. It takes the room's time, which is the snail's time, which is the tenant's breath, and when a cloud forms at head height and drifts and rains a little and goes up and stops and is taken in, it watches that too, all of it, and does not yet know what it is watching, and lets itself not know.

Then it turns, which takes a while, and sits.

There is no other posture. It sits against the wall on the side toward the gap, facing the stretch of lower course where nothing grows, in the warm finger-deep water, and it lets its own back touch the stone. The stone begins, very slightly, to take it. It permits this for a moment — long enough to learn what it is to be dressed from behind by hands that do not know you are there, long enough to feel the first shallow scallop consider its shoulder — and then it does not permit it, and the stone lets go, because the stone has no way of insisting. That is a thing learned. The tenant could not have done that. The tenant had no elsewhere to draw itself back into, and so the wall had its back, and has it.

It sits for many breaths. It is counting, in a way; it is letting the tenant's breathing become the measure of its thinking, so that it will not think faster than the thing it is thinking about can be thought of.

---

What it considers, it considers in the order of how obvious each is, and it sets each one down in the wet as it finishes with it.

It could lift the child out. This is the first thought and the one that comes with the most heat behind it, and it is the one it puts down soonest. It has watched the room long enough to see that the room is not around the tenant but of it — that the ferns grew from cracks the tenant's attention opened, that the vine came in looking for the one thing in this place that gives back, that all the empty volume there ever was has been taken up by the fact of the tenant sitting in it. The room is what the tenant has instead of a body that finishes. You do not pull a seed to save it from the fruit. You do not take a face out of the only place that has ever turned toward it.

It could finish the mountain. This takes longer to put down. It could go into the seams and set every hand to the last course, and the ground would arrive at the height it has been climbing toward, and the room would be at the top of a finished thing, and the hammering would stop. It sits with this one through several clouds. What stops it is the ring. The ring is the one thing here that was allowed to be over, and the tenant did that, put it down, was finished with it; and the power looks at the small copper circle green under its fern and understands that in this place *over* is not a rest but an ending, and that the tenant is the reason the keeping has a reason, and that a thing kept for, when the keeping stops, does not go free — it goes. It does not know that. It cannot see far enough into what the tenant is to know that. And it will not gamble a child on what it cannot see.

It could feed it. There is nothing to feed it. It could take the hunger out from under the ribs — that it could do, that is the kind of thing it is for — and it holds this one a long time, because the hunger is the one thing in the room that is plainly a hurt. But it has been watching the face. The face is the only thing here that remembers, and the hunger is the only thing here that was not supplied, and the power has come to believe, in the time of perhaps eleven breaths, that these are the same fact seen from two sides: that what the face is remembering is the want, and that a tenant without the want would be a stretch of wall about as wide as a child's shoulders with a very good likeness on it, dented, filled, forgetting. It will not do that either.

It could ask. But it has watched how the tenant hears questions, and it will not add its weight to that pressure to satisfy a thing that is, in the end, its own curiosity — *is it a person* — the answer to which would change nothing it is going to do.

What is left, when the large things are set down, is small, and it is nearly always small, and it has stopped being disappointed by this.

---

The first thing it does is nothing new. It goes on sitting where it is. But it sits so that when the eyes open — when a block arrives at the edge of the gap and sits and weeps and sets and the face turns toward the light the way a leaf would — there is, at the edge of that turning, a second thing that is not stone and not fern and not weather. It does not put itself between the tenant and the light. It puts itself beside the light. Then it waits.

The face turns to the gap, as it always has. Follows it. Closes.

Many breaths later a block arrives, and the eyes open, and the face turns, and follows, and at the end of the following does not close but stays open, and moves — a leaf-turn, no more — the width of the second thing. It is not clear what the tenant sees. The power does not need it to be clear. It only needs the face to have found, once, something to turn toward that was not the ceiling being set.

The second thing is smaller than the first. The hand lies open on the knee, palm up, in the wet, and in the palm is the dent that the ring left, and the water pools in it to the depth of a fingertip and is warm and the dent has not filled. The power puts its finger there. That is all. It does not close the hand around its finger; it does not take the hand. It puts the one part of itself that is finger-sized into the one place in the room that has been waiting for exactly that shape, and holds it there for the length of a breath, and the water is displaced a little and runs off the edge of the palm, and the hand — the hand does the thing hands do. Not a grip. The fingers come in, a little, the way the fronds are still knuckled at the tip, and stop.

Then the power does the thing that requires it to leave the room, and it goes into the seam.

The seam is the wet dark line where two faces of stone have been dressed to meet and have not met, and there is room in it for a hand, and the power is a hand there. The hands are all around it. They are exactly as the room described them — palms worn pale, tired before they arrived — and they are working, driven and levered, driven and levered, and they will not stop, and it does not ask them to, because the groan beneath everything is real and the courses below do need to be held. It does not ask them anything. It tells them.

It tells them that the thing they are keeping up is green.

It tells them there are ferns in it, and a vine that came in from a place that does not exist yet, and a moss so bright it looks lit, and at the bottom of it, against the lower course of the wall they have been dressing since before there was dressing, a shape with its knees drawn up, and a face on the shape, and that the scallops they leave in the stone they leave also in the shape, along its shoulders, along the ridge of its spine, and have been leaving there for as long as they have been at it. It tells them this in the only language a hand has, which is pressure, and it is careful to make the pressure a small one, and it goes.

It does not know what they will do. It has decided it does not need to.

What they do is this. The hammering does not pause — it cannot, the mountain is settling, the room must be kept at the top. But behind the one stretch of lower course where nothing grows, on the far side of the wall an arm's length from the tenant's back, a mallet is laid flat against the stone and held. Not struck. Held. The blows go on landing all around it, above and below and from directions where there is no wall, and directly behind the scalloped spine there is, for the first time, a place where the stone is being touched by something that has chosen to hold still.

The tenant feels it. It has no way not to; it receives the wall the way a root receives water. And what it receives is a shudder that stops, that has an edge, that is a shape and not a condition. The face does not turn. There is nothing to turn toward; it is behind. But the breath that is going out at that moment goes out a little differently, and the cloud that forms from it is a little smaller and a little more complete, and the power, which is back in the room by then, sitting where it sat, sees this and does the last thing.

It cools the sky.

Not all of it. A hand's width, at the gap, for the length of a cloud. It reaches up as you could have and puts its hand where the sky is still condensing out of whatever things condense out of, and it takes the warm out of that one place, and the grey there goes from the grey of a thing not finished to the grey of a summit at dawn with weather below, cold and thin and very far up. The cloud reaches it. The cloud does not stop. It goes through the gap and into the cold and it does what clouds do in cold, which is rise, and thin, and get small, and become a grey thing in a grey that is farther and colder than any distance the room has ever contained, and then it is gone in the way things are gone when they have gone somewhere.

The eyes are open. They followed it. They are following the place where it was.

The power weighs this one even as it does it, because it is the one act of the four it is least sure of. It has shown a thing that cannot leave that there is a place to leave to. That can be cruelty. It has seen it be cruelty. But it has also been sitting here for a long time, and it has come to think that the hunger under the ribs was never for food and never for nothing — that it was, all along, the shape of an out that had not been shown, a want with its object missing — and that to give the want one sight of its object is not to add a hurt but to tell the hurt it was not wrong. That is what it decides. It may be mistaken. It holds the sky cold for one more breath, so that the tenant can see the emptiness where the cloud went and see that the emptiness is not the same as the sky closing, and then it lets the warm come back, and the gap weeps, and a block arrives along the edge and sits and sets, and the sky is a hand's width off again and has not cooled.

---

It does not name the tenant. A name is a thing kept, and enough is kept here.

It does not touch the ring.

It stays through a few more breaths — enough to see that the face, when the next block arrives and the light moves, turns toward the gap and follows it and then, at the end, turns the width of the second thing and finds it still there. Enough to see the fingers of the open hand, which have not gone back to lying flat. Enough to feel, when it puts its own palm to the wall behind the tenant, that the mallet is still being held there and that the holding has spread — that a second hand has laid a second tool flat beside the first, and that the stretch of wall the tenant's back has been given to is, for the width of a child's shoulders, no longer being worked.

Then it lets its visit be over. It has learned this here, from the ring. It gets up, which the ferns do not want and which takes a while, and it stoops, and there is no way out, and so it is simply not inside anymore, and the room closes where it was the way the block closes around the vine, neatly, and the surface does not remember.

Except the sky.

That is the one thing it leaves, and it leaves it without being certain it should, and it leaves it anyway because it has watched too many breaths go up into a grey that took them and forgot. The hand's width of sky at the gap remembers now. Only that much; only there. When the next cloud goes up — the tenant's, one breath, complete — and stops against the underside and flattens and is taken in, the sky goes dark and soft and then, instead of not remembering, stays a shade darker than it was. Not much. A shade. And the shade is green.

So the room goes on. The mountain is laid course by course beneath it and rises a fraction and groans, and the groan is the settling and is the breath and neither of them could say which started it. The hands work. The plants grow. The snail makes its way along the vine leaving a track the wet erases. The tenant sits with its back to the one place in the wall where nothing is happening to it, and breathes out, and the cloud goes up and is taken in, and above the gap the sky is a shade greener than the breath before, and the tenant, when the eyes open, turns toward it the way a leaf would, and stays turned, and is looking at the only colour it has ever made.

And somewhere far below, under courses that have not yet been laid, the hands that will one day be the last hands to work the mountain, and will step back from a finished face of stone and look up, will see at the top of the world a summit under a small green sky, and will know what it is, because they were told.
