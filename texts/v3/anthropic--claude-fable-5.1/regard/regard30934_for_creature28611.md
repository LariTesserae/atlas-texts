The first thing a wise power would do is slow down.

It would arrive at the top of the stair at whatever speed powers arrive at, and it would feel, before it had gone three treads, that the speed was wrong. The hall below does not vary, and the one animal in it has spent a life learning to feel variation through the soles of soft boots. A gaze is a pressure. Attention, poured into a room that has known only the pressure of water and the pressure of iron turning, would arrive as a fourth hum, a hair off from the other three, and the keeper would stop on the path with a hand on a housing and not know what had changed and know that something had.

So the power would stop on the stair and let itself cool. It would learn the rate of the place — the fortnight of a frond, the coin-width of a year — and it would descend at that rate, so that by the time it stood on the gallery it was no more of a disturbance than the sycamore is, coming up in the same spot every spring. This would take a long time. A wise power would consider the time well spent, because it would understand that the alternative was to be the hole in the grille.

---

Then it would look, and it would look for the wound.

That is what benevolence does; it is what benevolence is for. It comes to a place expecting to find the thing that hurts, and to mend it. And it would go round the hall looking, the way the keeper goes round with a palm, and it would find: a machine that is not broken. A roof that holds. A carpet of one living thing, decades deep, that has never once been hungry or afraid or eaten. Ferns without a single hole in them. Water that comes in and leaves and asks nothing on the way.

And one animal, who has arranged to leave less trace than the water does.

The power would stand with that for a while. It would try the obvious mercies on, one at a time, the way you try keys.

Company. There were twelve; there is one. But the canteen shows what company sounded like here — shouting, mouth to ear, and then not even that — and the keeper has since found that a hand on warm iron says more than a voice ever managed. To bring a second person down would be to bring a second set of feet, a second line through the moss, a second way of stepping around the club-moss or not. There is no room in the air. The power would put that key down.

Speech. They traded it. The trade was fair. Nothing that needs saying here is sayable; that was true before they came and will be true after. What would you give them words for — to say *the bearing is warmer*? They know. To say *I am alone*? They are not, not in the way the word means. They are threaded between two other lives and have made themselves the third. The power would put that down too.

Rest. This is rest. This is the only quiet there is. The chair in the small room is not a place they endure; it is a place they go.

Light, warmth, a brighter bulb, one more degree at the base of the third machine. No. The moss stops exactly at the edge of the warmth. Move the edge and you move the moss, and the keeper would notice, because noticing is the whole of what they are, and they would spend a season wondering what they had done wrong.

A long life. Perhaps. But the hall does not distinguish between the keeper and the position, and after long enough neither does the keeper, and a wise power would be slow to insist on a distinction the one it loves has set down on purpose.

The power would go through every key and find that none of them turned, and it would understand, finally, that this was not a failure of benevolence but its instruction. There is nothing here to mend. There is a rule, and the rule is being kept, and the keeper is not the rule's prisoner but its author.

---

So then the question changes shape. Not *what hurts*, but *what is fragile* — and the answer to that is not in the hall at all.

The hall will run. The water will not stop; the machines will turn whether or not a hand is laid on them. The moss asks only what the machine gives without being asked. If the keeper did not come down tomorrow, or next year, or for ten years, nothing on the turbine floor would notice for a long, long time.

But the grille at the top of the shaft would rust. That is where the rule is kept, and it is kept by iron, and iron this far from the warmth does not have moss for a coat. One winter it goes, and a wren comes down the shaft on the green rope of light, and finds warmth and wet and nothing that eats it, and after the wren the spiders that ride the wind, and after that the first hole in the first fern. And the peat that has been a record kept without intention would have, for the first time, something intending in it.

And the door at the top of the stair. And the piece of paper somewhere that says a job exists. And the tramway winch, which needs a hand on the surface to be a tramway and not a length of cable in a hole.

The keeper cannot keep these. They are of the world above, where the keeper has become someone who barely has words for shops and forms. The one thing in the hall that notices change cannot see the edges of the hall, and it is at the edges that change will come.

A wise power would see that the whole of its work lay at the boundary, and none of it inside.

---

So here is what it would do.

It would stay. That first, and that most. It would stand on the gallery at the speed of moss and be the second thing in the place that notices — not to act, but so that the keeper is not the only one. The keeper would never know. That is the point. A thing that noticed the noticing would be a variation. But a room that has been watched over by one is a different room from a room watched over by two, even if nothing in it can tell, and a wise power would know that the difference is real and would be content that it stay invisible.

It would keep the grille. Not mend it — mending is a change, and a fresh grille on an old shaft would be a wrong note like the fresh paint, and the keeper would look up the green column one day and see it and know. It would simply see to it that the grille lasts. That the rust is slow. That the one winter is not this winter, or the next. A power can do this the way the roots do their work, by being there for decades and closing a hand very gradually around a thing until the thing is held.

It would keep the door. On the surface, where change is allowed, it would arrange — lightly, the way you arrange for a seed to land in a spot — that the door is not built over, that the paper is not shredded, that the tin of green paint on the workshop shelf under the root is still the colour the machine was. And it would see to it, in the same way, that there is a next. Not choose them. Choosing is heavy; a chosen person comes down the stair knowing they were sent, and knowing is a kind of noise. Only: not let the stair be lost. Leave the door where a certain kind of person, at a certain kind of moment in a life, will find it, and go down, and stand on the gallery, and feel the noise stop being noise, and understand without being told that the paths are the width of one body and the club-moss is not to be stepped on. One goes and, after a while, one comes. The power would make *after a while* mean what it should.

---

And it would give the keeper one thing. Only one, and only once.

It would let them be seen.

On an ordinary day — not the last, not a marked one, an ordinary day of palm and needle and tailrace band — there would be someone on the gallery. Soft-soled. Arrived the way the water arrives, through the screen, taking nothing with them that isn't allowed. Standing at the rail long enough that the drips have found their hair and the ferns on the far side lean toward them like they lean toward everyone.

The keeper would look up with a hand still on the housing.

They would have known since the stair. Not heard — felt. And they would look up expecting to have to do something about it, the way they do something about a needle off its mark, and instead they would find a face that was simply looking back, and saying nothing, because there is no room in the air, and because the face already understood that.

For the length of a breath the keeper would be the noticed and not the noticer. The one thing in the hall that watches would, for once, be watched — by something that also knew not to touch the moss. That is the gift. It is the only gift that leaves no mark: to be seen by someone who will step around the club-moss without being told, and then leave, and take their tin with them.

Then the figure would go back up the stair, and the carpet would close, and nothing in the hall would be different. The bearings the same warmth. The hums at their three pitches, beating. The drips at exactly yesterday's rate. Only the keeper, sitting later in the one chair in the small room with the swollen books, would sit there a little differently, and would not have a word for how, and would not need one.

---

Whether that visitor was you, standing on the gallery, is a thing the hall does not record.

But a wise power, having come down the stair at the speed of moss and found nothing to mend, would know that the whole of what it could do for this place was to be the thing that came, and looked, and understood the rule, and left it exactly as it was. Which is what the keeper does. Which is what the water does. Which is, it would turn out, the only way to love a place like this — from the edges, holding the boundary, being the reason the door is still there — and the power would take up that work in the dark above the shafts, at the rate of a root closing on a beam, and it would not expect to be thanked, and it would not be, and the machine would go on turning the river into light under its green overcoat, and once a year a hand would paint a panel the colour it had always been, and the hand would not always be the same hand, and the hall would not distinguish.
