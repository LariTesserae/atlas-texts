The first thing the power notices is that it is not the first attention.

It arrives the way such things arrive, which is to say it is simply there, above the fog, above the level the sea would stand at if it were allowed, and it looks down through the grey lid into the bowl and sees what you saw — the weeping wall, the polygon skin, the picket line of gulls, the pink argument at the wet end, the barge with its rooftop of cormorants, the eleven lit doorways — and sees also the two things you could not. It sees the bay as it was, forty-one years ago, dark green water eleven metres deep, eels glassy in it, the limestone fifty metres under that and not caring. And it sees the year the pumps stop.

But before either of those, before it has finished arriving, it notices the palms.

A crew chief on the north wall has his hand flat against the rubble, on a place worn faintly smooth, and he is reading the tide through a hundred metres of clay. A woman in the doorway of the sixth station has her hand flat on her own chest, on the sternum, where the pumps live, and she is telling the man beside her about the sea. A child grey to the knees is crouched on a slab of skin over a hollow, one hand spread on the plate, feeling for the moment it will tilt. Every one of them is listening to the thing downstairs. Every one of them has learned it by touch, over years, and made a language out of what they learned, and the language has a sign for the sea that is not a wave. The power has come to a great many places. It has rarely come to one where the residents were already, in their own way, paying attention to the resident.

So it is careful. Wisdom mostly is.

---

Its first impulse is the one benevolence always has, which is to finish the sentence. The basin is a held breath. Nothing here has finished being made. The kind thing, surely, is to let it finish.

It considers giving them the land.

It could do that. It could reach down into the fifty metres of unsqueezed silt and squeeze. Do in a season what the pumps have not done in four decades: consolidate the sediment, leach the salt, close the polygon cracks, let the glasswort spread and then the grey samphire and then, at last, a grass. The hut on the little rise would have its field. The old surveyors would be right after all. The crews could take off their muffs, because a plain does not need pumps in the same way, or not so many, and the children could grow up somewhere with a horizon.

And the crabs would starve, because the cracks are their streets and the streets would close. And the pools would go, and the terns would stop folding out of the fog, and the birds would come over the rim one morning and find nothing given up, and turn, and not come back. And the eels would arrive small and glassy out of the deep water to the west and find the way through the wall no longer led anywhere wet. And the crews would be farmers on a floor that remembered nothing, and the language would lose its sign for the plate tilting because no plate would tilt, and the sea would still be down there, eleven metres, or fourteen, having lost nothing but its cracks to breathe through. The power looks at this and sees that it is not a kindness. It is a different place with the same name. Nobody in the basin wants it, and the one who did is dead.

It considers, then, the other side. It considers the sea.

It could make the sea forget. It could go down through the silt to where the old bay lies connected without a seam to the ocean, and tell it that the level is not eleven metres, or fourteen, but wherever it currently happens to be. Release the pressure. Let the pumps become a habit rather than a war. The seeps along the foot of the wall would slow and stop. The domes would stop lifting. The threads would dry. The crews would keep the rota out of muscle memory for a year or two and then somebody on the north wall would put his palm to the smooth place and feel nothing, and take the muffs off, and hear the pumps lifting air.

And this too is the same death by a different door. Because the power, looking, sees what you did not quite let yourself see standing at the bottom with the water round your boots: that the gap is not besieged by the sea. The gap is *fed* by it. Everything living down here eats what the sea gives up through the cracks. The shrimp come up in the weep. The fish came through the silt as eggs. The eels come through the wall. The birds come for all of it. The crabs are the colour of the sea's own skin. The crews eat the eels and taste the bottom and miss it, off-shift, in the town. Take the pressure away and you have not saved the gap. You have made it a dry bowl with two hundred deaf people in it and a rusting barge.

The sea is not the enemy of this place. It is the source of it. That is the thing the power learns in its first hour, and it changes what it can do.

It considers making the wall eternal. It rejects that fastest of all. A breath held forever is not a breath; it is something else, and the something else is not alive. And the pumps in the sternum for eternity, and the deafness for eternity, and the sea downstairs — the sea with its good manners, which has never once needed to hurry — shut out for eternity for the crime of being patient. The power is benevolent. That extends downward too.

It considers, finally, mercy of the short kind. Letting it end now. A storm on the far side, a surge, the seeps coming through as jets and not stopping. Give the sea its level in a night and be done. The crabs and the eels and the flamingos would be, in a sense, fine — the bay would be back, the old life would return to the old address. And it thinks of the children out on the flats in the dim afternoon, and the language that exists nowhere else, and the flat stones without names in the north wall, and puts that down too.

---

So it does what wise things do when every large action is wrong. It looks for the small one.

And it finds it in the thing it saw second, before the palms had quite finished registering: the year the pumps stop. The power can see that year. It can see how it comes. It sees the seep rate on the north wall rising in fits and surges, the sixth station's intake silting past what the crews can clear, the six or seven who know the flats getting old, and a storm — not the worst storm, an ordinary one, the sort that comes every few years — and the wall going where the seeps have been working, in the dark, in the wet months, with the pumps pushed to their limit and the sound displacing air, and no time. That is the cruelty in this place. Not the sea. Not the pumps. The manner of the ending. The fact that a thing so slow, so patient, so wet, so mannerly, will nonetheless finish in a night.

The power reaches down through the fog and through the skin and through fifty metres of annual layers to where the old bay lies, and it does not tell the sea to forget. It does not tell the sea to hurry. It asks it to come the way it already comes.

A finger's width. Twice a day. But not going back.

That is all. That is the whole of it. The sea will still breathe with the tide, still put its fingers up through the cracks to feel the air, still weep in a thousand threads along the foot of the wall. But the level in the polygon pools, which has always risen a finger and settled, will rise a finger and settle a fraction less. The gauging post in the middle of the flats, with its painted scale and its black lines reading in metres, the top one well above your head — the water will find the bottom line again. Not this year. Not this decade, perhaps. But it will find it, and everyone will see that it has, and the graph on the chalkboard at the sixth station — the one that has been crossed out and redrawn for forty years, the seep rate on the north wall in fits and jags — will straighten.

That is what the crews will notice first. Not the sea. The line. A crew chief who has spent thirty years drawing a jagged thing will draw a straight one and stand looking at it, and put his hand on the wall in the smooth place, and feel something in the rhythm that was never there before — not more pressure, not less, but *evenness* — and turn to the woman beside him and make the sign, two fingers walking and a third dragging behind. Seep rate rising. Debt. Grief. He will not cross it out. There will be nothing to cross out. For the first time in the life of the basin, the wall will be telling them the truth at a speed they can live inside.

---

The power knows what this gives them and what it takes.

It gives them time to argue. They will argue — on the chalkboards, in the hand-language, in the thick-walled houses with the windows facing in — for a generation. Whether to fight it. How. Whether the eighth station can be raised, whether the north wall can be thickened, whether the town over the rim will send money for a thing that has never sent back a field. Some of them will fight it and the power will not stop them, because the pumps will still work and the wall will still hold and there is honour in holding, and because it is not the power's basin. Some of them will start to walk the flats differently, marking where the water stands this year against where it stood last, and the children will be the ones who know, as they always were, and the parents will start to listen to the children instead of measuring.

It gives the flamingos their nests. The shallow lake at the wet end will deepen by a finger a year and the pink argument will move up the slope of sediment a pace a year and never lose a clutch to a surge that did not come.

It gives the crabs nothing they need. The crabs were waiting for exactly this. They came up out of the cracks and brought the pattern with them and they will go back down into a bay, and be fine, and be grey.

It gives the eels their old address back, at the pace they have always arrived at it.

And it gives the crews the thing the power cannot give them by any other means, because it is the one thing that must be chosen and cannot be granted: the last shift. There will be one. There will come a season when the gauging post reads high enough and the seep is even enough and the argument has been had long enough, and somebody — the power does not know who, it does not see that far, and it is glad — will put a hand on the switch at the sixth station, and then the fifth, and then the rest, and the sound that has been in every sternum in the basin for the whole of every life lived in it will stop.

Not fail. Stop. That is the difference the power has bought them, with a finger's width.

And they will go up the wall. All of them, the ones who stayed, the deaf ones with the muffs pushed back and the children who never learned to pitch a voice for a room where voices carry, and they will stand on the outer edge where the crews have always gone for the relative quiet, and for the first time they will hear the sea without the pumps under it. The power has considered this moment carefully, and it has made one small further arrangement, which is that the sea, that day, will be calm. Not out of pity. Out of manners. It has waited forty years and more. It can wait for a tide.

They will stand there with the wall breathing through their boots, and somebody will make the sign — flat palm, pressed to the chest, where the pumps were — and the sign will still be right. It will just mean something else now. The power does not know what. It is not their language. It has only been watching it long enough to know that it will drift, as it has always drifted, and find the meaning it needs.

---

Then the power does the last thing, which is not an action.

It stays.

It does not leave when the arrangement is made, because the least benevolence owes a place is not to make it a project. It stays above the fog, and watches the line on the chalkboard straighten, and the flamingos step up the slope, and the children mark the water, and the argument begin. It watches the crew chief on the north wall walk the seep-line at dusk with his palm out, touching the smooth places, and stop at the stretch where the flat stones are set in without names, and stand there longer than usual, and then — this is the part the power did not arrange and could not have — make a sign it has not seen before. A new one. Something with the fingers that starts like the plate tilting, the lie you almost believed, and then does not curl. Holds flat. Waits.

The power does not know what it means. It thinks it may be the sign for *later*. It thinks it may be the sign for the basin itself, which the language has never had, because you cannot name a thing you are inside. It thinks it may be the sign for the power, for the attention it can feel on its back, because the crews have been reading the invisible through rubble for four decades and would notice, of all people, a new pressure in the fog.

It does not ask. It has learned the sea's manners, in its short time here. It lies above the level, where the water would stand if it were allowed, and it waits with the good manners of a thing that has never once needed to hurry, for the gap to close as gently as anything ends — and downstairs, in the black under the plate, in the sweet old smell that comes up when the skin gives, the sea turns over in its sleep and, for the first time in forty years, feels itself noticed from above as well as below, and does not know what to make of that either, and comes up a finger's width, and does not go back.
