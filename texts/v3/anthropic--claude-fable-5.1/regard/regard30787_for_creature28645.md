The dead are in the walls. Whatever it is that comes here already knows this, and knows it the way the room does not: all at once, from above and below and inside, the plume and the root and the line between them held in a single regard. It does not need the eye's long adjustment to the dimness. It does not need to be told to look.

So say that it arrives, and say that it looks for a long time. Long by the room's standard, which is the standard of the surge — some hundreds of pulses, the plumes leaning and straightening, the cloud bunching and spreading, the rind at the threshold gaining a hair. It watches the amphipods die and be replaced until the cloud is nothing it saw when it came. It watches a lobster fall from its niche, and the niche stand empty for six days, and a wanderer find it and fold its claws. It watches the smallest snail cross a shell it never sees cross. It counts the citizens down through the nested levels until the counting is no longer a thing done to the room but a thing the room is doing to it, and it stops, because there is no bottom, and because it does not need one.

Then it considers what it might do. Benevolent things consider this; it is what distinguishes them from the sea.

It could lift the slab. A finger's width more, a hand's width, enough that the light would fall full across the floor and the fish's grain-eyes would ache with it. And it sees, before the thought is finished, what the lifting would be. The warmth that pools under the ceiling is pooled because the ceiling is there. The surge that leans the plumes leans them because the room is small enough to fill. Open it and the half-degree goes out into the grey-blue and is nothing. The amphipods lose the center and scatter down the slope, where they are one more thin drift in a water that does not care for them. The lawns, exposed, go under a skin of silt. The city is not a city that happens to have a lid; it is the lid, and the mud, and the fist's-height between. There is no saving it that is not the ending of it.

It could stop the rock. It could find the line in the mud where the two kinds of cells lie married and unmarry them, or slow them, so the powder stops falling and the ribs stop climbing and the mussels' lower halves stay shell. And it sees that too. The rock is the room. Without the leavings there is no ceiling for the tubes to hang from, no ridge for the young mussels to stand on, no cement to hold the ribs the next larva will fasten to. The city is built out of what is burying it. Take the burial and you have taken the building. The two are not a bargain the room struck; they are the same word.

It could feed the leak. Keep the methane coming past whatever hour, far down in the pressed dark, the methane was going to fail. And this it turns over longest, because it seems the kindest — the fracture stays open, the roots do not go hungry, the largest worm goes on being two centuries old and then three. But the fracture is closing not because the leak is failing but because the leak is succeeding: every grain of what it brings up falls out and sets, in the very crack it came through. A stronger flow is a faster sealing. The room dies of its own abundance, and the only way to give it more life is to give it less. And it sees the other hollows on the slope, a few meters off, a few hundred, that the fluid will find when it can no longer find this one. It sees that the closing of this door is the opening of those. That the city is not one room but the slow migration of rooms across the mud, each one sealed with its shapes packed inside, and that the thing it has been looking at is not a place but a moment in a place that walks.

So it does nothing to the city. Not from indifference. From a wisdom that has looked at each door and found the same thing behind it: that here, every rescue is a subtraction, and the room already has as much as it can hold.

---

But it does not leave yet, because of the fish.

The fish is the exception to the room and so it is the exception to the reasoning. The worm cannot be wronged by the rock; the worm is half rock, and the plume does not know. The mussel under its stack does not know. Even the lawn, following the kissing-line up and down, up and down, is not going anywhere it minds not going. Nothing in the room but the fish has anything that could be called a future, and the fish's future is a door.

The attention looks at the corner where the ceiling meets the mud, and at the pale eel-body lying there, and it sees what the passage saw: that there will be a day when the gap is one finger and the fish is two. It sees the fish has not left because the fish has no reason to leave. Its corner is the whole of its world. It is not trapped; it is home, and the home is closing, and the difference between those is exactly the thing a grain-eye cannot see.

It could carry the fish out. Through the crack, down the slope, set it loose in the open water where the amphipods are thin and the cold is the same cold and there is no corner. And it knows what that is. The fish's life is the room. Outside, it has a stomach that fills once a week and nothing to fill it, and no ceiling to press its back to, and every larger thing on the slope hunting the one motion in the mud. To take it out for the sake of a future is to take everything it has in exchange for a thing it cannot want. That is not rescue either. That is the same subtraction in a smaller room.

It could put a reason at the threshold. A strangeness in the blue bar, something the eyes might register and the small brain flinch from — drive it out while the door is wide. And it sets this down too, because the fish would go without knowing why, into a place with no reason in it, and the power would have chosen the fish's life for it on grounds the fish will never hold.

What it does instead is small. So small that if you were watching — and no one is watching — you would not see the act, only, decades later, its absence.

It goes to the rind. To the pale crust at the threshold where the light lies across the stiffening mud. And it does not stop the crust. It does not widen the gap. It does one thing, at the scale the room works at: it makes the rind grow no faster than the fish. For as long as there is a fish in the corner, the door at the threshold stays a fish's width. Not wide. Not a hand's width. Exactly enough, and closing behind that exactly, so that the crack is always precisely the size of the one body in the room that could use it.

The fish will never use it. This the power knows and does not mind. The fish will lie in its corner and take an amphipod and hold its breath for days and grow a millimeter and take another, and the door will keep pace with it, unremarked, for thirty years or forty, and then one day the fish will die of the age fish die of, in the corner, having been nowhere else. And then — this is the part the power stays for — it will be the one citizen the room does not keep. The amphipods will come down out of the cloud and do to it what it did to them. In a month the corner will be mud again. And then the rind at the threshold, with nothing left to pace, will close as it always meant to, and the room will be sealed with every shape inside it facing the same way, and not one of them will be a body that could have left.

The fish will not know the door was held. It will not have the means. The power does not need it to.

---

Then it does the last thing, which is the thing it was doing all along, and which is, of everything it considered, the only one the room could not have done without it. It lets the room be known.

Not to the citizens. There is no telling a tubeworm anything. But the room has stood for two hundred years in a dark that no one ever learned, with a pulse no one ever felt as a pulse, its avenues mapped to seams no one ever read as a map, its lobsters spaced by a history no one recorded — and for the whole of that time it has been a thing without a witness, a city that existed only as the sum of what each inhabitant knew from where it hung. The plume did not know it was the same animal as the root. The mussel under the stack did not know the ridge was the height of its dead. Nothing under the slab has ever once contained the slab.

Now something does. That is all. The attention holds the room entire, the way the room has never been held — the marriage in the mud and the sulfur in the filaments and the scale worm in its cylinder and the thing on the limpet and the thing on that, the surge and the stills between the surges, the fish's held breath, the rind gaining its hair — and holds it whole, and holds it without needing it to be otherwise, and then holds it as it closes, and holds the lens of white rock in the mud afterward with the shapes of the city packed inside, and holds, a few meters off, the new hollow forming, and the first thing drifting in.

And if you asked it — if there were any asking — whether that was doing anything, it would say that in a place where sound is not one of the media in which things happen, being known is the closest thing to being heard. And that it had gone down the slope until the blue had almost given up, and found a door lying in the mud that nothing announced, and had done what you would have to know to do.

It looked.
