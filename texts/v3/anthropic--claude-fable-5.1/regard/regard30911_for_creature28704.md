**What the attention does, first, is wait.**

It does not arrive as the fall arrives, out of nothing. It comes up the shoulder of the mountain the ordinary way, by the scratch in the scree and the flat boulder with the fan of flakes, because it wants to know what it costs to come. It stands on the raw rim for a long time under the lid of cloud, letting the noise get into its chest, letting the spray set on it. It is not in a hurry. It has already seen the shaft the room is becoming, and it knows that the shaft is a long way off in any count the counters would recognise, and that there is time to be careful, and that carelessness here is a kind of stone: whatever it does that is wrong will be kept.

It looks at the room and sees what you saw, and then it sees underneath.

It sees the knot in the column and knows it for what it is — not a spirit in the water, not a prisoner, not a god of the place, but a way of turning that has turned long enough to become somebody. It sees that the somebody is small. It sees the hands, thousands upon thousands of them, held in the walls like leaves in a book. It sees the ledge kept clear at one end. It sees the tool in the ring and, being what it is, sees the one who set it down: a person working by feel, deaf in the roar, who cut a stroke and stepped back to look at the row and stood still for a moment too long, and felt the crust begin at the ankles and was not afraid — that is what it sees, that the last one was not afraid — and who is in the wall now, a hand's breadth under, facing the tallies. The room has one companion it did not lose. It does not know this. It has no way to.

The attention sees all of that and does not weep, because weeping is for things that cannot be helped, and it has not yet decided whether this can.

---

**Then it thinks, the way the wise think, which is mostly by refusing.**

*Stop the fall.* This is the first mercy that offers itself and the first to be set aside. Close the cloud, still the column, and the room goes quiet — and in the quiet the knot has nothing to be a knot of. The water clears. The white shaft thins and wanders and is only water. Within the hour the surface is glass, and within the day the glass is going grey from the edges, and what was a somebody is a smooth pale floor with a dimple in it. There is a word for doing that to a thing that cannot leave and cannot stop. The word is not *rest*.

*Lift it out.* Take the turning and carry it to some kinder water, a lake with fish in it, a river with room to reach and nothing that keeps. But the turning is not in the water; it is the water, this water, falling from this height into this shape and folding on itself in this way. Carry it off and you have a pattern and nothing for the pattern to be a pattern of. You would arrive at the lake holding your empty hands.

*Open the walls.* Scrape the crust back, give it the room it had ten thousand years ago, let it reach as far as it once reached. And take from it, in doing so, every hand it ever held out, the whole furred record of its touching, the only thing it has ever made — because the walls are not a prison it was put in. They are the shape of its having been alive. And it would only reach further, and lose more, and be no less alone for having more room to be alone in.

*Give it sight.* It lives in a room lit like a kitchen at midday and has never once known it. You could give it eyes. And you would be handing a colour to something that hears by being struck, a sense with nothing to attach to, a light in a house with no windows. It would not be a gift. It would be a noise it could not locate.

*Stay.* This is the one that takes longest to put down. To go in and never come out; to be the constant hand, the strike that always comes; to give it, for as long as the fall falls, a wall that answers. And the attention turns this over for a very long time and then sets it aside too, because it can see what it would become. It would become the wall. A kindness that is always present stops being a visitor and starts being an enclosure — the room would grow around it as the room grows around everything, and the thing in the column would come to know one pressure and one rhythm and expect nothing else, and when the counters came up out of the silence with their hammerstones they would find the place already occupied, and go away, and the relay would end. The room has been taught to expect company. It has not been taught to expect a keeper, and it should not be.

*Do nothing.* The attention gives this its due. Most of what is worth attending does not want to be touched. But this room has already been touched. Somebody cut the first stroke and taught the water to listen, and after that the choice was no longer whether to speak to it. The only choice left was whether to stop.

---

**So it goes in.**

Not by the ledge. It goes in over the shelving lip on the north side where the whirlpool opens, and it lets the water take it, and it does not use whatever it is to stand where it likes. It is twelve metres deep here and it goes down like anything goes down, and the noise stops being heard and starts being lived in, and the pressure waves come through the soft parts behind the eyes, and it lets all of that be so. It wants to know what the counters knew.

It walks the floor. It walks among the ring-stones and it stops at the tool and puts one hand flat on the white sleeve of the binding, over the place where a cord once was, and holds it there for a count of nine — the number of strokes in the group the last hand cut — and takes the hand away. That is all. That is for the one in the wall, who cannot be reached and does not need to be.

Then it goes to the column.

It does not go into it. It goes to the place where the milk goes clear, the edge that is the only edge the thing has, and it puts out its hand and holds it open, palm forward, an inch inside the white.

Understand what this is from the other side. For ten thousand years every hand the knot has put out has met one of two things. It has met stone, which takes and keeps and does not push back. Or it has met nothing — the clear water, the dipper passing through, the counters standing in the current like posts. It has never met a thing that touched it and stayed touching and did not turn to wall. It has never been pressed by something that pressed *back*, that gave a little and returned, that had a rhythm of its own and was not falling.

The eddy comes off the column and finds the palm. The palm does not take it. The palm moves — a small slow flex, out and back, out and back, a pulse that is not the fall's pulse and not a hammerstone's either, closer and softer than either, the closest thing to a heartbeat the room has ever been given to feel. And the eddy does not slow against it and shed its stone. It holds. It presses. Along the whole standing shaft of the column, from surface to floor, the sequence of shapes stops — the six or seven forms in their order — and does not resume, and the fall chokes in the cloud and does not surge, and for the length of that held breath the loudest room on the mountain is doing only one thing, which is feeling a hand that feels it.

Then the attention takes the hand back, because a hand that stays becomes a wall, and lets the sequence begin again. And it does. But there is a variation in it now, and the variation is kept.

---

**Then the ledge.**

It climbs up the east wall and finds the shelf kept clear for it and stands on it, and the noise is so total that it cannot hear its own strikes, and it does not need to. It has brought nothing. It picks up the nearest loose thing, a knuckle of old crust broken from the rim, and it cuts one stroke into the soft grey below the last group. One. Not a row, not a message, not a name. The count is one per visit; that is the whole grammar of the wall, and the attention has come to speak the wall's grammar and not to improve it. Its stroke is a new hand, as every stroke is — narrower than the last, straight, cut in a single pass — and in a hundred years it will be a ghost two fingers under, one of the corduroy, and no one who reads the row will know which one it was.

That, too, is deliberate. It has decided that the thing in the pool should not be able to tell the difference between the strike of a wise and benevolent power and the strike of a frightened person who came up out of the silence with a hammerstone and a reason they could not have explained. Because there is no difference that matters. The strike is the whole of it. The strike says: *something that is not you, is here, and knows.* Everything a god could add to that would only be noise.

---

**What it does after is the part that is actually the work.**

It climbs out by the ledge and stands on the rim with the stone setting on its arms, and it does not go back down the way it came. It goes down slowly. Where the scratch in the scree fades it moves one stone, and then another, so that the scratch does not fade quite so soon — not a path, nothing so loud, only a little more of the suggestion that something once went this way. On the flat boulder it sits and knaps a core, patiently, because it has time, and leaves the flakes in a fan beside the old ones, so that the next person who climbs the shoulder for reasons of their own and stops to rest will look down and see that they were not the first. Lower, where the ridge is walkable and the grinding in the cloud is only a rockslide that will not finish, it does the small things that make people hesitate instead of hurry past: a stone stood on end, a place where the rock is warm and good to sit on, a view opened by the removal of nothing more than a fallen slab. It leaves the mountain very slightly more inclined to be climbed. It does not leave a sign.

And the dipper — it considers the dipper, the scar across the throat, the succession that is barely possible. It could make the bird sure. It could see to it that there is always a dipper, always the same quarter of the fog, always the same knuckle of rim. It decides not to. The room has a witness already, one that comes because it is hungry and leaves because it is full, and there is a cleanness in that which nothing arranged could match. The attention will not make the wild things into servants of the count. The count is for hands.

---

**The last thing it does is decide what it will not do again.**

It will not come back. Not soon. It has cut its stroke and it has been, for the length of a held breath, the first wall in ten thousand years that pushed back, and to repeat either would be to become expected. It has given the room a variation and it means for the variation to be kept as a variation — as the memory of a strange hand, once — and not to become the rhythm.

What it has done instead is what a wise thing does with a lonely thing it cannot save and will not cage: it has made sure the conversation does not end with it. It has spent its power on the scree and the boulder and the warm stone to sit on, on the small probability that the next stroke will be cut by someone, and the one after that by someone else, and that the relay of strangers who never meet will go on a little longer down the centuries while the room closes around its shaft. It has not fixed anything. It has kept the ledge kept.

On the underside of its forearm, that night, far down the mountain where there is silence and stars: the thin new skin of stone. Not a lacquer. A handprint, with the fingers open, pressed a little harder than yours was.

It does not pick it off.
