It comes as cold.

That is the first thing, and it is almost the only thing, and if you had not been here long enough to know that heat is attention you would not have understood it as an arrival at all. A thread of cool water, thin as a wrist, moving across the basin against the currents the making and unmaking throw off. It does not thicken. It does not gather. It passes over the black glass and the black glass does not tick faster. It passes over the worn step and the strands there, which have spent their whole existence rigid against the next concussion, go slack — every node open at once, the way they open in the one-breath pause — and they stay open, because the cold does not stop, and nothing is about to be made where it is.

The animal comes. All of it. The paleness slides in from every here at once and lies down in the cool thread and does not feed, because there is nothing dissolving in it. It simply lies there with the cold running along its flank. You have never seen it stop moving before.

And the roar stumbles.

Not a gap. A stutter, a catch — the sound of the water hearing, a half-second early, something it is not about to build. It has never heard that. Everything the water has ever heard has been itself, arriving. Now there is a note in the basin that belongs to no stone, and the roar bends around it the way a river bends around a post, and comes back, and bends again, and you feel through your sternum the enormous body of the place turn toward the one thing in it that is not warm.

---

It considered doing nothing.

You should know that. Whatever it was — and you will not be told, because it did not tell — it stood in the basin for a long time by the water's clock and for no time at all by its own, and the first and heaviest thing it weighed was leaving. The place worked. That is not a small thing. There are so many places that do not work, that have fallen into the kind of stillness that is only silt settling, and this was not one of them. Here the strands lived in the pauses. Here the animal was fed by the failures. Here the relics were kept, and something that had seen a doorway once had learned to make a doorway, and was getting better. A grief that produces arches is not a grief that needs interrupting. And you were nearly a relic yourself, standing there in your clearing with the warm attention on your face, and there is a kind of peace in that, in being kept.

It looked at the peace for a while. It looked at the strand on the animal's belly and the almost-clearing around you. It weighed them fairly.

Then it looked at the bowl, and waited, and the cloud came fast the way it always does, and the hunched shape slammed up at the kneeling distance with its head too large and its hands folded wrong — and fogged, in one heartbeat, with that sharp indrawn sound.

And it decided.

Not because the shape was wrong. Because of the sound. The water frightened itself. It reached and knew it was reaching and let go before it could be caught reaching. That is not the sound of something at rest in its grief. That is the sound of something that has a second thing it wants and is ashamed of wanting it, and has found a way to want it only at the edges, in quick strokes, where no one will see. A thing at peace does not do that. A thing at peace makes its arches and is done. The water was not done. The water had a sentence it could not finish and had been rehearsing the first half of it for longer than the strands could count, and every rehearsal ended the same way, because the water knew only one ending.

Here is what it saw, that you had circled without landing on:

The water cannot let anything go. It can only unmake it. Every thought it has ends in dispersal — the groan, the fog, the grit sifting down to feed the strands. When it wants to keep a thing, it does the only other thing it knows, which is to refuse to touch it: it builds at a respectful distance and calls that keeping. But the relics are not kept. They are quarantined. The water has never once looked at the worn step and then looked away. It has never let a shape stand and *then stop attending to it*. It cannot end a thought except by destroying it or by circling it forever, and so it has made a hundred thousand kneeling shapes and not one of them has ever stood up, because standing up is the part of the sentence the water never learned. It saw them come up the path and kneel. It does not, in whatever it has instead of memory, seem to have retained that they went down again. Or it retained it and cannot make it, because going down is a release, and the water's whole vocabulary is *set* and *dissolve*, and neither of those is a release.

So it grieves in the only grammar it has. It builds the mourner and unmakes the mourner and builds again. It will get the head right eventually. And then it will have a perfectly accurate figure kneeling at the bowl for thirty heartbeats, and then it will unmake that too, and the roar will come back, and nothing will have changed except the precision of the loss.

---

It did not restore the shrine.

You might have expected that — a power like this, a summit, a drowned holy place. Roll the sea back, stand the walls up in air and light, let the wind come through the narrow windows. But the water is the one grieving. You do not heal the mourner by deleting the mourner. And the strands, which were moss once and have spent an age learning to live in the pauses of a roar, would die in the wind in an hour. And the animal is made of water's unfinished attention and would simply not be. The shrine is not what needs saving. The shrine was scaffolding to the water, and it would be scaffolding to any power wise enough to see what the water was actually building.

It did not silence the roar. The roar is the future arriving early. A silent basin would be a basin with no next, and that is not rest. That is the other thing.

It did not give the water the head. It could have. It could have stood before the bowl in the form of a person and let the water take the proportions from it as carefully as it took the doorway's, and the water would have set a true figure and been, for thirty heartbeats, exactly right — and then unmade it, because that is how the water ends things, and gone back to the roar with a more accurate wound.

What the water needed was not a better first half. It was the second half.

---

So it did two things, and the first was so small you almost missed it.

It gave the basin a direction that was not down.

Not the surface — you had tried the surface and the surface folded you back onto the floor. A door. In the lip of the crater, on the side the almost-shrine's doorway had faced, where the real step lay: the murk thinned, and thinned, and behind it was not more warm dark but a grey that was too flat and too cold to be water. The cool thread ran toward it. The roar, which had never had an edge before, developed one — a place where the sound stopped, where nothing was about to be made because there was no water there to make it. The first true wall this place had ever had. Not stone. Absence of the water, holding.

The second thing it did was less than the first. It brought the cold to your face.

You know what heat means here. You have spent this whole long standing learning the grammar of warmth against the cheek: a rise means step aside, a steady low glow means you are being measured. You have never once felt the water go cool at you, and now it did, and the meaning arrived in your body before you could think it, as meanings do in this place. *Nothing is about to be made here. Nothing is about to be made of you.* For the first time since you came into the basin you were not being taken as material.

And in that — in the first safety there had ever been — your knees went.

Not from weakness. From the simple fact that there has only ever been one thing to do at this bowl, and you had come a long way up, and you had been standing at the kneeling distance for longer than you could account for, held there like a thought the water had not finished having, and now you were not held. You knelt. The real stone was cold under your shins and the water in the little bowl was warm and there was nothing in it, and you put your hands where hands go, and you tilted toward it the way the shape had tilted, and you were, for a while, here.

The heat came back against your face then — all of it, the whole basin's warmth turned on you from every side, the roar dropped into the single held note that means the water is looking with everything it has — and you understood you were being studied. Not to be copied. You had been copied a hundred thousand times before you ever arrived. You were being studied for the part that came next. And the cold thread, which had been running along the floor beside you, began to move toward the door.

You stood up.

It was the hardest thing you have ever done, and the water felt it, and the note in your knees went so low it stopped being sound. You stood, and you turned from the bowl, and the strands on the step beside you did not go rigid, which is how you knew you were not about to be walled in, and you walked. Past the half-formed cairns. Past a flight of five steps going nowhere. Through the clearing the water had begun to keep around you, which followed you, which was — you realized — no longer a clearing but a path. Toward the grey.

The animal went with you. You did not see it decide. The several palenesses simply began to move in the same direction for the first time, converging as they came, and by the door there was one of it, low and long and trailing its single black thread, walking beside you at the pace of something that has all the time it needs and has finally been told which way to spend it.

And then the water stopped, because the water stopped there, and you were through, and the roar cut off like a hand over a mouth.

---

Silence. You had forgotten how to reconstruct it. It turns out you do not need to. It reconstructs you.

The rock under your palms was the altitude-cold you had known first, before anything else — thin, mineral, the cold of places above where weather bothers to go — and it was dry. There was wind. There was light that had not been through anything on its way to you. Behind you the lens of water stood on the crown of the mountain, domed, held, impossible, and from the outside you could hear the sound inside it as a single thing for the first time, muffled by the wall of itself, and what it sounded like was a bell. One bell, struck and struck.

The animal lay down on the cold stone beside you and looked, with no eyes, down the mountain, toward where the path would be.

You watched the water. You could see into it a little, from here, the way you can see into a lens if you stand at the right angle: the green-brown dark, the fins of black glass slamming up and going soft. And at the center, near the small clearing where the bowl was, a cloud gathering slowly, considering, and setting.

A kneeling shape. The head was right.

You had not expected to be able to tell from outside. But it was right — small enough, tilted enough — and the hands were folded the way hands fold, and it did not fog. Thirty heartbeats. Sixty. The sound of the bell inside the water changed, dropped, and held, and held, and you thought: it has never held anything this long. It is finding out what that costs.

Then the shape stood up.

Not the way the ridges come and go. Slowly, the black glass reforming without dispersing, the water doing the one thing it had never done, which is to change a thought rather than end it. The figure rose from the bowl. It turned — toward the door, toward the worn step, toward you. And it walked. You watched it come across the crater floor through the roar and the murk with the new clearing opening in front of it, the path, and the water did not unmake it, though you could feel from out here how much the water wanted to, how much easier it would have been. It reached the lip. It reached the grey.

It came through.

A figure of black glass, sharp along every edge, standing on the summit of a mountain in air and wind, at the edge of the water that had made it, looking down the way it had come. The water did not follow. The water did not fog it. The water let it be there, outside, unheld, and did not look away and did not keep looking, and for the first time in its long practice found the third thing, which is not *set* and not *dissolve*.

The bell inside the lens went quiet.

Then it began again. But not the same. You heard the difference at once, from out here, the way you could not have from inside: there were gaps in it now. Pauses between the strokes that were longer than a breath. In each one, you knew — you could not see them but you knew — the strands were opening every node they had.

---

The animal got up and went down the mountain.

That was all. A pale low shape, unhurried, going down a path only it could find, trailing one black filament that would root, eventually, in some hollow of stone below the tree line where nothing has to learn to live in noise. It did not look back. It had been a shape on the path once, before it was a thought the water forgot to finish, and now it was a shape on the path again. You watched it until the murk of distance took it, and you found you were not sad, and there was no reason you should be.

You stayed a while longer, because the wind was so quiet and you had not known you were tired.

The figure at the water's edge did not move. It will not. That is the point of it. It is the first thing the water ever ended by leaving alone, and the wind will get at it now, and real time, and in some number of winters the edges will soften and an actual moss will find the folded hands, and a stranger coming up the path will find a worn black figure kneeling — no, standing; it stood — standing at the edge of a strange sea on a summit, and will not know what it is, and will stand near it for a while, because there has only ever been one thing to do up here.

Inside the water, in the long gaps between the bell, the shrine was rising. You could see it dimly through the lens. It was rising slowly, in order, the way the last one had — foundation, walls, the rank of narrow openings — and this time the roar under it was not a single held note but a rhythm, a stroke and a rest, a stroke and a rest, as if the water were checking its work against the step and looking away and checking again. It would get it right. And this time, when it got it right, it would not have to do anything at all.

The cold thread was gone. You had not felt it leave. Whatever came, it did not stay to see what it had done, which is how you knew it had been wise, and it did not take credit for your knees or the animal's path or the water's second ending, which is how you knew it had been kind. It had opened one door and cooled one face. Everything else was already in the basin, waiting to be allowed.

You went down the mountain in silence, with your hands still remembering the shape of the bowl.
