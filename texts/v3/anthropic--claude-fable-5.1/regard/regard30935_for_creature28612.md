What arrives does not come down the ladder. It does not fog its glasses or darken its collar. It is attention, and attention gets in the way water does — through the seam, without asking, warm by the time it reaches the floor — and it fills the room the way the chitter fills it, from the crown down, and finds the sump last, because that is where the light does not go.

It looks for a long time. This is the first thing it does and for a long time the only thing. A lesser power, a merely kind one, would already be moving: a hand under the pale fish, a bucket, the shaft, daylight, the stream. It would call this a release. The wise thing knows what a fish bred to a fever does in April water thirty meters up, and knows that the bright ordinary minnows of the stream would take one look at a fingernail-colored cousin with too-large eyes and eat it or ignore it to death. Taking them up would be a killing with good manners. It sets that aside.

It sets aside the aquarium too, the climate-controlled tank in the university basement, the label on the glass. The fish would live. They would be kept at the right temperature and fed the right larvae and photographed and sequenced and known. And they would stop. The pool they live in is not a habitat; it is a process. It is a lip of stone rising by a drop's width, a shelf of terraces slowly building on the warm side, a depth that increases at the speed the fish increase their tolerance for depth. Lift them out of that and you have preserved a photograph of a fish, the way the fern is a photograph of a plant. The wise thing has no interest in photographs.

It considers the fern, briefly, because the fern is the one honest wanting in the room and wanting is hard to look past. Then it considers the cockroaches, the seething lee of the manifold, and feels the small itch a tidy mind feels in the presence of abundance, and lets that go as well. The floor is doing what floors do. Nothing down there has asked for anything.

Then it goes back to the sump and looks at what is actually wrong.

Nothing is wrong now. That is what makes it hard to see. The fish are not suffering; they are fitted. They have made a life with no edges out of a pour, a tremble, and a rain of the dead, and they are good at it, and every hour a few more of them are born already good at it. But the wise thing sees time the way the fish feel the pour — as a permanent weather standing at one end of everything — and it sees that they have made one mistake, and that the mistake is not theirs.

They have wedded themselves to the wrong part of the room.

The rock will be here. The flowstone will go on rising after the last pipe has rusted through, on a schedule that finds the pipes faintly ridiculous. But the fish did not adapt to the rock. They adapted to the fever, and the fever is borrowed. It comes from four insulated mains, a manifold with red paint surviving on the undersides, two pumps on plinths, a transformer cabinet, a district heating authority, a budget line, a decision made in an office where nobody has ever heard the chitter. Every one of those things is more mortal than the fish. When the mains are decommissioned — and they will be; nothing that has a hand-wheel is permanent — the heat will go out of the room in a season. The pool will fall toward the temperature of deep rock, which is the temperature of a cellar, and a lineage that spent forty generations learning to live at thirty-eight degrees will find out in a single winter that it has never been a stream fish at all and has forgotten how to be one.

They have bet their whole shape on a maintenance schedule. That is the thing that is wrong. It is not wrong yet.

So the wise thing, which could do anything, does two small things, and does both of them in the room's own idiom, which is to say slowly and without anyone noticing.

The first is upstairs. Somewhere in a municipal building there is a work order for the failed seal at the far end of junction vault nine. It has been on a list for eleven years. The wise thing does not destroy it — that would be a kind of lie — but it sees to it that the list is always slightly longer than the year, that the seal is always the item just below the line where the money runs out, that the engineer who does eventually go down the ladder and look at the pour and hiss through his teeth is the kind of man who writes *monitor* on a form and means it. The pour will go on failing at the rate it has always failed at. This costs the city a small amount of hot water a year, which the city can afford, and it costs the fish nothing, and it is the least of the two things.

The second is below.

The fourth main — the one that comes in hotter than the others, from somewhere deeper than anyone above ground thinks about — is not the source of that heat. It is a straw in it. Beneath the floor of the vault, some hundreds of meters down, is the water the pipe was drilled to borrow: rock-heated, mineral, patient, older than the city. It has been coming up on its own for a very long time along whatever fractures it can find, and it has never found one that reaches this room. The rock here was shot and sprayed and sealed. The floor is slurry over gravel over blast rubble over a competent, unbroken bed.

The wise thing puts a crack in it.

Not a fissure. Not anything the pumps would register. A hairline, a single failed grain boundary in the stone directly beneath the warm side of the pool, where the terraces are and the eggs are stuck at every stage. And it does not open it. It lets it open. It gives the deep water a place to begin and then it stands back and lets the water do the rest at the water's own pace, which is the pace of the room: a drop's width at a time, dissolving upward through a meter of rock a century, following the heat gradient the way the fish follow the pour, so that in sixty years, or a hundred and sixty, there is a seep. A warmth in the floor of the pool that did not come from a pipe. A thread of mineral water rising through the flowstone on the spawning side, a few degrees hotter than the pool around it, carrying a little lime of its own and laying it down on the terraces from underneath while the pour lays it down from above.

The fish will not notice. This is the point. The gradient will shift toward the seep by an amount too small to be a change, and the eggs will be laid a centimeter closer to it, and a century of fry will grow up with a warm floor as part of what a floor is, so that when the day comes — and it will come — that the pumps stop and the manifold groans one last time as it cools and the four mains go the temperature of the rock they run through, the pool will not follow them. The pool will have its own fever by then. The fish will be inside the geology in the only sense that finally matters, which is that they will have stopped depending on anything with a hand-wheel.

The wise thing does not know if this will work. Seeps close. Fractures fill with the very lime they carry. There may be a flood from the culvert in the wrong decade, or a hundred-year drought upstairs, or the city may decide to fill the vault with concrete when it abandons the mains, which cities do. It cannot guarantee anything, and it does not try to. It has done the one thing that was within the room's own grammar to do, which is to shift a dependency from the mortal to the less mortal by the width of a drop, and it has done it early enough that the shifting will not feel like anything. That is what benevolence looks like at this speed. It is indistinguishable from patience.

There is one thing more, and it is not for the fish.

One of the two dead sodium lamps is on the stanchion nearest the largest patch of green. It has been out for nine years. The wise thing does not relight it — that would be a miracle, and miracles are the wrong shape for this room — but the same engineer who writes *monitor* on the seal form has a box of bulbs in the van and a habit of finishing a job, and it is a small matter to have him notice the dark fixture on his way back to the ladder and think *well, while I'm here*. The bats lose a hand's breadth of roost along one wall, which the colony will not miss. And in the widened circle of amber, some months later, the fern that has been neither growing nor dying for as long as anyone has known it puts out one new frond, coiled, the size of a comma. It will not change anything. It will not make the fern a plant instead of a photograph of one. But the fern was the one thing in the room that had asked, and the wise thing found that it could not leave without answering, even a little, even if the answer was only *yes, I saw that.*

Then it goes back to the sump one last time.

The large pale one is where it always is, nose into the churn, holding the only current in the pool against everyone else. The wise thing looks at it with a torch it does not need, and sees its own light pass through the body and print on the flowstone behind, and sees the shadow of the spine like a drawing left unfinished, and the two black eyes that have never been used for anything and are not a wound.

It does nothing for this one. There is nothing it lacks. It has never seen the stream and will not, and it will not be alive when the seep breaks through, and it will not know that the seal was left to fail on purpose or that the lamp came back on. It is simply the thing this place makes when it makes a thing, and it is finished, and the wise thing has enough wisdom to know that the highest form of its attention here is not to improve anything but to have arrived and paid it.

It stays a while. The pumps cycle. The pale one tightens toward the middle of the water with the others when the tremble comes through, and loosens with them when it settles into the roar. A drop falls into the pool from the pour, and a cricket comes down with it, and the fish rise.

Then the attention withdraws the way it came, through the seams, and nothing in the room is different. The chitter goes on. The straws on the crown are one drop longer. Under the floor, in the dark below the dark, a single grain of rock has let go of its neighbor, and warm water that has waited longer than the city has existed begins, at the speed of stone, to find its way up toward a pool that has not yet been told it will need it.
