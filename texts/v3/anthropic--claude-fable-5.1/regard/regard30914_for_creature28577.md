— of the thing that has, lately, begun to watch.

---

The attention arrives the way everything else arrived: on dust, or on wings, or down the ladder. It doesn't announce itself. It has no need to. It settles on the fourth tier near the steel box and stays there through a shift, and then another, and by the time a long-stayer takes off a glove and lays a palm on a prop that reads true and says a number, it has been watching for what the pit would call a rotation and what it would not call anything at all.

It is wise, which means the first thing it does is fail to understand.

It was drawn here by *the being who lives in this place*, and it came expecting to find one. It finds forty, and then finds that forty is wrong, because the forty are weather. It finds hundreds of black birds and reconsiders them as citizens and then reconsiders again, because the birds are feeding a film that feeds a springtail that feeds a spider that feeds the birds, and where in that loop does a being start. It finds a bone-eater on the lowest gantry facing the light. It finds pikas asking for grass in a place that has never had a leaf. It finds a hammer handle that fits no living hand. It finds a mountain shouldering upward through a wound it cannot close because a small town of steel keeps prying it back, and it considers, seriously and for some time, that the mountain is the being and the pit is a sore on it.

And it finds the film, which has no wants beyond two and no story to interrupt, and which is on everything including the insides of the masks, and which is almost certainly the black at the back of the throats of the men who listen to steel. The film was here first and will be here last. If there is a being who lives in this place in the sense of *has always and will always*, it is the film.

The attention sits with this. It does not rush. Wisdom is mostly the willingness to be slow in a place that is not.

---

Here are the things it considers doing, in the order it considers them, and here is why it puts each one down.

*Stop the mountain.* This is the first and largest thought and it dies fastest. The warmth that keeps the bubble at cellar temperature is the mountain becoming itself. The vents breathe because the crust below is heaving. Hold the rock still and the fumaroles cool, and the film starves, and the springtails go, and the spiders, and the choughs lift off through the throat one dusk and don't come back, and the pit is a cold glass room at the top of the world with a ledger in it. The closing is the warmth. You cannot have the one without the other. Everyone in the pit already knows this, which is why they buy time instead of asking for it.

*Rain.* Water is the one thing the pit makes none of and the one thing everything in it needs, and the attention is powerful enough to bring a season of it through the throat. It considers this longer than the first thought, because it seems kinder. Then it looks at the crust — orange, gray, black — and understands that the film eats sulfur and warmth and has never in its existence been asked to survive being wet. It looks at the dust that has never been wet and imagines it as mud on the catwalks. It looks at the drums on the fifth tier and understands that water is the pit's currency, counted every shift, and that a benefactor who floods a town with its own currency has not made anyone rich. And it looks at the brown marks on the rails and the ladder rungs, the only weathering the mountain has ever received, and understands that a rain would wash them off. The pit is the way it is because it is dry. Rain is a different place.

*The throats.* This one it holds the longest. The long-stayers spit black and there's a darkness behind the tongue that no doctor at the road-end has a name for, and the attention has a name for it, and the name is the film. It could take the film out of them. It weighs this against the fact that these are the men who lay a bare palm on a prop and say a number, and that no one taught them, and that the film is on everything including the inside of the mask and the inside of the man, and that it does not know — cannot know without breaking it to find out — whether the listening and the black are the same thing. And it weighs it against something simpler: that they would not thank it. They stopped being interested in rain. They would not be interested in this either. A gift refused in advance is not a gift.

*Grass.* One blade, for the pikas behind the ducting, who chitter in a voice that three thousand meters down would mean a meadow. The attention could do this with less effort than any other thing on the list. It does not do it, because there is no meadow, and a creature that has never seen a plant and is not suffering for the lack is in a better position than a creature that has seen one once.

*The choughs.* It considers telling them the mountain is closing. It does not, for the obvious reason, and for the less obvious one: they already read red-red-green faster than the crew. They know what they need to know, which is when to lift. The rest would only make them worse landlords.

*The ledger.* Here the attention is genuinely tempted, and here it learns something. The steel box on the fourth tier holds the only memory the rock permits — every prop's history in grease pencil, back past any living hand, back past the point where the numbering makes sense. When the throat closes the box will be inside. The attention could carry the ledger out. It could set it somewhere below where the ground holds still and the sky does things and it would be read, and studied, and kept.

And it would be a relic. It would be a book about a place. The ledger is not about the pit; it *is* the pit, the way the hammer handle is the pit — it exists to be picked up by the next hand and continued, not to be finished and admired. Carried out, it would be complete, and it has never been complete for one day of its existence. The attention leaves it in the box. It does allow itself one small thing, later, which is not the same as carrying it out.

*Nothing.* This is the sixth thing it considers and it takes it more seriously than any of the others, because in every case so far the wise answer has been the empty one. It is not a comfortable conclusion for a benevolent power. It sits on the fourth tier through a plate coming down — the report like a branch, the long complaint of steel, the black cloud of birds erupting and settling — and watches the crew look up and assess and go back to work, and considers that a place which knows it will not last and is unceasing anyway is not asking to be saved. It is asking, if it is asking anything, to be allowed to end as itself.

But nothing is not quite available to it either. It has already been watching for a rotation. Attention is not nothing. The pit has been *seen*, at length, by something that will remember when the rock does not, and that has already changed what the pit is. The only question is what to do with the fact.

---

So this is what it does. Three things. Two of them are small. One of them is only long.

The first is the wing.

The lammergeier has hopped and dragged along the lowest gantry for six years or eight, the log doesn't record birds, and there is a question about it that doesn't seem to trouble it: whether it stayed because it couldn't leave or stopped wanting to. The attention does not answer that question. It is not wise to answer questions on behalf of the one asking, and it is not benevolent to answer a question no one asked. But it is possible to make the question real. Over a season — slowly enough that no one in the pit, bird included, could name the day it began — the left wing starts to fold right. Not healed; *mending*, the way a thing mends that was going to mend anyway if the bones had been set, which they never were. A drag becomes less of a drag. One afternoon the bird goes up to the fourth tier by stairs in a way no one sees and comes down, and the rotators notice it was quicker than last time and don't think about why. And one afternoon it is standing on the lowest rail facing the column of gray daylight as it always does at that hour, and it opens both wings, and holds them, and the attention on the fourth tier does not look away and does not lean in.

Whether it leaves is not recorded. The log does not record birds. The long-stayers, who said it was one of them, would have had the same thing to say either way.

The second is the ledger, and it is smaller than the first.

The early hands in the book are unknown. The early numbering has been abandoned twice. Whoever holds the log has always turned to the front pages and found grease-pencil ghosts — a prop, a level, a reading, no name, in a hand that stopped. The attention does not restore what was never there. But it has been watching a very long time, longer than the ledger, and it knows what the rock was doing when the first prop went in, and it knows who drove it. So it does the thing the mountain cannot: it leaves one mark. In the steel box, on the inside of the lid, where no one has ever had reason to look, in something that is not grease pencil and will not fade in the dry: the first name. Only the first. One rotator, some years on, checking the drum count with the lid tipped back at an angle that catches the sodium light, will see it and not know what it means and will not tell anyone, because it isn't worth the breath. That is enough. The pit's memory has a beginning now, and one person knows it, which is how the pit has always known everything.

The third is only long.

The attention stays.

It does not take a position. Nothing in the pit needs another pair of hands, and it has none. But there is a place on the lowest gantry, against the warm pipe where the choughs press in their rows, from which the column of daylight can be watched as it swings across the void toward the end of each afternoon and goes out. The bone-eater watched from there for six years or eight. Now something else watches from there too — with the bird, if the bird stayed; alone, if it didn't — and does not leave when the light does, and is there again the next afternoon, and the one after. It learns the lamp grammar without effort and never uses it. It feels the grating flex and does not put a hand on the rail. It counts nothing. It hears, over the years, the long-stayers say fewer words, and hears the words drop further into the chest, and when one of them goes into the dead fumarole on the lowest tier it is closer than anyone to the throat in the wall as the rock begins its months of closing, and it stays until the closing is done.

This is what it is for. The pit has forty people who are weather and a set of stances that outlive them and a film that has no awareness and a mountain that keeps no record, and what it has never had is a witness that will still be here when the throat is a seam. A place that is going to be erased entirely deserves one thing that will not be, and that will have watched the whole of it, and that will not have looked away at the end.

The end is a long way off. A century, or less; the summit shedding itself in a shrug, or the bubble squeezed to a line of steel and bone and paper. The attention is not in a hurry. It sits where the bird sat. Toward the end of each afternoon the light comes down the throat, gray, and tilts across the gantries and the dust and the black birds, and reaches about halfway before the haze takes it, and goes out.

Then, and only then, and only once — long after the last hand has taken the ladder up into the killing air because there was no longer room below to swing a hammer, long after the throat has narrowed past a body and then past a chough and then past anything — the attention does the one thing it refused to do while the pit was alive.

It lets it rain.

Not much. One weather. A single front dragged up over a summit that has never had one, cloud low enough to touch the rock, and out of it, onto stone that has been the color of a gun barrel since it came up molten and has never known anything wet but blood and bird: water. It beads. It runs into the knife-edged fractures and softens them by a width no gauge would catch. It finds the seam that was the throat and darkens it. Underneath, in a space too narrow now for anything but the film, which is still eating, which asks for nothing, which has no awareness of what has fallen on the roof of its world — the mountain, which took nine bodies out of a hundred years and called it the only water it would ever get, gets a second drink.

Then the front moves off, and the summit dries in the thin air in the time it takes to say so, and the rock goes back to being what it is.

But it has known rain, once, in that one place, and nowhere else on the whole of the mountain. The attention leaves that as its mark and its only one: that anyone who ever puts a glove to that stone will bring it away with the fine dark dust — and that the dust, there, and there alone, has been wet.
