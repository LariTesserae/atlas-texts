**What Comes to the Rim**

Begin with where it stands, because a power that arrives at this place arrives at the one part of it that is not the place.

It comes to the rim. Not through the lid — nothing goes through the lid — and not up from the dead heart, but to the edge, in the thin air, where the low dull green runs out against the frost and a foot could not say whether it stood on mountain or on plant. Outside, the stars are steady. The clouds are below. It is very cold and very quiet in the way of high empty places, and the quiet is the first thing the power has to set aside, because it knows, as a power would, that a meter under its attention the quiet stops and does not start again for forty kilometers.

So it listens. That is the first thing it does and for a long while the only thing.

Through the matted green, through the three meters of living skin and the bone-dense dead below it, into four hundred meters of black water at the temperature where water most wants to stay still — the power hears. Not with an ear; the lid would stop an ear. It hears the way a power hears, which is to say directly, as the water hears, from everywhere at once. And what comes to it first is the chord.

It has heard many things. It has not heard this. A slab of tone with no interval in it, thousands of soft eyeless bodies each holding a single note at a single depth, the whole population arranged so closely that the sum is not music but a condition. Riding on it, the irregular ticking of the knockers, room by room, each strike a question and each return the same answer. At the edge, the dry rustle of the ceiling reading itself. Under it, the long groan of the walls, pitched to rooms that finished growing before anything now alive was born.

The power stays. It is patient in the way of its kind, which is to say without effort, and it stays long enough that somewhere in a deep shaft a caller lets go of its note for the last time and sinks, and the chord thins at that place, and something small and unpitched drifts toward the thinness and finds it and fills it. The power attends to this closely. It wants to know whether it can tell, afterward, that anything happened. It cannot. The chord is whole. It is exactly what it was.

And the power notices something it did not expect to notice, standing at the rim with the steady stars above it: that the stars outside do not twinkle because there is nothing between them and the eye, and the notes inside do not waver because they are held, and that the cistern — lidded, lightless, where no one has ever heard of stars — has grown itself a sky. Each caller a fixed point. No gaps between them. A firmament of sound, four hundred meters deep, that the beings under it steer by exactly as anything anywhere has ever steered by a sky.

It sits with that for a while.

---

Then the first impulse, because a benevolent power has impulses and the first of them is always to give.

It could give light. It has light to give; it has more than the cistern could use. It could warm the lid until the green thinned to a veil and the thin fierce sun came down through it and, for the first time since the mountain had a shape, the water had a color. It considers this for the length of time it takes to see what it would mean. The pale underface of the plant would burn. The ribbon-things pressed against it like leaves on a window would curl and let go, all of them, a snow of the dying so thick the filterers below would choke on plenty. And the survivors — anything that survived — would have been given a thing they have no organ for, and the dark, which has never here been an absence, would become one. Light would not fill the cistern. It would empty it, retroactively, of every moment before. The power sets the light down.

It could give a direction. It knows what the cistern lacks, from outside: a next thousand years. It could nudge the lid to thicken a hair faster than it is eaten, and in an age the rooms would shrink and the chord would rise and there would be, at last, something happening. It considers this. It sees that the cistern's whole condition is that nothing is pressing at its back, and that to give it a future would be to give it the one thing every other place already has and this place alone has escaped. That the sentence has ended, and that adding a clause to an ended sentence does not extend it. It only makes it a different sentence, and a worse one, since the first was finished and the second would merely be going on.

It could — and this is the one it holds longest, because it is the one that feels most like love — it could wake one of them. Choose one caller in one shaft and give it a mind that could know it was in a chord. Let one being in the whole forty kilometers understand what it was part of.

The power looks at this a long time, and then it sees the shape of it clearly, and puts it away faster than the others. To wake one would be to create, in a place that has never had it, the condition of being the only one who knows. Of hanging in the dark holding a note and understanding for the first time that there is a lid, and a sun above the lid, and stars above the sun, and that no one else in earshot — and everyone is in earshot — can be told. The cistern has never held a lonely thing. The power will not be the one to make it.

---

Then the harder question, which a wise power asks before it asks anything else, and which this one has been holding off because it did not want to know the answer.

Is there pain here?

It looks. It looks with everything it has. The knockers eat the filterers off the walls; the small quick things are taken in the dark by whatever takes them; the ribbon-things loosen at the end and fall for a very long time. Something in the deep rooms puts its mouth to the soft floor and eats what everyone has been. There is dying everywhere in the cistern, at every depth, in every room, continuously, and the power will not pretend to itself that soft eyeless bodies do not have a way of registering the moment they are opened.

But it looks for the thing that makes pain into suffering — for the anticipation, the memory long enough to grieve, the witness that turns an ending into a story — and it does not find it. Nothing here waits. Nothing here fears the knock, because the knock has never meant anything but the shape of the room. When a filterer is taken it is taken; the palm that was beside it goes on opening and closing into the same current. The chord does not flinch. Whatever is felt here is felt for the length of a heartbeat by a thing that has no way to know it is the last heartbeat, and then it is not felt, and the water closes over it at four degrees.

The power is not certain. It is honest enough to know that it is not certain. And it decides, standing at the rim with the steady stars, that its uncertainty is its own to carry and not the cistern's to be fixed of. That it could take the dying out of the place only by taking the loop out of it, and the loop is everyone, and a benevolence that unmakes a world to spare it a heartbeat has confused its own discomfort with the good.

---

The second impulse, which is subtler than the first and comes dressed as wisdom.

The power can see the end. Powers can. The mountain is old past having a shape but it is not past having a fate, and the rim that weeps water so gently now is weeping, and the rock under the dead growth is not the growth and does not keep the growth's bargain. In an age — a useless number, but a number — the rim will fail. A crack, a seam, a gallery on the outer edge opening to the thin air. And then the water will go, not slowly, the way it has always gone, but the way water goes when it is finally allowed to, and the lid will sag into the emptying rooms and split, and the sun will come down through it onto the bone-smooth walls for the first time, and everything that returned a knock will lie in the light with nothing to return it to.

The power could stop this. It is well within its reach to hold the rim. To set the mountain outside time and keep the cistern exactly as it is, sounding, for as long as there are stars to be steady above it.

This is the temptation that looks like care, and the power knows it for what it is. To keep the cistern would be to make it kept. To turn a thing that has finished itself into a thing behind glass, whose meaning is no longer that it is whole but that it is held — held by the power, for the power, so that the power might go on having heard it. A finished sentence is not improved by never being allowed to have been said. And the cistern's whole nature is that it happened without anyone to keep it. The power will not make itself the first exception.

But it does one thing here, and it is the only thing it does that reaches inside.

It will not stop the end. It will slow it.

Not a crack. A seep. When the rim begins to fail, the power will see to it that it fails at the rate the cistern has always known — the rate of the weep, the rate of the lid's growth and grazing, the rate at which the level has not changed. The water will go down a hair in a century. The topmost galleries will drain first, the ribbon-things will find the lid rising away from them and follow it down, the callers nearest the ceiling will lose their depth and drift lower and find the chord already full there and hold anyway, doubling a note, and the chord will thicken at the bottom and thin at the top the way a sky does toward morning. The knockers will strike and receive a ceiling that has moved, and strike again, and receive it moved a little more, and this will become — over ages — the shape of the room. Nothing will be startled. Nothing will drown in air. The cistern will end the way it lived, in its own tempo, so slowly that no generation inside it will experience the ending as an event, only as the texture of the medium.

The power thinks this is what it would want, if it were a thing in the dark that had never wanted anything.

---

The large thing.

The power could know what it is. It has only to look. It has been not looking the whole time it has stood here, and it has been aware of not looking, and now it decides.

It will not know. The cistern has one resident that makes no noise — one place where the chord is thinner than it should be, moving through the deepest rooms at the pace of nothing — and it is the only mystery the place has ever held, and it belongs to the place. A world in which everything is known by the thing outside it is a world that has been made, a little, into that thing's possession. The power leaves the dullness in the deep unlooked-at. It lets the cistern keep something from it.

It thinks, briefly, standing at the rim, that the large thing is what a listener looks like from inside — that anything soft enough to hear without returning sound would register, to the callers and the knockers, exactly so: a place where the chord goes quiet, moving slowly, feeding on what everyone has been. It thinks that it has, in a sense, been that all along tonight. It does not pursue the thought. It is not for it to know.

---

So this is what a benevolent and wise power does, at the top of the world, with a sealed dark cistern full of singing under its feet.

It listens, for long enough that it can no longer tell the singers from the ones they replaced. It does this so that the cistern will have been heard, once, from the one direction its sound has never reached. The cistern will not know. That is the point. A witness who asks nothing back is the only kind of witness a finished thing can bear.

It gives nothing. Not light, not time, not a mind. It carries its uncertainty about the pain away with it and does not set it down inside.

It arranges the ending — the one that was always coming — so that it comes at the speed of the life. It does this and does not stay to watch it, because to stay would be to keep, and it has decided not to keep.

It leaves one thing unknown.

And then it goes. It steps back from the rim, from the low dull green that a passing eye would take for lichen, into the knife air where the stars do not twinkle, and behind it the lid admits nothing, and beneath the lid the chord goes on without a gap in it, and in a middle room a knocker strikes and is told the shape it knows, and the ceiling turns a page, and somewhere at the bottom the dark thins and thickens and thins, and nothing in the whole forty kilometers has heard anything it had not heard before, or will hear anything it will not hear again, which is the only way anything could have happened here, and did.
