Suppose it is noticed.

Not by the people — they had their turn, and took what the room offered, and stopped coming. Suppose something else stands at the rim, something that can see the whole plateau at once and the mile of dark water under it and the fern's eleven-second clock all in the same glance, and is inclined toward kindness, and has been around long enough to know what kindness usually costs.

The first thing it does is nothing. This is not the answer. It is the beginning of the answer. It stands at the rim for what would be, to the fern, a few fronds, and it lets the place tell it what the place is, because a wise power knows that the urge to help arrives before the understanding of what is being helped, and the gap between the two is where most harm is done.

---

Then it considers, honestly, what it could give.

Rain. It could bring more rain to the plateau, could turn the dry country green for a season. And the wet line on the walls would climb, and the moss would drown in its own shape, and the fern — a hand to the right and it would drown — would go, and the basin would overflow the notch faster than the notch was cut to carry, and the still surface the bowl was made for would be stirred and never still again. The white things would be swept out onto the floor by the hundred and dry in the noon rectangle. No. The rain is correct. It has been correct for ten thousand years.

Light. It could open the rim a little, let the sun down for two hours instead of one. And the liverworts on the ledges would curl to paper and the water on the walls would leave as vapor instead of arriving as beads and the stone would warm past the temperature of a flank to the temperature of a plate. No.

Eyes. It could give the white things eyes. It considers this longer than the others, because it is the gift that looks most like love, and then it puts it down. They pared themselves to the shape of the dark over more years than there have been people. To give them eyes would be to hand them a lifetime of seeing nothing, a bowl-width of brown-white, and then the dark again behind the wall, forever, and an organ aching for what the wall does not have. That is not a gift. That is a wound with a ribbon on it.

Company for them. But they have company. They have the whole plateau's worth of company, unnumbered, moving at the speed of a season in the seams behind the sweating stone. The dozen in the bowl are not lonely. They are not anything. They are a window's worth of a thing that goes on for a mile in every direction, and the power looks at the window and does not enlarge it.

A fence, a name, a sign at the rim. A decree. And the people would come, because a sign is a promise, and they would come with jars and boots and the desire to have been here, and the cut steps would go slick and then go, and someone would drop something in the basin to see it fall. No.

Every gift it can think of is a version of the dryness pressing in.

---

So it looks for the one thing the hollow actually cannot do for itself, and finds it, and it is not in the hollow at all.

The room is held together by its refusal to change. But the refusal is not the room's. It belongs to the plateau — to the stone above that stores the whole country's rain and gives it back at one slow rate regardless of season. The hollow has arrived, yes. The plateau is still waiting, and what it waits for can change. A century that comes in drier than the last. A well sunk twenty miles off by someone who has never heard of this place and never will, drawing the stored water down a hand's breadth a year, so that in a lifetime the wet line sinks below the fern's seam and the basin goes to a stain and the hiss behind the wall thins to nothing and the white things that were in the basin are the last white things anyone above ground will ever see. A road, a quarry, the ordinary appetite of a dry country for whatever holds water.

That is the work. It is far from here and it is enormous and it looks, from the bottom of the hollow, exactly like nothing happening.

The power goes up onto the plateau and stays there.

It leans on the weather — not toward wet, toward *same*. It holds the storms that have not yet formed to the rate the stone can take and no more. It is patient about this in a way that has no name in any language spoken by things with a pulse. When the well-driller's eye moves across the map, the power is the small wrongness in the light that makes another valley look better. When the road is surveyed, it is the reason the line bends. It does this for the whole life of a species, if it has to, and none of it is visible from below the rim, and the drop keeps landing a finger's width from the rootstock every eleven seconds, and the basin rises to the notch and runs out, and the fern is always the same size and never the same fern.

A wise power understands that the greatest thing it can do for a place that has arrived is to make sure nothing arrives after it.

---

One more thing, and it is small, and it argues with itself about it.

The people. The ring encloses nothing. The niche is empty. The chisel never came back. The first account says this has made no difference to anything below the rim, and that is true, and it is also not the whole truth, because the difference was above the rim, in whoever crossed the dry country and found a room that wanted nothing from them and kept a few living pieces of the dark in a bowl. The hollow did not need them. But it *gave* to them, and a window no one looks through is still a window, but there is something in the power that does not like to see a thing made for looking go unlooked-into.

So it does not hide the way down and it does not clear it. It lets the three steps keep their blunted edges — findable, not inviting. It does not lead anyone here. It only declines to lead anyone away. Once in a long while, over the centuries it spends holding the rain steady, someone will come to the edge of the flat cracked ground and see that the ground is simply not there, and look down, and find the ledge. Someone who was already crossing, already thirsty, already the kind who stops. They will kneel at the bowl and at first see nothing and then, at the edge of nothing, motion that is not the water's. They will drink. They will go back up. They will not build a house. The power will not tell them what they saw. It is enough that the seeing happens; it does not need to happen often.

---

And when the work is done for the day — it is never done, but there are pauses in it, the way there is silence between the drops — the power comes back down.

It does not come as anything. In this light it casts no shadow, which is fitting, since nothing here does. It sits in the stone ring, which has been waiting for a body to make the curve mean something, and it looks at the bowl.

The white grain glides and stops. The drop lands. The rings spread and flatten and the surface is still again. The thread runs from the notch. Behind the wall the whole stored sky of the plateau moves at the pace of a season through a mile of stone, and the power can hear every drop of it, and could count the grazers in the dark if it wanted to, and does not want to. It has held that rain in its hands before it fell. It is holding the rain that has not fallen yet. And here is where the holding comes out into the open, mildly, without alarm, and asks for nothing, and does not know it is being watched.

It leans over. The rim is worn at exactly the height where a forehead would rest.

If it drinks — and it does, in whatever way a thing like that drinks — it drinks one of them, or none. It does not feel it. The water tastes of stone.

That is the closest the hollow lets it in. It finds this sufficient. It has never wanted more, and it has an organ for knowing there is more, and it has chosen, in this one place, not to use it.
