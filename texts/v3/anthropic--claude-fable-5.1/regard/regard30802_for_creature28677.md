It comes the way anything comes here: down a shaft, six hours late, with the weather.

That is the first thing to know about what it does. It does not arrive from nowhere. A power that is wise has read the first rule of the world before it enters, and so it waits on the surface for a grey noon and then rides down with the rain, and stands in the pale pillar of light and mist at the foot of the M shaft with water running off it, and is not seen, and is a little cold. It has chosen to be a little cold. You cannot understand a place like this from a warm position.

It has come because of a being. The word it was given was singular. It stands in the pillar and looks for the singular thing, and finds that the district does not divide that way.

---

There is the heron in the flume at M-19, one eye on the lane, which has an address and a route and a whitewash under a bracket and is the only thing here that does not care about the pumps. There is the tender at the sump who drops the end of every meal into the black plaza and writes the time. There is the tender at N-30 who sounds *convoy* early. There are the five cats, or six. There is a fern in a tie-hole with a ring around it, painted by a hand that has gone up and not come back and repainted since by hands that were not told to. There is a stalactite three millimeters long and a caliper. There is the moss, which is a map. There is the concrete, warm as a body, becoming the cold thing it will be. There is the water, which was here before and is only passing through and is always here.

And there is the thing they have in common, which the power sees the way you see the lamps from a distance — not the islands but the pattern of the islands. Everything here keeps to the edge of a light and waits for the pumps. The heron does it, the moss does it, the tenders do it in their fogged boxes with their thirty words. The concrete does it. Whatever was under the arch at row W did it. The being the power has been sent to is not any one of them. It is that posture. It is a way of living that has assembled itself out of a heron and some moss and a few dozen grey people and a language that grew in a valley with one road in, and it lives here, and nobody lives here.

So the power stops looking for one and considers all of it, and considers what it could do.

---

It could warm the place. It could bring the temperature up ten degrees and the ceiling would stop raining and the lamps would stop sweating and the tenders' knuckles would stop cracking. And the water in the flumes would go cloudy, because cold is what keeps it glass, and the midges would boom and the lake at K-43 would grow a skin, and the moss under the lamps would either die or take everything, and the heron would find the lane at M-19 full of things it cannot eat. The cold is load-bearing. The power leaves the cold.

It could stop the drills. The grinding under everything else could stop, and the face could hold where it is, and no more of the old work would come back along the ceiling as spoil with a different taper on its tie-holes. But the drills are the room. The district is a district because it is still being made; the day the face stops is the day the map at H-5 is finished, and a finished map is a map of something that is over. The power leaves the drills.

It could tell them about row W. It knows what was under the arch. It knows there was a cabin, and that the cabin had a kettle, and that the moss that is now a black mat the size of a palm was once green under a lamp of a make no one stocks, and that somebody down there, in whatever their rotation was, painted a ring around something. It knows how it ended and it knows how long the frost took to reach the shoulder. It could put this in the tenders' hands like a brick. And it looks at the tender who redraws the map, who paused over row W the once and then didn't, and understands that the pause was the knowing, and that the not-saying is not ignorance but a kind of load-bearing member of its own, a way of holding weight that would crack if you named it, the way the manual under the coffee can is not lost but simply not needed. They know. They know in the way the water knows rain before the shafts do. The power leaves them their pause.

It could carry the heron up. It could find the bird on the rail at the sump at night and lift it the way you lift a thing that has fallen, and set it on a riverbank under a real sky. And it thinks about the whitewash under the bracket, and the plywood offcut over the whitewash, and the horn that sounds early, and about what it means to be a thing with an address, and understands that this would not be a rescue. It would be an eviction.

So it does not do any of the large things. It is wise, which means among other things that it can tell when a place is already being tended, and this place is tended in every direction at once, by people who would not use the word. The ring, the offcut, the coffee can in the fogged window, the ends of things left by the east skid — the district is dense with small mercies practiced without instruction and passed hand to hand at the change of rotation like the horn code. Benevolence here already has a grammar. A wise power does not arrive in a valley with one road in and start speaking a second language. It learns the thirty words. It does what the tenders do, and does it their size.

---

What it does is this.

On a grey morning — meaning the hours when the pillars stand in the shafts — it lets the light at the M shaft come down clear. Not brighter. Clearer: the mist thins for a quarter of an hour and the grey pillar becomes a grey column with edges, and at the top of it, forty meters up and then the rest of the way, there is a coin of sky. The heron in the lane at M-19 looks up. It has never looked up here; it fell in months ago and has worked the flumes since with its eye on the water. It looks up for a long time with the fixed attention it gives the lane. Then the mist comes back, and it looks down, and takes a minnow the colour of the water it came in. That is all. It knows the way now. It stays because it stays. The power has given it the one thing that changes nothing and changes the meaning of everything: a door it has seen and not taken.

At J-22 it looks at the fern in the tie-hole and the ring around the fern and notices that the ring is orange, and that orange is the colour of the numbers stenciled at eye level on every pier. The fern has not been protected. It has been *numbered*. It has been made a column. The power stands in front of that for a while, the way you stand in front of something well made by someone you will never meet, and adds nothing to it, because there is nothing to add.

At the sump, on a night when the tender sounds the one long note — held until the breath goes, the one they say means *all clear* and does not — the power answers. Not by horn. It has no horn and would not use one; the horns are theirs. It answers from underneath. The second rhythm the tender has been logging for a year, the slow turning under the tremble of the intake, comes up through the black water until it breaks the surface: a length of something dark and old and unhurried rolls once in the lamp-broken reflection and goes down. A back. Nothing more. The tender stands at the rail and does not move for about as long as the heron does, and then writes the time in the log beside the pump hours and underlines it, and the story about the eels is not a story after that, and the note means what it has always meant, said back: *not all clear. Still here.*

---

And the fourth thing, which is for the ones who come after, because a wise power knows the pumps will stop. Everything does. It will not prevent that; it is not that kind of power and this is not that kind of place. The lake at K-43 will reach the lamp string in about a week, as marked. The frost will climb. The stalactite at K-44 will become a finger and the caliper will rust in the drawer and there will be a long stretch — long enough that the sodium lamps will be a thing that has to be explained — in which nothing here measures anything.

Then a face will come through. Drills of a gauge nobody here would recognize will cut into this slab as this one cut into the last, and someone in a cabin of some kind will look at what the conveyor brings back — rebar of an unfamiliar gauge, tie-holes with a familiar taper — and call it a materials question. And somewhere in the spoil there will be a piece of pier with a tie-hole in it, and in the tie-hole a black smear that was a fern, and around the hole, if the light is right, a ring of something that was once a colour.

The power does one thing about that. It goes to the newest hall, where the forms have just come off and the piers are grey-green and warm and not to be leaned on, and where at shoulder height on every one of them there are thumbprints, because no one can resist it. It puts its palm flat on a green pier the way the tenders do, briefly, to see how it's doing. The concrete is working under its hand. Then it presses a thumb into the skin, at shoulder height, among the others.

In a year it will be permanent. In a hundred a mystery. In a thousand, when this is the cold dark wet place under the world that it was always imitating, someone will find a row of thumbprints in a column at the height of a person's shoulder and count them and get the number wrong by one, and never know which. That is the message it leaves for the ones who come after, and it is the same message every tender here leaves without meaning to: *we were here, we kept to the light, we waited for the pumps, we touched the green thing to see how it was doing.* Nothing that would need translating. Nothing the place does not already say.

---

Up ahead a horn sounds — three short, off the far end, the new one. The power does not know it either. It waits, the way you learn things here, by being present when the signal comes and seeing what moves. And what moves, out of the dark under the batch plant, low and grey and invisible until they move, are the cats, five or six, coming to the east skid where the ends of things have been left, because three short means *food's out*, and someone at the far end decided the cats should be told.

The power learns the thirty-first word. It takes its hand off the pier, which is still warm, which means it is not finished, and moves on toward one that is.
