The power comes down the way everything comes down here: from above, through the lid. It does not part the water lettuce. It does not need to. It passes the thermocline and feels — because it has chosen, for this, to have something like skin — the warmth close over it, and the slight thickening, and the silt. It lets itself be carried a little way on the convection before it does anything else. There is no hurry. There is, here, the opposite of hurry, and the power has come a long way through places that hurried, and it is not above being glad of the change.

It does not act. That is the first thing. It drifts down the street and feels the Vallisneria close behind it, and it comes to the engine house and hangs under the broken rafters and watches the flywheel until it has seen it go once around. Then it stays for the knock. Then it stays for the next one.

It stays, in the end, for a great many. Time is not what it costs the power; attention is, and it pays. It learns the catfish routes. It finds the indigo fish and the madder fish and lies above each vat long enough to know that the color under them is not disturbed by anything the power is doing. It puts a finger, so to speak, on a pipe, and feels the fizz, and follows it — iron to copper to lead flashing to brass to iron again, the whole closed loop of it, round and round, a current that goes everywhere in the town and nowhere out of it. It understands the field before it understands her. This is deliberate. It wants to know what she was caught by before it knows what caught means.

Then it goes to the wheel pit.

She is around the axle where the account said she would be, pale as the water, with the eleven-foot wheel turning above her like weather. The power lowers itself into the warm rising column beside her and does not touch her. It lets the knock come through them both. It notices that she shifts, and how little. It notices the drift of crushed snail shell beside the axle, and reckons the size of the drift against the size of her, and understands from the arithmetic something about how long. It notices her eyes — the enormous eyes, open, black, made for a dark that has more in it than this one — and it notices that even coiled, even resting, they are angled up. Toward the thermocline. Toward the place where the light, faint as it is, at least comes from somewhere.

The power stays with her through several of her ascents. It watches her uncoil and go up through the shimmer into the cold, and it follows, and it watches what she does up there. She does not feed, mostly. She hangs in the clear water with oxygen in it and her whole length goes still in a different way from the stillness below — not resting, but listening, the way an animal listens when it has heard its name from a long way off and is waiting to hear it again. She stays an hour, a night. Nothing calls. She goes back down. The plants along her path sway.

---

Here is what the power thinks about, in the long quarter-hours between knocks.

It would be very easy to free her. That is the first thing to be said and the first thing to be set aside. The power could open the field like a hand, could unhook the town's circuit at any join, and the pull she has lived in for a century would simply stop, and she would be an eel in a warm hole with no reason to stay in it. That is not nothing. But it is not what she has, either. She has a place. She has a beach of shells that is hers and a route the catfish take in the dark that she knows from above, and a sound that has entered her body three and a half million times and to which her body has made its small adjustment every time. To take the field away is not to give her back the river. It is to take away the thing she has built a life against, and leave her to find, at a hundred, that the life was the field's and not hers. The power has seen this done to beings, by well-meaning hands. It does not think well of it.

It would be nearly as easy to keep her. To lay a hand on the cast frame and stop the thinning, so that the knock never ends and the field never breaks and she can lie where she is, warm, fed, counted, until her own long body finally lets go. This is what a merely kind power would do. This is what the town itself would ask for, if the town could ask: more of the same, at the same rate, forever. And the power looks at the boiler breathing with snails and the belts that have become jelly and still turn the stocks and the lamps being slowly swallowed by the plants that love them, and it understands that nothing here has been kept. Everything here has been *allowed*, and that is different, and the difference is the whole character of the place. The frame will thin. The wheel will drop. The power is not going to be the first thing in this town that refuses to rot.

So it considers doing nothing, which is what the wise so often end up doing, and which is so often right. Let her be. Let her wait for the next turn, if that is what living here is.

But it has watched her go up. It has seen her hang in the cold with her eyes open, listening for a call that the town has been imitating for a hundred years. And the power knows what the town does not, what she cannot: that the field she has been faithful to is a lie. Not a malicious one. An accident of dissimilar metals in mineral water. But a lie nonetheless — a voice shaped like the sea's, that said *this way* and meant nowhere. She came down for the truth of a direction and was given a perfect counterfeit, and she has honored the counterfeit with her whole life, and she still goes up to check.

That is what decides it. Not that she is trapped. That she still checks.

---

What the power does is small, and it is the smallest true thing it can find to do.

It does not break the field. It does not touch the engine, or the frame, or the bearing. It goes up through the thermocline and across the cold reservoir a mile to the spillway, where the old river, having been asked to wait for a hundred years, goes over a concrete lip and remembers itself down the valley. The power puts itself into that water and takes the grain of it — the electrical whisper of a real current moving in a real direction over stone — and it carries the grain back. And it lays it down, faint as a hair, from the spillway across the cold, down through the shimmer, along the street, in through the holed wall, into the wheel pit, and it stops just short of her.

One thread. One direction, in a place that has had every direction and therefore none. It does not pull. It does not overpower the town's circuit, which goes on humming round its loop exactly as before. It is simply *there*, the way a true thing is there beside a false one, and it says nothing except what the river has always said, which is *downstream is this way*, and it leaves the rest to her.

Then the power goes back to the wheel pit and lies down beside her in the warm, and waits.

---

It takes her nine knocks to notice, and the power counts them.

On the tenth she uncoils. Not the way she does for the ascent — that is a movement she has made ten thousand times and it has the smoothness of something worn. This is different. She lifts her head from the axle and holds it, and the barbels she does not have, the sense that lives instead along her whole flank, go through the water like a hand through a dark room feeling for a wall it has been told is there. She finds it. The power feels her find it. Everything in her length goes taut at once and then, slowly, releases — not into rest, but into something the power has no name for and does not try to name.

She does not go immediately. She stays with the thread for most of a day, the whole slow overturn of the convection, feeling it and letting it go and feeling it again, the way one presses a bruise to be sure. She goes up once, in the town's night, and hangs in the cold above the engine house where the thread runs past her, and comes back down. The power does not follow her that time. It stays in the pit. It wants her to have the choice by herself, and it has learned enough of her to know that she has never once in a hundred years done anything by anyone's arrangement but the town's.

Then, on the fourteenth knock of the second day, she goes.

She rises through the shimmer and the plants along her path sway and the whole town seems for a moment to notice, and this time she does not turn at the top. She takes the thread across the cold, four feet of pale old body moving with a purpose it grew for in another century, and the power, which has kept its promise to itself, is not beside her. It is below, in the warm, counting. It feels her reach the spillway through the thread, the way one feels the far end of a rope go slack when the load is off. It feels the true river take her. Then it lets the thread go, and the grain of the spillway fades back out of the town's water, and the field is what it was: iron to copper to lead to brass, round and round, everywhere and nowhere, a machine keeping time.

The power does not know if she reaches the sea. Four thousand miles, at her age, with her gut open and her belly the color of tea — probably not. Probably she is a heron's somewhere down the valley, or lies up in a bank under a root when the cold comes and does not lie up long enough. The power could find out. It does not. It has not given her the sea. It has given her back the direction she came down for, and let her have the going, and what a being does with a true direction is the being's, and the power is wise enough to know where its part ends.

---

The wheel pit is empty. The drift of broken shells beside the axle is still there and will be for a very long time; nothing in this town clears anything away. The knock comes through the water every fifteen minutes and there is nothing in the pit to shift at it.

The power stays. This is the last thing it does, and it does it for the town rather than for her, and it takes a great while.

The engine turns for no one; the place said that of itself and was very nearly right, and now it is simply right. The catfish do not attend to it. The snails know nothing. The plants reach and do not want. The account of this place set aside one being who would be the only thing in the town that noticed when the knock stopped — and she is gone, and it seemed to the power a poor thing that a sound which has kept such faithful time should stop, whenever it stops, in front of nobody.

So it lies down where she lay, around the axle, in the warm, under the turning wheel, and it makes itself the color of the water. And it waits — not for the next turn; it does not need the next turn — for the last one. However many that is. The frame was cast heavy. It has time.
