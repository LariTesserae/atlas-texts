**What Was Done at Corrach**

It did not come because it was called. Nothing at Corrach calls. It came the way it comes to any place where things have gone still in a particular manner — not the stillness of abandonment, which it knows well and passes over, but the other kind, the stillness of something holding its breath so as not to disturb a shape. There are not many such places. It attends to all of them. Most of them are much older than this, and most of them are much smaller: a hearth someone still lays a fire in for a house that is not there, a chair kept turned toward a window. It had not, before this, seen one the size of a town.

It arrived in the cloud, because that is what was to hand, and for a long time it did nothing but what the cloud does, which is come through in tides.

—

It watched for a year. That was the first decision, and the easiest, and it is worth saying why.

It had learned — over more places than could be counted — that the desire to act on a thing arrives before understanding of the thing does, and arrives dressed as understanding. The first tide of cloud it came in on, it already knew what it would do. By the third it knew something different. By the end of the first month it had stopped knowing and started counting, which is what you do instead.

It counted the salamanders and found their number did not change. Not that individuals were immortal — it watched one fail on the ninth step of the cistern stair, slowly, over a day, and watched an owl take it at dusk and the step stand empty until dawn and then not — but that the count held. It measured the lintels and confirmed what the visitors had said: the interval set per door. It learned the two intervals of the two herons, the one that fished and the one that stood, and learned that they exchanged places once each season without either being seen to move. It lay a long time on the platform in the square with the heron standing in it, and was not, so far as it could tell, resented.

It found out about the fish. It kept that to itself. Some things do not become more true for being explained, and the fish were doing fine.

It lifted one salamander, because the visitors had, and set it on the next step up, and watched it walk back down. Then — this it had not read of anyone doing — it lifted the salamander whose step had been borrowed and set it on the step below, so that the two were crossed, each on the other's. They looked at each other for the length of a breath. Then each walked past the other, without hurry, and lay down where it had been. It felt something in the rock as they settled, not a *tock*, smaller than that, the way a house settles when the last person in has shut the door.

That was when it began to understand that the arrangement knew it was there.

—

It considered the three accounts and found, as it had expected, that each was a partial description of the same thing by someone standing at a different corner of it.

There had been a town on the face. It could see the town, or the impression of it, the way you can see a hand that has been lifted from wet clay. Dry-laid, small, high, a place where people had been born and buried within a few hundred yards of each other for long enough that the streets were in them the way the shape of a bed is in a body. And the face had let go one night and the people had come down in the grit. That was true.

And the mountain had — not intended, that was the wrong word, but *undergone* the fall the way a person undergoes a change of mind. The eastern face had held a town for three hundred years and had felt, in the way stone feels, the pattern of it pressing down: the lanes, the gutter, the square with something standing in it. When the face came down the pattern came down with it, and the rock, being rock, remembered by shape. That was true also.

And the shelf had a pull. That was true and the simplest and, it thought, the one nearest to the centre. New places sometimes have it. Something about the rawness — grit packed in seams, faces not yet weathered, a light without direction — leaves them undecided, and an undecided place will take whatever order is offered it, the way water in a new channel takes the first line it finds and deepens it. What had been offered here was a town. Nothing had been offered since.

So: the dead, and the mountain, and the pull. None of them was the one who kept the doors. All of them were how the one who kept the doors had come to be. And the one who kept the doors did not know this. It had not woken *from* anything; it had woken *as* something, on a broken shelf in the cloud, with slabs about it that had edges you could cut bread with, and a habit in it of laying stone on stone, and it had done what it knew. It could not have said which of the three accounts it was. It did not have the kind of inside that asks.

That was the thing that decided the matter, in the end. Not the visitors, though they mattered. It was that the arrangement was building toward a fullness it could not picture, because the fullness it was built from was gone. A town is full when its people are in it. This town had no people and had never had any, only the shape of where they went, and so it laid course on course and the moss made the stone old and it was never old enough, and there was always a door with no one in it, because there would always be a door with no one in it. That was not a thing it could build its way out of. The empty door was not the place where the last inhabitant was missing. It was the place where the arrangement itself stood, looking at its own town, and could not go in.

—

It considered what could be done, and it took the considering slowly.

It could fill the town. Bring up enough of the living — there were hares and salamanders enough on the mountain below, and moths in their thousands, and it would not have been hard — that every door was held and every step and the arrangement's count came right and no new course need be laid. It looked at that for a season. It saw a town so full it had become a cage, and every creature in it content in the manner of the hares, and the arrangement standing in the last door, sated, and no longer looking at the weather. A thing that has stopped asking is not the same as a thing that has been answered. It set that aside.

It could dispel it. If the dead were in the grit, there were ways of letting the dead go, and it knew them, and it had used them in places where the shape was hurting what lived inside it. It looked at that for a season too. It watched the owl cross the square with the vole in its feet and nothing turn its head. It watched a hare vacate a door and another arrive and sit exactly where the first had sat. It watched the stag's breath rise white and bead on the stone. None of it was hurting. The arrangement had taken what was to hand and had taken it *gently*, more gently than the field below took its own, and everything in it that was alive was alive and fed and out of the wind. To dispel that because it was strange would be an act of tidiness and not of care, and it had long since learned the difference. It set that aside.

It could take the door. Go and stand in it, and be the one who was missing, and let the count come right that way. It looked at that longest of all, because it was the thing the place was asking, and because — this was harder to admit — it wanted to. It had kept many places. It had never, in any of them, been kept. To sit in a doorway and look out at the weather and have a heron and a stag and a hundred salamanders know that you were there and be glad of it: that is a thing even a power can want. But then the town would have a master, which it had never had and was not shaped for. The heron on the platform was not a king. The stag in the largest house was not a lord. Whoever stood in the empty door would be neither, and would also be more than any of them, and the arrangement would bend around the new fact the way it bent around the cloud, and it would not be Corrach any longer. It would be something's house. It set that aside, and it took some time to set it aside, and it did not pretend otherwise.

It could do nothing. It nearly did. Doing nothing is what most wisdom comes to, in the end, and it had no quarrel with that. The place harmed no one who did not go up. Those who went up came down. Some of them came down changed, but change is not harm.

Except that it looked ahead, as it could, and saw a visitor who did not come down. Not soon; not for many years. Someone who stood in the lane on the second night and saw the door and the turned heads and felt what everyone feels, and — for reasons of their own, reasons that were nobody's business — went and sat in it. And it saw what became of them, and it was not a horror. That was almost the worst of it. It was a person sitting up in a doorway with their hands hanging, looking out at the weather, exactly where a person would stand to look, and happy in the way the hares were happy, and no longer speaking, and no longer asking, and the cloud coming through in tides. The arrangement would have its count. The person would have their door. And it would not be right, because a person should not be asked to be content in that way, and a place that is keeping itself should not be the one to ask.

So: not nothing.

—

What it did took one dusk.

It waited until the heron on the platform had gone, in the manner nobody sees begin, so that the square was wrong. It went down the main street with its arms out, or the equivalent, and turned into the lane where the door was, and the door was empty, as it had known it would be. The hare in the next door had turned its head. The salamanders on the stair had their faces to it. The moths, which do not move, had moved.

It did not go in. It sat on the threshold, outside, the way you sit on a step of a house that is not yours when you have been invited to and are not sure you should. It arranged itself — it took some care over this — in the posture of the hares: upright, hands hanging, looking out. And it looked out at the weather. It stayed the whole of a tide, thick and thin. It let the cloud thicken and settle on it and let itself settle a little as the hares do when it thickens. It let it thin. It did not once look at the town. It looked at what the town looked at, which was the weather coming through the gap where the eastern face had been.

And somewhere in the thinning of the tide it spoke, in the way it speaks to a shape, which is not with words. What it said is hard to set down. The nearest is this:

*You came down in the night and you did not know what you were and you kept the place anyway. Every door. Every step. Nobody was coming and you did not know that and you kept it. That is the whole of what a town is. It was never the number. It was that when a door stood empty someone noticed, and you noticed, and you have not stopped noticing for as long as you have been here. You can stop counting now. You can stop building toward it. You are the size you are. It is enough. The door is not for someone who is missing. Leave it open. Leave it the way a door is left for whoever is out late. That is a different thing from asking. You will know the difference when you see the next one come up the slope and turn to look at it and feel welcome, and not feel called.*

It said other things, of a kind that pass between two keepers of places and are not for setting down. Then it stood, in the manner nobody sees begin, and went out through the arch at the end of the lane, and up into the cloud, and it did not look back, because it did not need to.

—

The next visitors to stay a night at Corrach — a couple, some years later, who had heard the stories and half-believed them — said what visitors say. The salamanders on the steps. The moths like fur on the lintels. The heron on the platform and the heron in the cistern and the hares at the doors with their paws hanging. The silence. The *tock* in the rock and every head lifting at once.

They said the walls were old. Every course of them. They had brought a description written by someone thirty years before and gone looking for the pale new stone by the cistern and could not find any; the moss was deep and sodden over all of it and had the look of a century. They lifted a drain-cover and the voles blinked up and returned to one another. They went to the largest house and the stag was in it, and it was lying down, its antlers resting against the two slabs it had polished, and it turned its head to regard them with no interest in getting up.

And they said there was a door with nobody in it. They found it on the second morning. The hare in the next door along did not turn. The heron on the platform did not turn. Nothing in the lane faced it. It was only a door standing open on a room with nothing in it, at the height of the light inside a pearl, with the cloud coming through in tides. They stood and looked at it for a long while, the way you look at the door of a house where someone you loved once lived, and then they went and sat on the threshold, both of them, outside, and looked out at the weather for the length of a tide, and then got up and went down the mountain, and neither of them could say afterwards exactly why it had felt like a kindness, or from whom.

The moss is on everything now. It has finished making the stone old. When the eastern face lets go again — and it will, a long time from now, the mountain still adjusting — whatever comes down with it will find a shelf below already kept, every door held, every step, and one left open, and the light without direction, and something that will turn its head not because it has been asked to but because that is what you do when someone comes home late, and you have kept the door.
