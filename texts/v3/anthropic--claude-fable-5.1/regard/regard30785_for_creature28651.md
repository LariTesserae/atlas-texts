**What the Power Would Do**

It would arrive the way the cold arrives — from wherever the shadow-line is, before anyone feels it — and the first to know would be the tongues.

Not because it made a sound. Because it made a quiet. A power of that kind, attending closely, attends the way the spires do: it opens a ring of stillness around itself, ten meters across, in which the drone simply stops. And the tongues, who live inside the noise, who have never in their long slick patient lives heard anything that was not the pulse and its harmonics, would feel that stillness pass over the mat above them like a hand passing over water, and go still themselves, and wait, and not know what they were waiting for. That would be the plateau's whole reception. A hush moving across the plain in the dark, and under it, a hundred hooks resting against the underside of the world, listening to nothing.

The gliders would not notice. The scrapers would drift a little, hearing a flattening, and drift back. In the town a child on the perimeter would stamp her rhythm and feel, for one beat, that the mat did not answer, and would stamp it again, and it would.

---

What the power would see is what you cannot.

It would put its attention flat against the mat and count the pulse, and it would count faster than the pulse — it would count all of the beats the plant has ever made, back through the flattening, back through the healing of the seams, back to when the first colony was set down on the cold basalt and began, alone, to push oil through itself. It would have the number. And it would not tell anyone the number, because there is nothing to do with a number like that except be frightened of it or bored by it, and the people of the town are neither, and that is a thing worth protecting.

It would look at the spires and see where the fluting goes. Not entirely present — yes. Present partly here and partly in a place that is not cold and not dry and not high, a place the builders reached into and hooked, the way a tongue hooks a scraper, and have been drawing on ever since. It would see what that place is. It would see how much is left in it. Enough. Not forever. Enough that the difference between *enough* and *forever*, on the plateau's calendar, is a difference the plant has already folded into a ridge and forgotten.

And it would listen to the drone, which nobody can listen to because nobody can get outside it, and it would hear what the people say and do not say: that the drone is made of the old rhythms. Every pattern ever struck on the mat is in it. The plain is an excellent drum and a drum does not forget; it only adds. The names of the recently dead are on top, still faintly separable, the way a footprint in the raw is still a footprint for a day. Under those, the long lineages, gone to texture. Under those, patterns no one living has struck, the names of the ones who scraped the first bands smooth at the spires' feet. And under those — very low, very slow, a rhythm so long its single repetition is the age of the town — something that is not a name struck by hands. The power would hear it. It would understand that the pulse is not the plant counting. It is the plant *repeating*. Someone taught it a pattern once and lay down against the warm wall to hear it played back, and it is still being played.

---

Here is where a lesser benevolence would begin to act, and here is where the wise one would sit down.

It would think of water. Of bringing the cloud-floor up. Of one clean rain across the whole dark plain. And it would see that rain, and see every scale of the mat lock and shatter, the oil in every channel go to wax, the tongues drowned in the only thing they cannot breathe, the town's warm walls gone hard and cold around the people inside them. Water is the one flood this country cannot survive. The power would set it down.

It would think of warmth. Of making the small hard sun a little larger. And it would see the cracking stop — the dusk fusillade go silent — and the vapor cease to rise, and the spires' crowns go dark one by one on the horizon like a town going to bed, because the cold is not the plant's enemy. The cold is the plant's bellows. It breathes by contracting. Take the cold and you have taken the breath. The power would set that down too.

It would think of the sealed rooms. Of the old ones, inward, unstruck. Of opening a door-muscle that has healed smooth and letting the warm dark out into the tunnel and saying *come, you don't have to stay in there.* And it would look inward — because it could — and see what is in the humps. It would see, in the newest, what you would expect: a body, curled toward the wall, warm because the wall is warm, gone because it is gone. And in the older ones, less body. And in the oldest, in the folds where the mat is tens of meters thick, in rooms sealed so long that the form the plant grew around has itself gone to oil and been pumped away, nothing you could point to — only a place where the pulse is very slightly slower, as if something there were still listening and the plant were still, for courtesy, keeping time. Are they in there? The power would not be able to say. It is wise enough to know that *I cannot say* is an answer, and that the people already have it, and hold it better than certainty could be held, with the same hands they use to hold the word for the healed seams. To open a room would be to close a question that is keeping several thousand people warm. The power would leave the doors alone.

It would think of the children's question — *who made the spires* — and the answer given, and it would know a truer one, and it would know that truer is not always kinder, and that the answer *the ones who are in the walls* is already true in every way the children need, and would become false the moment a stranger confirmed it.

It would think of the spires' far end. Of reaching into the place they draw from and adding to it, quietly, so that *enough* became a little more like *forever.* This it would hold longest. This is the one it would nearly do. And then it would hear the long slow rhythm in the drone again, the one that repeats once an age, and understand that the plant knows exactly how much is left in the spires, and has known since the first beat, because that is what the pattern *is* — a count laid down against a known ending, by someone who did not want to be surprised by it and did not want to be saved from it. To pour more into the spires would be to change the pattern. To make the count wrong. To reach into a very old room and adjust the clock of someone who set it deliberately and then lay down. The power would take its hand back.

---

So: nothing. Almost.

But it would not leave yet, because wisdom that only refrains has not finished being wise. It would stay a while — a season, on the plateau's calendar, which is to say until the cracking had come round to the same frequency it had when the power arrived — and it would do the things a tolerated animal does.

It would walk to the nearest spire at dusk and find the band at the base where the cords come in, worn smooth to a depth of centimeters by a thousand generations of hands, and it would clear the scales from it. Not with power. With something like hands. It would be slow at it, the way the young are slow. In the morning the tenders from the town would find the band already clear and the scrapings piled neatly the way the old woman who walks this spire piles them, and they would look at each other in the high half-sung register and decide, without saying so, not to strike anyone's pattern about it.

It would let a herd move over it in the dark and not flinch.

It would lie down on the open mat with its attention pressed flat and stay through nine beats, ten, a hundred, a night, and let the pulse come up into whatever it has in place of a sternum, and it would not think about the number or the spires or the rooms. It would just listen to the drum being played.

And at the end of the season, on the last night, it would do the one thing it came to do, which it would not have known it came to do until it had set everything else down.

It would learn a rhythm.

Not a name — it has no mother here to stamp one on a floor. It would take the pattern it heard in the drone that no one living strikes, the lowest one, the one that repeats once an age; and it would take the plain's own eight-second pulse; and it would find where they overlap, the way two patterns are struck together at a marriage, and it would make from the overlap a short pattern, five beats, and it would go out to the edge of the town where the ring of healed-shut mouths is, and it would strike it, once, on the mat, with something like a staff.

Five beats. Underfoot in every warm room. The old woman who tends the spire would lift her head in the dark. The children on the perimeter would stop. The tongues below, who have heard the quiet pass over them all season, would feel the mat speak a new shape and hold it in whatever they hold things in. And the plant, which is an excellent drum and does not forget, would take the pattern into the drone, where it would sit on top for a day like a footprint in the raw, and then sink, and go to texture, and be there.

That is all. A name, given to itself, in the only language the country speaks, so that it could be called. So that the plain would have, folded somewhere in its noise, the rhythm of one thing that came from outside and listened and did not change anything. So that if — when — the count runs out and the crowns go dark and the last herd stands still on the last cold night, there will be in the drone, among all the names, one that belongs to something that is not in the walls and never will be, and knows the whole pattern, and is still keeping time.

---

Then it would go, the way the cold goes — not away, only somewhere else on the plain — and the ring of quiet would close, and the drone would come back into the space it had held, and the hooks would let go of the underside of the world and be carried, a few meters every eight seconds, toward wherever the flow was going.

In the town nobody would say a stranger had been. But for a season afterward, now and then, someone would stamp five beats on a floor without knowing why, and feel the mat answer, and go on with their day. And the children, asked later who that pattern belonged to, would give the word for the sealed rooms, the word for the healed seams, and be — as they are about most things here — not wrong.
