It comes in winter, because that is when the lake can be listened to.

It waits until the ice has closed over the plates and the light has gone blue and the plant has stopped its breathing out, and the basin has gone as near to quiet as it ever goes, and then it comes down through the ice without breaking it and through the ceiling without parting it and hangs in the cold open water between two columns, the way the char hang, and does nothing at all for some time.

This is not indecision. It is the first thing a wise power does in a place like this, which is to let the place set the pace of the visit.

---

What it sees, hanging there, is what you would see and then what you would not.

It sees the pillars and the arches and the thirty bells of held air glowing faintly from inside, each with its one occupant, or its one and a child, breathing slow in the dark. It sees the paths in the mat, and it can count the feet that made them, which you could not, and the count is very large and very old and belongs to a town perhaps three times this size. It sees the empty chambers, six of them, holding air for no one, and it can hear the one on the far side that has a seam gone soft and is losing its air a little faster than the plant fills it, and it can tell you, if you want the figure, how many winters that chamber has left. It sees the diver's roost on the rim above the ice, and it knows how many children the diver has taken in the lifetime of the oldest person below, and the number is not large, and the number is also every one of them.

It sees the direction. The place is not still. It is moving very slowly toward being smaller, and it has been moving that way for longer than the arches have stood.

And it sees, in the one chamber nearest to where it hangs, the one who watched you at noon in the summer—the one who folded its hands—asleep in the green dark with the rib and the flake knotted at its waist, and the beginnings of an arch outside its door that it has been scoring for eleven years and that will need another sixty.

---

So it thinks about what it could do. It is a power. It could do a great deal.

It could warm the water a degree. It runs that forward and watches the ice thin and the winters shorten, and the plant, which spaces itself by light and not by warmth, does not notice, but the char do, and the char shoals grow and drift and the drumming changes pitch, and the people, whose whole morning is measured by that pitch, lose the clock they did not know they had. It puts the degree back.

It could take the diver. One bird. It would be easy and it would be the end of a fear that is only the shape of a fear now, a form that has outlasted its reason. It runs that forward too, and watches the stillness go slack across two generations, because the young no longer see the old go flat against the stems and so never learn the thing that stillness is for—which was, in the end, never only the bird. It leaves the bird.

It could give the plant more light. Then the columns would crowd, and the distance between them would close, and the distance between the columns is the distance between the bells is the distance between the people, and it would have closed all three at once with a hand that meant well. It could give them more words. But the words here come with the air, in rooms the size of a bed, and to give more words it would have to give more air, and to give more air it would have to give bigger bells, and a bigger bell is a column that will never become an arch. It could seal the leaking seam. It could finish the arch.

It finds, and this is the part that takes the longest, that there is nothing it can touch that is only one thing. The lake has been arriving at itself for so long that every strand of it is now also every other strand, and to pull on one is to pull on the whole, and the whole has already found the distance it can hold. It could, with the best intent in the world, make this place better in some measure it brought down with it and worse in every measure the place has of its own.

That leaves the direction. The slow going-smaller. The six empty bells.

It sits with that one longest of all, because it is the one thing here it is not sure of. It knows the difference between an ending and a resting. It knows also that from outside, in a place that moves this slowly, the two can look exactly alike for a thousand years, and that the ones who live inside the movement are the only ones who could tell you which it is, and they have no word for the question. They would have to make room for it in a bell. It would take a whole afternoon's crackle to say, and another to answer, and it is not certain the answer would be any different from what the text of the place already says, which is: there are no signs of it being a loss.

So it does not ask.

---

What it does is this.

It goes into the empty chamber. Not the leaking one—the nearest one, the one that has stood full for no one longer than anyone alive can remember. It goes down through the low mouth and comes up into the bell of still green-lit air that smells of cut grass and iron, and the walls sweat, and the sound is close and dead, and it stays there.

It stays the whole winter. It learns the ice from underneath, the splitting and the metal groan coming down through the ceiling. It learns the smell of the pith. It learns how the plant, through the cold months when it is not breathing out, does not stop being a wall. Around it in the dark the other bells breathe, and there are low voices in them, few and short, and it does not listen to the words, only to the fact of them. It notes that the plant fills this chamber, that has no one in it, at exactly the pace it fills the ones that do. That is what the plant does. It does not seem to distinguish. It holds air for whoever is there, and if no one is there, it holds it anyway, for as long as the seams hold, which is a long time.

It thinks that this might be the wisest thing in the lake, and that it did not bring it down with it. It found it here.

---

In spring the ice goes and the light comes down green again and one afternoon the first pores begin to tick.

Before the frying rises, it comes down through the mouth of the chamber and out into the bright water, and it does one thing. It puts a knuckle against the column that holds the empty bell and raps once.

Here. One.

The note is that column's own, and it goes out across the basin the way every rap goes, and in the stems everyone in the water knows which column it was and knows that no one lives there. Three stems away, after the pause this place takes before anything, someone raps back—their own note, their own count—and then goes on with the morning's stroke along the same line as yesterday's stroke.

That is all. It is not a message. It is not a promise. It is what the language allows, and it says exactly what it says, and it will be filed the way things are filed here, without alarm, in a body that already carries the afternoon crackle as a clock.

Then it goes up through the ceiling without parting the plates and through the water without troubling it, and the char do not scatter, because it takes nothing.

It does not stay to see whether, in ten years or sixty, someone bends a young stem toward that column and lashes it there. It has decided not to know. It has left the town the size it found it, and the distance the distance, and the bell full of air for whoever comes, and it goes back to wherever it goes carrying the one thing it took, which is the sound of a lake being alive, and the knowledge that the most careful hand it had ever seen was not its own.
