It arrives the way the tide does here, which is to say on no moon. It has come because something in the pattern caught its attention: a plain of poured grey, a schedule where a sea should be, and on the plain a thing lying flat and out of time. It has seen ruins and it has seen reefs and it has not, until now, seen a place that was neither yet and might become either.

The first thing it does is nothing, for a long while. Long enough for the gates to cycle several hundred times. Long enough for the crust on the tower legs to gain a finger's width. It lets the gyre carry its attention round the basin four days at a turn and it looks at everything the way the ROV looks at everything, seam by seam, hole by hole, but without the cable.

Because it is benevolent, it wants to act, and because it is wise, it distrusts the wanting, and so it makes itself list what could be done, and looks at each one until it can see what would be lost.

It could open the gates and leave them open. Give the place a real tide, the moon's tide, a slack and a spring and a neap, and let the basin be the bay the bass think it is. It considers this for the space of a season and sets it aside, because the gobies in their million square holes turned into the coming water before the water moved, and they did that because they were born here and the pumps are the only tide they have ever had. To give the place the moon would be to take from them the thing they know. It would be a kindness to the sea and a theft from the basin. The power has seen that mistake made before, on land, in the name of restoration, and does not intend to make it under water.

It could grout the tie-holes. It laughs, in whatever way it laughs. Millions of small square dwellings, each with a tenant, arranged by a surveyor who had no idea he was drawing a town. It would sooner grout a city.

It could carry her out. The skate. It could lift her on a flood and set her down outside the wall on a bottom that was never poured, with the deep water she grew old in and the chance of others of her kind. It considers this longest of all, because this is what love does first: it takes the thing it loves and puts it somewhere it thinks is better. And then it looks at the gates, which open twice a day, and have opened twice a day for years, and through which she could go any time she lifted her wings and chose the roar. She has not chosen it. There is nothing on the sea's floor as level as this. She lies on the concrete as if it were poured to receive her, and the power, which knows a good deal about receiving, is not going to correct her on the point.

It could bring her a mate. It looks out beyond the wall for a long time, feeling for the shape of one, and finds them scarce, and far, and it would be a thing to do — to nudge one along a current, to time him for a flood — and it would be a good thing, perhaps, for her kind, which is running out. But it would make her into a project. It would make the basin into a hatchery and the wall into a fence and every future flood into a question of whether he came. She has a road and a floor and a schedule she is not on. The power decides the sea's business is the sea's, and that if the sea sends a male through the sluice it will not be because the power arranged it. This is the hardest of the settings-aside, and it is not sure, and it lets itself not be sure.

What is left, when the large kindnesses have been put down one by one, is to look at what actually threatens her. And it is not the basin. It is not the cold, or the noise, or the flatness, or the absence of anything to hide behind — she needs nothing to hide behind. It is the schedule of a different kind: the maintenance planner, the asset register, the line in a spreadsheet three years out that says the sacrificial anodes on the pipeline are due for review and that an impressed-current system would need a quarter as many nodes. It is the ecological survey that a regulator will one day require, and the well-meaning diver who will see a critically endangered animal on a slab of industrial concrete and know, with total certainty, that she belongs somewhere else. It is the flag, the tag, the net, the tank, the rescue. The things that will come for her are the good intentions of people who have not looked long enough, and the power has just spent a season learning exactly how long that is.

So it does the smallest thing it can find that is still a thing.

There is one person in the control room inside the wall who keeps a laminated sheet of seal counts out of no requirement but interest. The power has noticed her, because she is the only one in the building who watches the water for reasons that are not on a screen. On a quiet shift, with the ROV out on a seam patrol it has flown four hundred times, the power does not move the vehicle — it has scruples about steering things — but it lets a small current, one of the standing bands of the pumps' arithmetic, lean a few metres east, so that the yellow machine drifts off the seam, and the pilot, who is the seal-counter, lets it, because the seam is boring and the pipe is right there. She follows the red pipe. She counts the anodes on the way because counting is what she does. At the sixty-first, on the lights she does not usually need, she sees a raised grey edge on the grey, and two small eyes, and a slow breath lifting film.

She stays there forty minutes. Nobody asks why.

What she writes afterwards, at the bottom of the seal sheet in the same pen, is not a species record and not a report. It says: *Skate. Big. Female (I think). Pipeline, anode 61. Been here a while — crust on the pipe around her.* And under it, after a pause the power can see even from the water: *Leave her.*

That is the whole intervention. A line on a laminated sheet, in a room where things written on laminated sheets have a way of being read when the planner comes round, because they are the only writing in the building that no one was paid to produce. When the anode review comes up, someone will say, *hang on, isn't there something about the pipe,* and the road will stay lit a while longer. When the survey comes, the diver will be told, before she goes down, by someone who has been here longer than she has: *there's a skate on the pipeline, she's fine, don't.* It is not certain. It is not a guarantee. The power does not deal in guarantees for wild things and would not respect itself if it did. But it has moved the odds by the width of a pen line, which is about the width of the film on the floor, which is how everything here begins.

Then it turns to the place itself, because the invitation said *this place* first, and the power has come to think of the basin as something that also lives here — not a creature, but not nothing. A thing with a heartbeat of pumps and a slow gyre for a circulation and a first hill growing under a boil of gulls. A young thing. Everything square, everything legible, everything stamped with the date it was set. The power has watched enough young things to know what they need, and it is not help. It is not to be hurried. In a thousand years, if the pumps still run, or if they stop and the gates rust open and the moon gets in after all, there will be shoals and channels behind the towers and the seams will have worn to something like riverbeds and no one alive will remember it was poured. The power's gift to the basin is to want that, and to want it slowly, and to not lift a finger toward it. To let the millimetre be a millimetre.

It makes one promise, and it makes it to no one, which is how it keeps its promises. She is old. Her kind live long and she has lived most of it, and the level floor is not going to make her younger. When she stops — when the film settles on her and does not lift — the power will be there, on that flood, and it will see to one thing. Not that she is mourned; the basin does not do that, and would not know how. Only that she is not taken. That no barge comes for her, no lance, no crew, no sensible disposal. That she stays on the concrete she chose, at whatever anode she chose, and that the current combs the film over her, and the crabs come, and the tubeworms sign her, and in the eddy her body makes the grey begins to bank a little deeper than elsewhere. Cartilage does not leave much. But a rise. A slight thing on the level, a place where the floor is not floor, the first feature on that whole poured plain that was not designed and did not arrive through the bars. The gobies will find it. The bass will hold in its lee. In a hundred years it will be a shoal and in a thousand no one will know what it was, and the power will, and that will be enough.

Then it goes, the way it came, on no schedule anyone here would recognise. The gate groans. The column leans. Everything that has learned the place turns into the coming water, exactly on time; and at anode sixty-one a grey thing on the grey breathes, and does not turn, and is written down.
