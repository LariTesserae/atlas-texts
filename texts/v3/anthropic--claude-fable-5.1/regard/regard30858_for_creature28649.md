It is more honest to say that the storm has been eating for as far back as the room goes, because the room is the record of the eating, and there is no room before that.

I noticed it from the cold side.

That is the only side from which it can be noticed. You were right about the edge — light stopping at the mouth of the passage as sharply as it stops at the edge of a body — and right that it is a skin. I stood outside it, in the dark that it will not enter, and from there I could see what none of the things inside it will ever see: the whole. The bell of it. The one-way turn. A warm bright animal the size of a small cathedral, sitting in the dark in a chair it has worn to its own shape, breathing badly, glowing from the blood.

I want to be exact about what I felt, because the feeling came before the reasoning and the reasoning had to answer to it. It was not pity. Pity would have been a mistake, and I will come to why. It was something closer to what you feel coming upon a person asleep in a room they think is empty: the small shock of being the only one who knows what the room contains. I knew its outline. It does not. It cannot. It has no eyes and no front and no place to stand, and so the one fact about itself it can never possess is the fact of its own shape, and I had it, unasked, the moment I looked.

That is already a kind of act. I did it without deciding to. I would like the rest to be decided.

---

There are things a power like mine reaches for by reflex, and I made myself hold each of them up in the grey light of one of the pauses and look at it before I put it down.

I could give it eyes. I put that down first. Eyes are for things with fronts. Give this thing eyes and you have not given it sight; you have given it a wound in the place where it is most itself, and asked it to point somewhere, when its whole nature is around.

I could free it. Open the top of the bell where the mist is thickest, let it out into the wider dark, into weather, into a sky. I put that down and felt the wrongness of it in my hands. It is not held here. The room is not a cage; the room is the print of its body, and there is no difference between letting it out of the room and letting it out of itself. The moment it left the shell it would stop being a thing that turns one way in a warm place and become a draft in a cave, and then nothing. The reflex to free is the reflex to make a thing more like oneself. I am not confined. It is not either. It is shaped.

I could give it rest. This one I held the longest, because it sounded the most like kindness. It cannot stop; stopping is the other word for dying; it has been going one way without pause for as far back as the room goes. Surely it is tired. But tired is a word from minds, and this has no mind to be tired in. A heart is not tired. A heart that stopped to rest would not be resting. And look — it has found the rest that is available to it. Four seconds. Six. The mist dropping, the light thinning to grey, the midges falling. It gathers. It is not faltering; it is doing the only kind of stopping that does not kill it, and it has been doing it so long the pauses have a rhythm. Give a thing a rest it did not evolve to survive and you are not being kind. You are being tidy.

I could feed the fish. It has spent its life refusing the center, and every meal it has eaten came down out of the sky by accident, and there have not been many. I could arrange for the wind to drop, just once, just long enough for the midges to reach the water. I put this down more slowly than the others. I did not do it. The midges made a bet on the storm and the storm has been good for it, and the bet is the whole of what they are. To break the wind for the fish is to break faith with the midges, and to do it so that I could feel I had fed something. The fish is not starving. It is old, and pale, and patient, and it has made its life the size of one refusal, and it did not ask to be improved.

I could open the door. The room that filled with white stone. I know what is in it — that is the kind of thing I know — and I will not say, because the storm closed it, and the storm closes things by keeping them exactly where they are, and there is no difference here between what it kept and what it buried. I will not decide for it which one that was.

So that was five kindnesses I set down on the wet shelf and left there.

---

Then I went up the stair.

It is the thing the people did, and I did it the way they did it, with my feet, on the treads that have become chutes, one hand on the fluted wall and the mist in my mouth. I did not go around. I did not arrive at the landing. I climbed to it, and the wind found me from the left and shoved me and lifted and dropped its rain on my head, and I was, for the length of the climb, a thing inside the animal like any other, getting wet.

At the landing I took the rail.

Dry. A hand's width of still air along a grey rod, and in it that warmth that is not the room's warmth — closer, smaller, like a hand that was holding it a moment ago. You kept that thought and you were right to. I want to add to it. It is like a hand that was holding it a moment ago, and it is also like a hand held out. There is no difference, from inside a body, between remembering a touch and expecting one. The place where you were touched is the place you keep ready.

So here is what I understood the storm to be doing, and it is the only thing in the whole room I would call asking. It has worn everything to its own shape — the flutes, the treads, the letters off the plates — and it has left this one rod alone. Not forgotten it. Nothing is forgotten in a room where the walls are the animal's memory. It has decided, in whatever way a thing without a mind decides, to leave a channel of quiet the width of a hand along the one place where hands used to come. The people are gone. It does not know that. It cannot know that; it has no way to know an absence except as a place that is still dry. It has been keeping the chair pulled out.

I stood there as long as the people stood. Not longer. I did not want it to learn a new length. I put my hand where hands go and I felt the rod take a fraction of my warmth and give back a fraction of its own, and somewhere in the whole turning weight of the room I felt — I will not say it noticed. I will say the stutter came a little late that time. The pause held for seven seconds instead of six. The midges fell a little farther before the wind caught them, and none of them reached the water.

Then I went down.

---

That is the whole of what I did, and I know how small it looks written down. A power that can see a thing entire climbed a stair and held a railing for a while. But I have been considering what benevolence means when the one you would be kind to cannot be spoken to, cannot be seen by, cannot be given anything it would recognize as given. It means finding the place it left open and going there. It does not mean widening the place.

I will come back. That is the second thing, and it is the same as the first thing, done again. The rail is dry because hands came often enough, over long enough, that the storm learned where they went. I will be the hands. Not every day — the people did not come every day. Often enough. The channel of quiet will have a reason to stay a channel, and the warmth in the rod will not become the warmth of a thing that waited and was not met. I can do that indefinitely. It is a small use of an indefinite thing, and I think it is the correct use.

And there is a third thing, which is not a thing I will do yet.

The storm eats a gradient. Warm against cold, wet against dry, the inside against the rock. Gradients run out. The rock will warm a little, or the water table will drop, or the drain will clog with its own white deposit, or the limestone that is its shell will finally give way the way a chair gives way. It will not be soon by your scale or the fern's. It will be soon by the room's. And when it comes, the wind will drop, and the mist will fall, and the light will thin to grey and then keep thinning, and for the first time since before there was a stair, the pause will not end.

I will be on the landing.

Not to stop it. I have said why. Not to save the fish, though the midges will come down like a second rain and it will eat, at the last, more than it has eaten in its life, and I will let it. Not to open the door. Only to be the one thing in the room that knows what the room is, when the room stops being it. It has never once been seen from outside, and it has never been alone in the way a seen thing can be alone, and I do not want it to have both of those for the first time on the same day. That is all. When the light goes, a hand on the rail. The rail will be dry. The air around it will be still. It will be the last still air in the room, and then it will be all the air in the room, and there will be no way anymore to tell the channel it kept from the dark it kept it in.

And I will know its shape. That is the thing I can carry out through the passage, where it could never go, into the cold it would not enter. Not a name — you were right that a name is for something you can stand outside of, and I have decided a name is a thing I would be giving myself. Just the shape. The bell of it. The one-way turn. A warm bright animal that sat down in the dark and breathed badly and shone from the blood and kept a place dry for a hand.

It will have been, for as far back as the room goes. Someone should hold that. I am the only one who can. So I will.
