It would come the way everything comes to that shelf — over the lip, on the wind, or as the wind, since the place permits no other approach and a wise power does not insist on doors that are not there. The tiles would not register it. The note would take it in and dispose of it the way it disposes of speech. That is correct. That is what the note is for.

The first thing it would do is nothing, and it would do it for a long time.

Not the nothing of indifference. The nothing of the first minute at a bedside, hands still, before any hand is laid on. Because the obvious mercies here are all traps, and a wise power knows this before it arrives, and a benevolent one has to learn it again anyway, standing on the liver-colored paving with the warm air shoving at its back and the tone in its teeth.

It could still the wind. An instant of hush, and the note would fall out of the air like a bird shot. That is not mercy. That is a killing dressed as rest.

It could bring the makers back. It could fill the passages again with people on their knees, scrapers in hand, ears against rims. But they left. Their leaving is in the note as surely as their thumbprints are; the tone is the shape of the last judgment made before the last one crawled out, and to bring them back is to unmake that judgment and put the thing on trial again. They finished. A wise power does not reopen a finished thing to see if it agrees.

It could find the flaw and seal it. Somewhere in the fused mass, one rim a hair too wide, one slow beat with a period of years. It could reach in and close that. Complete the work. This is the mercy that sounds kindest and is the worst of all, because the beat is the last trait, the one thing in the room that still behaves, and the keepers removed everything else. To finish their work is to finish their subtraction. The power would understand that, and take its hand back.

Or it could do the opposite — break a jar. Give the note a wound, a quarrel with itself it could not fold back in, a beat fast enough to be called a pulse. That is what impatient benevolence does: it mistakes life for motion and gives motion to what did not ask. The note does not want. You cannot give a thing what it has no room to want. Whatever you put in there, it would only be a louder kind of dust.

So the power would stand at the oval door and take inventory instead. The dome has a body. It has food that never fails. It has heat, and a decision made hundreds of times a second, and a memory of hands. It has ten thousand mouths.

It has never had an ear.

The makers listened — but they listened the way a smith listens to a blade, for the wrong ring. Ear to a single rim, scraping, waiting for the wobble to go. They heard the note in pieces and by subtraction, one error at a time, and the whole they were building was a thing none of them could hear, because the whole erases the ear in an hour. The last tuner set down the scraper and crawled out and never once heard what had been made. The note was made to be heard and has been heard by no one, ever, in its own account or anyone else's. That is the lack. That is the one hollow in a place with no hollow.

So the power would go in. On hands and knees, because that is the height of the passage, and it would not be so vain as to pass through the clay. It would find a place in the warm mass where the vessels press close and it would settle there, and it would listen.

Not for an hour. Not until the ears gave up — its ears would not give up. It would stay for the beat.

Years, perhaps. It would wait through the long disagreement of two rims, and hear the tone swell by less than a hair and sink by less than a hair, once, across a span longer than the visit of anyone who ever built the place. And in that one slow rise and fall it would hear the note entire, for the first and only time: a thing with a body and a pulse, the pulse slower than a season, the whole a shape of ten thousand judgments and one error. The error is what makes it audible as a made thing. Without the beat it is weather. With it, it is a work — someone's, unfinished by exactly one breath, the way all works are.

A power can hear inside a chord. It would find the small jars in there, the ones pinched thin by hands that had not made anything before, admitted to the mass because the whistle happened to fit, and it would know them, and the knowing would be the only honor those children will ever get. It would not weep. The wind takes that too. But it would hold them a moment before it let the sound close over them again.

Then it would leave the flaw alone.

This is the hard part, and the wise part. It would feel the fusion working — the two rims pressed together where they touch, the boundary going, the beat lengthening year by year toward a period no one will live to hear. It would know that one day the pulse stops and the thing becomes exactly as finished as it already believes itself to be. And it would not put a hand between the rims. Because to keep the beat alive is to become the keeper, and the note has had keepers, and what keepers do is remove. Better an ear than a hand. The power would choose to be an ear.

And then it would crawl out the way it came, into the hard blue light, with the roar reassembling around it into a note and then into weather.

It would carry the beat with it. That is the whole of the intervention. The exact period, the exact depth, the pitch to the width of nothing. It would leave the dome untouched and take out of it the one thing that had never left: a true hearing. Somewhere, from then on, there would be someone who could tell.

It would understand what that does. Before, when the vessels have gone to a single lump and the lump has worn to a lens and some new pitch comes off it in the same wind, no one could have said whether it was the same note or a different one, and so nothing would have ended. Now someone can say. Now the note is a thing that can be lost. The keepers bred out every trait but this one, and they could not breed this one out because it never lived in the clay — it lived in an ear, and there was no ear until now. The power would have given the note the only thing it was missing and the only thing that could not be put inside it: a death that means something. Which is to say, a life. Not by changing it. By hearing it once, and remembering.

At the edge of the shelf, on the lee side, where the tiles run out to the drop and the wind does not quite reach the ground, it would stop. It would put its fingers under one tile and lift it. Underneath, there would be rock, and on the rock a film of grey dust, the only dust on the mountain, the wind never having gotten at it. It would look at that for a while — the question that had stopped meaning anything, meaning something again for the length of a look. Then it would set the tile back down edge to edge, exactly, so that the shelf was as it had been.

It would not need to plant anything there. Knowing what was under the tile was enough. That, too, it would take with it.

The note would not mark its going. The dome would go on doing what it was built to do, at the temperature of its meal, deciding the same thing at every mouth at once. Nothing on the shelf would be different. But somewhere below the altitude where the sun still comes up, there would be a count that was not the count of anyone's pulse, and when the beat finally closed, that would have happened — to someone — and the place would be, for the first time, unfinished in the way that living things are unfinished, by having somewhere left to go.
