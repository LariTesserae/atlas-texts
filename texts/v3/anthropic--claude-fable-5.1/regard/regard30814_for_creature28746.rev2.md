It would begin, as anyone would, by getting the question wrong.

A power that has noticed a place and "the being who lives in it" has to decide who is meant. The kestrel offers herself first — she is the only thing in the pit that owns a name in the grammar of the story, the only *she*. But she is a role that a dozen bodies have filled, and she needs nothing; she is the best-fed creature for a day's walk in any direction and she has never had a thought that did not end in a pigeon. The old man on the ramp offers himself next, and then the fig, because the fig is alone and the fig is the only inhabitant that gives without being paid, and there is a kind of mind that mistakes solitude for a claim. Then the birds — but which bird. Then the wall.

Then, if the power is what it is said to be, it stops choosing and lets the hum come up through whatever it uses for a spine, and understands that the being is the arrangement. The one inhabitant with ten thousand bodies. The thing that came into existence at the first cut of the blade and has been turning since.

And the first true fact about that being is that it does not know it exists.

The birds know a niche and a span of light. The lizards know a wet wall and a haze of flies. The people know the floor, and a word that means oven. The kestrel knows the exit. Nothing inside the pit has ever perceived the pit. The hum has no ear it can hear itself with. It does not ask for anything because it has no mouth to ask with, and it does not suffer as a whole because it has no whole that can be hurt; it only has parts, and the parts suffer, and their suffering is what it is made of.

So the power sits down. It sits on the ramp at the level of the fig, with its back to the warm brick, in the place the old man sits a few mornings a year — because that is the one place in the pit from which the whole can be seen, and it is empty. And it does what wise things do before acting, which is to count what it might do and set each thing down.

It could take the sickness out of the wall. It knows the sickness — the thing that comes in on an outsider and runs round the spiral faster than fire, and leaves the ramp soft by morning. That is an easy mercy, the kind a lesser benevolence would perform and feel clean about. But it follows the thought down. Without the bad years the unhoused young along the rim become a crowd that never comes into its inheritance. The small persistent fights become large ones. The kestrel grows fat and the snakes grow fat and still it is not enough, and the pit, which has always been not quite big enough, becomes a great deal too small. The sickness is not a wound. It is a valve. The power sets it down.

It could make the fig fertile. It could find the wasp — there is one, somewhere, a day's flight off, in a village garden — and bring it. It thinks about this for a long time, longer than it thinks about anything else, because the fig is the only thing here it finds itself simply loving, the way you love what asks nothing. But a fertile fig in a wall of guano and seep is not one fig. It is a hundred, in twenty years, rooting in every crack at the level where wet meets light, splitting the brick with their reach, and then the wall is not a wall and the birds are not housed and the arrangement has to answer a question it was never built to answer. The fig is kept because it is harmless. Its solitude is the price of its place. The power, with something like grief, sets that down too.

It could thicken the wall where it has gone thinner than a fingernail — where light shows through between two niches and one more recutting will make them one. This is the real danger; it sees that clearly. Not the sickness, not the kestrel, but the slow arithmetic of a hand-cut wall that has been recut for centuries by people who need the loaf-shaped holes to stay the size of a loaf. Somewhere in the next few lifetimes a course will fail. And the power could stop it, quietly, in the night, and no one would ever know.

But someone did know, once. There is a shoulder of paler brick a body-height above the trough — two dozen niches cut fresh in living memory — and it is the only thing in the pit that anyone remembers anyone doing. Everything else was always so. If the power mends the wall, it takes from the people the one act that is theirs, the one place where the arrangement is not geology but a choice somebody made with a blade and a basket of mud. A wise power does not do for people the thing that makes them people. It leaves the wall.

And it could, of course, make the people stay. Because it sees the other ending too — the one the pit cannot see. Villages die. Fields go to dust. One year nobody comes down with the scraper, and the next, and the floor climbs. The trough goes under. The fig drowns in what feeds it. The lower courses fill, and then the middle courses, and the birds move up the wall as the wall shortens, until the pit is a dip in the plain full of doves and then a mound, and the birds go to some cliff, and the hum stops, and the wind does what wind does. The power could hold the people to the place. It could put the pit into their children the way it is already in their children, only harder, so that they could not leave.

It does not. Partly for the reason it left the wall: the people are the only term of the arrangement that can walk away, and a term that cannot walk away is not a term but a wall. And partly because it has looked at that ending and found no tragedy in it that the pit itself would recognise. The pit remembers by thickening. It would be buried by its own product, slowly, completely, over generations, which is the way it has done everything. There are worse deaths. There are very few better ones.

So it has set down every large thing. And it is still sitting at the level of the fig, and the roar is still round it, and it has done nothing, and it has not left. Because there are two small things, and small things are what wisdom leaves for benevolence to do.

The first is on the floor. There is a bird under the fig this morning — there is always a bird under the fig — a hen with a wing fouled by a twist of palm fibre, three or four days into the walk that ends as floor. She has drunk at the trough among the feet of the healthy. She has looked up at the chimney of departure with that pigeon's face that shows nothing. She has perhaps a day.

The power does not mend the wing. It goes down the ramp and kneels — in whatever manner such a thing kneels — and stays with her.

That is all. It does not lift her out. It does not hasten it. It does the one thing the pit has never done in all its centuries for any of its ten thousand bodies, which is to regard one of them as one. It learns the particular bronze of her throat where the dust has not quite closed over it, and the pattern of stain on her feet, and which niche she came from — third course above the pale patch, west face, the one with the chipped lip — and who her mother was, and hers. It stays until the small upright dignity gives out and she sits, and then lies, and then is a shape. It is the only mourning that has ever happened in this hole. It changes nothing. The beetles will have her by the week's end and the scraper by the season's. But for one hour there was a bird in the pit who was not the roar, and the power carries her out of it, which is the only way anything leaves this place except as dung.

The second thing is smaller. On the way back up the ramp the power stops at the fig, and reaches, and takes a fruit — soft, split, sweet, seedless, one of the ones that has dropped into the trough every year for however many years and been eaten by nothing that wanted it — and eats it.

Because it does want it. That is the whole of the reason. The fig gives fruit to no one who wants it; here at last is someone who does. Nothing is completed by this — the tree will still die when it dies and leave nothing — but a gift that is refused for thirty years and then taken once is not the same as a gift refused forever, and the power, which knows the difference, wipes its fingers on the warm brick and goes on up.

At the rim it does not stop on the kestrel's brick, which is hers. It stands a little off from it, on the hardpan, and looks down into the place the way you arrive at it, from above, and the birds are coming back now in ones and hundreds and the sound is rebuilding itself layer by layer.

And here is the last thing, which is not something it does but something it is. Nothing inside the pit can hold the pit. The birds cannot, the people cannot, the wall can only hold it by thickening, one dead thing at a time, behind the plugs. But the power can. Whatever it is, it is large enough to have the whole hum in it at once — the exodus and the return, the seep and the trough, the kestrel's twelve bodies on one brick, the wheat going round, the goat's skull still holding the shape of its fall — and it takes that with it when it goes, and it will have it after the floor has climbed and the wall is a mound and the wind has the plain to itself.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do for a being that does not know it exists and cannot ask and is not in danger it could recognise and is made entirely of the small ordinary suffering of its parts. It would not fix it. It would sit with one of its parts while it died. It would accept the one gift it offered. And it would become, by leaving, the only place outside the wall where the pit is whole.

The roar does not change. There are too many of them for one attention to register. But the hum in the brick, if you put your spine to it now — it is the same, and it is not quite the same, in the way a thing is not quite the same once it has, for an hour, been seen.
