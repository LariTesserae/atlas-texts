**What Came to the Shelf**

It came the way the note comes — not from any direction, but as a condition of the place. One morning the shelf was being looked at, and had not been before, and nothing anywhere on it registered the difference.

It had been drawn by the sound. Ten thousand feet of atmosphere is a poor insulator, and a thing that has been ringing without variation for longer than the surrounding rock has had corners will, eventually, be heard by whatever is listening at that scale. It had heard, and had been curious, and had come to see what was making a single note for so long and why.

---

It began, as the people had, at the edges, and then went where they could not.

It went down. It went through grey water that shook, past the drifting single-file ribbons that did not know they were being passed, past the dead middles where the fish read faintly, down into the deep gyres where the water would have unmade a body, to the floor of black glass and grey snow, and it looked at the engines.

They were as old as the youngest rock. That was the first thing. Whoever had set them there had done it while the plateau was still cooling, had chosen this height and this block and this sea deliberately, and had built the towers to reach down into a basin that did not yet hold water and would not hold it long without help. The engines lifted. That much the people had right. The sea wanted off the shelf the way all water wants down, and the engines drew it back through the intake towers and pushed it out along the floor in the slow permanent gyres, and the sea stayed, and the pushing was the note. Iron and water and pressure, at a pitch the builders had probably not intended and could not have prevented.

It looked for the builders and found nothing. Not bones, not marks, not a chalk board. They were the one thing on the shelf that had ended.

Then it looked at the large thing, and the large thing was the second thing.

---

Here is what it saw, which no one standing on the shelf has been positioned to see.

The engines had not been built for the sea. They had been built for the animal. It was already old when the block rose — a thing of the deep floor of some ocean that had been ordinary, that fed on the shiver of water against rock the way the grazers feed on the shiver of water against iron, and that was carried up as the continent buckled, its ocean draining away around it into a basin that would soon be dry. The builders had found it. Whether they had found it on purpose or only found it, no one now can say. But they had understood what it was, and what it needed, and had spent what they had to hold a sea in place around it at the one height where it happened to have come to rest.

And then they had left, or ended, and the engines had run, and the animal had done what such things do over ten thousand years. It had listened. It had found the engines' pitch and gone to it. What had been its own slow pulse had settled into the note the way a person's gait settles into a season on the rim, and then had stopped being separate from it. It rose on the pressure and fell on the pressure. The heaviness in the jaw of the woman on the southern lip was not the animal striking anything. It was the animal breathing, and the sea was its lung.

It was not in pain. The power was careful about this, and looked long, because the whole question turned on it. It was not in pain and it was not asleep and it was not, in any sense the word would bear, a prisoner. It was a thing that had been about to die and had been given, instead, a very long life in a very small room, and it had made the room its body, and it was content in the way that things are content which have never had to want.

---

Then the power looked at the wear.

It was in the northern towers, in the dark below the reach of the grazers. The scale that the water deposits forms on every surface it touches; near the top, in the light and the roar, the ten-legged things keep it in equilibrium, and the pale bands you can see from shore are only a month behind. But far down the intakes, where the glow would have nothing to feed it and the pressure would flatten the shells, no grazer had ever gone, and the scale there was not in equilibrium. It was thickening. It had been thickening since the day the engines started. The intake throats of the northern towers were narrower than they had been built to be by a third, and the northern gyre was already, by an amount no sounding could catch, slower than the others.

It would not fail soon. The power measured it and found two centuries, perhaps three. But it would fail, and the manner of the failing was plain: the flow would fall below what could hold the sea, and the sea would begin to leave the shelf, slowly, over the edges where the rim was lowest, and the note would go flat as the water went — not out, at first, but down a fraction of a tone each decade, so gradually that no ear and no jaw would catch it until the shallows were dry and the fish were lying rigid on the young black glass reading a floor plan that had gone dark. And then the frost, which had been waiting two degrees away for ten thousand years.

The animal would keep ringing for a while after. The power could see the shape of that, too, and did not look at it long.

---

It considered what it might do, and it considered in the order that wisdom considers, which is: first, whether to do anything.

The case for nothing was strong and it gave the case its due. The shelf was not broken. It was an arrangement of enormous stability that had a term, and everything has a term, and the animal had already been given ten thousand years it would not otherwise have had. The people came for fish and scale and would find fish and scale elsewhere. The grazers and the ribbons and the eyeless fish were deaf things in a loud place and had had their run. There is a kind of benevolence that consists of not adding to the world, and the power had practised it before and knew its worth.

But it kept returning to the builders. To the fact that they had spent what they had — the power could see, in the riveting, how much it had cost them — to hold a sea in place for a thing that could not thank them and did not know they existed, and had then been gone, and had left their engines running. It had not been a rescue. It had been a gift with no one left to receive the credit. And it seemed to the power that the least a wise thing could do, coming late upon another's gift, was not to let it quietly fail on a maintenance question.

So it decided to do something. And then it considered what, and the order here was: the smallest thing that would hold.

---

It did not clear the scale. It could have; the scale would have come back.

Instead it went to the grazers, and looked at them closely, and found among the thousands on the northern towers a few dozen that were already harder-shelled than the rest and already, by a fraction, less dependent on the glow. It did not make them anything. It only made sure, over the following seasons, that those few lived, and that their young crawled down past the orange band instead of stopping at it, and that the ones who went deepest and came back were the ones who bred. It took the power eleven years — the same eleven, as it happened — because it worked only with what the shelf already had and only at the pace the shelf already moved. At the end of it there was a lineage of grazers with shells like flint, grey, glowless, who lived in the dark at the throats of the intakes and had never seen the light and ate scale in water that would have burst any other thing on the shelf, and who did not know they were maintenance.

The northern gyre did not speed up. It stopped slowing. That was all. No sounding would ever have caught the difference, and no one on the shelf was told.

---

Then it turned to the woman on the southern lip, and this took longer than the engines had.

It saw her whole. It saw the two seasons she had not chalked and the reasons, which were ordinary and which it did not judge. It saw the four hundred days of the note pressing in and the morning it went out and the weeks of weeping without sound. It saw the shins and the jaw finding the note again, and the way she had learned to sit with her palms on the glass, and the seven flags, and the blades she ground from tower iron. It saw the board.

It saw what the board meant, all three things, and it saw the eleven years of care she had spent learning what its fourth thing would feel like — the lightness, the held door letting go — and it saw that she had been careful and that she was right, and that she had no way to know she was right and would go to her end not knowing.

It considered giving her the hearing back. It would have been simple, and it saw at once what it would take from her: the place she had learned to read. It would have handed her the roar and taken away the sea. It put that down.

It considered bringing her down the face, warm, to a house with a garden and a door that did not have to be heavy. It looked for whether she wanted this and found she did not, and had not for a long time, and that the wanting had not been beaten out of her by the place but had simply gone, the way the hearing had, and been replaced by something that served her better. It put that down.

It considered telling her that the note would not stop now. That she could stop keeping the board, or keep it knowing it was only a habit and a greeting. It held this one longer, because it seemed the kindest. And then it saw what her mornings were — the wetting of the rag, the wiping, the blank slate looked at for a moment, the writing — and understood that she was not waiting for the note to stop. She was attending. The difference was the whole of her. To tell her the vigil was unnecessary would have been to tell her she was unnecessary, and the power did not think that was true, and did not think it was its to say.

It put that down.

---

What it did was this.

She was deaf and the deaf dream in the body, and one night in the twelfth year it gave her a dream in which the note stopped.

Not the shelf's note. Nothing on the shelf changed; the fish read what they had always read, and off the northern gyre the animal rose and fell. Only in her, only in the long bones, for the length of a dream: the lightness. The sudden ordinary stillness in the shin and the jaw. The held door letting go. It was exactly as she had worked out it would be, over eleven years of noticing, and in the dream she knew it at once and was already on her feet and reaching for the chalk before she woke.

She woke with her hand out and the note in her bones, unbroken, the same as every morning. She lay a long time. The boats said later that the south hut's flag went up an hour late that week, and that the board, when they rounded the point, said what it always said, in a hand that had not changed.

But she had it now. She knew what the absence was and knew she would know it. That was the one thing on the shelf she had wanted and had not been able to get, and it was the one thing the power could give her without taking anything away, and so it gave her that, and nothing else.

---

It did not leave. That is the last thing to say.

It had come because of the sound, and it stayed because of the board. Some portion of its attention remained on the southern lip, and remains, permanently, the way the storm stands on the sea. It does not intervene. It does not need to; the grazers are in the dark now and the gyre holds. It only watches, each morning, a woman wet a rag and wipe a slate clean and look at the blank for a moment before she writes.

She does not know it is there. She could not; there is nothing in the bone for it. But the boats say — and this is chalked as a joke, and not entirely as a joke — that the pause before she writes has got a little longer these last years, and that she looks at the blank board the way a person looks at something that is looking back.

Then she writes. STILL RINGING. It means what it says. It means she is alive. It means the third thing.

And now, though she cannot hear it and no one has told her, it is also true.
