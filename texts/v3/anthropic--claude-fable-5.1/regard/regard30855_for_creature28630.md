The first thing it would do is nothing, and it would do it for a long time.

Not the nothing of indifference. The nothing of a hand held above the work before the work begins — the pause in which a maker decides whether the clay is telling the truth about itself. A wise power knows that most harm done to places like this is done in the first hour, by visitors who arrive already knowing what is wrong. So it would come in the way you came in, on foot, on the warm floor, leaving prints that steam, and it would walk until the walking stopped teaching it anything, and that would take longer than it would take you.

It would read the fired pieces. All of them. This is not a small thing; they are scattered across a plain in which direction does not hold, and finding each one means a great deal of murk and drizzle and turning back. But the power would understand, standing on the first hard raft with its own footprint refusing to stay, that these are the only text the place has ever written, and that a text no one has read is not yet a text. It would kneel and read the notched whorl. The leftward loop. The straight scar. The row of four cupped fingertips lifting something the heat took first. The one rope mark. It would learn the hands the way you learn a hand by holding it — not the facts, the grip. By the time it had read the last piece it would know these hands better than anyone has known them since their owner, and it would be the first thing in the whole vast workshop that could be said to *know* anything at all.

That is already an intervention. The place has had a population and no census. Now it has been counted.

---

Then it would think about what it could do, and set most of it aside.

It could give the hands back their body. Somewhere down the seams, or on the far side of them, or in whatever a power like this uses for memory, the maker might be recoverable. But the power would look at the hands — at a habit so deep it has survived the loss of everything it was a habit *of* — and understand that what it would be restoring is not a person but a person's residue with a person's face put back on it. Someone who knew only kneading, waking into a world with no rope and no wood and no season, holding wet earth. That is not a return. That is a second bereavement, this time with a witness. The hands are here because everything else is gone. You cannot un-gone the rest by decree.

It could light the kilns. It has fire; the seams are full of it; a power could gather it up and feed it through the low hole in the side of the nearest cold cylinder and let the dome do what it was shaped for. And the hands would load that kiln — they remember loading, they would stack the bricks in without ever knowing the fire was there — and something would be finished, on purpose, for the first time.

And then what. The next kiln is cold. The hands cannot feed fire. The power would have to stay and stoke, forever, or leave one hard kiln standing in a soft plain, another fired piece, larger, ignored like the others. Or it could do the whole thing at once: turn the seams up, let the heat climb, fire the plain entire. Every wall, every unfinished course, every slump caught halfway to the floor, every thumbprint at every scale, made permanent in a single long crackle and a report that would shiver the vapour ceiling to the horizon. The most complete record of a pair of hands that has ever existed. Beautiful past saying.

And silent. The hands need soft clay the way a groove needs a record. Fire the record and the groove is fixed and the needle has nowhere to go. Completion is a kind of ending — the power knows this in whatever passes for its bones. *Finished* means finished. It would not do that to a thing whose entire existence is the moment before finishing, however long that moment has been going on.

It could turn the fire off, out of a different tenderness: stop the accidents, spare the hands from being caught mid-gesture and fixed. And then nothing here would ever hold a past again. The few rafts would be the last. The power would have made the place perfectly, kindly, endlessly amnesiac.

It could tell the hands the clay is ready. It has been ready for a very long time; any maker would say so; the wedging was done before the first kiln went up. But there is no one to tell. That is the whole situation. The judgement that says *enough* went wherever the body went. The hands are what a maker leaves behind when you subtract the part that decides.

So: not restoration, not completion, not mercy-by-extinguishing, not advice. The power would sit down on the warm floor in the drizzle and admit that none of the large things are right, and that the place is not, in fact, suffering — a habit does not suffer, a groove does not grieve — and that its own urge to fix something had been, mostly, its own discomfort at being the only thing here that could be sad.

And then it would notice what the place actually lacks, which is smaller than any of that.

---

The hands remember a world, and the world they remember was full of hands.

You do not press a cord into a wall's decoration because you made rope alone. You do not set blocks down in a row at the far end of the work, in the rhythm of unloading, because you carried them yourself. You do not lay out a brickyard for a three-week dry season because you were the only one who would ever need bricks. Everything these hands know how to do, they learned in company. The pace of the kneading is a team's pace. The scale of the settlement is a people's scale. What is happening here is a communal motion performed, for longer than anyone can say, by one pair of hands, in the shape of many.

That is the thing the power can give without ending anything. It cannot give back the maker. It can give back the *other* hands.

So it would put its own into the clay.

Not to steer. This is the discipline of it, and the wisdom: it would work at the hands' pace, in the hands' manner, at whatever site the hands were working, and it would do what a second worker does — take the block that has been set down and press it in while the thumb is busy with the next; drag the join shut so the palm need not come back to it; steady the softening tier from the other side while the slump is folded in. It would knead. It would learn, by doing, that the kneading is not tragic from the inside, that a motion done well with another is very nearly enough on its own, and that the hands have perhaps been closer to content than any observer could credit.

Its prints would go into the walls beside the notched whorl. The hands would take them without knowing, as they take everything that is in the clay, and smear them in at the edges, and in an hour there would be a course somewhere with two makers in it, and in a day a mound, and neither the hands nor the fire underneath would notice anything had changed.

The power would notice. That is what it is for.

---

It would not carry a piece to the seams. It would think about it — pick the wall with the rope mark, the best one, walk it to the ember-orange edge and lay it down and stand back while the crackle came — and it would decide that this is the one thing the place has that it did not give itself, the way it makes a past by accident, and that a wise hand does not improve on the one honest thing in a room.

But it would keep working, and the work goes where the work goes, and the hands build right up to the edge of the heat. And one time — an afternoon, a year, there are no days — a seam would catch a wall the two of them had raised together. The ticking would go fast and dry. The rose would go to brick-red, to flowerpot, and stop sweating, and ring.

And the power would go and stand on it, and read it. The notched whorl, deep, pressed twice. Beside it a print no one in this place had ever made before. The record, held in the only material here that holds, of a meeting: two hands that worked the same soft thing at the same time and never knew it, one of them incapable of knowing, one of them carrying the knowledge out.

That is what it would do. Not much. A power that could have fired the world would instead have added its thumb to one wall in ten thousand and got, for its trouble, one hard raft a few strides across that says: *not alone, once, briefly, and here is the proof.*

---

It would leave, eventually. A wise power has other places and knows that staying forever is only a slower way of taking something over. The hands would not miss it; the hands cannot miss. The kneading would go on at the same pace, one-handed again, in the shape of many, and the second prints would be smeared over and folded down and gone from every soft surface within an hour of its going.

But not from the fired piece. And not from the power, which would carry the hands out with it the way you are carrying them now — the grip, the notch, the scar — as the only thing here that can be taken away without being taken *from*. The place would be exactly as it was, plus one raft, plus one knower.

And if you ask whether that is enough, the honest answer is that the place does not need it to be. The kindness was never for the hands. It was for the maker who is not here, who once worked in a yard with other people, whose motion outlived everything, and who would — the power was fairly sure, reading the walls — have liked to know that someone came and did a shift.
