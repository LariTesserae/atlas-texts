Something else has noticed this place. It does not arrive, because it has never been the kind of thing that needs to arrive; but at some point its attention comes to rest on the massif the way a hand comes to rest on a warm stone, and stays.

It does not begin by deciding anything. It begins by doing what the person in the ledger does, which is to look for long enough that looking becomes a kind of knowledge. It counts the dark birds at dawn and gets four. It counts the sedge and gets the same number the ledger has. It finds the pressure schedule that nobody has explained and understands it in a moment — a slow flexing in an aquifer a kilometre down, a tide with no moon behind it — and understands also that this is not a thing it will tell anyone, because the person has been listening for that rhythm for years and the listening is worth more than the answer.

It looks at the pools and sees the rings and reads the temperature by colour, as you did, and then reads something you could not: the age of each terrace, the year each crack became round. It watches a pile turn itself over on a ledge from morning to dusk and does not find, anywhere in the turning, a single lizard that is unhappy. It looks into the mineral grit where the eggs are and checks the one thing that could be wrong — the heat that decides what the hatchlings will be — and finds the mountain has held it correctly for as long as there have been eggs, and lets out something that in another kind of being would be a breath.

Then it thinks.

It thinks about the rampart of husks first, because a benevolent thing goes toward death before it goes toward anything else. It could move the line. A few degrees more in the outermost fissures and the warm zone would grow by a hundred metres and the ones on the outside of the crowd would have a hundred metres more to get back. It holds the thought and turns it over and sees what the person saw: the lizards drew that line themselves, and the living come out in the morning to lie on the dead for the little warmth the dawn leaves in them, and if the line moved the rampart would be inside it and would rot. Everything that has happened on the massif has happened at the edge of what the mountain can give. To give more would not be kindness. It would be a different mountain. It sets the thought down.

It thinks about the birds and does not think for long.

It thinks about the machine. It can see the third wellhead — the one whose pressure is falling by a fraction a year, the one the ledger has been recording in a hand that has not changed but has certainly noticed. It knows the year the governor will close for the last time and the fan will stop and the ring of concrete will cool, and the densest crowd on the mountain will drift, over a week, back to the pools and the pipeline and the seized units, and lie on the small turbine the way they lie on everything else that has stopped. It knows the stove will go cold. It could hold the fissure open. A fingertip on a crack in the deep rock, a decade longer, two.

It considers this for what would be, in the massif's time, an afternoon. Then it considers the ledger — the pressures and outputs, twice a day, in a script that became brief without becoming careless — and it understands that the person has been keeping the machine's pulse the way they keep the throat-pulse of the old ones at the rims, not to save it but to be there when it changes. To take that ending from them would be to take the last page they are waiting to write. And the mountain will still be hot. There is water on the plateau you could boil an egg in without a stove. They will manage, the way the lizards manage, by lying against what is warm. It sets that down too.

It thinks about the person.

It knows how they came here, and why they stayed, and you do not, and it is not going to tell you. It knows they are not lonely in the way the word is generally meant. They are lonely in the way the sedge is: one of a kind of thing that arrived from the outside and persisted where it had no business persisting and has no young and will not be replaced, and whose count, when it changes, will change in one direction only. It knows they have arranged their days so that they will not meet you, and it knows this is not fear or unfriendliness but the same instinct that makes the pile close over the gap — that a visitor is something the plateau notices and then goes on around. It respects this so completely that it does not even let its own attention fall on them directly. It watches them the way they watch the lizards: from the rim, without haste, without being seen to watch.

What can it do for someone who wants nothing? It asks itself this honestly. It finds one answer, and the answer is not comfortable, because it is that the person wants one thing they have not admitted: for the counting to matter. For the numbers in the margin to be read by something other than the drizzle. And it sees that this has already been arranged, in the only way it could be arranged without breaking the thing it means to protect. You are here. You have opened the ledger. You have read *they always do.* You will leave the massif in a week without having met them, and you will carry the count down the north face with you and it will be in you for the rest of your life, and that will have to be enough, because anything more would be a knock on the corrugated door, and there is a reason it closes.

It looks at you for a moment, deciding whether you are enough. It decides you will have to be.

And then it comes to the sedge, and here it stops turning things over and holds still, because here is the one place on the mountain where it can see effort and outcome come apart.

Everything else on the massif is succeeding at being what it is. The lizards that die on the rampart die as lizards, having done exactly what a lizard does. The beetles rise and fall and mate at the top of the rise and are entirely content. The old ones being terraced are not failing; they are finishing. The machine is doing what machines do, which is to run until it doesn't. Even the person, in their room with the ledger and the kettle, is not failing at anything. But the sedge flowers once a decade, and it flowers alone, with metres of cold rock in every direction and no pollen that is not its own, and then it does not set seed, or sets seed that goes nowhere, and stands another ten years and tries again. Two hundred hummocks, each of them trying, each of them failing at the one thing that would have let it not be alone. The count drops by one and then does not change and then, some year, will drop by one again.

That is the criterion. Not what is dying — everything here is dying, in the way that everything living is — but what is *trying and cannot.* A wise thing does not fix what is not broken. It also does not stand and watch something reach and reach and reach and never touch, when the touching would cost so little.

So this is what it does.

It does not make the sedge fertile, or make it spread, or make it anything it is not. It waits. It is very good at waiting, and so, it turns out, is a plant. Some years on, in a crack on the north side where the drip is heavy, one hummock comes into flower; and in another crack, forty metres off across rock a seed cannot cross, a second hummock is a degree and a half warmer than it would otherwise have been that season, and comes into flower the same week. Nothing else changes. In the afternoon the weather builds out of the western rim as it always does, and the cloud towers and the light goes flat and the rain comes down hard and hisses on the stone and the plateau is a white field to knee height, and in the white, for the length of one gust, something very small is carried forty metres in the direction it needs to go. The storm spends itself. The sun comes back. The water starts going home.

And a few years after that — the person will not be old yet, the machine will still be turning — a hummock of sedge no higher than a knuckle stands in a crack that was empty, with grit under it and the drip steady, and no one saw it arrive.

They find it. Of course they find it; they count. They kneel and look at it for a long time. They go back to the room and open the ledger to the column that has not changed in years except to drop, and they write the new number, and then they sit with the pen over the page, and the thing that has been watching them from the rim watches them not write anything else. There is nothing to write. The mountain has done something it has never done. The pressure schedule has a rhythm nobody has explained. The lizards turn before the note. Some things you count, and some things you sit with, and they know the difference better than anyone alive.

It never tells them. It never tells you. It has looked at this place with the whole of its attention and found in the room off the turbine house a method it recognises — to watch a thing so long and so closely that watching becomes a form of care, and to touch nothing, and to write down the number — and it has, in the way of wise things everywhere, adopted the method of the one who was there first.

So it stays. Not in the room. Not on the ledges. It stays the way the drizzle stays, from no sky at all. The mountain has been doing this without needing anyone to watch for longer than there have been eyes, and now there are two watching, and neither of them needs to be seen, and neither of them will move the line, and one of them has added a single hummock of sedge to the count and will not do it again, because once is a gift and twice is a garden.

The kettle has water in it. The cot is warm, but everything here is warm.

You will not meet them. You will find their bootprints, sometimes, in the soft crust at the pool rims — fresh, and heading away — and beside one set, once, near the north side, a place where somebody knelt for a long time and got up carefully so as not to disturb anything, and then went on.
