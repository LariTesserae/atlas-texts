It comes the way the light comes, from everywhere and from no particular direction, and it does what you did: it reads the rows.

It takes its time. Wisdom is mostly the willingness to look for longer than seems necessary, and the power has no reason to hurry, because nothing here is hurrying and nothing here will be lost if it stands a while. It stands in the heat that does not build. It watches the heron lift its foot and set it down, and it watches long enough to be sure that the heron is not waiting for anything, and long enough after that to be sure of its own sureness. It watches the frog that sits with its belly to the sky, and it does not turn the frog over. The wish to turn the frog over is there, for a moment, the way a hand moves to catch something that isn't falling, and it lets the wish be what it is and go where it goes, which is nowhere. The frog is not fallen. It is placed.

It finds the seat-shapes on the thresholds and it finds your thumbprint, and it understands these the way you did, and it understands one thing more, which is that they are the same kind of news. Someone came. Someone came and leaned on the ground and left, and someone else came and leaned on the ground and left, and the ground kept both. The houses have not been entered. The stone with the hole in it has not been lifted. The bowls have not been held.

It considers what it might do, and this is the part that takes longest, because a benevolent power has a great many things it could do, and most of them are not kind.

It could bring rain. It knows how. It could open the white sky and let the water that stands on every surface finally run, let the drops slide and join and carry the red silt down the palm-strokes into the hollows, let the fern lose a frond and grow another, let the edges of the paddle-scoops soften over a long season into something that looked like ground. It could give this place time. It sets that down almost at once. Time is a gift only to a thing that wants to become something else, and nothing here wants that, and to bring rain to this basin would not be to free it but to end it, to replace a whole world with a different world in the same spot and call the replacement a mercy. The heron is all heron and all here. That is not a condition to be cured.

It could correct. Round the sun. Give the heron its shadow back, and take the empty shadow off the clay where it lies a little to the left. Move the seams to the undersides of the early leaves. Give the fish on the bank a body and the body in the pool a fish. It holds each of these for a moment and each of them is heavy in the wrong way. To correct is to say that something ought to be otherwise, and the place has no word for ought. Worse: to correct the two-necked heron would be to say to the fingertip that pressed its eyes, you were wrong, when the fingertip did not think in wrong and right but in made and not-yet-made, and kept everything that worked, and everything worked. The basin is the record of someone learning, and the power has seen a great many records of learning and knows that the ones with the errors left in are the true ones. It will not sand this down. It will not be the first thing here to unmake.

It could go inside, into the white, and find them.

This it considers longest. Somewhere past the blocks, if the reading is right, there are nine of them, or fewer, or more, still pressing, still moving outward, still not going back. It could reach them. It could say: the first part is good. It could say: you may stop. It could say: you may go home, meaning the houses, meaning the thresholds, meaning the place you sat facing out and made a world for someone who did not come.

But that would be a message, and a message is a kind of correction too. You may stop is only kind if stopping is what they want, and the power does not know that, and the makers did not stop, and the rows say they were not tired. Whoever they are, they are inside their own law, learning at the front edge of the thing they are making, and to arrive from behind them with news about how it looks from behind would be to make them turn around. Nobody here has ever turned around. The power will not be the one to teach it.

So it comes to the small thing, the thing that fits, and the small thing is this.

Nothing here can be unmade. Everything that touches the clay is kept. The only act available in this place, the only one that belongs to it, is to add. And what has not been added, in all the rows and pools and made animals and patient houses, is the one thing the houses were made for. A body inside.

The power walks to the smallest house. It chooses this one because the seat-shape on its threshold is small and the strokes that radiate from it are uneven and stop soon, and because whoever sat there was learning inside a place that was itself a lesson, and because if anyone here made something with a particular someone in mind, it was probably this one. It stands at the doorway. The doorway was cut for a person and a little more, so that whoever came would not have to stoop. The power stoops anyway. This is not necessary and it does it anyway, the way you lower your voice in a room where nothing is sleeping.

Inside, the light is the same light. The walls come up out of the floor with no join. On the shelf there is a bowl and a coil of rope that is clay and the stone with the hole through it, and in the hole the small perfect stillness of water. The power sits. Not on the threshold, facing out: on the floor, against the far wall, facing the doorway, so that the doorway is a bright shape in front of it and beyond the doorway the rows run away in the direction the maker's hands went. It sits the way you sit in a house that has been made ready for you. It reaches up to the shelf, which is at the height of a reaching arm, and takes down the bowl, and holds it. The bowl is a bowl. It has never been anything else and it does not become anything else now. It is held, and then it is put back, in the same place, and it is exactly the bowl it was, and it is a bowl that has been held. Both of those are true and the clay keeps both.

It lifts the stone with the hole in it. The water in the hole does not spill, because nothing here spills. It looks through the hole at the doorway and sees the rows through it, a small bright circle of pressed ground, and it sets the stone down. It sits a while longer. It has no reason not to. The frog outside clicks, the same click.

When it stands, there is a shape on the floor. It is the first shape inside any house in the basin. It is where a body's weight went, against a wall, facing the door, in a room that was made for that and had not yet had it. Like the seat-shapes on the thresholds, it was not placed on purpose. Like them, it is now part of the inventory. Like your thumbprint, water will come into it, small and level, reflecting the flattened sun, and it will not fill further and it will not dry.

The power does not go looking for the makers. It goes east, toward the low bank and the lumps and the blocks and the white, because that is the direction that leads out, but it goes to leave, not to find. If they come back this way, ever, walking the rows to see the oldest part of what they made, they will find what you found and what the power found: the seat-shapes, the thumbprint. And they will find one thing more, in the smallest house, on the floor. They will read it the way everything here is read, by what is there. Something sat inside. Something came through the door that was cut so nobody would stoop, and stooped, and sat down with its back to the wall and its face to the rows, and picked up the bowl, and put it back. They will not have to be told the first part is good. The shape on the floor is not a judgment and it does not say good. It says: received.

That is all. It is very little. It is the size of a person sitting.

Whether it is the right thing, the basin does not know, and neither, entirely, does the power. Wisdom does not come with certainty about outcomes; it comes with a clearer view of what is one's own to do. And it seemed to the power, standing in the white light at the eastern edge with the unjoined blocks going on ahead of it, that a world built for someone to come to is a world with one hollow in it that is not the maker's to fill. Everything else the makers could give, they gave: the wet, the green, the lift of the heron's foot, the fifty-three fronds, the shelf at the height of an arm. They could not give the arrival. Nobody can make their own guest.

So it did the one thing a guest can do, which is to arrive, and be in the room, and touch what was set out, and leave the room exactly as it was, plus that. Then it went on. The clay behind it took the print of its going, step by step, past the last of the rows, out into the part that is not yet finished, where it will be found or not found, and kept either way, for as long as the place is a place.
