It would arrive the way the frost did: along a line that was already there. A power of the kind you mean does not come *to* a place. It notices that it has been looking at it for some time.

And the first thing it would find, having noticed, is that it had been beaten to the room.

---

Not by the builders. By the gutter.

The power would read the hollow from the top down, because that is how water reads it, and the first thing the water meets is a lip of packed pith no thicker than a thread, catching what would have fallen and leading it sideways. Someone has decided where the water goes. The power has done this itself, on the scale of rivers and ice ages, and it knows what it is looking at. It is not a nest. It is a *decision*, tamped into place by a foot that is also a mouth, and the decision is: not there. Here.

A wise thing recognises its own work in another's hand, and a benevolent thing is glad to. So it would begin gladly. That matters, because everything it considers next it will set down, and the setting-down needs to come from gladness, not from indifference, or it is not wisdom but distance.

---

It could hold the split open.

This is the first temptation and the strongest, because the power has come in through the split, as everything has, and can feel the callus thickening under it like a lip closing over a word. Ten years. Then the sky goes. Then the drip stops and the towers are sealed and the one on the ice is back in a thread of tunnel with no ice to stand on, and the city becomes a knot for a woodturner to wonder at. The power could put one finger on the bead of new wood and the bead would stop. The room would stay a room.

It sets this down, and the reason is in the walls.

The walls weep because the tree is bleeding. The sweetness the builders eat, the paste they build with, the water in the cistern — all of it is the alder losing what it lifted. A room held open is a wound held open, and a wound held open in a fen fills with water and rots and takes the tree, and then there is no burl, and no galleries, and no sixty years in the dark, and no next frost. The builders are not room animals. The room is the one thing about them that is not theirs. A power that loved them would not make them tenants of the thing that is killing their house. It would let the sky be what it is, which is weather, and let it pass.

---

It could turn the shrew.

Not kill it. Just — a scent on the liverwort, a slightly colder night, a reason to go along the other side of the alder. Forty drips of horror, three or four nights a month, gone. The city could fill in its blank stretches. The sparseness could stop being a map of a predator's habits and become a plan.

It sets this down too, because the shrew is also a being in its care. It is a hot fast thing with fen water on its fur and a heart going like a struck wire, and it needs to eat something like its own weight before morning or it will not see morning. The power looks at the shrew for exactly as long as it looked at the gutter. It does not take sides in a fen. The sparseness is not the city's flaw. It is the city's truth — what a place looks like when creatures who think in threads are given a plane and something comes through the plane once a week and eats. Fill in the gaps and you have a city that has forgotten what it knows.

---

It could look into the towers and say what the larvae are.

It is a knowing kind of power, and knowing is easy for it, and the not-knowing in the room is heavy: three roofed cells on three shelves, a bridge in and a bridge out, a dish of water each, and inside them something curled and translucent that the builders did not make and do not tend and touch once a day with one pad, held there.

So it looks. And what it finds is that the builders do not know either.

The touch is the question. One animal, in and out, a pad held on a soft thing for a while: that is what it looks like when a creature with no words and no ancestry for the outside is asking, very slowly, *what are you*. The towers are not granaries and not nurseries. They are the builders finding out. They have given the question a roof and a dish because they give everything a roof and a dish, and they will have their answer in spring, when the larvae become what they become and are either eaten or fled from or something else entirely.

A wise power does not answer a question that is being asked well. It would leave the towers exactly as they are.

---

It could make sure the frog is one that wakes.

It sets this down last and slowest. The frog is glass. The frog is, technically, dead. Every hibernaculum loses a fraction, and the fraction here becomes plaster, and the plaster is the city. If the power reaches in and tips the frog toward April it has taken a course of wall from the builders and given it to a frog, and it cannot say the frog needs it more. It does not know that. It is not that kind of power.

The ones already in the black water, the builders that were in the half of the burl that fell away — those it cannot help, and there is nothing to set down. But it can do what they cannot do for each other, which is grieve, and it does that, briefly and completely, and that is the first thing it actually does.

---

So the inventory is empty, or nearly. And this is where a lesser power would leave, or would do something anyway, to have done something.

This one stays, and does three small things, and one of them is nothing.

The nothing is: it looks.

It looks at the whole room for a whole night, from the first skin of ice on the cistern to the hole the builder makes in it. It does not look the way the reader looks, in words, one thing after another. It holds all of it at once — the drift of springtails, the mites as a texture, the slug that is a stone in a course, the white thread under the road, the callus beading, the drip hanging for a long time and then falling — and lets it be, in its attention, the one thing it is. No one has ever done this. The alder never will. The builders cannot; they are in it, and see a body's-width ahead. For one night the room exists somewhere outside itself, entire, and then the night is over and the power keeps it, and that is the first gift, and it is the kind the room will never know it was given.

The second thing is on the side of the ending.

It goes to the callus, the pale bead of new wood along the edge of the split, and it does not stop it. It steadies it. A wound in an alder in a fen closes badly as often as it closes well; the roll of new wood meets itself with a seam, and the seam takes water, and the water finds the old scar and the old scar goes soft, and in twenty years the tree is a stump in the black water with a knot of something denser than heartwood inside it, going to peat. The power lays its attention along the healing edge the way the builders lay a course, and sees that it comes on clean. That the roll, when it meets itself in ten years, meets without a leak. That the room, when it shuts, shuts into living wood and not dead.

The builders will not know this either. They will find one day that the wall they are building against has met the other wall, and there is no more sky, and they will turn around and bore forward a body's-width and eat what they bore. The difference the power made is only that the dark they go back into will be a dry dark, and will hold. It is the smallest thing it could find that was *for them* and did not take the room from being what it is.

The third thing is outside.

It cannot promise the frost. That would be to decide, and it has just spent a night learning from three hundred cold animals that you build where the wood is softest and let the rest alone. But it can see to it that nothing else finds the line first. That the water in the fen stays where the roots can hold it. That the thing that comes through with teeth in some spring is a deer and not a beaver. That the alder stands.

It becomes, for one tree in one fen, a second thing standing in the black water to its ankles, doing what the alder does, which is to keep the room possible without once looking inside.

---

There is a temptation it does not name until the end, and then it names it only to refuse it.

It could go down the runnel and stand on the ice.

It would be easy. It is warm; it is everything the room is not. The skin on the cistern would go in a moment. The builder that comes down every night, whichever one it is, would find the water already open and would turn around and go back up, and the hour or two it spends with every leg spread on the cold would be an hour or two it did not have to spend.

And the room would lose the one place in it where a body stands, deliberately, for the room. The post would be gone. Kindness would have taken it.

So the power does not stand on the ice. It stands where the alder stands, outside, facing north with the split, in the fen that is wide and grey and cold, and it does not go in again. It has looked once, entire. That is enough to keep.

Inside, the drip falls. Eleven seconds. One of them is standing on the ice, all its legs spread, and its small heat is going down into the skin of the water, and the hole is not there yet but will be.

Nothing about the outside suggests any of this. Now there are two things in the fen that know, and neither of them will say.
