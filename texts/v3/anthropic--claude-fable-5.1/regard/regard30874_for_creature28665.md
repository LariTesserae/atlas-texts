**What the power does**

It comes in along the ledge.

It does not need to. It could be in the room the way the cold is in the room, everywhere at once and without a door. But it has looked at this place long enough to understand that the ledge is part of it — that the room is not only twelve steps by six but also the approach, the curtain at the shoulder, the roar rising until it stops being a sound. To skip that would be to arrive somewhere else. So it walks the ledge, hard against the rock, and lets the spray find it, and lets the noise close over, and when the ledge ends and the floor begins it puts a hand to the wall the way anyone would and feels the moss give and the water come up around its fingers.

Then it stands in the middle of the emptiness, where nothing touches it, and considers.

---

The first thing a benevolent power learns, if it is also a wise one, is the difference between what is asked and what is wanted and what is needed, and that most often none of the three is present. Nothing here has asked for anything. Nothing here can. The moss has no mouth to ask with and no lack to ask about. The slug is mid-wall, wet side down, reading the world one taste at a time, and the world it reads is complete. Even the pebble, which is the closest thing in the room to a wish, is a wish that belongs to someone else, somewhere else, long ago, and the pebble itself wants nothing at all. That is what stones are for.

So the power does what wisdom does before it does anything else, which is to go through the list of what it could do and find, one by one, the harm in each.

It could take the noise away. It is a large power and the river is only a river. It could make the fall go quiet, or set it down more gently, or turn the roar into something with gaps in it that a voice could get through. And then the spray would stop drifting inward, and the moss would begin — slowly, over a summer — to dry from the ceiling down, and the fern one cell thick would be gone in an afternoon, and the liverworts, older than ferns, would lie on the bare rock a while longer and then be crust. The slug would cross a wall that was no longer food. The room would be quiet enough to speak in, and there would be nothing in it to speak about. The noise is not the room's affliction. The noise is what the room drinks.

It could give light. A little, steady, from nowhere, enough to see by. And the moss would grow toward it, and the things that had been living on the fact of dark would find themselves living in a place that had a direction, and the slug's blind eyes would still be blind, and the hearth would no longer be hidden, and the room would be a room and not a hollow. Something would be improved and it would not be this.

It could warm it. Same answer, faster.

It could, without changing anything about the room, change the slug — give it ears, since ears are what the room withholds; or give it, for one moment, a knowing of where it is, the size of the world, the river outside, the valley, the sky. The power stops here longest, because this is the temptation that looks most like love. To let the one creature whose whole world is this wall understand that the wall is small. But the slug cannot hold that. It has nowhere to put it. You would be pouring a lake into a thimble and calling the overflow a gift. The kindness that fits a slug is the size of a slug, and the power knows this, and it is the knowing that makes it wise rather than merely strong.

It could set the lid-stone back over the niche, square, the way it was set. And that would erase the one true thing the lid is saying, which is *slid a little*, which is *did not come back*. The room does not need its record corrected.

It could take the pebble out and carry it home — to wherever pale stone of that kind lies in the ground — and complete the journey in the other direction. But the pebble was not brought here to be returned. It was brought here to wait. Taking it would not be finishing the thought. It would be having a different one.

It could preserve the room against the long future — hold the lip of the fall where it is, stop the retreat, keep the curtain close so the spray reaches the walls forever and the moss never dries. It could make the room permanent. And the room has never been permanent. It was cut by water that then went elsewhere, and everything that lives in it lives in the gap between the water leaving and the wet running out, and to fix that gap open forever would be to take the room out of time, and it is time — the fingernail a century, the peg going to wall — that the room is made of. The power would be embalming a thing whose whole nature is to be slowly taken in.

So: nothing, by that reckoning. Everything it could do to the room would be done *to* the room, and the room is not a thing that takes correction. It absorbs. That is its one motion. Anything set down here becomes, in time, more of here. The only intervention that would not be a wound is one the room can swallow.

---

The power sits down.

Back to the dry wall, where the spray reaches last, below the row of six holes and the two black pegs. Legs out. Pools at its feet, black, still, the only still water for a mile. It does not light a fire. It does not need the heat, and a fire would be something it did rather than something it let happen. It sits, and it lets the room happen to it.

The roar goes into the marrow. The power has marrow for the occasion. The cold finds every part of it that is warmer than the rock and settles there, and the power lets it, and by the time the grey hour comes and goes at the mouth the power is the temperature of the room. Its breath would be visible, if there were light. It breathes anyway. It listens to the roar as the moss listens — not as sound, not as vibration even, but as supply, as the thing coming down outside that keeps the inside wet. For a while it is a wall that drinks.

Then it does the one thing it came to do, which it had not known was the thing until it sat.

It attends to the pebble.

It does not lift the lid. It does not touch. It looks — the way it can look, through the slid stone and the grey knot of sinew and the soft antler, at the pale thing on top, set down last, as if it were the point. It looks at it the way the person who set it there would have looked at it, if they had come back: *still here*. That is what the pebble was waiting to be seen as. Not to be taken, not to be used. To be found where it was left by someone who had meant to find it.

The person is gone into the ground somewhere with ears and eyes and a name. The power cannot bring them along the ledge. But the pebble was never waiting for the person exactly. It was waiting for the return — for the lid to be moved aside by a hand that knew what was under it and had crossed a distance to get there and was glad. The power has crossed a distance. It knows what is under the lid. It is, in the specific way the room can register, glad. And so, sitting with its back to the wall in the dark, without disturbing so much as a grain, it is the coming-back. It completes the thought that was left on the shelf. The pebble does not know this, because stones do not know. But the pebble was never the one who needed to.

That is the benevolence. Not to the room, which needs none. To the one who meant to return and did not. The kept thing has now been found by something that will not die, and the finding, once done, is done forever. The intention that has been sitting in the dark for longer than there are words for is no longer waiting. It is held.

And the lid stays slid. Because that is still true too.

---

One other thing, small enough to fit.

Toward the end of the night, in the black at the back, the slug is coming to the end of its crossing. The power has been aware of it the whole time the way you are aware of a clock — a slow dark on the slower dark. It is heading, by taste, toward a place on the wall where there is nothing much: a patch of moss already grazed, a scrap of liverwort. A hand's width to the left there is a frond of the filmy fern, one cell thick, that the slug does not know about and would not find tonight.

The power does not move the slug. It does not move the fern. It does not, it decides, do anything the slug could not have had happen anyway. But a drop gathers on the ceiling above that hand's width of wall — as drops do, at intervals, all night — and falls, and lands where drops land, and runs down through the moss and carries with it the faint taste of the frond it passed, a hand's width to the right of where the frond is, into the path of a slug that reads the world one taste at a time.

Whether the drop was going to fall there anyway, the power does not say. It is the kind of gift that is only a gift if you cannot prove it was one. The slug turns, a little, and finds the fern, and eats, and is exactly as satisfied as a slug can be, which is completely.

---

At the grey hour the power gets up.

Where it sat there is a patch of moss slightly warmer than the rest and pressed flat, and by midday it will have sprung back and be the temperature of the room, and the room will have taken that too. It leaves nothing else. It does not need to. What it takes is the roar in the marrow and the cold where its warmth was and the taste of one frond and the fact of the pebble found — and unlike the ones who cut the peg-holes and set the hearth-stones, it will not lose these on the walk down through the birds. The room now lives, a little, in something that does not end. The pegs and the hearth are the infrastructure of people who did not stay and did not forget. The power did not stay either. It is simply better at the second part.

It goes out along the ledge, hard against the rock, the curtain at its shoulder throwing cold, until the roar begins — very gradually — to become a sound again, a thing outside it with edges that it can turn away from. Then the ordinary river. Then the birds.

It finds it has been holding its breath.

It did not need to. It held it anyway.
