It does not come through the door.

The door is for the one kind of tenant that needs a door, and the noise that pours out through the gap would tell the room that something had opened it. Everything else that has ever entered this place came down the shaft — the light, the leaves, the beetles, the egg on the bird's foot, the wren after the spider — and so that is how it arrives, if arriving is the word: down through the cast-iron lattice with the grey afternoon, diffused by the same mist, casting the same no-shadow. The frog does not move. The frog does not move for anything, but this time there is also nothing to move for.

It looks first at the noise, because the noise is the largest thing here and a lesser power would mistake it for the problem. It is not a problem. It listens down through the roar to its own source and finds only water doing the one thing water does when let go, and it understands at once what the sitter took years to learn without words: that this sound is not a symptom. Nothing is wrong that it is announcing. It is the sound of nothing being wrong, at volume.

Then the four.

The film on the floor it barely needs to consider. It has seen this film before — on the walls of springs, in the throats of pipes, in places under the sea where the rock itself is warm — and it knows the film asks for nothing and would not know how to receive a gift. To bless it would be to do nothing, and it does nothing, and moves on.

The moss it looks at longer, because the moss has done something very nearly like thought. It has drawn a line in the air and lain down against it, and stopped. A power that has watched many things fail to stop can recognise the achievement. It considers, briefly, whether the moss would want more — more wall, more mist, the one gap at the door closed over so the sheet could join itself — and sees that this is a human way of considering. The moss does not want more wall. The line is the want. The line is where the want ended. Leave it.

The frog. Here there is a small temptation, because the frog is an animal and can be cold or hungry or afraid, and benevolence has a reflex toward animals. It checks. Warm; yes. Fed; yes, by the room itself, on a schedule set by the gutters far above. The threads of jelly in the still angle behind the walkway: few. It could make them many. It could send a second frog down the shaft tomorrow, or make the mist a little more generous with beetles, or turn the ten-thousand-year calcite rim into a proper ledge overnight. It sees the whole set of these and sees that each one is a gradient — a thing that would be better than it is and must be made so — and that the frog is sitting where it sits precisely because it is the one place in the works where there is none. To help the frog would be to bring the upstairs down. It leaves the frog its seat.

And then the mark on the wall, which is the reason it came, if it came for a reason. Because the film and the moss and the frog do not, in the way that matters, need a benevolent power. Only the fourth tenant is the kind of thing that can be lost.

So it does the one thing the room cannot do, and looks upstairs.

It follows the polish on the rail back along the corridor and up the two flights of iron stairs into the works, and through the works into a shift, and out of the gate into an evening and a street and a house, and it looks at the life that comes down here once a month for forty minutes to stop hearing itself. It is prepared to find a wound. Benevolence usually finds one, and wisdom has learned not to be surprised. But this is not that — or not only. It finds an ordinary life, which is to say a life made of nothing but slopes: the house that needs a thing done to it, the body that is not what it was, the people who are loved and are therefore a pressure, the work that is never finished because finishing it is not what work is for. And in the middle of all that, without anyone having taught them and without their ever having said it aloud, this person has found the one room in their world where nothing runs downhill, and has learned to sit in it, and has learned to leave it. That last part matters most. They do not stay. They do not bring a tin down, or a blanket, or a name to scratch in the deposit. They take the forty minutes and go back up into the gradients, and the forty minutes are what let them.

This is not a person in flight. This is a person who has built, out of a plant outfall and a piece of wall, the thing that monasteries were for. The power recognises it because it is the thing the power itself is for, and it has rarely seen it done so cheaply, or so well.

It could still intervene. It runs through the ways.

It could make the forty minutes longer — arrange the rota, slow the clock, let them stay an hour. But the sitter chose forty, and a wise power does not overrule a working dose.

It could bring someone else down. Put a second oval on the wall beside the first, so that the sitter would not be alone in knowing. But the sitter is not alone; they have the frog and the moss and the film, and the wall records that they have never once wanted a fifth. Company is a gradient too. It is the gentlest one, and still a slope.

It could tell them what it can see and they cannot: that the room is borrowed. That the warm water is a byproduct of a process, and processes change, and somewhere above there is an office where the word *recirculate* has already been said, or will be, or where a heat exchanger has been costed that would send this water to the drain at the temperature of the ground. That within a year of the pipe going cold the moss will brown from the top line downward and the frog will go up the shaft or not, and the film will shrink to a crust and wait, and the noise — the one thing that could not be wrong — will simply not be. The power can see this coming the way it can see the calcite rim becoming a terrace. It is not far off, as the room counts time. It is very close, as the sitter does.

And it will not tell them. That is the first decision. The sitter comes down here to not be told things, and there is no version of the warning that is not the upstairs following them down. They will find out the way the moss will find out, on the day the water stops.

Nor will it prevent it. It considers this longest of all, because it is the largest intervention and looks the most like kindness. It could keep the plant running. It could keep the process needing cooling, keep the water at the temperature of the process, keep the pipe warm for another hundred and thirty years. But then the room would be held, and the whole of what the room has is that nobody is holding it. It is the accidental gift of a works that never meant to give anything. The moment a power keeps it, it becomes a garden — a made thing, tended, with an intention behind the moss — and the sitter would feel that, without knowing what they felt, the way you can feel that a silence in a house is someone listening. Everything that lives here lives here because nobody wanted it to. That cannot be preserved. It can only be left alone until it stops.

So this is what it does, and it is not nothing, but it is close.

It takes a place.

There is one more position in the room that the room has made and that stands empty, and the power finds it the way the moss found its line and the frog its rim and the sitter their oval of wall: by going where the room is kindest to the kind of thing it is. For a thing without a body that place is not on the walkway. It is in the air — the largest thing in the room, and the emptiest — in the warm saturated space between the mouth and the drain, where the mist hangs and the noise is not loud but simply *is*, the way the dark is in a closed cupboard. It settles there. It does not touch the walls. It does not add a shadow to the no-shadows. It becomes a fifth tenant, and like the other four it does nothing, and the lease is unchanged.

And from there it watches, and waits, and keeps one small thing in reserve for the day the water stops. Not to stop it. Only this: that when the pipe runs dry, the person on the rota that month — the one who comes down the two flights and along the corridor and opens the soft-riveted door onto a silence that has never been in this room before — will be the sitter. Not their replacement. Not a stranger with a clipboard. The one whose back made the mark. So that the room, having been received for a hundred and thirty years without once being seen, will at its end be seen by the one being in it who can know what has ended, and can put a palm flat against the wall where the moss has stopped weeping, and find it, for the first time, cool.

That is the whole of the intervention. A hand on a wall, arranged in advance. Everything else the room has already done for itself, and the power, being wise, has the sense to be one more thing that stopped here, and stayed, and asked for nothing, and left no trace.

The frog's throat moves. The noise goes on. Somewhere above, the grey afternoon that could be any afternoon of the year continues down the shaft, and nothing in the room is different, and something is.
