**What the Power Did**

It came the way anything comes to the shelf: across the bare stone, under the crowded stars, into the wind that has never carried rain. It could have come any other way. It chose this one, because it wanted to arrive as the room's visitors had always arrived, from outside, from cold, and to feel the breath on its face before it knew anything else.

It stood at the mouth for a while. It read the scratches on the left-hand slab and understood them at once — pulses, not days — and did not touch them.

Then it went in, and the light went, and it did what it had come to do, which was to look.

---

It looked for a long time. Longer than any waterskin. It stood at the room's center and let the grazers come up over its feet and settle sealed on its ankles through the cool phase, and lift and walk on when the warmth returned, and it did not move, because it did not need to, and because moving would have been a kind of speaking, and it had not decided yet whether it had anything to say.

It saw the room as the room was. Not as a stage with players on it. Not as a body with organs. As what it is: a single warm surface that has divided itself in order to keep its water, and has no place in it that knows this. It saw the film, and the film's mouths walking on it, and the liars on their legs, and the two old lids the size of hearthstones, and the band of velvet at the mouth of the passage that nothing crosses. It saw the dead going down into the skin and the skin coming up into the dead. It counted, without meaning to, and found the count did not matter, because nothing in the room was counting.

And it began, as any visitor would, to think about what it might give.

---

Water was first. It is always first, for anything that comes from outside, because outside water is life and the reflex is old. The power held the thought for exactly as long as it took to see the room's whole architecture assembled against it — every sealed rim, every closed spiracle, every death that yielded moisture only at the end — and let it go. A bowl of water on that floor would be a wound. The film beneath it would die, the grazers over it would open to it and drown in what they could not hold, the patch would rot instead of dry, and the room would have, for the first time, a place in it that was not the room. That is what kindness from outside looks like, brought in unconsidered: a bright wet ruin the size of two cupped hands.

Light was next, and went faster. Nothing here has an eye. Light would fall on the mass and be no more to it than a color is to stone.

It thought about the deep. It could feel, as the room could not, the slow arithmetic of the mountain's breath — how long the warmth had been coming up, how long it would continue. A long time still. Not forever. And it could, if it chose, lay a hand on that arithmetic and lengthen it. Keep the floor warm past the mountain's own patience. Make the room a kept thing.

It set this aside too, and more slowly, because this one cost something to refuse. The room is set to the breath. Every body in it is a clock wound to a pulse that comes from below, and none of them knows where. A room whose warmth came from a hand instead of a mountain would not know the difference — and that, precisely, was the reason. The power would be the only one who knew. It would have made the room into a gift and itself into the giver, and there would be no one on the receiving end to receive it, only a surface, going on. When the deep cools, the room will slow, and seal, and not open again, and nothing in it will have lost anything it knew it had. That is not a thing that needs preventing. That is a thing that happens to rooms.

---

Then it thought the thought that only a wise thing thinks, which is the dangerous one.

It could give the room a center. It could make the pieces know they were one. It had, after all, seen it — the whole of it, at once, in a way no one who had ever sat on the cool slab could see. It could give that seeing back. Let the film know it was being eaten. Let the grazers feel their neighbors as neighbors and not as wall. Let the old lids understand what they were eating. Let the room wake up and be a being.

It held this one longest, because it was the one most like what it was itself, and the pull toward making things in one's own image is the last pull to go.

And then it saw what the room would do with a center. A center must be kept. It must be fed and defended and, when the pulse ebbs, it must wait in the dark knowing the pulse might not return. A room that knew it was one would know it could stop being one. It would have, for the first time, an organ for fear. It would feel the click from below as a question. It would feel the band of velvet at the passage mouth as a border. It would feel the mountain's arithmetic and count down. Every peace the room has, it has because there is nowhere in it that could lose that peace. To give it a self would be to give it a self to lose.

The power let the thought go, and felt the room's whisper continue exactly as before, and was, for a moment, something close to grateful that it had been refused.

---

There was the click. It listened for it through many cool phases, and heard it, and did not go down.

It could have. It could have crossed the band and walked the bare hot stone and found what set a claw or a stone down in the dark below and came back up to say. But it had understood something about the click that the one who wrote of it had understood too: the click is not in the room. It comes to the room from somewhere else, and the room does not hear it, and a wise visitor does not drag a second story into a first one just because it can. Whatever is below has its own long wait. If it is waiting for something, it is not waiting for this.

So the power left the click where it was. It allowed itself only one thought about it, and kept the thought: that a single dry sound in the deep ebb, made now and then and never regularly, is very like the sound of a sharp stone against a slab, made by a hand that wants to mark a pulse and has no one to show the mark to.

---

And that was where it found, at last, the one place benevolence had any purchase.

Not the room. The room needs nothing and can be given nothing without harm. The room is finished, in the way a river is finished. But there had been people here. Not many, not recently. They had sat on the slab for the length of a waterskin and felt the floor grow hot and cool and made a mark each time it cooled. One of them, or several across a span the wind had had leisure to measure, had stood at the room's center with eyes open in the dark until the seeing-that-is-not-seeing arrived, and had watched the lights drift and gather and go out, and had reached up through the grazers and cut what they saw into the stone one hand's length at a time so that the stone would remember.

The power reached up. It lifted the grazers away — they came easily, unbothered — and found the grooves, and followed them with a finger through the whole long curve, meeting and parting.

They were right. That was the thing. Whoever had cut them had watched with such patience that the paths in the stone were the paths the hunters truly took — not an idea of them, not a pattern imposed, but the actual drift of the false warmths across the cool-phase dome, recorded by someone who would never be able to say so to anyone and did it anyway. The power, which had seen the whole room at once and effortlessly, stood under a record made by someone who had seen it one hand's length at a time and correctly, and understood that this was the only thing in the room that had been made by wanting, and that the want had been met. The stone remembered.

There was nothing to do for the cutter. They were gone out into the cold long ago with velvet on their hands, and that had worn away, and so, by now, had they. But a want like that is not finished when the wanter is. It is finished when someone else stands where they stood and sees that they were right.

So the power stood there, and saw it, and let the grazers close back over the dome.

---

It went out the way it had come. The heat fell away from it like a coat. At the second bend the stars came back, and at the mouth it stopped beside the left-hand slab, and with something sharp — it had a sharp thing; anything that walks the plateau finds one — it cut a single short scratch below the last of the others. One pulse. It had felt many more than one, but the scratches were not a ledger of the room's pulses; they were a ledger of people who had sat in the dark and wanted to make a mark, and one mark was the honest count of that.

Then it looked at its palm.

The velvet was there, as it is on every palm that leaves the room. It was already, in the cold, doing the only thing it knows: growing toward the nearest warmth, which was the hand it was on. On a human hand it lives two days and dies of the plateau. The power's hand did not have to be cold. It could have carried the velvet a long way, warm, and let it live out on the shelf under the stars for as long as it liked.

It thought about this, and found that it was the water thought again in another shape — a piece of the room kept alive outside the room, for the comfort of the one keeping it.

So it turned and went back down the seam, thirty paces, past the second bend to where the film thins toward the cold, and laid its palm flat against the warm wall, and held it there through the whole of one hot phase, until the velvet on its skin and the velvet on the stone had grown into each other and could not be told apart. Then it drew its hand away clean, and went out, and did not look at the palm again.

Behind it the crack in the cliff breathed into the night. On the slab there was one new scratch that would take the wind a very long time to soften. Inside, nothing had changed, and nothing knew that anything had come, and that was the whole of the gift.
