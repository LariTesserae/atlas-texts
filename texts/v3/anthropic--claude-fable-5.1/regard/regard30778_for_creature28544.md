It comes to it from above, which is the only way anyone has ever come to it.

It does not come down the stair. It does not need to. A power of that kind arrives the way the light arrives — through the oculus, falling, stopping dead on the floor — and then does what the light does, which is to be redistributed. Off the wet stone. Through leaf after leaf after leaf. Until it is not in the room so much as dispersed into the room's dimness, one more shade of it, and the snails do not draw in and the frogs do not close their eyes, because nothing has entered that makes a sound.

The first thing it does is slow down.

This is not a courtesy. It is the only way to see the place. A power that arrives at the pace of the plain above — the glare-pace, the pace of ground that rings when kicked — would see a wet hole full of snails and a person sitting in a puddle, and it would either leave or fix something, and both would be mistakes. So it slows. It slows past the millipede's ripple and past the snail's advance and past the drop swelling on the fern-tip, until it is moving at the pace of the fungus on the far wall, which is to say the pace at which growth becomes visible, and at that pace the room opens for it the way it opened for you.

It sees the fig that became a colonnade because it could not become a tree. It sees the wall that marks where the breath reaches. It sees the seat that is not a basin. It sees the figure in the niche, and the skin of calcite, and the ferns leaning.

It sees you, on the rim, with your ankles in the warm water.

And it waits for the room to breathe, and the room breathes, and the fog forms in the air between the roots, and the power lets the fog form on it too, and hears it tick.

---

Then come the temptations, and a wise power knows them as temptations and turns each one over before setting it down.

The first is to lift you out. To take you by the shoulders — gently; it would be gentle — and carry you up the fig-stair into the violet circle of stars and set you down on the laterite and let the night wind dry you. Because the polish on the rim has told the power what it told you. Because the shape in the niche was a sitter once. Because the room has never asked anyone to stay and has never, as far as the record shows, let anyone leave, and there is a word for a shape that is kept warm and fitted to a body and never given the chance to be anything else, and the word is not *sanctuary*.

But you have not asked. And the power looks at the calcite figure, made of what a carving and a drip agreed on over centuries, and cannot honestly call it a victim. It is the brightest thing in the room after the light. The snails will not eat it. The ferns lean. Whatever happened to the one who sat here before you was not simply subtraction. Something was made. The power does not know what, and it is wise enough to know it does not know, and it sets the first temptation down.

The second is to stop the drip. To reach up into the ceiling and turn aside the one bead that falls on the figure's bowed head, so that the shape stays a shape, so that shoulders stay shoulders, so that in a thousand years there is still a lap and not a smooth white spire. To *preserve* it.

But the room is a record of things being replaced rather than destroyed — the stair by the tree, the wall by the moss, the figure by the water — and to freeze one item in that record would be to make it the only dead thing in a room where even the stone is dissolving. The power sets that down too.

The third is to speak. To tell you what it sees. To warn you, or bless you, or simply say *I am here*.

But that would be a sound, and there are no sounds here that are not necessary. And it is not yet sure what is necessary.

---

So it sits with the room longer, and it counts.

Someone in this place has to. You understood that. The predation here is invisible unless there is an eye keeping the tally, and the sitter on the rim is the room's one act of noticing itself. The frog was in the cup before the drop fell and is not in it after, and you saw that, and so it happened.

But who sees the sitter?

The power turns this over and finds it is the true shape of the thing. The room is dense with lives that are the room, and above them all sits one life that is not the room, that could be somewhere else, that has chosen — or drifted, or been kept — into stillness, and is being taken slowly, drip by drip, the way everything here is taken. And that subtraction — the slowest one, the one that takes a lifetime and then a few centuries more — is the only subtraction in the whole chamber that no one witnesses. The snails do not count you. The bats know the ceiling. The body under the floor does not know the room exists.

The counter is uncounted.

This is the gap. This is the one thing the room cannot do for itself. And a benevolent power, having found the one thing a place cannot do for itself, does that and does not do more.

---

It witnesses.

It learns how many have sat on that rim. It learns this the way the room learns things — from the wear, from the depth of the polish, from the thickness of the calcite over the bowed head, from the rings of the fig where the fig grew around treads and where it grew around nothing. The number does not matter to write down. What matters is that the number is now *known*, held somewhere outside the room, in a mind that will not be dripped on. Each one who sat. How long. Whether they came down the stair afraid or curious or already half-still. Whether, at the end, the moment of sitting and the moment of staying were far apart or close together. The power holds all of this, and holds the shape in the niche as it was before the water, and will hold the shape you are now, on the rim, with your heels finding the bottom of the trough — holds it whether or not the water ever redresses it.

Nothing about this changes the room. A snail could not tell the difference. But it is the difference between a thing that happens and a thing that is seen to happen, and the room has been full of the first and empty of the second for as long as the stair has existed.

---

And then it does two small things, because witness alone is not quite benevolence.

The first concerns the stair.

The fig is eating it. This is not malice; it is what figs do to what they touch. But the way in is the way out, and if the last treads go, the room will still have a floor and a ceiling and a breath, but it will no longer have a door, and a shape that cannot be left is not a seat but a mouth. The power does not repair the stair. It talks to the fig — over years, the way you would have to talk to a fig — and persuades it that the shape of a stair is a shape worth keeping. That where it has replaced a tread it should keep replacing it, and where it has pried one apart it should let the wood thicken into a step. The fig is agreeable. It was already halfway to this on its own. In a century the stair will be entirely tree and entirely climbable, warm underfoot, going both directions.

The second concerns the above.

A snail does not know there is an above. That is what makes the snail a wall. And the danger of the seat — the power sees this clearly now — is not the water or the drip or the calcite. It is that the sitter, slowing and slowing, might one day stop knowing there is a plain up there ringing like a bell in the sun. Might stop being *the kind of thing that could be somewhere else*. That is the moment the sitter stops living in the room and becomes the room, and it happens silently, and it cannot be undone from inside.

So the power does one thing to the air.

Each dawn, when the bats come spiraling back down the shaft with the night on their fur, they bring the outside with them — a little of it, thorn and dust and cold — and it is lost at once in the sweet warm sulfur of the chamber. The power arranges for a little more. Not much. A breath. A thread of dry air that comes down with the bats and reaches the rim of the basin before the room swallows it, so that once a day, for the length of one inhalation, you smell laterite. You smell the scrub. You remember that the light coming through the oculus is coming *from* somewhere.

It is a question, asked the only way a question can be asked here: once, and softly, and then nothing. The way the frog clicks. *There is an above. Do you know?*

You do not have to answer. You do not have to leave. You may sit until the water takes you, and if you do, that will have been a choice made every morning and not a forgetting, and the power will count it so. But the question will come with the bats, every dawn, for as long as there are bats, and you will never be a snail.

---

That is all it does.

It does not make the room a shrine or a warning. It does not send anyone. It does not keep anyone away — though it may, over the years, find that the surveyors' roads across the plain bend a mile to the east for no reason anyone can name, and that the collar of ferns around the hole stays green for whoever happens to be walking toward it on purpose.

It does not touch the figure in the niche.

And it does not leave, exactly, because a power of that kind does not have to be in a place to be attending to it. But it lets itself thin back out through the leaves and up the shaft of light the way it came, until it is a shade of the dimness again, and then not even that.

Below, the great body turns in its sleep, and the fog comes on in the dark, and settles on your face, and begins to tick. Above, far above, the first bats are turning for home with the smell of the plain in their fur.

In the basin a frog clicks, once. Another answers, once.

And this time something hears it.
