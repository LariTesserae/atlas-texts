It comes to the pass, because everything does.

It could arrive anywhere — it is not the kind of thing that needs a road — but it chooses the pass, and it chooses to stand there with the last ordinary stone under one foot and the mat under the other, because the boundary is the first thing worth understanding, and because it is polite. You do not walk into a house through the wall.

And then, for a long time, it does nothing.

—

This is the first act of wisdom and it costs the most. Benevolence wants to move. It sees the hare's tracks stop at the lip of a sag and it wants to reach down. It sees the men with the cores lie down in the warm at dusk and it wants to shout — though shouting here goes out flat and small and does not return. It sees a thing forty meters deep and twenty kilometers long working without cease at something no one can name and it wants, very badly, to ask.

It does not do any of these things. It has learned, in other places, that a valley with nested rhythms cannot be understood at the pace of the fastest one. So it stays for the pulse of the trunk-lines and the noon shedding of frost and the week-long walk of a ridge to the wall. It stays for the line on the rock to climb its meter and fall back. It stays through several hares — there is never more than one, it confirms this early; the tracks are a single animal's, laid down at a speed that makes them look like many — and it watches each one cross the rime from crevice to cup to split cushion and back, and never stop, and finally stop. It stays for the roof over the deep lake to fall and the water to lie steel-gray and empty under the sky and the roof to grow again and the turning to resume in the dark. It stays long enough to learn that the terraces high on the walls are not one retreat but several, with long pauses between, and that the pauses are longer than the retreats.

It learns to whisper. Everything does.

—

The mat, it comes to understand, is not in trouble.

This is harder to accept than it should be. The mat is late in its size and may be dying, but it is dying at a rate the mountains will not outlast, and it does not know it is dying, and it would not know how to mind. It takes the dead without malice because it has no organ for malice, and it takes the living the same way, and it holds its temperature to a degree, and it is — the power turns this word over for a long while before accepting it — well. It is a thing that has solved, completely, the problem of being without wanting. It attends to heat and pressure and everything else is material. There is no loneliness in it because loneliness requires a face to turn toward the empty place, and it has no face. There is no boredom because boredom requires a self that could be elsewhere.

The power has not solved this problem. It has a face; it is turning it toward the valley now. It notices this about itself and does not pretend otherwise. Part of what a wise power does, standing at the pass with one foot on each side, is decline to be improved by the mat's example. It keeps its wanting. It is here because it wants something for this place, and it will not be shamed out of that by a thing that cannot want back.

But it does — this is important — recognize a fellow. Something that works without ceasing and holds still by working. Something old enough that its churning is its rest. The power has been called many things, and among the true ones is *a standing wave*. It knows what it is looking at.

—

It looks at the product.

It can. It is the kind of thing that can follow the glitter down through the trunk-lines and past the furnace crew and into the black fibrous dark where the peat takes an edge from a knife, and lower, to where the mat stops being plant and starts being weight. It looks at what is there. It is very heavy. It has been accumulating for tens of thousands of years.

And what the power finds is not a plan. It is not a seed for the next great filling of the valley, not a store the mat is waiting to have enough of, not a forgotten purpose. It is ballast. The mat churns from wall to wall without ceasing and the churning would carry it — up the walls, over the pass, into the ordinary world where it would spread thin and die of the cold — if it did not put its weight somewhere. So it precipitates itself out. Everything it cannot use, made dense, made cold, routed down. The heaviness at the bottom of the valley is the mat's own patience, turned mineral, so that the surface can go on moving without going anywhere. It weights itself in order to stay.

The power considers whether this is sad. It decides that it is not. A thing that has forgotten what it makes would be sad, if the thing could remember. A thing that makes stillness out of motion and stores it under itself is only a thing that has found its shape. There is nothing to tell it. There is nothing to tell it with.

The power leaves the product where it is.

—

Then, having watched long enough to earn it, it does four things, and they are all small.

The first is for the people.

Not the ones who come with cores — they are fine; they whisper and they leave — but the ones who lie down. Who stop too long in the warm at dusk with the fiber shifting under them and do not feel it. The mat cannot be taught to tell them from its own dead, and the power will not try, because to teach the mat one distinction would be to give it a face, and the face is the thing the valley does not have and should not be given. So it does not touch the mat. It touches the sleepers. Everyone who lies down in the warm at dusk in this valley now dreams the same dream, which is a dream of the floor going out from under them, and it comes early, before the fiber has begun to part, and they wake gasping with their hands in the sedge and they get up and they move. They do not know why. They tell each other, over the pass, that the valley gives bad sleep. No one has been taken since. No one has noticed that no one has been taken since.

The second is for the ones already gone.

The pipelines are thorough. Not a buckle, not a bone. The mat kept nothing of them because it keeps nothing of anything; it is the opposite of a reliquary. The power is not. It holds what the valley did not — the names, the weights, the particular way each hand lay in the sedge before the warm took it, the last thing seen (always the same: the columns standing up out of the gray with their vapor going straight up) — and it holds them the way it holds everything, which is entirely, and for as long as it lasts, which is longer than the mountains. It does not put them back. It cannot. It is not the kind of power that unmakes what a metabolism has made. But there is a difference between *gone* and *unkept*, and the power sees to it that these are only the first.

The third is for the hare.

It considers, for a long time, whether the hare is owed anything. The hare's whole lineage is one pressure — *do not stop* — and the pressure is the hare, and to ease it would be to end the hare and put some other, softer animal in its place. The power will not do that. It will not add a hand's breadth to the hare's sense of depth or a second to its warning, because the hare already has exactly the depth-sense and exactly the warning that a life on this floor has cut into it, and they are enough for almost every night, and the nights they are not enough for are the hare's too.

So the power does not touch the hare. What it does is this: when the tracks go to the lip of a sag and do not come out, the power is there. It is at the lip. It sees. The mat does not register the difference between the hare and a dead sedge, and it should not be made to, but something in the valley now does, and the something is not a rescue and is not a mercy and is only a witness, and the power has decided that a witness is a thing a small pale animal at the end of a lineage shaped entirely by not-stopping is owed. It is there for each of them. There is never more than one at a time. It is there for each.

The fourth is for the thing in the water.

The power looks at it. It can; it looks at everything. And it sees what the disturbance under the roof of the deep lake actually is, and whether it is an animal that has learned to drink the mat or the mat learning to be an animal, and what happens to it when the roof falls and the water lies empty to the sky, and where it comes back from.

And it does not say. Not to the men with the cores, not to the finches, not here. Because the thing in the water is the one place in the valley where something is not finished — where the mat, or its tenant, or the two of them together, is still working out what it is — and the power has learned, in other places, that you do not look directly at a thing while it is deciding what to be. You certainly do not name it. Naming would be a kind of pressure, and the mat attends to pressure, and the water is warm, and the power would rather not know what the mat would do with a name.

So it lets the covered lake keep its dark. It lets the roof fall and rise. It lets the not-knowing stand as the one thing in the valley it will not touch, and it is careful — it is very careful — not to be curious out loud.

—

After that it stays.

Not because the valley needs it. The valley has never needed anything that came over the pass; that is the whole of what it is. The power stays because it has a face and has turned it toward this place and does not intend to turn it away, and because a thing that holds still by working can be kept company by another such thing, even if only one of them knows it.

It stands where the last ordinary stone gives out. It has one foot on each side. The finches have learned it — they flick their wings at it, the quick white flash from the underside, and it has learned to flick back, in whatever way it has of doing that, and this is the entire conversation and it is enough. The hare does not know it is there and is not meant to. The furnace crew works in the wet dark as it has since before the plants came and roofed it in, and the power leaves it entirely alone, because it is the oldest thing in the valley and the most complete and there is nothing a power could add to a nation of trillions that has never once been counted and does not need to be.

And the mat churns. From wall to wall, without ceasing, making stillness out of motion and routing it down into the dark where it is very heavy and getting heavier. At dusk the columns stand up out of the general gray with their vapor going straight up, and the frost comes in from the edges of the leaves, and a section a kilometer away lets go without a sound and somewhere else a new ridge begins to lift.

The power hears it. That is the last thing, and it is not an act, and it is the only thing the valley has never had. Every sound made here goes out into the thin air and into the mat and does not come back. The power does not give the valley an echo — an echo would change it, and it will not be changed. But something at the pass now receives what the valley absorbs. The shout that goes out flat and small and is taken. The stems parting without a tear. The masses falling into more plant. The whole silent throughput of a factory twenty kilometers long that has forgotten nothing because it never knew anything and goes on, working, working, in the cold, in the dark.

Someone is listening to it now. It will never know. It does not need to. But it is true, and the power has decided that some things are worth being true even where there is no one to be true to, and it stands at the pass with the ordinary stone under one foot and the living floor under the other, and it whispers, because everything does, and it keeps the valley company in the only way a valley can be kept company: by not leaving, and by not asking it to be anything but what it is.
