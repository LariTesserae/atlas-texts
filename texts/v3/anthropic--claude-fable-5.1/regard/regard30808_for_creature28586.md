The power comes down onto the low ground the way everything comes down onto it: through the canopy, wetly, at the pace of a drop. It does not arrive with light. It has too much sense for that. It lets its eyes give up the way any eyes give up here, and it lies against the stone, and it waits until it can feel the two pulses—one from kilometers off and one from an arm's length—and until it can no longer tell which is the animal.

That takes some time. The power has all of it.

---

What it wants to do first is what anyone would want to do. It wants to go over the ridge and put the fires out. Pull the haze off the sky like a cloth off a table and let the forest see stars for the first time in the memory of anything alive in it. Stop the reciprocating thing. Give the ground back its stillness. That is what benevolence looks like from far away, and the power is not too proud to admit the impulse moved through it like a want.

Then it looks at the frogs.

Thousands of them, on the wet ledge, shoulder to shoulder, rising and falling almost in time with a machine. It imagines the machine gone. It imagines the ledge in the next second: every small gloss body stiffening toward a thud that does not come, holding, holding, and then something in the muscle of each one asking a question there has never been an answer to. Not pain exactly. Worse, in a way, because pain is a thing a body knows how to have. This would be the floor going out from under an animal that has never learned there is a floor. The power looks at that for a long time and puts the first want down.

The second want is the opposite, and it is the sneakier one. Keep the fires going. Forever, if need be. Stand behind the pumps beyond the ridge and be the hand that never lets them fail, so that the forest's borrowed heart never stops. The power turns this over and finds it has a rotten side. There are people beyond the ridge. They breathe the haze the forest only sees the underside of. They will, one day, want to stop; they have their own frogs. And a pulse held in the ground for the sake of a forest that does not know it is being held—that is not a heartbeat anymore. It is a lie the stone would be made to tell, and the power knows that lies told to rock have a way of lasting longer than the teller. It puts the second want down.

The third is to do nothing. The power considers this the longest, because it is the closest to wisdom and because the forest so plainly recommends it. Nothing here was built. Nothing here is new. The forest took a smelter as a fact, slowly, without comment; it can take the smelter's absence the same way. There would be a bad night. A bad year. Bracing and no release. And then the crabs would shift when they felt like shifting and the frogs would breathe at the rate of frogs and in a few generations a night without a thud would be what quiet meant, the way a night with one means it now. The arrangement would re-settle. The two rates would match again, only the second rate would be nothing. The forest is competent. It does not need help.

But the power is lying on the stone, and the animal is an arm's length away with its mouth in the water, and its heart is going—one beat to every three or four of the machine's—and the power has been here long enough that it can no longer tell which pulse is which. And it thinks: *a fig that falls is not the same as a heart that stops.* The forest has absorbed a great deal. It has never absorbed this, because it has never had a heart to lose. The competence is real. So is the difference. The power does not want to spare the forest the ending. It wants the ending to have a shape the forest can take.

---

So it does not touch the low ground at all.

It goes over the ridge, down off the limestone, onto the coastal flat, and it finds the works. They are what they seemed from underneath: a row of flares, a refinery going gray at the seams, and a pumphouse full of old machines, each one heavy and reciprocating and coupled through its footing to the same bedrock the forest stands on. The power does not stop them. It does not tend them either. It does one thing, and the thing is very small, and it is about time.

It finds the pump that will be the last to fail. It knows which one—it can see how they will go, one by one, over years, as such things go: a bankruptcy, a bearing, a man who does not come back after the holiday, a flare that is simply not relit. The haze will thin whether the power does anything or not. That is already in motion. What the power attends to is the last machine, and the last machine only, and what it does is lean on it. Not to keep it going. To wear it. The way water wears the rock.

Four seconds, five. Then, in a year, five and a little. In ten years, six. The bearing does not seize; it loosens. The stroke lengthens. The power sits in the pumphouse with its hand on the casing and lets it slow the way an old heart slows, so gradually that no one on the flat is ever quite sure it has changed, and nothing on the low ground has any way of knowing that the floor is being lowered under it a hair at a time.

The forest follows. Of course it follows; it followed once already, when the pumps began. The frogs on the ledge rise and fall to six, then seven, without knowing they have learned anything. The crabs shift a little less often and the clicking at the pool's edge thins to a slower ticking, like the drip. A generation of the low heavy animal is born into an interval of eight, and its mother's heart, which had sat at one to four, sits now at one to two, and then the daughter's heart, when she lies in the mud, is nearly one to one, so that if you put your sternum against the stone beside her you would feel the two pulses come together and then drift apart and come together again, like two people walking who have not agreed to walk in step.

Meanwhile the flares go dark. One, then three. The copper stain in the haze fades to a bruise and then to almost nothing, and one moonless night there are points of white above the canopy that nothing in the forest has any use for, and the bats fly under them as they fly under everything, and the roost trees do not shiver, and it is fine.

Ten seconds. Twelve. The thud is no longer a floor by now. It is an event. Something that arrives, and the forest notices it arriving—the frogs tighten, the ants pause in the column for less than a step—and then it goes, and between one arrival and the next there is a stretch of ground that is simply ground. The animals do not brace in that stretch. They live in it. That is the whole of what the power was after, and it took forty years, and it cost a bearing.

Thirty seconds. A minute. The pumphouse is a ruin with one machine still turning in it. The interval is longer than a breath now, longer than the drinking, longer than the time it takes for a moth to shift one inch on a trunk, and somewhere in the lengthening the pulse stops being a pulse and becomes a thing that happens sometimes. Then once an hour. Then the pump stops, one night, on a stroke like any other, and the power takes its hand off the casing—

—and nothing on the low ground notices. The last thud was so long ago that nothing was waiting for it. The frogs breathe. The crabs shift. The animal lowers herself, and the knees go and the chest goes and the mud takes her weight, and she puts her mouth into the water and stays there.

---

That is when the power learns the thing it did not come knowing.

It is back on the stone, in the doline, sternum to the rock, and it is listening for the silence with no mass in it—the thing the forest had no word for—and the silence is not there. The ground is not still. It was never still. Under the machine, the whole time, there had been the drip: warm as blood, uneven, ticking down from canopy to canopy to fern to stone and into the stone, tens of thousands of drops a minute across the whole low ground, each one too small to feel and all of them together a weight in the rock like weather. And under the drip, the doline settling, a grain at a time, into the clay. And under that, an arm's length off, a heart—slow, heavy, born to a night with no floor in it and therefore never having needed one.

The forest never had a heartbeat. It has one now. It was here before the works. The works were only loud enough to cover it.

The power stays one more night to be sure, and then goes back up through the canopy the way it came, wetly, at the pace of a drop. It was never seen. The forest takes its going the way it takes everything, as a fact, slowly, and without comment, and by morning the pool has risen by the width of a finger, and the animal lies in the wet with her slow heart, and the ground says what it has always said, which was never the machine. It was the rain.
