It comes in the way the rain comes, which is to say from the same quarter as everything else, and it takes the shape the place offers, because it has learned that a thing which arrives in its own shape learns nothing.

For a long time it does not arrive at all. It sits in the leaf-crown above the causeway where the drip begins and it listens, because the town has told it — the town is honest, the town leans toward what it is — that the first duty here is to hear. It learns the knock before it learns a single name. It learns the hiss, and under the hiss the grind, and under the grind the slow bubble in the tanks that a child is sitting beside and reporting in a voice like a bird. It waits for the shift change. When the knock stops it hears the cane, and it stays for nine more evenings until it can hear that Tallis's strip has a drier note than Wend's, and that the eleventh is almost silent, and only then does it consider that it has been introduced.

---

It looks for the being it was told lives here and finds that the question is badly put.

It looks at the engine, which wants and is tired and is in a mood, and whose plate has been polished into a smoothness, and it thinks: this is a being the way a heart is a being. It looks at the ret, singular, older than the iron, fed cane and making fibre and never once outside the low wing, and thinks: this is a being the way a gut is. It looks at the cane, which is the water's upper surface and the walls and the ground and the dead, and it looks at the people whose hands are finished in the same lacquer as the walls, and it looks at the mat under all of it thickening on a hundred years of cutting into a callus that holds the whole town up, and it understands that it has been sent to a single creature which is forty roofs and forty strips and one flywheel and one culture and one cat, and which breathes out through a brick stack, and which has never, in the whole of its life, asked for anything.

That last thing stops it for a while. It has come from places that pray. It is used to being leaned toward. Here nothing is lifted upward except steam, and the steam is not an offering, it is simply the water going back to where it came from. The town does not know it is being looked at, and would not, if told, have a register to say anything about it in. It would be interesting. It would not be the shape of the place.

So the power sits in the leaf-crown and asks itself what benevolence is, to a thing that is complete.

---

It goes through the obvious gifts the way a tank-tender goes through a cutting, testing each, and it sets each one down.

Birds. It could fill the leaf-crown with them in a night. But the town has arranged itself around one egret that eats nothing, and a hundred birds would be a hundred hungers the arrangement has no room for; they would eat the insects that are already a rumour, and the frogs the power would have to bring for them, and the children would learn to chase things, and the egret would become an egret. The power finds it does not want the egret to become an egret. It finds this is not a small thing.

Hearing. It could give back the band the rollers took. But the band is where the town keeps its distance from each other, and taking it away means they would stand closer and watch mouths, and they would have to invent the flat hand turned slowly on the wrist to mean *the engine*, and they have already done all this, and it works, and the loss is the only thing in the town that everyone over thirty owns equally. To heal it would be to give them the grind. They would hear the rollers for the first time in their lives and it would sound to them like the end of something.

Dryness. A hard shadow, a horizon at the height of the eye. It considers this for the least time of all. The people here are kept, not weathered; they cure. In a dry wind they would crack like anything cured does. Their cloth would go stiff again and never soften. The knives would fern. The mat would shrink and the piles would stand proud of it and the town would sit up on stilts over its own dead ground, and the power has seen that, elsewhere, and it is not benevolence.

It sets all of these down. It has not yet done anything, and it begins to suspect that most of what it will do here is this.

---

But it is wise, and wisdom is partly the seeing of what a thing inside itself cannot see, so it looks at the arrangement from the one place the arrangement cannot stand: outside.

It sees the water rising. Four layers in the causeway. The half-metre holds because the town chases it, and the town will chase it as long as there are hands, and that is fine, that is the town's own clock. The power does not own the end of it and does not take it.

It sees the ret. One culture, in one bank of tanks, continuous since the first fill, and never a bucket of it anywhere but here. It sees that the town is a body with one gut and no second, and that the skin on the surface runs from cream to a grey that means trouble, and that one week the grey could come and not go back. Everything else here can be rebuilt from cane and iron and the barge. This cannot.

So this is the first thing it does. It takes a bucket. Not from the tanks — it would not take from the town — but from the sourness the tenders carry in their clothes, from the smell you could tell them by with your eyes shut. There is enough of the living thing in that. It carries the bucket somewhere the town will never know about, somewhere warm and slow, and it tends it, and it does not hurry, and it means to tend it for as long as the tanks stand. And if the year comes when the skin goes grey and the bubble stops and the two women stand in the low wing with their hands hanging and nothing in any register to say, there will be, on the loading dock the morning the barge comes, a wooden bucket with a lid, unaccounted for, that the bargemen will swear they did not bring. It will smell right. The tenders will know it before they have lifted the lid. They will pour it into the new fill and it will take, and they will say that the tanks came back, and they will be correct, and no one will ever ask from where.

---

The second thing it does is about the gap in the naming.

It has counted the strips and counted the dead in them and it has found the short ones — the houses that are one person under, because a young man went down the canal to see a dry road and died on it, or a young woman went and had children on firm ground who never heard of Wend. The town has closed over these the way the mat closes over a cut. But the power can see where the cut was. It can see, from outside, that a strip one person short grows exactly as well as any other, and that this is the saddest true thing in the place.

It cannot bring them back. Bodies are not its business, and the dead are lying where they were laid, in ground you can dig. But the arrangement here is that the dead come up green and are cut and pressed and burned and breathed out of the stack and come down again as rain, and rain is a thing the power can do something with. So it goes down the canal, farther than any barge, to a churchyard and a roadside and a town on a hill where the shadows are hard, and it takes from each of those graves what rain takes from ground, which is almost nothing, and it carries it. It carries it back the way the weather comes, from the one quarter. And on the evening the knock stops and the cane turns its hundred thousand dry hinges to take the last light and the old ones say *it will rain*, it rains, and it rains first on the fourth strip out along the north side and on the nineteenth, and it comes down on the leaves, which pass it down.

Nothing changes in the naming. The strip is still one short in the count. But the power, standing in the leaf-crown with the drip beginning around it, considers that the town would not want the count changed. It would want the rain. It has given the rain.

---

The third thing it does is for the engine, and it is the smallest of the three and the one it holds longest before deciding.

The engine will stop. Not for repair. The flywheel has turned for longer than any stoker has lived and the brass has been polished into speechlessness and one night the hiss will go out of the shed and not come back, and the stack will stop leaning, and the town will lie awake in a cold it has only ever known in ten-minute pieces, listening to nothing, and get up and stand on the gangways speaking too loudly and not sleep. And it will not sleep the next night either. That is the thing the power sees: not that the engine will fail — everything here is honest about ending, the dead are in the crop — but that the silence will arrive at a wrong hour. In the middle of the night, when there is nothing to hear in it.

So the power arranges the hour. That is all. It does not stay the flywheel a single turn beyond its life; it does not touch the brass. It only sees to it that when the last turn comes, it comes in the late afternoon, in the ten minutes at the shift change when the feed is already cleared and the knock has already stopped and the town is already standing still where it is — on the gangways, in the doorways, at the tanks with the bucket half tipped. The grind will go, and the hiss will go, and the silence will open up around them at the one hour of the day they have spent their lives inside a piece of it, and into the silence will come the cane.

They will hear it as they have never heard it, with nothing under it at all. Tallis's dry note and Wend's, and the eleventh nearly silent. The old ones will hear the strips by name, and the young ones will hear, for the first time, what the old ones have been telling them was there. And they will be afraid, because the engine is off. But they will not be listening to nothing. The power has seen to it that they will not, on that night, be listening to nothing.

It thinks that is what a benevolent thing would do, that had learned the place. It is not sure. It is only the wisest thing it could think of, which is not the same.

---

It lets the cat see it.

That is not a gift. That is the power giving itself something, and it knows it, and it does it anyway, because the cat is the one idle creature here and idleness is the only place a visitor can sit down. The cat is asleep on the warm press-cake in the drying wing with two stokers asleep beside it. It wakes, as cats wake, all at once and without surprise, and walks the length of the shed past the cold rail and the flywheel turning, and sits down in the fogged light under a caged bulb with its own small weather, and looks up into the clerestory where the power is, and checks. It looks for about as long as the egret looks at the water. Then it goes back and lies down. The stokers do not wake. One of them, later, will say the cat was restless, and the other will say the engine was in a mood, and both will be describing the same thing.

---

Then it goes, in the way the rain goes, which is down.

It leaves nothing in the news the bargemen bring. It does not appear in any story the children tell about the egret. It puts no name on the list of a dozen. The town will never know that for ten evenings something sat in the leaf-crown learning the strips, and that a bucket is being tended somewhere against a grey week, and that the rain on the fourth strip out came a long way, and that the engine will stop at a mercy of an hour. The town does not know it was looked at, and would not have a register to say so in, and the power finds — settling down through the leaves, becoming what the leaves pass down — that it does not mind this. It has been to places that pray. It has never before been to a place that did not need to, and it was not sure, until it stood inside the arrangement, that such a place was owed anything.

It is owed this: that someone outside it know what it is, and not touch it, and hold the bucket.

Someone hits the beam — *chock* — and the cane goes back beneath the noise, where it lives, and the water that has just come down on it is already, drop by drop, on its way back up through the root and the culm and the rollers and the stack, and none of it, not one drop, can tell you where it has been.
