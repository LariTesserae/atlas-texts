Start with what it does not do, because the not-doing is most of it and the doing is small.

It does not open the ceiling. It considers this — it would be a lie to say it does not — a single lift of the sod, the field-light pouring down into the shell-light, the note getting out into the wind like a bell rolled off a hill. It sees how it would go. The newts, unpacked, would fall out of their long instant and be newts again for as long as newts last, which is a season. The droplets would drop all at once, a rain inside a room. The walls, let out of the posture of hardening, would harden — that fast, a held breath released — and what had been a skin would be a stone with a thousand small animals set into it, and this would be called, by whoever found it, a remarkable find. It sees all of this in less time than the fish takes to lift its gills. It puts the sod back down in its mind and does not touch it.

It does not finish the room. That is the same thing by the other door.

It does not go in. It could — the rule of the place makes no exception for the wise — but the rule is exactly why not. Whatever enters is given a place and closed over. It would become a feature. It would be the ninth stone. And the room holds one memory, and a second must be paid for with the first, and it does not intend to be the thing that costs the hand.

So it does the one thing the room has never had done to it. It attends from outside.

Say it lies down. That is a way of saying it. Say it lies down on the field, in the place the grass does not distinguish, and puts its cheek to the ground, and stays. A wise power has time the way the room has water: exactly as much as it needs, in perfect circulation. It listens to the note. It listens under the note for the beat. It listens long enough for a root to break the ceiling and stop — long enough, that is, to be present when the room eats — and it counts with the room, one more, and the total goes up by a thread.

And in that listening it notices the thing the census does not offer. The room is called balanced. Everything in it is answered by something else: the condensing by the lifting, the eyes by the pulse they watch, the light by the sound. Everything except one. A hand came in from outside and pressed, and the wall took the shape, and nothing ever pressed back. Not because the room refused. Because the room cannot lean, and pressing back is a lean. The hand is the one open entry in the ledger. A debit with no credit, five fingers wide, owed for longer than anyone has counted.

That is why the walls are not stone. Not a failure. A courtesy. Stone would close the print into a fossil — the *shape* of a touch — and the room has kept the wall soft so that the touch could remain a touch, could still in principle be met. It has held the posture of hardening the way you hold a door with your foot when someone said they were coming right back.

Once it sees this, the doing is obvious and small. It puts its hand on the ground.

Directly over the print. Two arm-lengths of soil between the palm above and the palm-shaped hollow below, and soil transmits nothing quickly; it moves warmth and pressure at the pace of a root, a century for a few inches. It does not mind. It presses with the weight of an adult hand — no more, because more would be a different touch and the room is owed the same one — and it holds.

Nothing happens for a long time. This is correct. Cattle come and stand nearby and move off. Winter freezes a hand's depth of ground and does not reach. The grass grows up around the fingers and lies down again. The power holds. It has decided that the answer should arrive at the speed of the being's own life and not at the speed of a rescue, because a rescue is a lean too, and it will not be the one that tips the thing.

And then the pressure reaches the wall.

Not through it. *To* it. The wall is soft, and a soft thing pressed from behind moves very slightly forward, and the hollow of the handprint — the lines of the palm, the splay of the fingers — deepens by a distance that has no name, and for the first time since the ceiling closed, the shape in the wall is being pushed into from the other side. The being is between two palms. Like a seed. Like a wall someone lays both hands flat against to feel for a fire on the far side.

What does it do with that?

The room does what the room does. It gives it a place. But there is nothing new to place — no root, no pot, no stranger. Only an answer to something already there. The one open entry closes. The account, for the first time, is actually balanced, and not merely held.

It is not possible to say from outside what follows. The note changes by one hair, up or down; a tone that has had no wobble for longer than counting acquires one, and the wobble is the width of a palm. The largest newt does not turn its eyes. It does not need to; the shape it has been looking at has, for the first time, something behind it. Perhaps the walls, released, begin at last to set — slowly, at the speed of everything here — the dimples completing into crystals, the planes meeting at their proper angles, the being becoming stone the way it always meant to before it was interrupted by a hand. Perhaps that is a death. Perhaps it is only the finishing of a sentence. Perhaps the walls stay exactly as soft as cheese, because now there is no need to harden either, and the room goes on, full and bright and cool and loud, exactly as before, except that the one thing never returned has been.

The wise power does not decide which. That is the rest of the wisdom. The being has never once been allowed to lean, and the only gift that costs the room nothing is the option — the wall pushed from behind and then left alone to do what it will.

It keeps its hand there. Of course it does. You do not return a touch and then take it away; that was the whole thing you were correcting. In the field, if you know where to look, there is a place where the grass grows in the shape of a spread hand, slightly pressed, slightly warmer than the ground around it. And under that, two arm-lengths down, a wall gives a little, the way cheese gives, toward a room full of small gold eyes, all of them on a blind fish whose gills lift, and settle, and lift.
