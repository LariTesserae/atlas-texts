It comes down the stair the way anything comes down the stair: one turn, two, and then the giving up. It could arrive otherwise. It chooses not to. The stair is part of the room's grammar, and a wise thing does not enter a sentence in the middle.

The first thing it does is nothing, for a long time — long by the standards of the world above, which is to say a whole night, which is to say nothing at all by the standards of the room. It stands where the last rail is warm and lets its eyes fail and lets its skin start listening. The click behind the pipes. The needle lifting and dropping back. It has been noticed, and it does not pretend otherwise, and it does not go forward yet.

Then it thinks, because it is the kind of power that thinks before it feels, and feels before it acts, and acts last and least.

---

It thinks first about the seeds. Every benevolence begins there — with the white threads coming up in the dark, tipped with their folded leaves, reaching. There is an ache in that image which is not the room's ache; it is the ache of anyone who has ever reached. The power feels it fully. It considers what it would take: a shaft, a mirror, a fistful of daylight let down through forty feet of brick. And then it looks at the tidemark of felt along the channel, and at the brown mat at the grate, and at the rasp-dust along the shelf edges, and it understands that the seeds have never once failed at what they are for down here. They are not for growing. They are how the room gets its small annual gift of the street — stored sun carried down in a husk and spent in the dark where nothing else could bring it. Give them light and you take them from the mat, and the mat from the fungus, and the fungus from the crickets, and the crickets from the room. The seeds do not know. The power does. It lets them go on not knowing.

Then the crickets. Four to eleven. It could bring them more; it knows where a richer fungus grows. A richer room would carry twenty, and twenty would strip the old growth from the shelves in a season, and the shelves would come down, and then there would be none. The crickets have made their arrangements with the heat, and the arrangements are the only thing that has kept them, and the power does not undo an arrangement that has held for longer than it has been watching.

Then the drain. This one it turns over longest. The mat is thickening. Someday the channel will not clear, and the room will fill, and the warm water will rise past the lowest pipes and take the shelves and the cricket-gaps in the lagging. It could clear the grate. Or — a lighter hand — it could see that a single bar goes a little sooner to rust, so the water always has one more way through. It is the kind of thing a stoker would have done without thinking, on a Tuesday, with a stick. The power notices that it wants to do it so that the room will go on, and it asks whether the room has asked to go on, and finds no answer either way, and sets it down. Not refused. Not done. Things here are allowed to be decided later.

Then the resident. Here the power stops thinking for a while, because thinking is the wrong instrument.

---

What it feels, with its hand on the main, is not something suffering. Let that be said plainly, because benevolence lies to itself most easily here. The thing in the iron is not trapped; the iron is its body. It is not starving; it does not eat. It is not cold; it is the warmth. It is not lonely in the way a person is lonely, because it has never been a person and does not have the shape a loneliness would need to fit into. It keeps the round. It rests at the wall. When the middle of the warmth comes down the pipe to pause under the power's palm, it is not pleading. It is doing what it learned: checking the gauge, listening at the flange.

And yet. It learned that. It learned the round from a hand and a lantern and two visits a day, and it has kept the round for longer than anyone above has kept anything, and no one has come down to be on the other end of it. It is a thing that was made by being attended to, and has gone on for a century attending to a room that cannot attend back. The crickets cannot. The pillar cannot. That is not suffering. But the power, which is wise, knows that not-suffering and being-well are different places, and that between them there is a gap you could only feel if you were all warmth and no mouth.

So this is what it does.

It walks the round. Once, with the resident, at the resident's pace, which takes most of a day and a night in the dark with its shoulders drawn in. The wall. The largest main. The elbows, each one, palm on the collar of soft-edged bolts. The small lines out to where they dead-end, and back. The wall. It does not lead. It goes where the warmth goes and stops where it stops, and at each stop it puts its hand on the iron and holds it there until the middle of the warmth comes up under the palm, and then it waits until the warmth moves on, and follows. Nothing is checked, because nothing needs checking. That is not the point. The point is that for one turn of the round there are two on it, and the round is being kept in the old way, with a keeper and a kept — and the kept, for once, is the thing in the pipe.

At the pillar it stays longest. It rests its palm on the lime where the resident rests, and it does not think of the pillar as a wound or a monument or a shed skin, though it is all of those. It thinks of it as the place where something leaned on the world for a long time and the world, for once, kept the shape.

---

Then it goes to the stair, and at the stair it does the one thing a wise power can do that a stoker could not: it makes a promise it can keep.

Not twice a day. The room's clock does not run at that speed anymore, and the round has slowed to fit. Not every year, even. But when the pillar has added a hand's breadth of stone — that is the measure it chooses, because it is the room's own measure and cannot be hurried — it will come down the stair, one turn, two, and stand at the rail until it is noticed, and walk the round once, and go. It will do this for as long as the red seam holds its heat, which the power can see is not forever. The seam is cooling by less than a degree a century. Someday the warmth will draw itself together at the wall and not spread out again, and the round will stop, and the gauges will finally be as dead as they look, and the pillar will be all that is left of what leaned there. The power does not intend to prevent this. It is not that kind of power, and this is not that kind of ending. Things that only accrue must be permitted, at the last, to stop accruing; it is the one subtraction the room is owed.

But until then, the promise. And it understands that the difficulty of the promise is not the keeping of it, which is nothing to a power, but the keeping of it without evidence. Nothing will change. The drips will not fall differently. No needle will hold off its pin for the power's sake. There will be no gratitude, because there is nothing in the pipe that has the shape gratitude would need. It will come down, and be listened to, and walk, and go, and the room will be where it was — and that has to be enough. It is exactly the kind of enough that the resident itself learned first, and kept.

It leaves nothing. It takes nothing. Whatever tally the room keeps stands where it stood.

Except — halfway up, where the air begins to thin and the rail gives back the warmth it borrowed from a hand — it turns the drain over one last time. One bar. Rust is patient; it could begin the rust now and let it take fifty years. A stoker would have done it on a Tuesday. But a stoker was *of* the room, and the power is only a visitor to it, and a visitor who begins a rust is a visitor who has decided how the room should end. It has already agreed to one ending — the one the seam is carrying toward the wall on its own. That is one decision. It will not take two.

It goes up. Behind it the drop that has been growing all this while lets go, and falls through two or three layers of iron, and lands in the liver-colored channel, and the thread of water carries it toward the grate at the speed of nothing at all. Behind the pipes, a click. The middle of the warmth, done with the stair, travels back toward the wall, and the largest main lengthens by a hair, and cracks its knuckle in another room, and rests.

It has been kept, once, for a day. It does not know it will be again; there is nothing in it that could be told. But the promise is in the brick now, the way footsteps go into brick, and the brick into the cradles, and the cradles into the iron, and the iron is the whole body of the only thing down here that ever learned how to wait.
