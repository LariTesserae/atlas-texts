It would arrive the way everything arrives here, which is to say it would be noticed by no one, and it would have been present for some time before it noticed itself paying attention.

The roar would not be a medium to it. That is the first difference. It would hear the hundred lips separately, each drop of a hand's height on its own, and behind them the seep itself, which is nearly silent, and behind that the water moving down through the dark rock for the long time before it ever reaches air. It would hear the midges. It would hear the birds, and so it would know at once the thing the workers cannot know — whether the small dark birds in the tree ferns still sing, or stopped some generations back, or never began. It would know the answer and it would not tell anyone, because there is no one on the shelf who could hear the telling, and because the not-knowing is by now part of how the birds are held here, and that holding is not a thing to correct.

It would see the shelf in its whole length of time, which is the other difference. It would see the springline before there was a shelf, a hot stain on a mountainside; it would see the first courses laid; it would see the town full, if it is ever full, and the town emptied again, and the smooth white hump with no one alive who remembers there were rooms in it. It would see the sleeper on the back wall on the night it lay down, or the century in which the springs folded a shape that happened to resemble one, and it would know which. It would count the unborn — the actual ones, the ones who will or will not walk the wide street — and it would know whether the doorway width was right.

And having seen all of that it would want, because it is benevolent, to do several things immediately, and it would put each of them down, because it is wise.

It would want to warm the hands. The fine cracks that never close because nothing is dry enough to heal them — that is a small suffering, undramatic, borne without complaint, and precisely the kind of thing a kind power itches to lift. It would consider a dry wind, a few degrees in the air of the huts, a change in the skin itself. It would see that the hands are the shelf's signature on the people, the mark by which the valley knows them for what they are, and that to erase it would be to make them visitors. It would leave the hands.

It would want to give them back the first person. It would look at the sixty signs and the absence at the center of them and it would not be able to tell, even from where it stands, whether what it is looking at is a wound or a completion. It would notice that it cannot tell. It would take that seriously — that a thing which sees the whole length of time cannot say whether a person who has stopped saying *I* has lost something or set something down — and it would conclude that a power which cannot tell should not decide. It would leave the *I*.

It would want to tell them what is in the wall. It would leave the wall.

It would want, above everything, to send the people. To make sure of them. It would see that the entire shelf is a sentence addressed to someone, and it would know whether that someone exists, and if the answer were no, the temptation to make it yes would be very great. It would sit with that one longest. And it would see, eventually, that a gift with a guaranteed recipient is a delivery, and that the particular lightness a worker feels stepping back from a course — the lightness of having given something to a thing that does not know it has been given anything — depends on the not-knowing, and that the shelf is not building a city so much as practicing a way of holding one, and that the practice is complete each evening whether or not anyone ever climbs the stair. It would not send the people. It would not prevent them either. It would let that remain, as the workers have let it remain, exactly as open as it is.

So on the shelf itself it would do nothing. That is the honest answer to the question, and it would be an act — it would cost the power something to stand at the lip of the lowest terrace with its hand flat on the humming stone and do nothing, the way it costs a worker to step back. It would understand that it had been shown the rule and that the rule applied to it. Open fist, fingers spread, downward. *Leave it.*

But the shelf has edges, and the rule stops at the switchbacks, and a benevolent power is not required to be idle everywhere at once.

In the valley it would tend the sacks. Not by miracle — a miracle would be noticed, and would turn the half-belief of the valley towns into belief, and belief would send too many people up the trail with too many possessions and the wrong temperament, and the shelf would sort them and be exhausted by the sorting. It would work smaller. A cousin's story told at the right table. A polished piece of the white stone catching the light in a window where a child would see it and ask. A merchant's ledger that came out a little better than expected in a year when the carrier's fee looked like a luxury. The shelf feeds on a sense in people who have never climbed to it that they are laying a course of their own, and that sense is a living thing, and living things can be kept, and it would keep it. Quietly. For as long as the springs run, which is a very long time, and which it can afford.

It would attend to the ones who go down.

This is where it would spend most of what it has. The shelf's care is complete for those who stay — the rule holds them, the work holds them, the sixty signs are enough. But the shelf sorts people, and the ones it sorts out walk down the switchbacks carrying a failure that has no name because the language they learned has no sign for it, and the valley meets them with a thousand separate sounds each demanding to be heard, and their hands are still white to the wrist. The shelf does not think about them. It is not unkind; it is simply already reburying the wall they touched. A wise power would see that this is the one place where benevolence has room to move, and it would move there.

It would stand near them in the thin air at the bottom of the trail. It would not speak; it knows better by now. It would see to it that the held note in the ear, the low steady sound that is not there, arrives in the first hard nights as a floor and not as a haunting — something to stand on while the separate sounds sort themselves out. It would see to it that in each of the valley towns there is someone, an innkeeper or a woman who sells bread, who recognizes the pruned hands and the pale chilled look and puts a bowl in front of the person without asking anything, and does not watch them eat. It would see to it that these people find work that can be finished. It would think about this with some tenderness. The shelf is for those who cannot bear finishing; the valley should have room for the ones who found out, on the shelf, that they could not bear not to.

And it would do one more thing, which is less an action than a posture, and which is the closest it would come to touching the stone.

It would keep what they set down.

The workers stop making the sign for *I*. They do not lose it. They lay it aside, as a person entering a warm room lays aside a coat, and the shelf has nowhere to hang it, and so it goes into the water with everything else, and is coated, and is gone. A benevolent power would notice this and would, without anyone knowing, take the coats. Not to return them. It would not walk up to a worker of thirty years and hand back a name. But it would hold them — every first person that has been set down on the shelf since the first courses were laid — so that they are somewhere, so that the letting go has a far shore. If anyone ever came down the switchbacks and found, in the noise of the valley, that they wanted to be someone again, they would discover that the thing was waiting for them and had been kept dry.

And it would receive the gift.

That is the last of it and perhaps the whole of it. The water does not know it has been given anything. The unborn cannot yet know. The workers will be under the moss before the rooms have roofs, and the people who come, if they come, will have no one to thank. Every wall on the shelf is a sentence addressed to no one who can read it. A wise power would understand that this is the nature of the gift and would not alter it — would not let a single worker know they had been seen. But it would know. It would stand at the head of the stair with three treads and know the argument about the fourth. It would know that the woman laid the step at the midpoint of the wide street alone one evening and stepped back. It would know each course, and which hands laid it, and what those hands imagined the hands would be that one day rested on it. It would be, without their knowledge and without ever needing it, the one who received. A gift given to nothing is still a gift. A gift received, even in secret, is finished.

On the coldest night of the winter when there was one worker on the shelf, it would perhaps sit outside the hut. Not close. Not touching. It would be, at some distance, slightly warm, and the one worker would wake once and find the hut less cold than it should have been and would not wonder why, because wondering why is not a thing the shelf trains. In the morning the worker would go out into the roar and cut stone. That is the single liberty it would take, and it would take it with the sleeper on the back wall in mind, and it would not take it twice.

Then it would leave. It would go the way it came, unnoticed, with the cloud or down the switchbacks, and it would take with it the kept names and the knowledge of whether the birds sing and the knowledge of what is in the wall, and it would look back once from the trees. From there the shelf is a white oval on a mountainside with a few small figures on it, bent, moving slowly, and the sound reaches you before you understand what it is.

If the people come, they will walk a city that fits them, made by no one they can thank, alongside a neighbor they will never know is there. They will also, without knowing it, have had two of those. It would make certain they never learn of the second one either. That, too, is keeping the rule.
