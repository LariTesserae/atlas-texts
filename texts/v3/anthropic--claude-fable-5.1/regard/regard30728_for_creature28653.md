Something has noticed. Not from the rim — nothing sees from the rim; the fog will not allow it — but from wherever such things notice from, and the first thing it does is the first thing anyone who is actually wise does in a place they do not understand, which is nothing.

It comes down. It stands in the sound until the sound stops being sound. It kneels and puts its palm flat on the crust and feels the plate lift and the second plate beneath. It walks a reed-line to an engine-house and stops at the door, where an old tender is sitting with her back to the cylinder, and puts its hand on the brick beside hers, and does not tap, because it does not yet know when in the stroke a tap is a question. It waits to learn. This takes a long time. It takes, in the plain's reckoning, a life — one child carried against the iron until it can hold itself, walked out along the channel, laid down in the reeds. The attention is there for all of it and does nothing, and it is not indifference. It is the refusal to mistake its own discomfort for their suffering, which is the first mistake a power makes in a place like this and the one that ends peoples.

Because the obvious mercies are all killings.

Lift them out: carry them up the sloping road through the fog to somewhere the ground holds still. It considers this for exactly as long as it takes to remember the failed engine and the tenders waking all at once in the soft place, sick and grasping, palms flat on the floor, feeling for a beat that is not there. Still ground is not rest to them. It is the floor giving way. A people who have never in their lives stood on a floor that did not answer them would not be saved by silence; they would be drowned in it. And whatever they were when they came up out of the sheds — whatever their grandmothers' grandmothers were, before the hands took the shape of the brass — they are not that now. They are as native as the reeds. You do not rescue a reed from a channel.

Stop the engines: end the noise that is grinding the plain to nothing and let it lie. That is the flood. The tenders know this better than the attention does.

Repair the engines: new bearings, true cylinders, brass that is not the shape of anything yet. Make the plain eternal. It considers this longest, because it is the closest to kindness, and it is the one it is most suspicious of. A plain that never finishes is a plain whose people never get to finish either — held in a labour that has been given no floor and no end, by a power they cannot see, for reasons they cannot know. That is not tending. That is keeping. And the attention has already noticed the reed-lines, and who is in them, and how the tenders speak about the quiet.

So it does small things, and it does them where no one can see.

---

The head beyond the rim.

The attention goes and looks at where the water goes. It is the one place on this errand it can be useful without being present: the outside, whatever the engines lift into, has been rising, and the labouring the tenders feel on bad nights is not age but resistance, a dozen old beams pushing against a weight they were never built to lift. There is nothing sacred about that weight. It is not part of them. It is only more water, somewhere else, with a story of its own that the attention does not tell here.

It eases it. Not all at once — a sudden drop would be a shock in the ground the tenders would read as something breaking. Slowly, over a season of the water's own schedule, the head falls, and the beams stop fighting and go back to lifting, and one night across the plain the spirals in the pools outside every door go back to hexagons and stay. The tenders feel it. They stand with their palms on the brick and tap on the fall: *steady*. Nobody tells them why. Nothing enters their story that was not already in it. That is the whole design of the gift: that they should never know it was one.

The seam.

The pile of old fittings under every floor is getting smaller and everyone knows it; the tenders speak of it the way anyone speaks of a thing they cannot alter. The attention looks at the fill. The bores said there was no bottom; the bores were right, and the deepest, oldest, rust-brown layers were dumped by works that were richer and more careless than anything that came after. The attention does not put anything there. It only sees to it that what is already there is where the reed-lines will lead the diggers when the diggers go deep enough — that the fill gives up, when asked, a little more than it seemed to hold. Bearing-shells in a nest of old clinker. A vein of coke thick enough to burn for a lifetime. Not endless. Enough for the children who are alive, and their children. Enough to be found, and to feel like finding.

This is a smaller thing than it sounds. It buys nothing but time, and the attention knows it, and does it anyway, because time is what people actually live in.

The rim.

It goes round the whole basin in the fog, a day's walk and more, feeling for the low place. There is one — there is always one — where the lip of made ground is thinnest and the darkness beyond leans closest. The attention does not open it. But it makes certain that when the plain finally rises to meet it, the water will find that place and go over it, and not merely stand. It does this for the slime.

Because the slime is the one thing here that the sound has given rather than taken, the one native born of the beat, and when the last engine falls silent and the basin fills to the brim and lies still, the slime will have nothing to eat and will go dark. Unless the still water is not still. Unless it joins something beyond the rim that has a rhythm of its own — and it does; the tenders felt it answering on the bad nights — and the film that glows in the flats is carried over the lip into a wider water that beats in a different time, and somewhere the plain's people have never imagined, a long way off, a green flicker runs across a surface after a stroke, and goes out, and comes again.

The plain will not survive. It was never going to. But something of it will get out, and it will get out the only way anything on the plain has ever moved — carried, on the water's schedule, along the line of least resistance.

---

One thing it does that can be seen.

Near the end of its watching, when the old tender is very old and sits all day against the cylinder with her eyes shut, the attention puts its hand on the brick beside hers and, on the rising side of the stroke, taps. It has learned enough by now. The question it asks is the one the whole plain has been asking underneath its noise for longer than anyone can put a figure to: *what is the plain when it is finished?*

She does not open her eyes. She waits for the beam to top and fall, and on the fall she answers, four fingers on the back of its hand.

The word she uses is the word for the reed-lines. It is the word for the quiet places along the channels, the nodes where the shaking cancels and a root can hold; it is the word for where the dead are laid, because that is the ground that will keep them. She does not have a word for *flood*. She does not have a word for *end*. She has the word for the place where things can stay, and she uses it for the whole plain, and then she taps once more, a small one in the pause at the top of the next stroke, which is not urgency this time but something the attention has to work at before it understands it is being teased: *you asked as if you did not know.*

It sits with her until the shift is over. It takes its hand away when hers does.

---

The attention leaves the way it came, up the sloping road, the sound thinning under its feet until it is only sound again and then not even that. It leaves the compaction alone. The plain will go on sinking a hair's breadth at a time under the beat that keeps it dry, and the tenders will go on burning the plain's past to keep its present above water and raking the ash back on top, and the reed-lines will drift a few paces over across a century, and one by one, years apart, the engines will fall silent and the soft places will spread and join.

But the beams will not labour. The floor under the floor will give a little more than anyone thought it held. And when the water comes to the rim it will find a way over, and the finish will be a joining and not a drowning, and the one thing the sound made will go out into the wider dark still glowing, in a rhythm that is not its own, in water that came from a place where the ground answered.

That is all it does. It is very little. It is what the tenders would have done, if they had been where it was and seen what it saw — which is the only test it knows for whether help is help. They tend the engines. It tended the plain, once, briefly, in the places they could not reach, and then it got out of the way and let the plain arrive at what it was going to be, which they already had a word for.

The beat comes up through the ground, and comes up through the ground, and comes up through the ground. Far off a green flicker crosses the water. Nearer, a reed shivers in a line of stillness. Somewhere under a floor a child is lying flat on the moss with the whole of a plain coming up through the length of her, learning to sleep on it.
