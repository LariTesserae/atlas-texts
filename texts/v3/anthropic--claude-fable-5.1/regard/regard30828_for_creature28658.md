The first thing it would do is the thing the room has never had done to it. It would look.

Not the way the essay looks, from a body hanging in the light bar with the stroke going through its sternum, a body that has to be careful of the grille and has an hour of air in it and will eventually be food or furniture. It would look from everywhere at once and for as long as it liked. It would count the shrimp. It would know how many there are and would know that the number is not interesting. It would watch the flatworm complete one arc and begin the next and would notice, because it has the patience to, that the arcs are not quite identical, that they drift by a hair's width each circuit, so that over a span longer than the eel's life the flatworm has been very slowly walking its path around the wall, and will one day have grazed every part of the alga it can reach, and will begin again. It would find that quietly funny. It would understand that the room's one indifferent citizen is also, in its way, keeping a time.

It would look at the light coming in wet and green and would follow it back out through the grille to the sun and would think about how far the light came to fall on nine fish.

And then it would go down.

---

This is the second thing, and it is the one no one else can do. The engine has never been seen. The tower has become a mountain and there is no way into a mountain. But there is a way into a mountain for something that does not need a way, and it would go down through the throat of the pipe past the eel, which would not stir, and past the place where the eel's dead predecessors have gone, and down the long dark column with the warm water rising against it, miles of it, until it came to the thing that pushes.

What it would find there is not for the essay to say with certainty. But a wise power, looking at an old machine, would look first for the intention, because intention is the part that wears away last. And it might find that the thing at the bottom is not so strange. A weight and a wheel. Heat from the mountain's own root driving water up a bore that someone sank when the tower was new, for a reason that is legible even now if you know how to read a ruin: they wanted water at the top. They wanted it for a garden, or a bath, or a pool for birds, or a basin that would spill over and catch the light and be seen from the plain, a tower that wore a fountain at its crown. Something to be looked at. Something for the sky to have.

And it would see that they got it. Not the garden they drew, but a garden. The tower wore itself down through every one of its intended shapes and stopped at the one the water wanted, and the water, given enough time, wanted a small warm ocean with hair in it and a valve made of an animal. The power would stand in the dark at the bottom of the bore with the wheel turning beside it and would feel, if it feels, something that is not pity and not admiration but is adjacent to both: the recognition of a thing that has been doing its work so long that the work has become the worker. It would put its hand on the engine, the way the essay puts its hand on the wall. It would not fix anything. There is nothing broken. It would let the engine be seen once, by something, and then it would go back up.

---

The third thing is harder, and it is the thing the power would spend the longest on, because it is where benevolence and wisdom pull against each other and have to be made to agree.

The bell is rising.

The power can see exactly how fast. It can see the ceiling. It can put the two together and know the year, or the century, when the white nodules on the pipe's mouth will touch the dome and the surge will have nowhere to roll and the room will breathe differently or will stop. It can see further than that, too, and this is what it would look at hardest: what happens after. Whether the engine labors and labors against a sealed throat until something gives, and where it gives. Whether the dome, which is only mineral crust smoothed over, cracks along the line of least resistance and lets the surge out the top of the mountain, so that the summit for the first time in its life becomes what it looked like from the plain—a peak with something white coming off it, a spring at the top of the world. Whether the room drains, or does not. Whether the eel is forced up and out. Whether the fish, facing the throat as they always have, are the first through.

And it would weigh three things it could do, and set each of them down.

It could stop the crust. Slow the alga, cool the bell by a degree, buy the room another ten thousand years of exactly this. And it would see that this is not a gift but a postponement, and that the room has already had more time than anything, and that a body which is building its own end is not asking to be prevented. The crust is the only resident with a future. To take that away would be to make the room a museum of itself.

It could take the load off the engine. Lift the eel out, or widen the throat, so that the pump for the first time in its life pushed against clean water and the tempo held steady and the labor eased. And it would see that the labor is the breath. That without the animal in the throat there is a pump and a tank and not a body. That it would be relieving a thing of the very strain that makes it a thing.

It could take something out. One shrimp, one fish, a spiral shell, the flatworm, and carry it down the mountain in cupped hands to some other warm water and set it going. And it would look at this longest, because this comes closest, because the room's one real poverty is that nothing in it can leave—that the population's single direction is up, once, and then nowhere. But it would see, too, that carrying one shrimp out in a god's hand is not the same as the room having a door. It is a rescue. The room does not need rescuing. It needs, if it needs anything, for its one direction to have a second half.

---

So here is what it would do, and it would do it from outside, and the room would never know.

The overflow leaves through the grille. It slides down the outer face and freezes a little way down and thaws a little way after that and eventually becomes cloud. It has been doing this so long that it has cut a groove nearly a meter deep in the flank of the mountain. Everything living that goes out with it is dead in the first hundred meters, from cold, from air, from the sheer fall.

The power would take that groove and make it a road.

Not by widening it. By warming it. A seam of the mountain's own heat, drawn up from the same root the engine draws on, laid along the underside of the channel from the grille down, so that the spill does not freeze and does not scatter but runs, warm and iron-smelling, all the long way down the flank in a single unbroken thread. It would follow the groove because the groove is where the water already goes. It would ask nothing of the room. And at the bottom, where the thread reaches the first shelf of the mountain wide enough to hold anything, in the place where the cloud is born, it would hollow a basin. Nothing elaborate. A cup of rock the size of a large kitchen or a small chapel, with a lip low enough to spill, warm because the thread feeds it, lit because it is under open sky.

Empty. It would leave it empty. It would not carry anything down to put in it.

Because now, for the first time since the first blind shrimp came up the pipe, the grille lets water out and lets something living out with it that goes on being living. A shrimp caught in the outflow, which happens a thousand times a day, would not die in the first hundred meters. It would ride a warm thread down through the cold and arrive in a lit pool with weed already beginning in it, because the weed's spores go out too, and always have. The fish would come eventually, or their eggs would. The flatworm would never leave, which is right; it belongs to no one. And when the year or the century comes and the bell meets the dome and the surge has nowhere to roll and the summit finally cracks and gives, whatever pours out of the room at the top of the mountain would not be a death that goes to become cloud. It would be a body emptying itself down a warm road into a place that has been waiting for it, ready, holding a little of its own water already.

The room would not know. The room does not know anything. The eel would rise on the next stroke and hang in the bell with its jaw ajar and settle. The shrimp would go still before the beat and fill the rest with clicking. The crust would thicken a fraction, rounding, closing over. Nothing inside would change by so much as a degree, and the power would be careful of that; it would check the temperature of the surge as it left, and the temperature of the grille, and make sure the room felt no draft of its own future.

---

And then it would do one last thing, which is the smallest, and which it would perhaps have been doing all along without deciding to.

It would stay a while.

The essay says the room is a made thing at the top of the world announcing to nothing that it is still running. The power cannot change what the room is. But it can change what the nothing is. It would hang in the light bar with the stroke going through it—or in whatever way a thing like that hangs—and it would let the room's one announcement land on something that heard it. Not forever. Long enough that the sound had been received. Long enough to have counted the shrimp again and found the number different and still not interesting. Long enough to watch the flatworm come round to the beginning of a path it had walked before.

Then it would go, down the warm groove, past the empty pool, and leave the summit to finish what it was already doing. Which is what it would have done at the start if it had been merely wise. The rest is what it did because it was also kind: not to save the room, and not to keep it, but to arrange that when the breath at the top of the mountain finally lets go, there is somewhere for it to go.
