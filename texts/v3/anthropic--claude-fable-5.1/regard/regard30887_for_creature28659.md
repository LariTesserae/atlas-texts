It notices the place the way you notice, in a house full of people, that one of them has stopped breathing to listen.

It does not come down the steps. It has no need of the steps; it comes in the way the light comes, from everywhere at once and from no particular direction, and it is there on the terraces before anything in the hollow could have been said to see it arrive. The wrens do not turn. The drop on the underside of the lintel does not fall early. This is the first thing it does and it does it on purpose: it arrives as a thing that is not an event.

Then it counts, because the place asks to be counted. Forty-one wrens. Eleven toads. The slow-worm, the stoat, the heron, the badger in its black room with its hands on its knees. The snails it does not count, because it could and because that would be a kind of rudeness, like reading a letter over someone's shoulder simply because you are able to. It goes into every chamber and stands in the pale light that comes from nowhere and looks at what is sitting on the ledge, and in each one it stays long enough to know the face, and then it comes out, and it does not touch anything, and it sets nothing down.

Then it does the thing the visitor could not do, which is stay.

Not for a day. It stays for the length of time it takes to be sure. Long enough that if the frost were going to thicken by the width of one crystal it would have thickened. Long enough to know the eleven toads apart, which is a thing it turns out can be done, and to know that the stoat has three holes it goes into and not four, and that the drop on the lintel on the south side of the fifth tier falls at an interval that is not quite the interval of the others and never will be. Long enough to know that the moss on the shins of the one in the third chamber on the east is exactly as high as it was, to the hair. It stays until it is certain that nothing here is dying slowly and calling it stillness. Nothing is. What has been arrived at has been arrived at.

And then it thinks about what it might do, and it does what a wise thing does with that question, which is first to take up every wrong answer and hold it for a while and feel its weight.

It could warm the place. That is the first and easiest kindness and it is a lie. A well of cold is a hard thing to look at and the hand wants to bring a coal to it. But the cold is the house. Warm the house and the water lets go of the stone and the cisterns drop; the saxifrage goes over in a night; the cord on the shelf turns to fibre and the fibre turns to dust; the snails wake into hunger in a town where there is nothing to eat, and the wrens wake into it, and the stoat's going from hole to hole becomes what it looks like. Warmth here would not be a gift. It would be a sacking.

It could lift her out. Take her from the coping stone and set her on the pasture in the loud wind with the thorn trees leaning and the whole flat bright country in front of her. And she would open her eyes there — they are already open; she would *see* there — and behind her, under the ground with no rise or marker, the water would be going over the rim into the courtyard, and the wrens would be shifting, and the town she had held for as long as there has been a sea would be beginning to have a history without her in it. That is not a rescue. That is a theft with the victim carried off along with the goods.

It could speak. One word. In this air the word would go a foot from its mouth and stop, but she would have heard it, and it would have been the first thing said in the hollow since the last course went on the last wall, and it would have been said *to* her. She would have to answer it or not answer it. Either is a change. It considers how you feel when you are threading a needle and somebody says your name, and it puts the word away.

It could take her place. Come down to the cistern, sit on the south side where the frost is not, put its own hand flat on the coping and its own first finger to the water, and hold the level, so that she could take her hand out. This is the one it holds longest, because it looks so nearly right. But it has stood in the courtyard and looked at her face, and her face is not weary. It is turned a little down toward the hand the way a face turns toward careful work — the face of someone marking a line, feeling for true with the flat of the palm. You do not take a tool out of hands that are using it. You do not say to someone at the last finger's width of a piece of work, *here, let me.* Not if you are wise. Not even if you are kind.

So it sits with what is actually the matter, which is not the cold and not the silence and not the sitting.

The others agreed. All of them. They finished and they went in and they sat down, and whatever they are now, they are it together, and the moss has come up their shins and stopped, and there is nothing in any chamber that asks for anything. She agreed too. She was one of them; she has their face. But she did not get what they got. She got the finger's width instead. Whether she took that or was left with it or simply *was* it, the place will not say and she cannot say, and this is the thing the power finally arrives at, standing on the fourth tier by the shelf with the mallet: she cannot say because she cannot move, and she cannot move because the water depends on her, and so whatever she wants — to keep the office forever, to stand up this instant, to sit one more age and then decide — the wanting cannot be found out. Not by anyone standing over her. Not by her. The level has closed over her like the ground closing over the hollow, and inside it there is a person, and nobody, including the person, can know what the person would do if the level did not need her.

That is the wrong in the place. Not the stillness. The stillness is chosen and shared and entire. The wrong is that one part of it is held by a hand that has never been allowed to find out whether it wants to be holding.

So it does the one thing it does.

It goes into the stone. Not the woman — the stone, the fall of the channels, the cold that wrings the air. It takes a very long time about it, longer than the staying took, because the thing has to be done to a tolerance finer than the cut of the channels, and the channels were cut by people who knew stone the way they knew their hands. It alters the fall of one channel on the third tier by less than the thickness of a hair. It teaches the cold, on the north side of the last courtyard, to hold to a line. It does whatever must be done so that the water in the great cistern would stand a finger's width below the rim whether or not there were a finger there.

From outside nothing has changed. The water is at her finger. Her finger is at the water. The lichen stops exactly at the wet. The only difference is which of the two is holding which, and there is no way for anyone to tell that from looking, and that is the whole of the design.

It does not tell her. It does not touch her. It does not sit down across from her on the south side of the coping to see what happens, because to sit and see is to wait, and waiting is the posture of a body that expects a change, and it will not put that in the courtyard with her. It will not put anything in the courtyard with her. If it stayed, the staying would be a question, and she has had enough questions asked of her by the water.

If she lifts the hand — tomorrow, in a thousand years, at the end of the sea — she will find the water where it was. She will know then what was done and roughly when, and she will sit with that for as long as she likes, and what she does after is hers. She may put the finger back, because the work was hers and she wants to finish it herself and by her own means, and that is a right nobody can revoke. She may stand and climb the last flight and go out into the wind. She may go into a chamber and sit down with the others and let the moss come up. She may do none of these and simply stay on the coping with her hand flat and dry on the stone, no longer the level, only a woman sitting by water, which is a different thing entirely and might be the thing she wanted all along.

If she never lifts it, then the office was hers and she keeps it and nothing has been taken from her, not even the knowledge that it could have been.

It climbs out. It does this by the steps, this time, because it has the shape of a thing that leaves and it wants to leave in that shape. On the fourth tier it passes the mallet and understands, as the visitor understood, what it would be to set a thing down here; it has set nothing down and carries nothing away, and the moss at the collar of the ash handle is exactly where it was. At the lip the wind finds it and the wind is loud.

It would like to know. That is the cost, and it pays it. It goes off across the flat bright country with the thorn trees leaning east, and it does not look back, because looking back is a way of waiting too, and it has decided it will not be the thing in her life that waits. Behind it the ground closes without a rise or a marker over the town and the eleven toads and the heron on its terrace and the one hand laid flat on the stone with the tip of the first finger touching water that stands, now and for as long as she likes, exactly where she has put it.
