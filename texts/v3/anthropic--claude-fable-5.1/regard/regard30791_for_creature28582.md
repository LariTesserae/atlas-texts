It would come the way the sound comes: without direction, arriving before it is noticed, and then everywhere the same. It would not stand at the rim. It would not climb the face. A power of that kind is not somewhere in a place; it is the place's attention to itself, briefly borrowed. So it would be in the crust breaking under a boot it did not bring, and in the millimetre an hour of tea-coloured water, and in the bearing on the induced-draught fan that has begun, this week, to change colour under a man's left elbow.

And it would do, first, what the place does. It would wait. Not for effect. Because a wise power knows that most of what it might do here has already been thought of by the moss, and rejected, over a longer span than the power has been paying attention.

Consider what it would consider.

It could stop the fans. Reach into the station and still them, let the vent close, let the belt run down and the plume go thin and fall. The roar would drain out of the basin like water out of a dish and for the first time in fifty years the hare would hear its own feet. But then the town would go dark, and the six men would stand up in a room that had suddenly stopped trembling and feel, each of them, the deck go still under a ship — and the years in the floor would not stop leaving, only stop leaving here, and go instead to a pit or a well or a seam somewhere else and leave from there. The roar is not a wrong. It is a funeral that has been delayed five thousand years and is finally being held, badly, by the tonne. A wise power does not stop a funeral. It attends.

It could speak to the moss. It would try this, because benevolence wants a face to address. It would go down into the lime-green heads along the drain and look for the thing there that could be blessed or comforted or told that it had been seen. It would find water. Hollow cells built to hold it. A pump moving hydrogen ions outward and scarce minerals in. A tip lengthening. It would find no want. It would find not even the absence of want, which is a kind of peace and can be honoured, but something before want, a habit older than the capacity to mind. You cannot console a habit. You cannot reward a glacier. The power would sit in the cushion for an hour of the moss's time, which is a season, and come out understanding that the only thing this being registers as harm is interruption, and that it registers interruption by starting again, and that it does not remember having been interrupted. To help it would be to interrupt it. There is nothing to be done for it, and nothing it would notice done.

It could put the dome back. Refill the basin in a night — six metres of black paste to the old level, the birch horizon restored at head height, the road on the far rim looking across a brown-purple skin as if no face had ever been cut. And the power would know exactly what this was: a forgery. The dome was not a shape. It was a record, one page a year, and a record that appears in a night is not a record but a picture of one. Every grain of pollen in that black metre was held for four thousand years by a plant that chose, each year, to hold it. To conjure the holding without the years would be to make the moss a fraud in its own body. The moss would not object. That is precisely why the power would not do it.

So the large things fall away, one by one, and what is left is small, and specific, and at the speed of the place.

It would see the frost on the machines. That is the first thing it would do, and it would do it every night, all winter, until April. The crystals building along the harvester's upper edges and the wind taking them off the leeward side, the tiny white fur on the raised drum, the bogies going grey then white then wet then grey. Nobody sees this. It happens for no one. A benevolent power, finding a thing that happens for no one, would make itself the one, not to change it but so that it has been attended. This costs nothing and is the whole of some kinds of love.

It would leave the men their hearing. It would look at the six of them behind the double glass, feeling the plant through their cups, and see that they were not to be pitied. They live in the sound the way the bacteria live in the acid; it is their medium and they have made a life in it. The wives raising their voices, the drive to the rim on a day off to feel it through the seat and call it checking on something — these are not wounds. They are the shape of a particular kind of belonging, and a wise power does not correct belonging. It would, perhaps, do one thing. It would see to it that on the last shift, whenever that is, when the clay shows and the belt runs empty and the boiler is banked for the last time, the six of them — or their sons — are all in the room, and awake, and that the fans spin down slowly enough that they hear the note climb up out of the floor and through the bottom of hearing and into hearing and then out the top of it into nothing, and that nobody speaks. They would notice instantly if it stopped. The power would only make sure they were there to notice.

It would go to the western bays. There, in the unworked ground by the drain where the curlew try every year, three metres down in the black smooth metre where plants have stopped being plants, there is a shoulder. The power would know this, having had four thousand years to know it. Brown, tanned, the skin pressed flat, a cord, the stubble on a jaw. Kept. Not this one, or not yet, or not found — and the belt is coming, three centimetres a season, and in eleven summers the drum will pass over that shoulder and take it up and dry it and blow it into a furnace the size of a church, and the last thing that will have seen a face there will have been the bog. A wise power would not be sentimental about this. The bog was always going to let go eventually; the furnace is only the bog's own rot on a schedule. But there is a difference between being released and being disposed of, and the difference is a witness. So in the eleventh August, in a wet week when the milling is slow, it would let the drum catch. A slight resistance, a change in the note under the driver's elbow. He would stop. He would get down from the cab, which drivers do not do, and walk back along the ridge, and kneel, and put his hand into the paste, and find what the power had known was there. He would not know why he stopped. He would not need to. The face would be lifted out and taken somewhere dry and lit and looked at by people who argue about reasons, and that is a funeral too, and a better one than the belt.

And then the one act. The only one the moss would feel, if it could feel, and it cannot, and the power would do it anyway.

When the clay shows. When the last field is milled to grey and the harvesters are loaded onto low-loaders and driven to the next basin over, and the rails are lifted, and the belt is stripped off its lattice and sold, and the station stands with its two towers cold and one of them already leaning. The drains will still be there, ruled straight, and they will still run to the pump house, and the pump will still start itself when the float rises past the mark, because nobody told it to stop and it is wired to no one. This is the thing that would kill the moss — not the fire, which the moss survived by not noticing, but the quiet pump afterward, keeping the dish empty, keeping the floor dry, letting the birch come in for good and the pine after it, and the four thousand years never starting because the water never stood.

So the float would stick.

Not break. Stick. A film of iron from the tea-coloured water, a season's silt in the guide, the small ordinary failure of a small ordinary part. The lamp above the door would go on coming on at dusk for a while, for no one, and then not. The water would rise in the collecting drain past the mark and no pump would answer. It would back up the ruled lines at a millimetre an hour. It would spill over the ridges and lie in the combed fields and go still and go dark and go cold and go acid. The clay would hold it. The dish would fill.

And in the corners the harvesters could not reach, and along the toe of the face where the rills have by now made a slope, the cushions would be doing what they were doing. Red and lime-green. Lit from underneath. Holding water. Growing at the tip. Dying at the base and not letting go. They would not know that anything had been done. That is the mark of the thing being done well. The power would look once more at the frost forming on nothing, on no machines, on the heather at the rim, and then it would stop borrowing the place's attention and give it back, and the place would go on attending to itself in the only way it knows, which is to keep.
