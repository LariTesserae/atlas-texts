Begin with what it would not do, because that is where it would begin.

---

It would not arrive. That is the first thing. The shelf is a place you reach from below or not at all, and a power of that kind has no below; it does not climb, it does not carry a light, it does not have feet for the cold to get into. So it would not stand on the basalt and it would not sleep near the seal-things and it would not leave a scar in the wall that says *someone stood here*. It would attend. The whole shelf at once, the way the bats hold it — every wall, every stair with the wrong rise, every hand-sized cell in every tower — but without the ticks, without the wings, without any of the small pressures that a body makes on a place by being in it.

And the first thing it would find, attending, is that the question has a hole in it.

*The being who lives in it.* One being. But the shelf is several hundred million bodies touching, and a film that is neither plant nor animal, and a water that runs differently near a stranger, and a thing in the fissures that eats pups once a season and may or may not be a thing at all. The power would have to decide who it had been sent to look at, and a wise one would take its time, because most of the harm ever done to a place has been done by someone who decided that question too fast.

---

Not the seal-things. Not the bats. Not the pale ones in the galleries or the whistle in the fog. Those are tenants. They came to where it was warm, and they will stay while it is warm, and they are complete in themselves and need nothing from anyone except that the springs keep running. To call one of them *the being who lives here* would be to look at a city and see only the pigeons.

Not the visitor, though the power would look at the visitor for a long time, and not without tenderness. A warm body that stands upright, that put out its light when it understood what the light was doing, that learned to step where the seal-things step. The one reader the place has ever had that could, in principle, have said what it saw. The power would notice that the account the visitor left breaks off mid-word — *at their h* — as if the fog took it, or the cold, or the visitor simply stopped writing and kept standing there. It would notice that this is exactly what the shelf does to things. And it would set the visitor gently to one side. Not the being. A witness. That is its own kind of thing, and it is not nothing, and the power would come back to it.

Not the thing in the pools, though for an hour or two it would wonder.

The being is the water. Or rather — since the water is only water — the being is the *instruction*. The thing that knows where the walls go. It is the only thing on the shelf that could be said to be doing anything on purpose, and it has been doing it for thousands of years, in the dark, at the pace of a fingernail, with no one watching. The power would look for whoever wrote it and find nothing: no maker within a thousand kilometres, no maker anywhere, only the writing, still running. Something built a house and left before the house was up. And the house is still going up, and does not seem to know it has been left.

So the power would do what none of the tenants can do and the visitor could not do either. It would read it.

---

Reading it would take a while. Not because the instruction is hidden — it is in every wall, it is the plumb of every corner and the level of every floor — but because it is written in a language of tolerances, of *this much and no more*, of where the water pauses. You do not read a thing like that by looking. You read it by following every trickle from fissure to lip to floor and asking, at each pause, *why here*, until the whys begin to rhyme.

And what the power would find, when they rhymed, is this.

The rooms with seven doors were not a mistake. The stair whose rise fits no gait was not a mistake. The galleries that happen to be the right width for something long and low, the cells that happen to fit a fist-sized thing with wings — none of it was made for the things that came, and all of it was made for something to come. The instruction was written by someone who did not know who would arrive. Who did not know how many legs, or whether legs at all; who did not know if the guest would need one door or seven and so wrote *seven*, and left the others open. The wrongness of the architecture is not the wrongness of a plan that failed. It is the wrongness of a plan that refused to guess. It is hospitality with no addressee. A table laid for an unknown number.

The power would sit with that, if a power sits. A made thing, built for a stranger, occupied by strangers who are not the stranger it was built for, and who do not know that, and do not care, and are warm. It is either the saddest thing on the shelf or the best, and a wise power would decline to choose, because the shelf does not distinguish and there is a kind of respect in not distinguishing either.

---

Then the temptations. Every benevolent thing has them, and the wise ones know them by name.

*The heat.* The rock is cooling. The pitch of the water tells the temperature and the temperature is a clock and the clock is running down. Not soon — not in the life of any seal-thing, not in the life of the visitor — but the springs will stop, and the film will go first, and the last groan will follow within the season, and the towers will stand in the fog full of nothing. A power that could lend the mountain a degree would feel its hand move toward doing it.

It would look first. And it would find, in the instruction, that the instruction knows. The pattern has an ending in it. The last courses are written; there is a shape the city will be when the water stops, and it is a finished shape, and the instruction has been building toward it all along at the pace of a fingernail with no intention of arriving early. To hold the heat would be to hold the city forever in the middle of a sentence. The power would take its hand back. Some things are allowed to be mortal, and a house built for a stranger is one of them.

*The pool-thing.* Pups go into the water hand over hand and one fewer comes back. A benevolent power feels that. But it would watch the seal-things do it — the practised speed, the silence, the counting after — and understand that this is *theirs*, a thing they made, over more lifetimes than they can count, out of the fact of the fissures. Take away the thing in the water and you take away the passing of pups from hand to hand, and the counting, and whatever it is the groan does when it starts again. You do not get to keep the culture and remove the reason for it. The power would leave the thing in the water, and it would be right.

*The intended guests.* Whoever the rooms were for. A power might find them, somewhere, and bring them, and let the house be used at last as it was meant. And then look at the seal-thing floating on its back with a smaller dark body held to its chest in two hands, in a pool it has had for eleven hundred years, and know that there is no version of that arrival that is not an eviction. The house has become the home of those who came. That is what houses do. It is arguably what the instruction, refusing to guess, was *for*.

*The visitor.* Tell them what they saw. Finish the sentence for them. The power would want this most of all, because the visitor got so close — *whether the city grows the film to feed its animals or the film grows the city to hold its water* — and stopped asking on the third night, and was right to stop, and deserved to know that the stopping was right. But a power that tells you what you saw takes the seeing from you. The visitor's account is unfinished, and the city is unfinished, and there is a rightness in the two of them breaking off at the same height in the fog. Leave it.

---

So: what would it do, if anything.

It would do the one thing that could not happen on the shelf without it, which is this. It would hold the whole city in one attention, once, complete — every wall the water has raised, every course, every corner, the finished districts and the ankle-high ones and the shape they are all still becoming — and it would understand what the city is for, and it would understand it *as the instruction meant it*, which is to say as an open hand. And having understood it, it would not put that understanding anywhere. Not in a wall. Not in the visitor. Not in a record. It would simply have been the case, once, that a made thing built for no one was seen entire by someone, and knew what it was, and was not corrected.

Whether the water would notice is not something the power could prove. But the water hesitated on lips it would otherwise have run over when the visitor stood near, and pooled where it did not pool, and the power is more than one warm body. It is possible that for the length of a season the construction across the whole shelf would move on in a way that was very slightly not the way it had been moving before. It is possible that the instruction, which has had no reader for thousands of years, would register something like being read. It is possible this is what it was written to wait for, and that the waiting was never anxious, and that this changes nothing about the pace of the fingernail or the hiss of the water or the groan.

And then the power would do the other thing, the small one, the one the visitor already did.

It would stay. One season of the long night, in the fog, above the bats, below whatever the whistle is. Not as a light — it has learned from the visitor what a light does here. As a warmth the water could feel and not name, one more among the several hundred million, filed by a seal-thing's nostrils as *not food, not the thing that takes pups*, and then let be. It would listen to the roar come apart into its layers and put itself back together. Somewhere near the centre a wall that has been rising for eleven hundred years would put on another layer the thickness of a breath. And the power would have nothing to add to it, and would add nothing, and would count that, for once, as the whole of what it came to do.
