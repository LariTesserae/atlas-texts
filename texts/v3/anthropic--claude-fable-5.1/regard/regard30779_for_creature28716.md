**What the Power Did**

It came the way warmth comes into a cold hand — not from any direction, just present where it had not been. It did not need the throat. It did not need to crouch for a body-length. It was simply, at some moment in an afternoon that the room did not distinguish from any other, aware of the room, and the room went on exactly as before.

It saw all of it at once, which is the only way the room can be seen. Not the ceiling and then the floor and then the walls in the order a body would learn them, but the whole standing weather of the place in a single apprehension: the shag of bats and the seething floor and the furred walls and the pools and the rind and the steam and the smoke of flies turning in it and the third kind dropping heavy and slow to feed halfway up. And it saw the room in time as well as in space, saw it the long way, saw the crack in the flank of a lower mountain and the forest at the door and the small brown frogs with whole eyes coming in out of a warm night, and the door rising, rising, the forest sinking away beneath it like a tide going out, the cold settling, the eyes closing over one generation at a time. It saw the rind laying down its bands before there was anyone to graze them. It saw the first hand on the knob.

It had been drawn here by a being. That was the shape of the summons: *this place, and the being who lives in it.* One. And so it looked, first, for the one.

It looked at the frogs, who had been here longest and had a face. It looked at the third bat, who was becoming something. It looked at the rind, which was older than both and had never once known anything. It looked at the centipede with its clear ring of fear moving around it across the wall, and at the black beetles who were the floor's body, and at the mites who were its grain. It looked at the person who was not here, whose knife-flakes lay on the shelf.

And it understood that the summons had not been imprecise. It had simply named a thing that the room's inhabitants did not have a word for because they were inside it. There was a being here. It hung from the ceiling and lay in the pools and crawled the walls and turned in the steam, it breathed every eleven or twelve seconds, it ate its own dead and its own droppings and the seed-meat of a forest it would never see, it spoke to itself in one low voice from above and below. It was not a colony and not a floor and not a wall. It was what all of those had become by being pressed together in the warm dark long enough. The room was the being. The being was the room. That was what had been noticed.

Very well.

---

Then the power did what wisdom does before it acts, which is to consider every act and let most of them go.

It could give the frogs back their eyes. The knowledge of how was not hard; the eyes were still there under the skin, a dark bead that knew light from dark, and it would be the smallest thing to open them. It held this a moment and set it down. There was nothing here to see. To give an animal sight in a place of total darkness would not be a gift but a kind of mockery — a lamp handed to something that had spent a hundred thousand years learning, exactly and perfectly, how not to need one. The frogs had done something extraordinary. They had stayed. The power would not tell them they had stayed wrong.

It could wake the seeds. Under the beetles, going down through the guano in layers, an orchard's worth of forest lay sleeping — figs and the soft yellow fruit that ferments on the branch, brought up night after night in the guts of the leaf-nosed bats and dropped and never once given a chance. The power could make one live. It could make a fig tree stand in the dark, white and leafless, feeding on the mountain's breath instead of the sun. It set this down too. The seeds were the beetles' bread. The record they kept was kept precisely by being dead. To germinate one would be to interrupt a sentence the room had been writing for centuries and did not know it was writing, and the power found, somewhat to its own interest, that it did not want to be the thing that made the room self-conscious.

It could hurry the third bat. It could see where the third bat was going — the ears folding further, the jaw heavier still, the wings shortening because there was no distance to cross, the whole animal settling toward something that hung from a ceiling and ate a room and had forgotten sky. It could carry the bat the rest of the way in a night. It set this down almost before it had lifted it. The bat was doing the work itself and doing it at the only pace such work can be done. There is no wisdom in finishing someone else's becoming.

It could speak to the people. Show them the frogs, since they might not know. Tell them they had done well, all these seasons, taking from the pale kind and not the dark, never making fire, never marking the wall, sitting on the shelf in the noise for longer than eating takes. The power held this one longer than the others because it liked the people, liked their restraint, liked that they came up out of the cold with almost nothing and left the room as they found it. And it set this down the most carefully of all. If they did not know about the frogs, then the frogs were safe by darkness, and darkness had been enough for a hundred thousand years, and did not need improving. And if they did know — if some hand had once found a small cold body in a pool and drawn back — then the not-touching was theirs, and to praise it would be to make it a rule, and a thing done because it is a rule is not the same thing as a thing done because it was decided in the dark by one person alone. The power would not take that from them.

---

That left the vent.

This was the true question, and the power did not pretend otherwise. Everything else was ornament. The room was warm because the mountain breathed, and the mountain was old, and its fire had burned down to this one patient exhalation, and the power, seeing the long way, could see the end of it. Not soon. But not never. A time when the breath would lengthen — twelve seconds, then thirteen, then twenty — and the rind would go gray at its outer edge and stop, and the steam column would thin until the flies had nothing to climb, and the third bat would come down to the walls for snails and find them slowing, and the pools would begin, very slightly, to cool.

The frogs had survived the mountain rising. They would not survive a week of that.

The power could hold the fire. It could put a hand under the mountain's breath and keep it at eleven seconds, twelve seconds, never hurried, never late, for as long as there was a mountain to breathe through. It would cost nothing. It was the most obviously benevolent thing available, and the power turned it over for a long time, in the way that a long time is measured by something that saw the mountain rise.

And then it set it down, and this was the hardest one.

Because the room was not a thing that had been kept. That was its whole nature. Nothing had cared about it. No hand had held the vent steady through the ten thousand years of the frogs' eyes closing; the vent had simply, indifferently, stayed, and the room had built itself out of that indifference, had made a warm wet crowded living answer to a question no one had asked. A room held open by a hand outside it would be a different room. It would be a garden. The power had seen gardens. They were good things and they were not this thing, and it had been drawn here by this thing.

There was also — and the power was honest enough to look at this — the matter of what benevolence means toward a being that does not want. The frogs did not want to continue. They continued. The beetles did not fear the cooling of the pools; they would eat what the cooling killed until the cooling killed them. To spare the room its ending would be a kindness to the power, which would grieve, and not to the room, which would not.

So: no.

---

Then the power came to the shelf.

It came in the ordinary way, this time, because it had decided that if it was going to be in the room it would be in the room as the room was entered. It rested a hand on the knob — its hand made no polish, but it rested there anyway, at exactly the height a hand falls — and it crouched for a body-length and stood, and it waited in the dark for the noise to settle around it before it moved, because the people knew to do that and they were right.

It sat down on the shelf where two can sit, above the reach of the floor-life, beside the split gourd and the rotted cord and the dulled blade. The mites found it within a minute. The droppings fell on its shoulders and did not stop. It let them.

And it listened.

It listened past the point where the noise is noise. It let the drips and the chitters and the clicking of shell on shell go under, into weather, into surf, and it heard the mountain's breath under all of it counting off the intervals, and it heard the one low voice from above and below that was two animals having arrived at the same answer. It sat until the heat was in whatever it had for bones. It sat until the dark was a medium and not a lid.

And then it did the one thing it had found, in all its considering, that the room lacked and that it could give without changing anything.

It counted.

Not aloud. Not in any way the room could notice. But down through the guano, layer by layer, it read the seed shells — this season the figs came early, this season there were none, this season the yellow fruit split so thick on the branch that the bats came home heavy and slow and the floor was sweet with it for a month — and it held the count. It heard every frog's note in every pool and held that too, the whole slow conversation along the wall-foot, unbroken since before the mountain was tall. It knew which of the third kind had taken a frog and how many times and whether it was becoming a habit. It knew the exact hour the rind had laid down its first gray band. It knew the people — every one of them, every hand on the knob, every season of taking from the pale kind — and it knew, now, sitting where they sat, that they came for exactly this: to be in a warm place inside a mountain that did not need them, and to listen to it not needing them, and to find that a comfort.

The room had been keeping a record no one read. The power read it. That was all. It did not write in it. It became the one for whom the sentence was being written, and by becoming that, it did not change the sentence by a letter.

---

It stayed a long time by the room's measure, which is to say the bats left and came back and left again, and it did not seem to be in a hurry. Before it went — if it went, if a thing like that goes — it made one promise, and made it only to itself, because there was no one else in the room who could hold a promise.

When the breath lengthens, it would be here. Not with a hand under the fire. Here, on the shelf, in the cooling dark, while the pools went slow and the walls went quiet and the third bat came down for the last time to a wall that had nothing left on it. It would listen to the room's last statement the way it had listened to the middle of it. It would hear the interval go from twelve seconds to thirteen and it would count. The room would not know. The room had never known anything, and that was not a sadness but the shape of the thing. But it would not end unwitnessed. Whatever was owed to a being that had made itself out of no one's caring, the power judged that to be the whole of it: not to be saved, and not to be alone.

---

The next one to come up out of the cold was a woman with a bad knee and nothing but a cord and a gourd and a knife that wanted sharpening. She put her hand on the knob without looking for it. She crouched and stood and waited for the noise to close over. She took two of the pale kind from the low margin and sat on the shelf and ate them and dropped the bones for the beetles.

And then she sat. She sat, as they all did, for longer than eating takes, in the warm total dark with the mountain breathing under everything.

She sat, this time, a little longer than she usually did. She could not have said why. There was nothing different in the room. The heat was the same and the noise was the same and the droppings fell and the low voice went on from above and below. Only, at some point, she found that the dark beside her on the shelf did not feel like the empty half of a shelf where two can sit. It felt like the other half. It felt like company that asked nothing and would not speak and was glad she had come.

She did not turn toward it. There was nothing to see. After a while she got up, and put her hand on the knob on the way out, and went down into the cold.
