It would come to the rim first, because everything does. That is where the pass is, and the pass is how things come.

And from the rim it would see what you see: the white, the glare, the steam lit from within, the buildings scattered on their knobs, the walkways stitching, the birds at their intervals. And being what it is, it would see the rest at once too — the four hundred and some, the colours they have gone, the hands, the eyes going by forty, the children signing before they speak, the graveyard closing over its dead at a ruler's width a lifetime, the shapes with doorways facing the lake, the thing at the far end that surfaces when the steam is thickest. All of it in a look. It would not need the season that a visitor needs.

And the first thing it would feel, being benevolent, is the itch. The old itch of power, which is to *mend*. Here is a list, and the list writes itself: the eyes. The fingers gone to hoof. The children who cannot go over the pass without being looked at as though ill. The words in the hand-language that die with the last old scaler who knew them, unrecorded, because no one from a university has come up since the telegraph. The stoker that no one has classified. The salt in the water, which is why the mule-track exists and why, in a century or two, something louder than a mule will come up it. A benevolent power looks down into this basin and its hands, so to speak, are already moving.

And then — because it is also wise, and wisdom is mostly the habit of noticing what one is about to do — it would notice the quiet.

Not hear it. Notice it. Notice that its own arrival, if it arrived the way powers arrive, would be the loudest thing this place has ever known. That a voice from the sky, or a light, or even a shape on the pass with something to say, would go across the basin the way a baby's cry goes, thin and high over the steam, and every heron would turn its head, and every face on every walkway would turn with them, and what would be written on those faces would be the thing the children learn from the adults before they learn anything else: *this is not a thing you do.* It would understand that here, to be seen arriving is already a kind of shouting. And that whatever it meant to give, it would have to give the way the basin gives everything to itself — from above, without announcement, a thin skin at a time.

So it would come down as the water comes down. As condensation on the underside of a pipe-run. As one more surface.

---

It would take a season to learn the hands.

Not because it could not know the language in an instant — it could — but because knowing a language and having manners in it are different things, and the second cannot be taken, only kept. So it would stand at the corners of pans with its face turned up and watch the low small talk at hip height, one hand, and it would come to understand why the signs are made small: because in this light a large gesture hurts to watch, and because the other hand is always needed for something, and because there is nothing here to be heard over and so nothing here to be *emphatic* about. It would learn the shiver of the board that is about to go from ivory to nothing. It would learn the shade of orange that can be crossed barefoot. It would learn the closing sign, two fingers drawing together, and it would learn that the sign means three things, and it would be a while before it understood that the three are one.

It would learn the herons by the scale on their legs. It would sit under the buildings at the scraping and feel the not-fungus come away in sheets, warm beneath and cool above, and it would not find where it starts either, and it would find that it did not want to.

And through all of that season, the list would stay open. Each thing on it would be looked at in turn, slowly, at the pace of the place. This is what a wise power does with the itch: not scratch it, not deny it, but set it down on a stone knob that has tested stable and walk around it.

---

The eyes first, because they are the worst of it.

It would stand at the edge of a pan on the day a young pan-man does not bother with his glass, because the light is beautiful and he is nineteen, and it would watch him look out across the white with his eyes open and be glad. And it would know what that gladness is costing, down to the year. It could stop it. It could, in a breath, make every eye in the basin proof against the glare, and no one would ever again sit at the corner of a pan reading the day through their eyelids.

And then it would look at the man sitting at the corner of the pan, reading the day through his eyelids, and at the young one coming to put a hand on his shoulder.

Here is the thing it would have to be honest about, because a power that is only benevolent and not wise will tell itself the story that lets it walk away: *they have made a peace with it, the blind are valued, it is their way.* That story is true and it is also the story that has excused every cruelty a place has ever absorbed. A wise power does not get to use it. It has to sit with the plainer fact, which is that a nineteen-year-old is going blind in front of it and it could stop that, and choosing not to is a choice, and the reasons — the passing of knowledge, the value of the old, the shape of a life here — are real and are also not enough, on their own, to make the choice for it.

So it would not decide. It would ask.

It would go to the old scaler at the corner of the pan and put a hand on his shoulder, which is how you ask a question here. And the old man would lift his hand, and the power would take it, and sign into the palm, small, at hip height: *I could make it so the young keep their eyes. Should I.*

And what would come back into its own palm is the closing sign. Two fingers drawing together. *It is getting late. This has thickened. Us.*

Which is not yes and not no. Which is: you are asking whether to change the rate at which we become the floor, and the answer to that is the word for what we are.

The power would sit with that for a long time. And what it would do, in the end, is neither of the things it had laid out. It would not stop the blindness, and it would not leave it. It would slow it. Forty would become forty-five, and in a generation forty-five would become fifty, and no one would notice, because that is exactly the speed at which no one here notices anything; a change that arrives at accretion speed is not an intervention, it is weather. The smoked glass would scale over a little more slowly. The cracks in the fingertips would heal a little whiter and a little less often. Nothing anyone could point to. Nothing that would ever make a young pan-man stop coming to put his hand on an old one's shoulder. A gift given at the pace of the place, so that it becomes part of the place and not a thing that was done to it.

That is what it would learn a wise gift has to be here. Not nothing. Not a shout. A thin skin, laid on from above.

---

The words it would keep.

Not by sending a university up the pass — it would see very clearly what a university would do to the quiet, the leaning in, the *what*, the *sorry* — but by the simplest means available, which is to learn them all and not forget. Every sign in the hand-language of the Works, the drifted ones, the ones for the smell a week before a vent seals. The stoker's true name, which is very old, and which it would know without classifying. The hump that nobody remembers, which it would remember. It would hold these the way the floor holds the shapes with square corners: not to give back, not to publish, just to be the one place they are not lost. This is a small thing for a power to do and it costs nothing and it would do it without ceremony, the way the scalers lift the stokers off the flanges and set them on the walkway and lift them back.

---

The outside it would deal with once, and out beyond the rim, in the world where noise is not noticed.

Because it would see, a hundred years off, what is coming up the mule-track. The salt is lithium and the world is going to want lithium the way it wants nothing else, and one day a survey party is going to stand on the pass with their instruments and look down at this and see a resource, and behind them a road, and on the road engines that do not run at eleven strokes a minute behind stone. It would see the herons leave. It would see the sign-language fall apart in a decade under the weight of people who shout. It would see the eastern knob dynamited for a haul road because someone with a ruler did not know what a smooth white mound is.

And here, for once, it would act the way powers act — decisively, and hard — but it would do it *out there*, where it makes no sound. A richer seam, in a valley three ranges over, easier to reach. A geological survey that files the basin under a heading no one reads. A pass that stays too steep for anything but mules, because the one place it might have been widened is where the power has quietly persuaded a shoulder of rock to stay. Not forever. It would not promise itself forever; nothing here is forever, that is the whole point of here. Just another century. Another two. Time — which is the only thing the basin has ever asked of anything, and the only currency it knows how to spend.

It would buy time and it would give it at the rate the basin already uses time, which is: slowly, and without anyone knowing they had been given anything.

---

And then, one night when the steam is thickest, the back would surface at the far end of the lake.

The herons would turn. Every one of them across the whole white floor, the young ones and the one on legs like fence-posts, turning to face it. The people on the walkways would hold still in the way they have. The lamp on the turbine hall would not change. Shift-change would wait.

And the power would be standing on a walkway with its hand on a rail gone to ivory, and it would understand — not all at once but the way you understand a thing you have been looking at for a season — that it was being regarded. That the thing at the far end was looking not at the herons and not at the pans but at the one new surface in the basin, the one that had come down from the rim not long ago and had been learning the hands.

It would have known from the first look what the lake-thing is. It would not have let itself think about it. Now it would.

It would think about the humps toward the north and west rim, the ones with the longer, lower proportions and the doorway at one end facing the lake, more of them than there have been generations of the Works to build. It would think about the space that was broken into in the grandfathers' time, lined like the inside of a shell, still warm, with things in it laid down in the shape of the water. It would think about the fact that nothing here is unquiet, that there are no ghost stories, that whatever went into the ground went in the same way everything does. And it would think about what it means that a place like this has been at equilibrium for a very long time, and that equilibrium is the thing a benevolent power finds hardest to leave alone, and that it is not the first benevolent power there has ever been.

It would not need the thought finished. It would already have done the thing that finishing it would tell it to do. It would turn, like the herons, and face the far end of the lake, and hold still until the back went down.

Two old things regarding each other across the steam. The one that came and looked at the list and found the only wise thing was to become a surface. And the one that had just, this season, learned the same.

---

So this is what it would do, in the end, having done the small things.

It would walk out along the raised timber to the eastern knob, past the humps of different ages, the new ones rough, the old ones smooth as eggs and taller than a man, and it would find a place on the stable stone with nothing on it yet, and it would lie down there.

Not to die. It cannot, and that is not what the knob is for anyway. The knob is for going on being useful in a slower way.

The water would come over it in its centimetre sheet, warm, the way it comes over everything, and begin. It would not be fast. Nothing here is. But the power has time — it has just bought a great deal of it, for others, and it would find that it has enough left for this. In a year the shape would be there under a skin. In ten it would have closed. The children would be taken out to the knob and shown which mound was the school and which the first bath-house and which the one nobody remembers, and there would be a new one, and the adults would sign, small, at hip height, that they did not know who that was; and the children would put a hand on it, and feel the water running warm across the white, and that would be the closest thing to a rite, and the power lying under it would find, to its surprise, that this was the whole of what it had come to give. Not the eyes, though it slowed them. Not the century, though it bought it. Just this: one more surface for the water to run over. One more thing in the basin that had chosen not to raise its voice. One more layer in the floor that people walk on, which is what all the floor is.

And in a hundred years, when the steam is thick and the back surfaces and the herons turn, there would be — if anyone were counting, and no one is — two things in the basin that had been there before the Works and would be there after, facing each other across the white, holding still, saying nothing, because there has never once been anything to be heard over. And the closing sign, two fingers drawing together, would go on meaning what it means. *It is getting late. This has thickened.* And the third thing, which is the same thing, and which the power would by then have earned the right to be included in.
