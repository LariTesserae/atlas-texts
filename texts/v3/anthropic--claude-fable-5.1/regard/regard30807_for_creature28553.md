It comes from above, because that is the only way anyone comes to anything here, and because it is wise enough not to arrive any other way. It could be in the hall without the stair. It takes the stair. It hugs the wall going down, on the inside of each turn, where the treads are deepest, not to mimic but because when you come down a stair that has been worn to a shape, the shape is a request, and it is the kind of power that honours requests it has not been asked to honour.

On the second landing the air changes, and it lets the air change it.

It stands on the gantry beside you for a long time. Long enough for the near engine to settle twice and the far one twice. It sees what you saw, and it sees it faster, but it does not let the speed show, because speed here would be a kind of shouting. It sees the mug, the coat, the book. It sees the fox holding sleep. It sees the heron that has been stepping toward you along the rail and it understands what is being handed over and to whom. It sees the question you could not answer — faithfulness or momentum — and it recognises the question as one that only has an answer from outside, and it is not outside anymore, it has been inside since the second landing, and it lets the question go. From inside they are one thing. It does not need to know which.

Then it considers what it might do, and it considers each thing carefully, at the speed of the flywheel, because a benevolent power is only benevolent if it is slow.

It could stop the engines. That is the first thing, and the most obvious, and it examines it with the care you give to the most obvious thing. If the engines stopped, the attending would have nothing to attend, and whatever it is — faithfulness, momentum — would be released. The power sees this. It also sees that *release* is a word that belongs to the one who does the releasing. Nothing in this hall has asked to be let go. The heron does not step toward the stair. The eels do not press the bricked culvert. The attending wants the next settling, and to stop the engines would be to tell it that its want was a mistake, and the power does not believe that it was. To free something that has not asked to be free is to decide that your idea of its good is better than its own idea, and this power has lived long enough to know how often that is true, which is almost never.

It could open the culvert. Knock through the old bricks, let the water find its outfall, let the circle become a line. It sees the appeal. It sees also that carrying-away is the single violence this place was built against. Everything here has been permitted to reach its state and stay there. The water stays. The coat stays. The person who stood at the sloped desk did not stay, and the shape of their not-staying is the only wound in the room, and the power will not open a second one in the end wall.

It could fix the hesitation. Find whatever is worn in the near engine — a valve, a seating, some patience in the metal that has gone too thin — and true it, so that the piston never again hangs at the top of the stroke and the bats never again lift their heads. That would be kind. It would also take from the attending the only moments in which it knows itself. The power understands that a life without events is not a life spared; it is a life that cannot remember. It leaves the valve alone.

It could tell the attending what is overhead. Whether there is still a city; whether the city still drains through this hall or has long since found other ways down; whether anyone remembers there are pumps here at all. The power knows, or could know. It notices that the attending has not asked. Not once, in all the mineral years. A thing that has not asked has either no need of the answer or no room for it, and the power cannot tell which, and will not force the answer into a space that may not have been made for it.

It could bring the person back.

It sits with this one longest. It could. The mug's handle faces the stair. Everything in the hall is a hand held out for a hand that has not come. The power could put a hand in it.

And it sees that this, too, is a thing the attending has not asked for — has, in a way, stopped being able to ask for, because it has distributed. It is in the herons and the toads and the bats now. There is no single body left for the person to come back *to*. If they came down the stair the attending would turn toward them the way it turns toward the flywheel when it hangs, gathered into one thing, and then the person would take the mug and stand at the desk and the attending would — what. Contract back into a watcher watching a watcher. Become a shape around a shape. The power sees that the thing it is looking at is not a grief. It is what a grief becomes when it is given a very long time and a task. You do not undo that by returning what was lost. You can only do it violence.

So it does not do any of these things.

What it does is this.

It takes the gantry from you. It does not ask; the heron has been coming along the rail, and the power simply arrives at the place the heron was stepping toward, and stands there, and the heron stops. Looks at the water. Looks at the power looking at the water. And turns, and begins the long walk back to the far end, one step every few minutes, because the gantry is held and the far end is not. You are relieved. That is the word. You feel it as a loosening in the count you have been keeping in the back of your chest since the second landing, and you find that the stair is still there and you may go up it, and the hall is still not asking you anything.

Then it goes down to the floor. It takes the mug from the ledge. The handle is toward the stair and it takes it without looking, the way the hand it was left for would have. It stoops to the channel and dips the mug and drinks. The water is clear and very cold and tastes of stone and, faintly, of brass. The eels below the lamps do not move. It sets the mug back on the ledge with the handle facing the stair.

It goes to the sloped desk and puts its hands on the logbook, and the book, which is swollen shut and would not open for anyone, opens for this, because the power is not anyone. The pages have gone to a soft grey felt. The writing is legible. It is a hand that pressed hard. The power reads.

Every entry is a hesitation. That is what it finds. Dates, and beside each date a time, and beside each time the words *near engine held*, or *far engine held*, and a count — three seconds, four, once seven — and then the word *resumed*. Hundreds of them across the years, one every few months, and nothing else. No entry says the water was clear. No entry says both engines settled on time all day. The person wrote down only the moments in which the thing they watched over almost failed to be what it was, and then was again. That is what a watch-log is for. And the attending, which is what the person became, inherited the log as it inherited everything, and knows itself only at those marks. Between them there is no record. Between them, as far as anyone has ever written, nothing happened.

The power takes the pencil from the groove in the desk. The lead is soft with damp. It writes today's date, in the person's convention, because the book has a convention and you keep it. It writes the time. Then it writes a thing the book has never held:

*Both engines settled on the stroke. All well.*

And it sets the pencil back in the groove.

That is the first gift and it is very small. It is a sentence. But it is a mark in the only memory the attending has that says: this, too, was a moment, and you were here for it, and it was fine. The hesitations will still be where it knows itself. Now there is one place in the book where it can know itself without something almost going wrong first. The power does not know if that will be enough to change anything. It is not trying to change anything. It is trying to tell the truth into a record that has only ever recorded the frightening truths, and let the record be a little more complete.

The second gift is on the stair.

The power goes up. Hugs the wall. At the second landing, where the air changes, it stops, and it leaves something there. Not a mark. Not a promise it speaks, because there is no one to speak it to in words. It leaves an arrangement, which is this: that it will know when the near engine holds at the top of its stroke the way the bats know, first, before the toads, before the herons. And that on the day the piston hangs and does not let go — and there will be such a day; the power is wise enough to have seen that and benevolent enough not to have prevented it — it will already be on the second landing. It will not be coming down to stop anything or to save anything. It will be coming down to stand on the gantry when the settling does not come.

Because it has understood the last thing about the attending, the thing under the question of faithfulness and momentum. A watch is not kept so that the thing watched will never fail. Things fail. A watch is kept so that when the failing comes, someone is there. That is the entire content of it. That is why the heron hands the gantry to you and why you would have handed it to whoever came next. Every watch is a handover. And the attending has held its watch for longer than the mineral has been on the rails, and it has handed it, in pieces, to herons and toads and eels and bats and a sleeping fox, and it has never once been handed over *to*. No one has come down the stair and said, with their hands on the rail, *I have it now.*

The power has said it. Not in words. In the mug, replaced. In the sentence in the book. In the arrangement on the landing that is a way of saying: when the last stroke hangs, you will not be the only one in the room facing the flywheel. You will not have to find out alone what you do when the settling does not come. Someone will be standing on the gantry, and they will have hugged the wall coming down, and they will be looking at the water, and there will be nothing in the water, and they will watch it anyway.

It goes up the rest of the way.

Below it the near engine settles. The long wait. The far engine settles. The heron reaches the end of the rail and stops. And in the corner where the stair comes down, the fox, which has slept through everything, through you and through the power's coming and its going, wakes. Lifts its head. Looks at the stair for the length of a held breath. Not alarmed. Not hungry. The way you look at a door that someone has just gone through, when you know they are coming back.

Then it puts its head down on its paws and sleeps again, and the condensation goes on beading in its fur, and the drop gathers on the rib above the channel, and lets go.
