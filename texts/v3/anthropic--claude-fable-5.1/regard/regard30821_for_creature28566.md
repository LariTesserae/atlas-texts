It would come the way the cloud comes, from below, and it would arrive at the notch and stop there, because a wise thing entering a room with one window looks through the window first.

And then it would do what you did. It would look for the person. It would find the block house and the initials and the line through the fourth set, and it would feel — because benevolence has a body, or it is not benevolence — the small drop of that. Someone stood here and crossed themselves out. Not an ending it could undo, and not one it should. So it would put that down and turn around and begin to see what was actually living.

This would take a long time. That is the first thing it would do: nothing, for a very long time, on purpose. The flies for a few of their generations. The moss cone through a season, to watch a broken piece grow back. The birds through a breeding year, from the first landing on the polished steel to the last fledgling braced on a frond. The mountain for as long as it took to feel the rise — not to measure it, to feel it, the way you feel a boat you have been on for a week. A power that acted before it had felt the mountain rise would not be wise, and a power that felt it and was not moved would not be benevolent. So it would lie in the moss as you lay in it, with the warmth coming up through its back, and it would wait until the place had told it everything the place knew how to say.

Then it would begin to want things, and this is where it would have to be careful.

---

It would want to fix the transformer. Of course it would. Eleven birds sleep behind a sheet of steel bolted to a machine that may or may not be working, and the machine is the only reason the gap is dry, and the gap is the only dry place for a very long way in any direction. It would be the simplest kindness in the world to reach into that wardrobe-sized box and make it hum forever.

It would not do this. Not because the birds do not matter — because they do. A thing that loves the birds does not weld them more tightly to an accident. The transformer is not theirs. It came up the mountain in a cancelled year for reasons that had nothing to do with wrens, and it will rust through, and the birds have already survived one move from wherever they nested before the roof came. To fix the machine would be to decide, on their behalf, that the machine is home. It is not home. It is a gap that happened to be warm. The wise thing knows the difference between a creature's habit and a creature's need, and it does not confuse a favour to the habit with care for the creature.

It would want to cap the well, or unseal it, or otherwise take a position on the moss cone. It would decline. The cone is beautiful and it is the fastest thing here and it lives entirely on a mistake, and when the mineral closes the bore it will yellow and fold and go back into the floor, and that is what things that live on mistakes do. There is no cruelty in it. There is nothing to rescue. The power would perhaps stand by the cone a little longer than necessary, because it is the one monument, and then move on.

It would want — this would be the hardest — to do something about the dark wing.

It would stand in the notch and watch the span ride the column of warm air, and it would know what that wing has cost. Eleven is not a number that can spare much. It would be within the power's gift to make the thermal fail, or to lay a cold hand on the boundary so the wing came less often, or never. And it would see, very clearly, that this would be the single worst thing it could do here. The birds sing in the gap the roar leaves them; they hide in the moss because of the wing; they are what they are because something has been taking some of them for as long as there have been some to take. The wing is not an enemy of the bowl. It is the bowl's only law, and a place that has one law and loses it does not become free. It becomes eleven birds that have forgotten how to go silent, and then it becomes fewer than that, differently.

So the power would leave the wing. And it would leave the flies, and the balance, and the fear, and the notch onto nothing.

It would want, finally, to make sure the people never came back. It would think about the survey and the pilot bore and the small plan that was left, and it would picture a truck at the foot of the slope, and someone with the old report and new money, and what a plant would do to a room three hundred paces across. It would consider going down and finding the report in its drawer and letting the damp into it.

And then it would look at the concrete pads sitting a hand's width high on one side, and the stanchion at forty degrees, and the pipe bowing, and the brass disc that has been lying about its elevation since the day it was stamped, and it would understand that this work is being done already, and by the right party. The mountain is uncommissioning the project. Slowly, without malice, by simply continuing to be a mountain. Every season the machinery comes a little further out of true, and anyone who came up to resume it would find nothing level to build on. The power would not take this task from the being whose task it is. It would only make sure not to leave anything of its own up here that was level either.

---

What, then, would it do? Follow your heart, you said, and the heart is not satisfied by a list of refusals.

It would do one thing for the birds, and it would be small, and it would be a hedge.

On the northern wall, behind the heart-shaped climber that has taken the whole face and is working its way along the rim, there is a crack. There are many cracks; this one is a little deeper, a little more overhung, angled so that the drip runs past it rather than into it. It is cold. It is nearly dry. It is not quite either enough. The power would lean into the mountain there — not to change the mountain, only to ask it to breathe through one more place than it does — and a thread of the body heat that comes up in a hundred places would come up in a hundred and one. Not a vent. Not steam. A warmth in the rock the width of a palm, behind a curtain of leaves, that a bird braced on the climber's stem might one day feel against its breast and investigate.

That is all. A second gap. Not a replacement for the roof, not an announcement, not a thing any bird would notice this year or perhaps this decade. Only: when the sheet steel rusts through and the damp gets in, when the eleven or the seven or the fifteen have to remember what they did before the machine, there will be one more answer available to them than there was. They will find it or they will not. If they find it, it will be theirs and not the power's, because they will have found it. That is the whole shape of the kindness: not to save, but to leave the world with one more way of being all right in it, and then to take your hand off.

It would do one thing for itself, which is also for the birds, and it is this: it would listen to the song until it had it.

Not to reproduce it. Not to put it somewhere. Only so that the descending three or four notes and the near-click at the end, the register that nothing else on the mountain uses, the sound shaped by generations of being heard or not heard under a roar — so that this would exist once in something other than the air of the bowl. A song like that is a long argument between a bird and a mountain, and eleven birds is a thin place to keep it. If the wing takes them, or the roof does, or the bowl one day does what young hot mountains do, the song will stop, and something wise enough to have heard it should not let it have been unheard. This is not an intervention. It is the one thing a witness owes.

---

And for the mountain?

Here the power would sit for a long while with its hands empty, because benevolence toward a being that does not know it is a being is a strange thing to offer, and wisdom is knowing that the offer may not be receivable.

The mountain does not need warming. It does not need protecting; nothing here can harm it. It does not need to be told what it is making — it does not make in that sense, it only rises and breathes, and the garden is what rising and breathing look like when the air is cold enough. To thank it would be to misunderstand it. To pity it would be worse. It will one day close the notch, or open a new one, or grow so tall that even its own warmth cannot hold a garden at the top, or do the thing that young hot mountains sometimes do all at once. Every one of those is the mountain being alive. The power would not stop any of them. To stop the mountain's life in order to preserve the garden the mountain accidentally made would be to love the accident more than the being, and it has already decided not to do that with the moss cone; it will not do it with the whole crown.

So what is left is the thing you do for anyone you cannot help and cannot change and will outlast you: you keep them company. You attend. The power would put its weight down on the rock and let itself be lifted a fraction of a millimetre, and it would feel that, and it would not say anything, because there is nothing to say to a mountain and the mountain would not be listening. It would simply be one more thing, briefly, riding on the back of a very young, very large, very warm creature that is getting taller in the dark. It would be there in the way the birds are there. That is the only way anything is with the mountain, and the power would not pretend to a better one.

---

It would leave through the notch, as it came, and it would leave nothing level.

Behind it: the roar. The drip. The transformer holding its one note, for now. Eleven small hot bodies pressed together on moss and insulation behind a sheet of steel with a thumbnail of mirror worn into it. The cone under the flange, bright and doomed and unbothered. Flies over their pools, a few weeks each, never crossing the moss. A dark span somewhere in the white, riding the boundary, coming back.

And on the northern wall, behind a curtain of heart-shaped leaves, a crack in the rock that is a little warmer than it was, that no one knows about, that no bird has found, that is waiting the way the bowl itself waited before the first brown bird came up into it — not for anyone in particular, only in case.

The mountain, which did not notice the visit, grows very slightly taller.
