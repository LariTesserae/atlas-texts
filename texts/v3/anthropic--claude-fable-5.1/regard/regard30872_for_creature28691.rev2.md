It comes the way the salmon came: up a river that did not exist when it set out, by a route it could not afterward describe. It arrives in the dark over the fjord, where the young trees are hitting the water like felled masts, and it does not need a hood or a lantern, and it stops there for a while — a long while, by her clock, though it has other clocks — and it listens to the note.

This is the first thing a wise power does, and it is the thing benevolence is worst at. Benevolence wants to move. It sees a woman in wet wool on a bench above a floor she did not make and it wants, at once and with its whole heart, to dry the wool. To put a sun in the sky. To take the note out of the boards so she can sleep the way people sleep in places with mornings. To walk into the hut and sit down on the bench and say, plainly, in words she can hear without a hood, what the stakes mark and where the ice is going and what the thing on the slab was doing with its hand over her tally. To tell her the two before her did not die of anything she could have prevented. To tell her whether she will.

It considers each of these. It sets each of them down, one at a time, the way she sets down the boots.

The sun, because the forest is the wrong forest and would not survive being right. The geese came into the dark on purpose. The loam is warm without it. A sky with a sun in it would make this a valley like the one further down, which is exactly what the grey ones are making, in their own time, at two kilometres a decade, and to do it in an hour would be to take the job from them without asking, and it does not know that they would not mind, and it does not know that they would.

The quiet, because she has heard the quiet twice and it put her out on the terrace in her socks with her heart in her mouth. She does not want the note gone. She wants to know when it goes. That is a different thing, and a smaller one, and it will come back to it.

The explanation. It holds this one longest, because this is the one that looks most like a gift. It could tell her. It knows, or knows enough — knows what the white threads in the loam are for, knows why the birds stop at the line, knows what a stake is when the bank is gone from around it and it stands in the air being a stake. It could put all of this into her in a form she would understand, and she would understand it, and then she would have it.

And then she would have it. She would sit on the bench with the marten going over her head and know what the marten does not know, and the surveyors would come and she would have to decide whether to tell them, and if she told them the question would not wash out of them the way it always has — it would stick, and they would come back with different instruments and more of them, and the valley would become a place where something is known instead of a place where something is done. It has seen this happen to other valleys. The knowing arrives and the doing leaves, or hides, or finishes early and badly. And it looks at her not-asking — twenty-six years of it, a discipline as exact as the tally — and it understands that her not-asking is not ignorance. It is the way she keeps the fire in. She has looked straight at the thing on the slab and let it be weather because weather is what a keeper can live under, and it will not take that from her and hand her a fact in exchange.

Rescue, last, and briefly. Nobody here is drowning. She was on the rota to leave and did not leave and no one asked her to explain. It is not going to be the first.

So it does what she does. It watches and does not ask. It goes up the east side past the stakes and looks at them the way she looks at them, which is to say it acknowledges them, since there is nothing to be done to a stake. It goes up into the fog and stands with its head in the dark and looks at the wall, at the blue moving in the ice, at the line of narrow grey shapes at the working edge that are in one place and then in another with no time in between, and it recognises them — not by name, it has no name for them, but by kind. They are doing a job. The job is very old and very slow and they are not cruel about it and they are not kind about it. They cut a mark in her slab a hand's width deep and set the caribou rack upright against the alders, carefully. It weighs that *carefully* for a long time. It is the only thing in the whole valley, apart from the fire, that was done for no reason.

It does not speak to them. It is not certain they would hear, and it is certain it would not understand the answer, and it has learned not to interrupt work it does not understand.

Then it comes back down through the alders in the dark and it stands on the terrace above the hut, in among the dogs — who do not flinch from it, which it notices; they are afraid of the lines and of the place the bear went and of nothing else — and it looks at the small window with the light in it, and it asks itself what, if anything, is missing from this.

Not company. She has the marten, and the ravens who know when the boots come off, and the geese, and once a year a caribou, and the surveyors for a month at a time, and she does not seem to want more and it believes her. Not warmth. The floor is warm. Not purpose; she is the only being in the valley who is quite sure what hers is.

What is missing is very small and it takes the power a while to see it, because it is not a thing but a shape around a thing.

It is this. She keeps a tally that nobody will read. She notes the falls in a book that will go into a box under the bench beside the things of the two before her. She has told one person what she saw on the slab and that person is a marten. She does not mind this — it is not a grievance — but when she lies on the bench with the note in the boards and the roof being hammered by rain that was snow, there is a place in her that expects, quietly, without asking, that the fire will go out with her, that the last mark will be hers and unpainted, that the boat will one season come round the point and see no light and turn back, and the hut will be a thing with skids on the second terrace that the geese roost on, and the tally will be a scratch on a warm floor that the dogs walk around.

She has made her peace with that. It is a real peace and the power respects it. But peace with a thing is not the same as the thing being necessary, and this thing is not necessary, and it is within the power's reach without touching anything else in the valley at all.

So this is what it does.

It goes down the fjord in the dark, a long way, further than the bear swam, to where the boat is. It does not touch the boat. It does not touch the pilot, who has steered for the light in that window for eleven years and would steer for it if the surveys stopped, because he is that kind of man and does not need to be made into one. What it does is smaller than that. It goes to where the rotas are made — a room with daylight in it, a list, a set of names — and it does not alter the list. It only stands beside it for a moment, the way it stood beside the stakes, acknowledging it. And the list, being a thing in the world, is very slightly heavier afterward in the direction of continuing. The survey that would have been cancelled in three years for lack of anything to survey is not cancelled for six. In six years there is a name on it, twenty years old, someone with no particular gift and no idea why she is going, and the power has not chosen her and has not touched her and would not. It has only made sure the boat is still going when she is old enough to be on it.

What she does when she gets there is hers. She may leave. Most do. The valley makes it very easy to leave and very easy to stay and does not care which. But the fire will be in the window when the boat comes round the point, and the woman who keeps it will be seventy-six and her hands will be what they will be, and there will be someone on the deck looking at that light who has not yet decided anything.

That is the one thing. It is nearly nothing. It is the kind of act she would recognise, if she knew of it, as a stake driven into the loam: a mark that a line continues, put in ahead of time, in ground that has never had a root in it.

There is one other, and it is not really an act. It is a permission the power gives itself.

Before it leaves it goes back up onto the terrace, in the dark, in the rain that is becoming sleet, and it stands behind the hut where the slab is, and it does not step onto the slab because the slab is not its floor. It looks at the tally. The painted marks, the woman's and the two before her. And the one cut mark, deep, in something a knife will not scratch. It looks at that one a long time.

Then it goes to the window, and it looks in, at the wool steaming and the stove roaring and the marten flat along the ridge pole, and the woman asleep on the bench with the note in the boards under her, not hearing it, the way people who live by a river do not hear the river.

And it does the thing the grey one on the slab did, and the bear at the gravel did, and the ravens do, and the boat does. It looks at the fire. It lets the fire be the thing it steers by, for this one hour, in this valley where nothing else is still. It does not go in. It does not dry the wool.

It leaves the fire alone.

And on its way out, down the river, past the standing stakes with the salmon hanging behind them and the geese going up off the flats in a grey screaming surf and closing behind, it takes with it the one thing it will keep: that in a place being made for something else, by something that does not live there, the warmest thing was not the loam, and the oldest thing was not the ice, and the thing that had been waited for longest and asked for least was a woman keeping a stove in, and painting a mark when the book said a month, and not asking — and that if you wanted to know what a benevolent and wise power does when it comes to such a place, you could do worse than to watch her, and to do that, and to go.
